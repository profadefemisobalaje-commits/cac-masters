## Lecture Script

### 01. Scope, Sources and Subjects `bumper`

### 02. What International Energy and Natural Resources Law Is `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Two. In our first session I argued that this field deserves a place in your programme at every level. In this lecture I must keep that promise by showing you what the field actually is, and I propose to do it through three questions that any discipline must be able to answer about itself. What does it govern? That is the question of scope. Where do its rules come from? That is the question of sources. And who acts within it? That is the question of subjects. These may sound like the dry architecture of a syllabus, and I will not pretend that this lecture has the drama of a nationalisation dispute. But I will say plainly that students who skip this structural work spend the rest of their studies guessing. They confuse a licence with a concession, a treaty with a contract, and a recommendation with an obligation. If you master this lecture, the remaining eighteen will be considerably easier.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

By the end of this lecture you should be able to do six things. First, to define the material and functional scope of international energy and natural resources law, and to say what falls outside it. Second, to distinguish the upstream, midstream, downstream and end-of-life stages of the resource chain, and to explain why that apparently commercial distinction carries real legal consequences. Third, to enumerate the sources of the field and, more importantly, to explain how they are ranked when they conflict, because conflict is the normal condition here rather than the exception. Fourth, to explain the role of contracts, standard forms and technical standards as sources, not merely as documents. Fifth, to identify the full range of subjects that act within the field, including the non-state actors who in practice do much of the governing. And sixth, to take this three-part framework and apply it to a resource dispute you have never seen before, which is the only test that matters.

### 04. Scope: What the Field Governs `section`

**Narration**

Let us begin with scope. What, precisely, does this field govern?

### 05. Every Resource Dispute Asks Four Questions `bullets`

- Who owns the resource? — title, sovereignty, tenure, customary and indigenous rights.
- Who may develop it? — licensing, concessions, qualification, national participation.
- On what terms? — fiscal regime, stabilisation, local content, environmental and social obligations.
- Who bears the loss? — expropriation, force majeure, change in law, decommissioning, remediation.

*On-screen note:* Scope is defined by function, not by a list of commodities.

**Narration**

A useful way to define the scope of the field is not by listing commodities, because any such list will be out of date within a decade, but by identifying the questions the field exists to answer. In my experience there are four. The first is: who owns the resource? That question engages the doctrine of permanent sovereignty, the constitutional allocation of resource ownership within a state, land tenure systems, and, increasingly, the rights of indigenous peoples whose territories contain the deposit. The second is: who may develop it? That question engages licensing regimes, concession awards, qualification criteria, and the requirement of national participation through state companies or carried interests. The third is: on what terms? That question engages the fiscal regime, the stabilisation and renegotiation clauses, local content and technology transfer obligations, and the whole body of environmental and social regulation. And the fourth is: who bears the loss when something goes wrong? That question engages expropriation and compensation, force majeure, change in law, decommissioning liability, and remediation of legacy contamination. Notice that these four questions can be asked of oil, of gas, of copper, of lithium, of timber, of fish, of fresh water, and of the electromagnetic spectrum. That is why the field holds together as a field.

### 06. Upstream, Midstream, Downstream — and What Comes After `diagram`

- Upstream — exploration, appraisal, field development, extraction. Title, licensing, concession, unitisation.
- Midstream — gathering, processing, pipelines, shipping, storage. Transit, third-party access, tariff regulation.
- Downstream — refining, marketing, distribution and retail. Competition law, product standards, price regulation.
- End of life — decommissioning, plugging, mine closure, remediation. Security for abandonment, legacy liability.

**Narration**

The second dimension of scope is the resource chain, and you must know its vocabulary precisely, because contracts, statutes and treaties all use it and all assume you understand it. Upstream covers exploration, appraisal, field development and extraction. It is the stage at which title to the resource is most contested and the state's role is most direct, and it is governed by mining codes, petroleum laws, concession and production sharing agreements, and unitisation agreements where a reservoir straddles a boundary. Midstream covers gathering, processing, transport by pipeline or ship, and storage. Its characteristic legal problems are transit rights, third-party access to infrastructure, tariff regulation and the security of supply obligations that bind exporters and importers alike. Downstream covers refining, marketing, distribution and retail, and here the dominant legal registers are competition law, product standards and consumer protection, with price regulation in many jurisdictions. And then there is a fourth stage that older textbooks omit and that you will spend a great deal of your career on: the end of life. Decommissioning, well plugging, mine closure, tailings management and remediation. The legal questions at this stage are mercilessly practical. Who is liable when the company that caused the harm has been sold, dissolved, or succeeded by an entity with no assets? Who holds the abandonment security, and is it enough? These are not fashionable questions, but they are where the money is now, and they will be where the litigation is for the next thirty years.

### 07. Where in the World the Field Operates `bullets`

- Land territory: the great bulk of mining, onshore oil and gas, forestry and freshwater, governed primarily by national law.
- Territorial sea and continental shelf: coastal state sovereign rights over living and non-living resources under UNCLOS.
- Exclusive economic zone: sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing natural resources.
- The Area: seabed beyond national jurisdiction, held as the common heritage of mankind and administered by the International Seabed Authority.
- Shared and global spaces: transboundary aquifers, international watercourses, straddling fish stocks, Antarctica, and the orbital positions on which grids and satellites depend.

*On-screen note:* The same commodity is governed by entirely different rules depending on where it sits.

**Narration**

There is one further dimension of scope that students routinely overlook, and it is spatial. The same commodity is governed by entirely different legal rules depending on where it happens to sit, and a lawyer who does not ask that question first will take the wrong advice. On land territory, which still accounts for the great bulk of mining, onshore oil and gas, forestry and freshwater, national law is primary and international law enters at the margins: through investment treaties, through human rights instruments, through the law of state responsibility. Move seaward and the picture changes completely. In the territorial sea, the coastal state has sovereignty, subject to innocent passage. In the exclusive economic zone, the coastal state has sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing natural resources, both living and non-living, and those rights are conferred by treaty rather than byTerritory. On the continental shelf, the coastal state has sovereign rights over the mineral resources of the seabed and subsoil, and the shelf may extend well beyond two hundred nautical miles where the geology permits. Beyond all national jurisdiction lies the Area: the seabed and ocean floor and its subsoil, declared the common heritage of mankind and administered by the International Seabed Authority, whose regime for deep seabed mining has been under intense negotiation and produced a significant advisory opinion from the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in 2024. And then there are the shared and global spaces that resist bilateral allocation altogether: transboundary aquifers, international watercourses, straddling and highly migratory fish stocks, the Antarctic continent under its own treaty system, and the orbital positions and radio spectrum on which modern grids and satellites depend. Notice the pattern. As you move from national territory toward global commons, the role of national law recedes and the role of treaty and institution grows. Knowing which space your resource occupies is therefore not a preliminary detail. It determines the entire applicable regime.

### 08. Sources: Where the Rules Come From `section`

**Narration**

The second structural question is sources. Where do the rules of this field come from, and how do we rank them?

### 09. Article 38 Is Necessary but Nowhere Near Sufficient `bullets`

- Treaties: global and regional conventions, bilateral investment treaties, transit and interconnection protocols.
- Customary international law: permanent sovereignty, expropriation and compensation, state responsibility.
- General principles: good faith, unjust enrichment, pacta sunt servanda, abuse of rights.
- Judicial and arbitral decisions, and the writings of publicists, as subsidiary means.

*On-screen note:* The formal list tells you what counts as law. It does not tell you what decides the case.

**Narration**

Every student of international law meets Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, and every student should know that in this field Article 38 is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. Treaties supply a great deal: the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, the Energy Charter Treaty, the climate regime, thousands of bilateral investment treaties, and the dense network of transit, interconnection and power pool protocols that bind states regionally. Customary international law supplies the doctrine of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, the rules on expropriation and compensation, and the law of state responsibility, all of which remain contested at the edges in ways that matter enormously in practice. General principles supply good faith, pacta sunt servanda, unjust enrichment and the prohibition of abuse of rights, and these do real work in arbitral reasoning. Judicial and arbitral decisions and the writings of publicists are formally subsidiary, but if you want to know what a tribunal will actually decide about a stabilisation clause, you will read the awards. The honest summary is that the formal list tells you what counts as law, while the real sources tell you what decides the case. Let us now look at those.

### 10. When Sources Collide, What Actually Decides? `bullets`

- Within a national order there is a hierarchy: constitution, statute, regulation, and then the contract, which cannot authorise what the law forbids.
- Between treaties, tribunals reach for lex specialis and lex posterior, and increasingly for express conflict clauses in the instruments themselves.
- Between treaty and contract, the distinction drawn in 1952 still governs: a breach of contract is not automatically a breach of treaty.
- Between national law and international law, stabilisation clauses attempt to freeze the former, and are the site of the field's bitterest arguments.

*On-screen note:* There is no master rule. There is a set of argumentative techniques and a forum that will choose between them.

**Narration**

The final question about sources is the only one that matters in a dispute, and it is this: when two sources collide, what decides? I want to be candid with you, because the honest answer is that there is no master rule, and any textbook that tells you otherwise is simplifying to the point of distortion. What exists instead is a set of argumentative techniques and a forum that will choose between them. Consider the four collisions you will actually meet. First, within a single national order, there is usually a clear hierarchy: the constitution, then statute, then regulation, and then the contract, which cannot validly authorise what the law forbids. That one is easy, and it is the only one that is. Second, between two treaties, tribunals reach for the maxims, lex specialis and lex posterior, and increasingly they look for express conflict clauses inside the instruments themselves, because modern treaties anticipate collision and older ones did not. Third, between a treaty and a contract, the distinction drawn by the International Court in 1952 still governs: a breach of contract is not automatically a breach of treaty, and elevating the one into the other requires an independent jurisdictional route, such as an umbrella clause or a denial of justice. Fourth, and most bitterly contested, between national law and international law, the stabilisation clause attempts to freeze the national law applicable to the investment at the moment of the bargain. Whether it can do so, whether it should, and what happens when the host state's constitution forbids it, is the single most argued question in the modern law of state contracts. When you read the awards, what you are watching is not the application of a hierarchy. It is tribunals choosing among techniques, and defending the choice.

### 11. Hard Law, Soft Law, and the Grey Zone Between Them `two-col`

**HARD LAW**
- Multilateral and bilateral treaties
- Customary international law
- National constitutions, statutes and regulations
- Contracts: concessions, PSAs, offtake, transit
- Binding arbitral awards between the parties

**SOFT LAW AND STANDARDS**
- General Assembly resolutions on permanent sovereignty
- IEA, IRENA and UN guidance and model agreements
- Equator Principles and IFC Performance Standards
- ISO and API technical standards incorporated by contract
- Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative rules

**Narration**

The third point about sources is that the boundary between binding and non-binding is far more porous than the textbooks suggest, and that in this field soft law frequently binds harder than hard law. On the left are the formally binding materials: treaties, custom, national constitutions and statutes and regulations, contracts, and arbitral awards which bind the parties and no one else. On the right are the materials that are formally not binding at all. General Assembly resolutions on permanent sovereignty are resolutions. Guidance from the International Energy Agency and the International Renewable Energy Agency is guidance. The Equator Principles are a private banking initiative. The performance standards of the International Finance Corporation are internal policies of one institution. The technical standards of the International Organization for Standardization and the American Petroleum Institute are voluntary. And the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative is a multi-stakeholder compact. Now notice what happens in practice. A project financier makes adherence to the Equator Principles a condition of lending. A contract incorporates the current edition of an API standard, so that a voluntary standard becomes a contractual obligation and its revision becomes a change-in-law dispute. A host state's constitution requires that resource contracts be published, and the transparency initiative supplies the mechanism. The result is that formally soft instruments are routinely hardened by contract, by finance, and by domestic law. A lawyer who dismisses soft law as irrelevant will lose cases that a careful lawyer wins.

### 12. In Practice, the Contract Is the Constitution of the Project `bullets`

- Standard forms circulate globally: model production sharing contracts, joint operating agreements, offtake templates.
- Many standard forms were drafted for a different commodity, a different price environment and a different politics.
- Stabilisation, renegotiation and change-in-law clauses allocate regulatory risk between investor and state.
- Boilerplate is not neutral: it silently distributes billions of dollars and decades of risk.

*On-screen note:* Read the definitions clause first. Then read the fiscal schedule. Then read everything else.

**Narration**

The fourth point, and I would argue the most important practical point in this lecture, is that for the working lawyer the contract is the constitution of the project. Standard forms circulate globally: model production sharing contracts, joint operating agreements, unitisation and unit operating agreements, offtake and tolling templates, pipeline transportation agreements. This is efficient, and it is also a source of profound path dependence, because many of these forms were drafted for a different commodity, in a different price environment, under a different political settlement. A production sharing contract template drafted for offshore oil in the nineteen seventies is now being used, with modest amendments, for onshore gas, for geothermal, and for carbon storage. That should worry you. Within those documents, the clauses that matter most are the ones that allocate regulatory risk: the stabilisation clause, by which the state undertakes not to alter the bargain; the renegotiation clause, which concedes that it will; and the change-in-law clause, which decides who pays when it does. My advice to you as a student of these documents is simple and unsentimental. Read the definitions clause first, because every other clause depends on it. Read the fiscal schedule second, because that is where the money is. And then read everything else, because boilerplate is not neutral. It silently distributes billions of dollars and decades of risk, and it does so in language designed to be skipped. Let me make this concrete, because it is the most practical advice I can give you in this lecture. When you open a production sharing contract, read it in a fixed order and never deviate. Begin with the definitions, because terms such as petroleum, development area, costs and profit will determine the operation of every clause that follows, and because draftsmen bury consequences in definitions precisely where readers skim. Read the fiscal schedule next: the royalty, the cost recovery ceiling, the profit split, any ring-fencing provisions, and any bonus or signature payments. Read the work and expenditure obligations next, because they tell you what the contractor is actually committed to do rather than what it hopes to do. Then read the stabilisation, renegotiation and change-in-law clauses as a single group, since together they allocate the risk of government conduct, and reading any one of them in isolation will mislead you. Only then read the rest, including the dispute resolution clause, which is where your own professional life will be spent. I would add two further observations. The first is that the draftsman of a standard form is, functionally, a legislator: the choices made in a model contract are applied to hundreds of projects and to dozens of countries, and they allocate wealth more decisively than most statutes. The second is that you are not dependent on private libraries to study these documents. Contract disclosure under the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, national publication requirements, and public repositories of resource contracts now make it possible to compare instruments across jurisdictions as a matter of routine. That comparative work was effectively impossible a generation ago. It is open to you now, and I urge you to do it.

### 13. Subjects: Who Acts in This Field `section`

**Narration**

The third and final structural question is subjects. Who, exactly, acts within this field?

### 14. States Are Necessary but No Longer Sufficient `bullets`

- States: as sovereign, as regulator, as resource owner, and frequently as commercial party through a national company.
- International organisations: the IEA, OPEC, IRENA, UNCTAD, the IAEA, regional economic communities.
- Companies: international oil companies, national oil companies, majors, independents, traders, miners, utilities.
- Non-state actors: lenders and export credit agencies, insurers, certification bodies, non-governmental organisations.
- Peoples: indigenous communities, project-affected populations, and future generations represented indirectly.

**Narration**

The classical answer is that the subjects of international law are states, and states remain indispensable here, but they are no longer sufficient to describe the field. Consider the multiplicity of roles a single state plays. It is sovereign. It is regulator. It is frequently the constitutional owner of the resource. And it is also, through a national oil or mining company, a commercial party with its own balance sheet and its own interests, which are not always identical to those of the ministry that regulates it. Beyond states sit international organisations, each with a distinct function: the International Energy Agency as the forum of consumers, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries as a producer forum, the International Renewable Energy Agency for the transition, the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development for investment and development policy, the International Atomic Energy Agency for the nuclear fuel cycle, and regional economic communities for power pools and transit. Then sit the companies, and here the taxonomy matters: international oil companies and national oil companies, the majors and the independents, commodity traders, diversified miners, junior explorers, utilities and grid operators. Then sit the non-state actors who increasingly determine outcomes: project lenders and export credit agencies, insurers and reinsurers, certification and standards bodies, and non-governmental organisations with the capacity to delay or derail a project. And finally sit peoples: indigenous communities with rights recognised in international instruments, project-affected populations, and future generations, who are present in this field only through the advocacy of others. A lawyer who cannot map the interests of all five categories will be repeatedly surprised by events.

### 15. A State Contract Rooted in the International Economic Order `case`

*Texaco Overseas Petroleum Company and California Asiatic Oil Company v. Government of the Libyan Arab Republic (1977) — sole arbitrator, Professor René-Jean Dupuy*

Following Libya's nationalisation measures of 1973 and 1974, the claimants invoked arbitration clauses in their 1955 Deeds of Concession, as amended in 1963. Libya argued that nationalisation was an act of sovereignty which no tribunal could review. In his award on the merits, Dupuy held that the concession was in principle governed by Libyan law, but that Libyan law had to be applied in conformity with international law, because the contract was rooted in the international economic order. He further held that although nationalisation is a lawful exercise of sovereignty, Libya had, in the Deeds, undertaken obligations whose breach engaged its international responsibility, and ordered restitutio in integrum.

> **Holding / significance.** The 'internationalisation' thesis: a state contract with a foreign investor is not wholly submerged in the municipal law of the host state. Sovereignty does not excuse a breach of an undertaking voluntarily given.

**Narration**

Our case for this lecture is one of the most influential arbitral awards ever rendered, and also one of the most criticized: the Texaco Overseas Petroleum Company and California Asiatic Oil Company against Libya, decided in 1977 by a sole arbitrator, Professor René-Jean Dupuy. The facts are uncomplicated. Libya nationalised the claimants' concession interests in 1973 and 1974. The claimants invoked the arbitration clauses in their Deeds of Concession of 1955, as amended in 1963. Libya's answer was that nationalisation is an act of sovereignty, and that a sovereign act is not reviewable by a tribunal. Dupuy's reasoning is where the interest lies. He held, first, that the concession was in principle governed by Libyan law, which was a concession to the host state's position. But he immediately qualified it: Libyan law had to be applied in conformity with international law, because the contract was rooted in the international economic order. That sentence is the origin of what scholars call the internationalisation thesis, and it has been argued over ever since. He held, second, that nationalisation is indeed a lawful exercise of sovereignty, which was a concession to the same position from the other direction. But he held, third, that Libya had undertaken obligations in the Deeds whose breach engaged its international responsibility, and he ordered restitutio in integrum: restoration of performance rather than payment of damages. Libya did not comply, and the matter was ultimately settled. Now, what should you take from this? Three things. First, that the applicable law question in state contracts is genuinely unsettled, and the answer has varied across decades and tribunals. Second, that the remedy question, restitution or compensation, is often more consequential than the liability question. And third, that a sole arbitrator deciding a single dispute can reshape the doctrine of an entire field, which is precisely why the legitimacy of this system is contested and why you should study it sceptically.

### 16. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**
- Memorise the four questions and the four stages; use them to organise any case you read.
- Practise classifying a document: is it a treaty, a statute, a contract, a standard, or guidance?
- Take one standard form and locate its stabilisation, renegotiation and change-in-law clauses.
- Build a glossary. Precision in this field is not pedantry; it is competence.

**Postgraduate level**
- Critique the internationalisation thesis against the later awards and the literature on the applicable law.
- Analyse how soft law is hardened through contract, finance and domestic incorporation.
- Assess whether the five-category map of subjects is adequate, and what it excludes.
- Design a methodology for studying standard forms comparatively, across jurisdictions and commodities.

**Narration**

Let me translate this into your programme. At undergraduate level, I want you to leave with two organising devices that you can apply cold to any case: the four questions of ownership, authorisation, terms and loss, and the four stages of upstream, midstream, downstream and end of life. Use them to take notes, because a case that is organised is a case that is half understood. Practise classifying documents, and do it out loud until it is automatic: is this a treaty, a statute, a regulation, a contract, a standard, or guidance, and what follows from the classification? Take one standard form contract, which your library or a public registry will give you, and find its stabilisation clause, its renegotiation clause and its change-in-law clause. And build a glossary as you go. In this field, precision in vocabulary is not pedantry. A lawyer who calls a licence a lease, or a concession a treaty, will be corrected in the first five minutes of a negotiation, and will not be taken seriously afterwards. At postgraduate level, I want critique. Take the internationalisation thesis from our case and test it against the awards that followed and against the scholarly literature on the applicable law to state contracts, and ask whether Dupuy's solution was principled or merely elegant. Analyse concretely how formally soft instruments are hardened through contract, through finance conditions and through domestic incorporation, and produce a framework for predicting when that will happen. Assess whether my five-category map of subjects is adequate, and identify what it excludes, because every taxonomy has a politics. And design for yourself a methodology for studying standard forms comparatively, across jurisdictions and across commodities, because that comparative work is where the publishable scholarship in this field currently lives.

### 17. What We Have Established `summary`

- Scope is functional: the field answers four questions — ownership, authorisation, terms, and loss.
- The resource chain runs upstream, midstream, downstream and end of life, each with its own legal regime.
- Sources include Article 38 materials, but also contracts, standard forms and hardened soft law.
- The contract is the constitution of the project, and its boilerplate allocates enormous risk.
- Subjects extend well beyond states to organisations, companies, financiers, civil society and peoples.

> **The one thing to remember.** Scope tells you what to ask, sources tell you where to look, and subjects tell you whose interests are in the room. Every case becomes tractable once you know all three.

**Narration**

Let me gather this together. Scope: the field is defined functionally rather than by a list of commodities, and it exists to answer four questions about ownership, authorisation, terms and loss. The resource chain runs from upstream through midstream and downstream to an end-of-life stage that the older literature ignored and that now generates much of the work. Sources: the Article 38 materials are necessary but insufficient, because in practice the operative rules are found in contracts, in standard forms, and in soft law that has been hardened by contract, by finance and by domestic law. Subjects: states remain indispensable but no longer sufficient, and the cast now includes international organisations, companies of several distinct types, financiers and insurers, certification bodies, civil society, and peoples, including indigenous communities and the interests of future generations. Our case, Texaco against Libya, showed how a single award can restructure the doctrine of the applicable law to state contracts, and how the remedy question can matter more than the liability question. In the next lecture we turn to the doctrine at the heart of the field, the permanent sovereignty of peoples and nations over their natural wealth and resources, and we ask what it means to hold sovereignty as a trust rather than as a licence.
