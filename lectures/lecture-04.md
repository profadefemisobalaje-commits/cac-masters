### 01. The Global Energy Order `bumper`

### 02. The Global Energy Order `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Four. Over the last two lectures we have been working inside doctrine: scope, sources, and the sovereignty of peoples over their resources. In this lecture we step back and look at the system as a whole. I want to ask what is, in my view, the most intellectually demanding question in the entire field, and it is simply this: is the global energy order governed by law, or is it merely governed by power, with law deployed afterwards to justify whatever the balance of power produced? I do not intend to answer that question for you. I intend to make sure that by the end of this lecture you understand it well enough to answer it for yourself, and to defend the answer. To do that we need to understand three things: the architecture of the order, the institutions built to manage it, and the points at which it is under strain. Let us begin with architecture.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes for this session. ### 04. The Architecture of the Order `section`

**Narration**

Let us begin with architecture, and with a distinction that organises everything else in this lecture.

### 05. A Physical Order and a Financial Order, Superimposed `bullets`

- Physical: reservoirs, wells, pipelines, tankers, refineries, grids, ports and the maritime chokepoints between them.
- Financial: benchmarks, futures exchanges, long-term contracts, dollar denomination, clearing and insurance.
- Law governs the physical order through property, treaty and licence; it governs the financial order through contract and regulation.
- A shock to the physical order is transmitted instantly to the financial order, and a shock to the financial order is transmitted slowly to the physical one.

*On-screen note:* Two systems, one commodity. Most legal error in this field comes from confusing them.

**Narration**

The global energy order consists of two architectures superimposed on one another, and most legal error in this field comes from confusing them. The first is physical. It consists of reservoirs and wells, of pipelines and pumping stations, of tankers and the routes they must take, of refineries configured for particular crude qualities, of storage caverns, of ports, of electricity grids and interconnectors, and of the small number of maritime chokepoints through which the great bulk of seaborne trade must pass. Law governs this physical order through property, through treaty, through licence and concession, and through the law of the sea. The second architecture is financial. It consists of price benchmarks, of futures exchanges, of long-term sale and purchase agreements linked to those benchmarks, of the denomination of trade in a small number of currencies, of clearing, of insurance, and of the credit arrangements that allow cargoes to move. Law governs this order through contract, through financial regulation, and increasingly through sanctions. Now, the relationship between the two is asymmetric, and this is the point I want you to carry away. A shock to the physical order, a pipeline closed, a strait mined, a refinery destroyed, is transmitted to the financial order within minutes. A shock to the financial order, a margin spiral, a sanction on a clearing bank, a benchmark dislocation, is transmitted to the physical order slowly, over months, and sometimes never. Lawyers trained only in contracts see the financial order and treat the physical order as somebody else's problem. Lawyers trained only in public international law see the physical order and treat the financial arrangements as mere paperwork. The competent adviser sees both, and can trace a shock from one to the other.

### 06. Shocks That Made the Order `timeline`

- **1956** — Suez Crisis: the canal closes, and Europe learns that supply routes are political.
- **1960** — OPEC is founded by five states seeking to assert control over posted prices.
- **1973** — Arab members of OPEC restrict shipments; the embargo transforms energy policy permanently.
- **1974** — The International Energy Agency is created by consuming states with a stockholding and sharing obligation.
- **2022** — Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the ensuing sanctions re-weaponise energy on a scale unseen since 1973.
- **2024** — Export controls and industrial policy on critical minerals become a principal instrument of statecraft.

**Narration**

The order we live in was made by shocks, and each shock produced a legal response, so let me take you through six of them. In 1956 the Suez Canal closed and Europe discovered that its energy supply depended on a waterway it did not control. In 1960 five states founded the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, principally to assert control over the posted prices that the major companies had until then set unilaterally. In 1973 Arab members of that organisation restricted shipments, and the resulting transformation of energy policy is still with us in every stockholding obligation and every diversification strategy that exists. In 1974 consuming states created the International Energy Agency, with an emergency sharing system and a stockholding obligation, which is a legal instrument designed explicitly to defeat a political weapon. In 2022 Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the sanctions that followed re-weaponised energy on a scale not seen since 1973, and did so in a market that was now integrated, financialised, and far more complex. And in the last few years export controls, subsidy races and industrial policy around critical minerals have made the resource question central to statecraft again. What I want you to notice is the rhythm. A shock occurs, states act, and then lawyers are asked to make the response durable. Almost every institution in this field was created in reaction to a crisis, which is why so many of them are better at emergency than at governance.

### 07. How the Price Is Actually Set, and Why Lawyers Must Care `bullets`

- Benchmarks: a small number of reference crudes and regional gas hubs anchor the overwhelming majority of pricing.
- Oil-linked pricing versus hub pricing: the two systems allocate price risk between buyer and seller very differently.
- Long-term contracts contain price review clauses, hardship clauses and change-in-law clauses keyed to those benchmarks.
- When a benchmark is sanctioned, distorted or abolished, every contract indexed to it becomes a dispute.

*On-screen note:* A pricing clause is a risk allocation. Read it as one.

**Narration**

There is one part of the financial architecture that lawyers cannot delegate to economists, and it is price formation, because contractual disputes about price are among the most common and the most valuable in this field. Let me give you the minimum you need. Most internationally traded crude is priced by reference to a small number of benchmark crudes, with the formula adjusted for quality and location. Gas is priced in one of two ways: either by indexation to oil products, which was the dominant method for decades, or by reference to a trading hub where gas is bought and sold competitively. The difference between those two systems is not technical detail. It allocates price risk between buyer and seller in fundamentally different ways, and the shift from oil-indexation to hub pricing in Europe over the last two decades was one of the largest transfers of commercial risk in the history of the industry. Coal, liquefied natural gas and increasingly electricity follow their own conventions, and the liquefied natural gas market has moved from long-term oil-indexed contracts toward a hybrid of term, spot and portfolio supply. Now, why must a lawyer care? Because long-term contracts in this field contain price review clauses, hardship clauses and change-in-law clauses, all of which are keyed to these benchmarks and all of which are triggered when the underlying assumptions fail. And because the events of the last few years have demonstrated something that the textbooks did not anticipate: when a benchmark is sanctioned, distorted by state intervention, or effectively abandoned, every contract indexed to it becomes a dispute at the same moment. A pricing clause is not a number. It is an allocation of risk, and it should be read as one.

### 08. Energy Security Has Two Faces, and Transit Is the Hard Case `bullets`

- Security of supply, for the importer: availability, affordability, and the resilience of routes.
- Security of demand, for the exporter: the worry that investment will be stranded by policy elsewhere.
- The legal instruments: emergency stockholding, take-or-pay obligations, transit guarantees and interconnection rules.
- Transit is the hard case, because the interests of producer, transit state and consumer diverge and no single forum resolves them.

*On-screen note:* Ask whose security is being protected. The answer determines the entire legal design.

**Narration**

I want to close this part of the lecture with a concept that is used constantly and defined rarely: energy security. It has two faces, and conflating them is the source of much bad policy. For the importing state, security means security of supply: that the commodity will be available, that it will be affordable, and that the routes by which it arrives are resilient. For the exporting state, security means something close to the opposite: security of demand, and specifically protection against the risk that a consuming region will adopt policies that destroy the market for the exporter's product, leaving it with reserves it cannot sell and infrastructure it cannot recover. Those two securities are in tension, and the tension is the central fact of energy diplomacy. Much of the litigation of the last decade can be understood as that collision. Now, the legal instruments through which security is pursued include emergency stockholding obligations, which hold reserves against disruption; take-or-pay obligations in long-term contracts, which give the seller revenue certainty and the buyer supply certainty at the cost of flexibility; transit guarantees in treaty and contract, which attempt to protect the passage of a commodity across a third state; and technical interconnection and balancing rules in the electricity sector, which perform an analogous function for power. And the genuinely hard case is transit. Transit is hard because three sets of interests diverge: the producer wishes to sell, the consumer wishes to buy, and the transit state holds the route and has its own politics. No single forum resolves all three, and the applicable legal materials, from the Energy Charter Treaty's transit provisions to the transit provisions of the Law of the Sea Convention to bilateral pipeline protocols, were each designed for a narrower problem. Whenever you are asked to advise on energy security, your first question should be whose security, because the answer determines the entire legal design.

### 09. Institutions and Instruments `section`

**Narration**

Which brings us to the institutions, and to an important distinction between those that coordinate and those that bind.

### 10. Most Energy Institutions Cannot Order Anyone to Do Anything `bullets`

- The IEA coordinates consumer policy, holds emergency stocks and publishes the analysis that frames global debate.
- OPEC and its wider grouping seek to coordinate production levels among sovereign producers.
- The International Energy Forum provides a neutral producer-consumer dialogue with no binding function at all.
- The International Renewable Energy Agency supports the transition, but does not regulate it.
- The International Atomic Energy Agency is the exception: it has genuine inspection and safeguards powers.

*On-screen note:* With one exception, the architecture of energy governance is coordinative rather than compulsory.

**Narration**

Here is a fact that surprises students who have studied the World Trade Organization or the International Court of Justice. With essentially one exception, there is no institution in the global energy order that can order a state to do anything. The International Energy Agency, created in 1974, coordinates consumer policy, administers an emergency oil sharing system, and publishes the data and analysis that frame global debate; its members undertake to hold stocks equivalent to a defined number of days of net imports, and that is a genuine obligation, but it is owed through a treaty among members rather than imposed by an institution. OPEC and its wider grouping seek to coordinate production levels among sovereign producers, and do so through quotas that are politically agreed and frequently exceeded. The International Energy Forum provides a neutral producer-consumer dialogue and makes no pretence of binding anyone. The International Renewable Energy Agency supports the transition through data and facilitation, and regulates nothing. The exception is the International Atomic Energy Agency, which does have genuine inspection and safeguards powers, because the nuclear fuel cycle is the one place where the international community concluded that coordination was insufficient. That exception proves the rule. Everywhere else, energy governance is coordinative rather than compulsory, which means that when a real crisis arrives, states do not apply to an institution. They act, and lawyers are asked afterwards whether the action was lawful. I would add one qualification, because the dichotomy is not quite as stark as I have drawn it. Coordinative institutions do exercise a form of power, even without the capacity to command. The International Energy Agency shapes behaviour through its data and its projections, because states and markets both act on its analysis. The International Atomic Energy Agency's authority derives partly from legal powers and partly from the fact that its judgement determines whether a state's nuclear programme is treated as legitimate. And OPEC's quotas, routinely exceeded as they are, nonetheless operate as a focal point around which expectations coordinate. The distinction, then, is not between power and no power. It is between the power to compel and the power to frame, and in the energy order the second has been far more consequential than the first.

### 11. Sanctions, Export Controls and the Return of Economic Statecraft `two-col`

**MEASURES DIRECTED AT ENERGY**
- Embargoes and import bans on crude, refined products, coal and gas.
- Price caps, which condition shipping, insurance and financial services on the price paid.
- Restrictions on technology transfer for refining, liquefaction and deepwater exploration.
- Asset freezes and designation of companies, vessels and insurers.
- Secondary sanctions reaching non-nationals who deal with designated parties.

**MEASURES DIRECTED AT MINERALS**
- Export duties and quotas on critical and strategic minerals.
- Licensing requirements and outright prohibitions on specified elements and magnets.
- Investment screening that blocks inbound capital in strategic supply chains.
- Domestic content and sourcing requirements attached to clean-energy subsidies.
- Stockpiling and offtake agreements concluded directly with producers.

**Narration**

I now want to draw a distinction that matters enormously in current practice and that most general treatments of energy law still blur. There are two families of coercive measure, aimed at two different stages of the resource chain, and they work in different ways. The first family is directed at energy itself. Embargoes and import bans on crude, on refined products, on coal and on gas. Price caps, which are the interesting innovation of the last few years, because they do not prohibit anything; rather, they condition access to shipping, insurance and financial services on the price paid for the cargo, and thereby use the financial architecture to discipline the physical one. Restrictions on the transfer of technology for refining, liquefaction and deepwater exploration, which damage the target's capacity rather than its current revenue. Asset freezes and the designation of companies, vessels and insurers. And secondary sanctions, which reach non-nationals and are therefore the most legally controversial of all, because their extraterritorial reach is disputed by states whose nationals are affected. The second family is directed at minerals, and it works quite differently. Export duties and quotas. Licensing requirements and outright prohibitions on specified elements, alloys and magnets. Investment screening that blocks inbound capital in strategic supply chains. Domestic content and sourcing requirements attached to clean energy subsidies, which is the instrument of choice in current industrial policy. And stockpiling combined with direct offtake agreements between importing states and producers. Notice that the first family mostly restricts the target's ability to sell, while the second mostly restricts its ability to buy or to process. That difference shapes every legal argument about them, including their consistency with trade obligations.

### 12. Chokepoints: Why Geography Is Still Destiny `bullets`

- A handful of straits and canals carry the great majority of seaborne crude and liquefied natural gas.
- Closure of any one of them cannot be fully compensated by rerouting, because tanker capacity and voyage time are finite.
- Submarine cables and pipelines share the same seabed corridors, creating correlated vulnerability.
- The law responds through UNCLOS transit passage, through insurance and war-risk regimes, and through naval protection.

*On-screen note:* Resilience is achieved less by law than by redundancy, and redundancy is expensive.

**Narration**

No account of the energy order is complete without geography, and I want to make one structural point about it. A small number of straits and canals carry the great majority of seaborne crude and liquefied natural gas: Hormuz, Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb, the Suez and Sumed system, Panama, and the Turkish Straits. The legal consequence of that concentration is severe, because the closure of any one of them cannot be fully compensated by rerouting. Rerouting requires additional tanker capacity that may not exist, and it adds voyage time that markets experience as an immediate supply loss. There is a second and less discussed vulnerability. Submarine communications cables and energy pipelines frequently share the same seabed corridors, which means that a single incident can damage energy supply and financial communications simultaneously. The law's response is layered. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea establishes transit passage through international straits and makes it non-suspendable, which is the principal legal protection available. The insurance market responds through war-risk premia, which price the danger and thereby allocate it. And navies respond by protecting traffic, which is the oldest and still the most effective remedy. The uncomfortable conclusion, and I commend it to you as a research question, is that energy resilience is achieved less by law than by redundancy, and redundancy is expensive. States buy it when they are frightened and abandon it when they are not.

### 13. From Fuel Flows to Material Stocks `bullets`

- Concentration migrates rather than disappears: from reserves to processing and separation capacity.
- Producer coordination is joined by buyer coordination: purchasing clubs, strategic partnerships and joint stockpiling.
- The long-term oil contract gives way to power purchase agreements, contracts for difference and mineral offtake agreements.
- Carbon border adjustments, methane rules and supply-chain due diligence extend regulation across frontiers.
- What does not change: concentration, chokepoints, and the fact that states remain the decisive actors.

*On-screen note:* The transition reorganises the order; it does not abolish it.

**Narration**

Finally, I want you to understand that the energy transition is not the end of this order but a reorganisation of it, because that is the single most useful thing a student can grasp about the present moment. Consider what changes and what does not. Concentration migrates rather than disappears: the bottleneck shifts from the location of reserves to the location of processing, refining and separation capacity, and the legal consequence is that control over a supply chain is exercised at the midstream and refining stages rather than at the wellhead. Producer coordination is joined by buyer coordination, in the form of purchasing clubs, strategic mineral partnerships and joint stockpiling arrangements among importers, which is a genuinely new institutional form. The long-term oil sales contract, which structured the industry for a century, is giving way to a different family of instruments: power purchase agreements in the electricity sector, contracts for difference and other price stabilisation mechanisms, and mineral offtake agreements in which the buyer finances the mine in exchange for future supply. Regulation is also extending across frontiers in new ways, through carbon border adjustment mechanisms, through methane performance standards applied to imports, and through supply-chain due diligence rules that make an importer responsible for conditions at the point of extraction. Notice what does not change. Concentration persists. Chokepoints persist. States remain the decisive actors, and the institutions that might constrain them remain coordinative rather than compulsory. The transition reorganises the order; it does not abolish it. And that is precisely why the study of this field, rather than being a preparation for a declining industry, is a preparation for a legal practice that is expanding faster than at any point since the nineteen seventies.

### 14. Conservation Is an Exception, Not a Licence `case`

*China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Rare Earths, Tungsten and Molybdenum (WTO, DS431, DS432, DS433, 2014)*

China applied export duties, export quotas and minimum export prices to rare earths and to tungsten and molybdenum, materials essential to a wide range of clean energy, electronics and defence applications. The United States, the European Union and Japan complained. China invoked the conservation of exhaustible natural resources, and argued that its measures formed part of a comprehensive programme of environmental and resource management. The reports found the duties inconsistent with China's Accession Protocol and the quotas inconsistent with the general prohibition on quantitative restrictions. The conservation defence failed on the design of the measures rather than on the object pursued.

> **Holding / significance.** Resource nationalism must be designed to survive scrutiny. A conservation objective is legitimate, but a measure that restricts foreign buyers while leaving domestic production unconstrained will not be accepted as primarily conservationist.

**Narration**

Our case for this lecture comes not from arbitration but from the World Trade Organization, and it is by some distance the most instructive decision on the legal limits of resource nationalism: China's measures on the exportation of rare earths, tungsten and molybdenum, decided in 2014. The facts are clear. China applied export duties, export quotas and minimum export prices to materials that are essential to clean energy technologies, to electronics and to a wide range of defence applications, and which it dominated at the processing stage. The United States, the European Union and Japan complained. China's defence was conservation: these are exhaustible natural resources, it argued, and the measures formed part of a comprehensive programme of environmental protection and resource management. The reports found against China. The export duties were inconsistent with the commitments China had undertaken on accession, which permitted duties only on a short scheduled list that did not include these materials. The quotas were inconsistent with the general prohibition on quantitative restrictions on exports. And the conservation defence, which is the part I want you to study, failed on the design of the measures rather than on their objective. The reasoning was that a measure whose practical effect is to restrict foreign buyers while leaving domestic production and consumption substantially unconstrained is difficult to accept as primarily directed at conservation. Now, I want to give you three lessons from this case, and the third is the important one. First, commitments undertaken on accession to an organisation bind, and states frequently discover later how much they conceded. Second, an environmental objective does not rescue a measure that is not designed to achieve it; the justification is assessed by examining the design, not the stated purpose. And third, the real significance of this decision is strategic. It demonstrates that the international trade system disciplines resource nationalism, and that a state seeking to retain control over a strategic material must design its policy to survive that discipline. The policy response to this case has been to design differently, not to abandon the objective.

### 15. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**
- Map the physical route of one energy commodity from wellhead to end user, and list every legal instrument on the route.
- Learn which institutions coordinate and which bind — and be able to say why the IAEA is different.
- Take one sanctions package and identify exactly which legal power each measure rests on.
- Practise explaining a price cap to a client in two sentences.

**Postgraduate level**
- Assess whether the WTO can discipline export restrictions on critical minerals effectively, given the design problem identified in 2014.
- Analyse the extraterritorial reach of secondary sanctions against the principles of jurisdiction in public international law.
- Evaluate whether emergency stockholding obligations remain fit for purpose in a market for liquefied natural gas rather than crude.
- Design a research question on whether chokepoint vulnerability is better addressed by treaty or by insurance.

**Narration**

Let me translate this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want you to do one exercise that will repay you for the rest of your studies. Choose a single energy commodity and map its physical route from the point of extraction to the end user, and list every legal instrument that applies at each stage: the licence, the contract, the maritime rule, the customs regime, the sanctions screening, the insurance condition. You will be astonished how long the list becomes, and that astonishment is the beginning of professional competence. Learn which institutions coordinate and which bind, and be able to explain why the nuclear safeguards regime is the exception. Take one current sanctions package and identify, measure by measure, the legal power on which it rests, because that is the first question a client will ask and the first one a generalist gets wrong. And practise explaining a price cap in two sentences, because the mechanism is genuinely counter-intuitive and clients need it quickly. At postgraduate level, I want assessment and design. Assess whether the trading system can in practice discipline export restrictions on critical minerals, given that the 2014 reports identified a design problem rather than an objective problem, and that a state wishing to preserve its policy can simply redesign it. Analyse the extraterritorial reach of secondary sanctions against the principles of jurisdiction in public international law, and say plainly which basis you find defensible and which you do not. Evaluate whether emergency stockholding obligations designed for crude oil remain fit for purpose in a market increasingly organised around liquefied natural gas, where storage is far harder and the cargoes are less substitutable. And design a research question on whether chokepoint vulnerability is better addressed by treaty or by insurance, since that question sits precisely on the boundary between public and private ordering that this field is about.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- The order consists of a physical architecture and a financial architecture, with asymmetric shock transmission between them.
- It was made by shocks: 1956, 1973, 2022, and now the minerals competition.
- Its institutions coordinate rather than command, with nuclear safeguards the sole real exception.
- Coercive measures divide into those restricting a target's ability to sell and those restricting its ability to buy or process.
- Trade law disciplines resource nationalism, but disciplines its design rather than its objectives.

> **The one thing to remember.** The energy order is not governed by an institution. It is governed by an argument, conducted through instruments designed for other purposes, in which law supplies the vocabulary and power supplies the outcome.

**Narration**

Let me gather the threads. The global energy order consists of two superimposed architectures, physical and financial, with shocks transmitted quickly from the first to the second and slowly in the reverse direction. It was made by shocks rather than by design: 1956, 1973, 2022, and the minerals competition of the present. Its institutions coordinate rather than command, and the nuclear safeguards regime is the solitary exception that demonstrates the general rule. Its coercive instruments divide into two families, one restricting a target's ability to sell and the other restricting its ability to buy or to process, and that distinction determines how each will be argued about. And the trading system does discipline resource nationalism, as the rare earths reports showed, but it disciplines design rather than objective. Which brings me back to the question I posed at the start. Is this order governed by law or by power? My own answer, offered for your disagreement, is that it is governed by an argument: an argument conducted through instruments designed for other purposes, in which law supplies the vocabulary, power supplies the outcome, and the lawyer's function is to make the outcome defensible. In the next lecture we turn to the branch of international economic law that most directly structures the resource bargain: trade, investment and development.
