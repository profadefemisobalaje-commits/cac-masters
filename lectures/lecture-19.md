## Lecture Script

### 01. Energy Poverty and Energy Justice `bumper`

### 02. Energy Poverty, Energy Justice and the Right to Energy `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Nineteen, and to what I regard as the most important lecture in this series. Not because it is the most doctrinally difficult, since it is not, but because it is the one that determines whether anything we have discussed in the previous eighteen lectures has a point. We have examined the law governing the extraction, transport, sale and financing of energy across the world. We have not yet asked the prior question, which is who gets it. And the answer to that question is not evenly distributed. Several hundred million people have no electricity at all. More than two billion people cook with fuels that damage their health and shorten their lives. Both figures are heavily concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and in South Asia, and both are falling too slowly. This lecture is about what the law has to say about that, which is less than you might hope and more than you might expect.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. First, to define energy poverty, and to explain why a connection is not the same thing as access, which is a distinction that changes the measurement and therefore the policy. Second, to describe the scale and the geography of the access gap, and to be specific about where it is concentrated. Third, to explain the energy trilemma and the three dimensions of energy justice, which together give you the analytical vocabulary for the distributive questions in this field. Fourth, to analyse the doctrinal position on whether a right to energy exists in international law, and to be honest about what the sources do and do not support. Fifth, to explain the legal instruments through which vulnerable consumers are in practice protected: social tariffs, disconnection rules and the treatment of arrears. And sixth, to evaluate the tension between universal energy access and climate mitigation, which is the hardest question in the field and the one I would most like you to have a view on.

### 04. The Gap `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the facts, and then with the difficulty of measuring them.

### 05. What Energy Poverty Means, and Why the Definition Matters `bullets`

- The traditional measure is binary: a household either has an electricity connection or it does not.
- That measure understates the problem, because it counts a household with a connection that delivers an hour of power a day as served.
- Better measures capture capacity, duration, reliability, adequacy for the tasks people need, affordability, and legality.
- The clean cooking gap is larger than the electricity gap, and its health consequences are more severe.

*On-screen note:* A household that can run a light but not a refrigerator does not have access. Counting it as connected hides the problem.

**Narration**

Let me begin with measurement, because the way a problem is measured determines what is done about it, and the traditional measure in this field has been a poor one. The standard indicator has been binary: does a household have a connection to an electricity supply, yes or no. That measure has the virtue of being countable, and it has been used to track the seventh Sustainable Development Goal. But it systematically understates the problem, because it counts a household with a connection that delivers power for one hour a day, at a voltage that will not run a motor, at a price the household cannot pay for the quantity it needs, as having access alongside a household with continuous supply. Better measures have been developed which capture the dimensions that actually matter. There is capacity, meaning whether the supply can run the appliances the household needs rather than merely a light. There is duration and reliability, meaning how many hours a day the supply is available and whether it is available when needed. There is adequacy for the tasks people actually want, including refrigeration, cooking, water pumping, heating, cooling, and the operation of a business. There is affordability, meaning whether the household can pay for the quantity it needs without sacrificing other essentials. There is legality and formality, since a very large number of connections in rapidly growing cities are informal. And there is safety, which matters greatly where the connection is improvised. The multidimensional indices built on these dimensions produce substantially higher estimates of energy poverty than the binary measure, and the difference matters for policy because it directs attention from connection programmes to supply quality. One further point, which is frequently neglected in courses of this kind. The gap in access to clean cooking is considerably larger than the gap in access to electricity, and its consequences are more severe: the indoor air pollution from burning wood, charcoal, dung and coal in enclosed spaces is among the largest environmental health risks in the world, and the burden of it falls overwhelmingly on women and on very young children.

### 06. Where the Gap Is, and What Is Happening to It `bullets`

- The number of people without electricity fell very substantially between 2000 and 2020, and then began to fall more slowly.
- The shortfall is now heavily concentrated: the large majority of those without access live in sub-Saharan Africa.
- Urbanisation is shifting the problem: the gap is increasingly urban and peri-urban rather than rural.
- Progress has been driven substantially by distributed solutions rather than by grid extension alone.

*On-screen note:* The number without access is concentrated in the countries with the least capital to extend grids and the fastest population growth.

**Narration**

Let me give you the geography, because the aggregate figures conceal a distribution that should shape your thinking. The number of people without access to electricity declined very substantially over the first two decades of this century, driven largely by national electrification programmes in Asia, and particularly in India and in China. That decline then slowed sharply, and in some years the number has increased, because population growth in the countries with the least access has outpaced the rate of new connection. The shortfall is now heavily concentrated. The large majority of the people without electricity live in sub-Saharan Africa, and within that region the concentration is further skewed towards a small number of countries, several of which face conflict or acute fiscal constraint. The same pattern holds for clean cooking, with the largest shortfalls in sub-Saharan Africa and in parts of South Asia. There is a second shift that is less often noticed. The problem has become substantially urban. Most of the people without access live in rural areas, but the fastest-growing category is the urban and peri-urban household in an informal settlement, which is frequently physically close to a grid connection that it cannot legally or economically obtain. That changes the nature of the legal problem, since the obstacle is not the cost of extending a line but the terms of connection, the security of tenure and the affordability of a supply. And there is a technological shift worth recording, which is that a large and growing share of new access is being delivered by distributed solutions rather than by grid extension: solar home systems, mini-grids, and pay-as-you-go arrangements that allow a household to purchase energy services in small increments rather than to finance a connection. The legal consequence is significant, because these solutions are delivered by companies under commercial terms that public utility regulation was not designed to supervise.

### 07. Justice `section`

**Narration**

Let me now give you the analytical vocabulary for the distributive questions in this field.

### 08. The Trilemma: Security, Equity, Sustainability `bullets`

- Energy security: the availability of supply, the reliability of infrastructure, and resilience to disruption.
- Energy equity: the affordability and accessibility of energy across the population and across generations.
- Environmental sustainability: the extent to which the system avoids damage to the environment and the climate.
- The framework's value is that it makes trade-offs explicit rather than leaving them to be discovered in a crisis.

*On-screen note:* Every energy decision is a position on the trilemma. The useful question is not whether there is a trade-off, but who bears it.

**Narration**

Let me introduce a framework that is used widely in policy and in industry, and that you will encounter constantly, and then let me tell you what it is for and what it is not. The energy trilemma holds that any energy system must simultaneously deliver three objectives, and that they are in tension. There is energy security, which concerns the availability of supply, the reliability and adequacy of infrastructure, and the resilience of the system to disruption, whether from a physical event, a market event or a political one. There is energy equity, which concerns affordability and accessibility, both across the population within a generation and across generations, since the depletion of a finite resource is a transfer from those who follow. And there is environmental sustainability, which concerns the extent to which the system avoids or mitigates damage to the environment and to the climate. The framework's value is not analytical rigour, since it is a classification rather than a theory. Its value is rhetorical and practical: it forces a decision-maker to state where a policy sits on three axes rather than to claim that a policy improves everything, and it makes trade-offs visible before they are discovered in a crisis. Now, the limitation, and I want you to see it clearly, is that the framework does not tell you who bears the cost of a trade-off. A policy may improve sustainability while worsening affordability, and the trilemma will show you that the trade-off exists but not who experiences the resulting harm. That is precisely the question the energy justice literature was developed to answer, and I want you to hold both frameworks, since one identifies the trade-off and the other identifies its distribution.

### 09. Three Dimensions of Energy Justice `two-col`

**DISTRIBUTION AND RECOGNITION**
- Distributional justice asks who bears the costs and who receives the benefits of energy systems and energy decisions.
- Recognition justice asks whose interests and identities are acknowledged, and who is rendered invisible by the categories used.
- Costs are frequently localised: a mine, a line, a plant or a flare sits in a particular community.
- Benefits are frequently diffuse and distant, which is the structural reason opposition is local and support is abstract.

**PROCEDURE AND RESTORATION**
- Procedural justice asks who participates in decisions, and whether participation occurs while options remain open.
- Restorative justice asks what is owed where harm has already occurred, including remediation and compensation.
- Procedural justice is the most immediately actionable, since it can be secured by law without resolving the distributive question.
- Restorative justice is the least developed, and is the dimension most often omitted from national frameworks.

**Narration**

Let me now give you the second framework, which is the one I would want you to be able to deploy in practice. Energy justice has been developed in the academic literature into three principal dimensions, to which a fourth has more recently been added, and they are worth knowing precisely because they map directly onto legal remedies. The first is distributional justice, which asks who bears the costs and who receives the benefits of energy systems and of decisions about them. The characteristic pattern in this sector is that costs are localised while benefits are diffuse. A mine, a pipeline, a power station or a flare is situated in a particular place, and the people near it bear the dust, the noise, the land take and the risk. The benefits, in the form of electricity, revenue or employment, are distributed across a nation or a region. That asymmetry is structural rather than accidental, and it is the reason opposition to projects is nearly always local while support for them is nearly always abstract. The second is recognition justice, which asks whose interests, identities and ways of life are acknowledged in the decision-making process, and who is rendered invisible by the categories the law uses. The clearest illustration is the treatment of indigenous peoples, whose relationship to territory is not captured by the legal category of land ownership, and which we examined in a previous lecture. The third is procedural justice, which asks who participates in decisions, at what stage, and with what information. It is the most immediately actionable of the three, and the reason is important: procedural justice can be secured by law without first resolving the far harder distributive question, which is why so much of the litigation in this field is procedural in character. And the fourth, which has been added more recently and which I think is the most consequential, is restorative justice, which asks what is owed where the harm has already occurred. This is the dimension most often omitted from national frameworks, and the omission is glaring in countries where extraction has been going on for a century.

### 10. Who Carries the Energy Burden `bullets`

- Where cooking fuels are unclean, the health burden falls overwhelmingly on women and on young children.
- Time spent gathering fuel is time not spent in education, in paid work, or in rest, and it is overwhelmingly women's time.
- Women are substantially under-represented in the energy workforce, in energy regulation and in energy executive roles.
- The legal response requires gender to be addressed in energy policy rather than left to general equality law.

*On-screen note:* An energy intervention that does not ask who in the household uses the energy will frequently fail to reach the person it is meant to help.

**Narration**

Let me take one cross-cutting dimension that is too often treated as a footnote, and which I want you to treat as central. That dimension is gender. The reason it is not a footnote is that the burden of energy poverty is distributed within the household as well as between households, and it is distributed on gender lines. Where cooking is done with unclean fuels, the person doing the cooking is exposed to the resulting indoor air pollution, and that person is overwhelmingly a woman, as are, by definition, the very small children who are with her. The health consequence is one of the largest single environmental health risks in the world, and it is borne disproportionately by women. Where fuel must be gathered rather than purchased, the time spent gathering it is time not available for education, for paid work, for care or for rest, and that time is overwhelmingly women's time; in some settings it amounts to many hours a week. Where a household connection is purchased incrementally, the priorities within the household determine what is bought, and those priorities are frequently determined by who controls the expenditure. And where energy services fail, the coping work, which means the search for alternatives, falls within the domestic sphere. Alongside all of this there is a participation gap: women are substantially under-represented in the energy workforce, in technical roles, in energy regulation and in the senior management of energy enterprises, and they are under-represented in the processes in which energy policy is made. The legal implication, which I want to state plainly, is that an energy intervention which does not ask who within the household uses the energy, and who decides, will frequently fail to reach the person it was intended to help. It is not enough for equality law to exist in general. Gender has to be addressed within energy policy, in the design of tariff structures, in the siting of distribution, in the specification of cookstove programmes, and in the composition of the institutions that decide.

### 11. Rights and Instruments `section`

**Narration**

Let me turn to the doctrinal question, which is whether any of this amounts to a legal right.

### 12. Is There a Right to Energy? `bullets`

- There is no express right to energy in either of the International Covenants on human rights.
- It is instead derived from other guarantees: an adequate standard of living, health, water, housing and development.
- The right of peoples to dispose of their natural resources provides a collective rather than an individual entitlement.
- In consequence the right is predominantly realised through national constitutional and statutory law rather than through the treaties.

*On-screen note:* The honest answer is that there is a set of derived entitlements, not a freestanding right, and the derivation matters.

**Narration**

Let me now address the doctrinal question directly, and let me give you the honest answer rather than the aspirational one. There is no express right to energy in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and there is none in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. What exists instead is a set of guarantees from which energy access has been derived, and the derivations are worth setting out because they determine the content of the obligation. The most frequently invoked is the right to an adequate standard of living, including adequate food, clothing and housing, since a dwelling without light, heat or a means of cooking is difficult to describe as adequate. There is the right to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health, which is engaged both by the indoor air pollution from unclean cooking and by the absence of refrigeration and of power for medical facilities. There is the right to water and sanitation, since the delivery of water frequently requires energy for pumping and treatment. And there is the right of peoples to self-determination and to freely dispose of their natural wealth and resources, which provides a collective entitlement and which, as we saw at the start of this series, is the source of the sovereign authority over the resource itself. The consequence of this derivation is important and it is the reason I have spent time on it. Because the right is derived rather than freestanding, its realisation occurs predominantly in national law, through constitutional provisions, through statutory guarantees of universal service, and through the regulation of utilities. It is in national courts, applying national constitutions, that the most consequential decisions on this subject have been made. Let me now show you one of them.

### 13. Disconnection Without Notice Is Unlawful `case`

*Joseph and Others v. City of Johannesburg and Others, Constitutional Court of South Africa, [2009] ZACC 30*

The applicants were tenants in residential premises in Johannesburg. The City disconnected the electricity supply because the owner of the property was in arrears, and it did so without giving any notice to the occupiers, who were not in arrears themselves. The applicants challenged the disconnection. The Constitutional Court accepted that there is no express right to electricity in the Constitution. It held however that the state has a constitutional obligation to take reasonable legislative and other measures, within its available resources, to achieve the progressive realisation of the right of access to adequate housing, and that the provision of access to electricity is one of the measures that must be taken. It further held that the disconnection of an existing electricity supply to a home, without notice to the occupier, was procedurally unfair and unlawful, and it required the City to give notice to tenants before disconnecting and to afford them an opportunity to make representations.

> **Holding / significance.** Even where there is no freestanding right to energy, the supply of electricity is a measure required for the realisation of the right to adequate housing, and an occupier is entitled to procedural fairness before an existing supply is terminated.

**Narration**

Our case is Joseph against the City of Johannesburg, decided by the Constitutional Court of South Africa in 2009, and it is the most instructive decision available on how these questions are actually resolved. The facts are ordinary, which is what makes them useful. The applicants were tenants in a block of residential premises. The City disconnected the electricity supply because the owner of the property was in arrears. It did so without giving any notice to the occupiers, who were not themselves in arrears and who had no contractual relationship with the City. The applicants challenged the disconnection, and the case reached the Constitutional Court. The Court's reasoning proceeded in three steps, and each is worth noting. First, it accepted without difficulty that there is no express right to electricity in the Constitution. That concession did not end the matter. Second, it held that the state has an obligation to take reasonable legislative and other measures, within its available resources, to achieve the progressive realisation of the right of access to adequate housing, and that the provision of access to electricity is among the measures that must be taken, since a dwelling without electricity cannot readily be regarded as adequate housing. That is the derivation in action, and it is the mechanism through which most of the world's energy access law is made. Third, and this is the operative holding, it held that the disconnection of an existing supply to a home without notice to the occupier is procedurally unfair and unlawful. The City was required to give notice to the tenants before disconnecting, and to afford them an opportunity to make representations. Let me draw out three lessons. The first is doctrinal: a derived right is not a weak right, and anchoring energy access to housing, health or an adequate standard of living can produce enforceable obligations. The second is procedural, and it is the most practically useful: the decision protects people against disconnection by requiring notice and a hearing, which is a remedy that a court can supervise without determining the far harder question of how much energy the state must provide. And the third is a point of general importance about this whole series: the case was decided under a national constitution by a national court, and that is where the law of energy access is made.

### 14. Protecting the Vulnerable: Tariffs, Arrears and Disconnection `bullets`

- Lifeline and social tariffs provide a basic quantity of energy at a subsidised rate, targeted by consumption or by status.
- Disconnection rules commonly prohibit termination in winter, for specified vulnerable households, or for medical reasons.
- Arrears are increasingly addressed by prepayment, by repayment plans, and by the transfer of debt protections on change of occupier.
- The recurring problem is targeting: means-tested support reaches fewer people than it should, and universal support is expensive.

*On-screen note:* The most consequential regulatory decisions in this field are not about wholesale markets. They are about whether a poor household can be disconnected.

**Narration**

Let me close the doctrinal part with the instruments through which vulnerable consumers are actually protected, since these are the provisions that determine whether the rights we have discussed mean anything. There are four principal instruments, and they operate at different points. There are lifeline and social tariffs, under which a basic quantity of energy is supplied at a subsidised rate. The design question in these is how the subsidy is targeted, and there are two approaches: a block tariff in which the first increment of consumption is cheaper for everyone, which is simple and universal but poorly targeted, since it benefits high consumers as well; and a status-based social tariff available to households meeting defined criteria, which is better targeted but requires an administrative apparatus to identify them and systematically fails to reach the people who need it. There are disconnection rules, which are the most direct protection. These commonly prohibit termination of supply during a defined winter period, prohibit it altogether for households containing specified categories of person such as those with critical medical needs or with very young children, and require a defined sequence of notice, offer of repayment, and opportunity to object before any disconnection is effected. There are arrears mechanisms, since the underlying problem in most disconnection cases is not refusal to pay but inability to pay: repayment plans, budget billing, the writing off of debt after a defined period, and the treatment of a debt attached to a meter rather than to a person, which is a recurring problem where a tenant inherits the arrears of a previous occupier. And there are prepayment arrangements, which solve the arrears problem for the supplier and create a new one for the household, since a household that cannot afford to top up disconnects itself, and the incidence of self-disconnection under prepayment is substantial and is now a recognised regulatory concern. The recurring problem across all of these is targeting, and I want you to notice that it is the same problem that arises in every area of social law: means-tested support reaches substantially fewer people than are entitled to it, and universal support is expensive and politically fragile.

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want four things. First, a definition of energy poverty that goes beyond connection, and an explanation of why the binary measure understates the problem. Second, the three elements of the trilemma and the dimensions of energy justice, and I would particularly want you to be able to use procedural justice as an analytical tool. Third, an explanation of why there is no express right to energy in the International Covenants, together with the guarantees from which it is derived, and I would single out the right to an adequate standard of living. And fourth, the holding of our case, with the three steps in the Court's reasoning. At postgraduate level, I want you to take positions. Assess whether a freestanding right to energy ought to be recognised in international law, and if so state what you think the minimum core content would be, since a right without a minimum core is difficult to enforce. Analyse whether the derived-rights approach, which is what we actually have, produces stronger or weaker protection than an express right would, and consider whether the answer depends on the jurisdiction. Evaluate the tension between universal energy access and climate mitigation directly, and propose a legal resolution, because this is the hardest question in the field and it will not be resolved by asserting that both goals are compatible. And critique prepayment metering: does it protect households from the accumulation of unmanageable debt, or does it transfer the burden of disconnection from the supplier, who must follow a procedure, onto the household, which need only fail to top up?

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Connection is not access: capacity, duration, reliability, adequacy and affordability all matter, and the cooking gap exceeds the electricity gap.
- The shortfall is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, is increasingly urban, and is now addressed substantially by distributed solutions.
- The trilemma identifies trade-offs; energy justice identifies who bears them, and procedural justice is the most actionable dimension.
- There is no express right to energy in the Covenants, but derived entitlements produce enforceable obligations in national law.
- Protection is delivered through tariffs, disconnection rules and arrears mechanisms, and the recurring difficulty is targeting.

> **The one thing to remember.** The decisive legal questions in energy access are procedural and local: notice before disconnection, the terms of connection, and the affordability of a supply that exists.

**Narration**

Let me close. Connection is not access, and the distinctions that matter are capacity, duration, reliability, adequacy and affordability, while the clean cooking gap is both larger than the electricity gap and more damaging to health. The shortfall is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, is increasingly urban rather than rural, and is now being addressed substantially through distributed solutions regulated on commercial rather than utility terms. The trilemma identifies the trade-offs, and energy justice identifies who bears them, with procedural justice being the dimension most readily secured by law. There is no express right to energy in the human rights covenants, but the derived entitlements produce genuine and enforceable obligations in national law, as the Johannesburg case demonstrates. And protection is delivered in practice through tariffs, disconnection rules and arrears mechanisms, whose recurring difficulty is that support targeted on means reaches fewer people than it should. In our final lecture we bring the series together, and consider why a student at your level must study this subject at all.
