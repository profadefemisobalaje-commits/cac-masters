## Lecture Script

### 01. Climate Change Law and Fossil Fuels `bumper`

### 02. Climate Change Law and the Future of Fossil Fuels `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Thirteen. We arrive now at the largest question in this field, and I want to state at the outset why it belongs in a course on energy and natural resources law rather than in a separate course on the environment. The climate regime is not an environmental overlay on the energy system. It is becoming the instrument through which the energy system is governed. It determines which reserves may be developed, which plants may be built, which fuels may be exported, which subsidies are lawful, and increasingly who may be held liable for the consequences of past extraction. That is not a metaphor. It is what the advisory proceedings of 2024 and 2025 have made explicit. Let me take you through the architecture, then the litigation, then the consequences for the industry itself.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. ### 04. The Paris Architecture `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the treaty itself, and with the question of what, exactly, parties have promised.

### 05. What Paris Actually Binds Parties to Do `bullets`

- The temperature goal is expressed as a collective aim, and is not itself a binding obligation on any individual party.
- Each party must prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions, and pursue domestic measures to achieve them.
- Each successive contribution must represent a progression and reflect the party's highest possible ambition.
- There are binding obligations of conduct: to prepare, communicate, maintain, account for and report — but not an obligation of result.

*On-screen note:* Paris binds the process tightly and the outcome loosely. That design was deliberate, and it is the source of both its universality and its weakness.

**Narration**

The Paris Agreement has near-universal membership, which is a remarkable achievement, and it achieves that universality through a particular legal design that you must understand precisely. The temperature goal, holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below two degrees above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit it to one point five, is expressed as a collective aim. It is not an obligation binding on any individual party, and no party can be said to breach it by failing to reach it. What binds each party is a set of obligations of conduct. Each party shall prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions that it intends to achieve. Each party shall pursue domestic mitigation measures with the aim of achieving the objectives of those contributions. Each party's successive contribution will represent a progression beyond its then current one and reflect its highest possible ambition, reflecting its common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities. Each party shall account for its contribution, and shall provide the information necessary for clarity, transparency and understanding. And there are binding obligations to communicate and to report on a defined cycle, subject to technical expert review. Now, the consequence of that design is the point I want you to carry away. Paris binds the process tightly and the outcome loosely. It does not oblige any state to achieve a particular emissions level, but it does oblige every state to declare what it intends to do, to explain itself, to be reviewed, and to return with something more ambitious. Whether that is the right design is a genuinely open question, and the answer depends on whether you believe that transparency and peer pressure can substitute for enforceable targets.

### 06. Common but Differentiated Responsibilities `two-col`

**THE PRINCIPLE**
- It appears in the Framework Convention and in the Paris Agreement, in slightly different formulations.
- Under Paris it applies in the light of different national circumstances.
- It justifies differentiated expectations for mitigation, for finance, and for reporting.
- Developed countries should continue to take the lead by undertaking economy-wide absolute reduction targets.

**THE PRACTICE**
- Differentiation is now expressed largely through self-differentiation rather than through annexes.
- Contributions vary enormously in form: absolute targets, intensity targets, peaking, policy statements.
- Finance, technology transfer and capacity building are the obligations through which differentiation bites.
- The boundary between developed and developing remains the most contested question in the negotiations.

**Narration**

The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities is the doctrinal core of the climate regime, and it deserves careful handling because it is frequently described imprecisely. The principle appears in the Framework Convention, where it is tied to the historical responsibility of developed countries for the accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, and it appears again in the Paris Agreement, where it applies in the light of different national circumstances. Let me distinguish the principle from the practice. As a principle, it justifies differentiated expectations: developed country parties should continue taking the lead by undertaking economy-wide absolute emission reduction targets, while developing country parties are encouraged to move over time towards the same kinds of targets. It justifies the obligations on finance, technology transfer and capacity building, which fall principally on developed countries. And it justifies flexibility in reporting and review. In practice, however, the mechanism of differentiation has changed fundamentally. Under the Kyoto Protocol, differentiation operated through annexes: some parties had quantified binding targets and others did not. Under Paris, differentiation operates largely through self-differentiation, since each party determines its own contribution and its own scope, and the treaty instead prescribes the common procedural obligations that apply to all. The result is that contributions vary enormously in form: some are economy-wide absolute reduction targets, some are intensity targets, some are peaking years, some are policy statements, and some are conditional on finance being provided. That flexibility secured universality, and it has made comparability extremely difficult. The boundary between developed and developing remains the single most contested question in the negotiations, and it is litigated in substance every time a finance commitment is discussed.

### 07. The Advisory Opinions `section`

**Narration**

Now let me turn to the most significant legal developments in this field for a generation.

### 08. Three Tribunals, Two Years `timeline`

- **2023** — The United Nations General Assembly requests an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice on the obligations of States in respect of climate change.
- **2024** — The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea gives an advisory opinion on the obligations of States Parties to the Law of the Sea Convention in respect of climate change and the marine environment.
- **2025** — The Inter-American Court of Human Rights issues an advisory opinion on the climate emergency and human rights.
- **2025** — The International Court of Justice delivers its advisory opinion, addressing the applicable law and the consequences of wrongful conduct.

**Narration**

Between 2024 and 2025, three separate international tribunals were asked to state the law on climate change, and the result is the most significant clarification of obligations in this field since the Paris Agreement. Let me set out the sequence and then tell you what is genuinely new. In 2023 the General Assembly adopted a resolution requesting an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice on the obligations of states in respect of climate change, and specifically on the consequences for states that have caused significant harm to the climate system. In 2024 the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea gave an advisory opinion on the obligations of states parties to the Law of the Sea Convention in respect of climate change and the marine environment, treating anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions absorbed by the oceans as pollution of the marine environment within the meaning of that convention. In 2025 the Inter-American Court of Human Rights issued an advisory opinion on the climate emergency and human rights, addressing the obligations of states in the inter-American system to respond, including obligations of a preventive nature and obligations towards individuals and groups in situations of vulnerability. And in 2025 the International Court delivered its advisory opinion, addressing the whole field of applicable law, including the climate treaties, customary international law, human rights law and the law of state responsibility. What is genuinely new here is not the proposition that climate change is a legal problem. It is the convergence. Three tribunals, applying different bodies of law, reached conclusions pointing in the same direction: that states have binding obligations to act, that those obligations are informed by the best available science, and that a failure to act may engage responsibility.

### 09. Why These Opinions Matter to an Energy Lawyer `bullets`

- They supply an authoritative statement of obligation that domestic courts can and do apply.
- They connect the climate treaties to customary law, human rights law and the law of the sea, which had been argued about for years.
- They strengthen the arguments of claimants in domestic constitutional and administrative litigation, where most of the action now is.
- They inform the interpretation of investment treaties, and therefore the defence of states facing claims from fossil fuel investors.

*On-screen note:* Advisory opinions do not bind, but they state the law, and courts everywhere read them.

**Narration**

Let me be precise about why these opinions matter, because students often assume that advisory opinions are declaratory and therefore inconsequential, and that assumption is wrong in this field. An advisory opinion is not binding in the way a judgment between parties is binding. But it states the law as the highest court available understands it, and it is read, cited and applied by domestic courts, by regional human rights bodies, and by arbitral tribunals. Four consequences follow, and each of them will affect your practice. First, the opinions supply an authoritative statement of states' obligations which domestic courts can apply directly, and which several have already begun to apply in constitutional and administrative litigation. Second, they connect the climate treaties to bodies of law whose relationship had been contested for years: to customary international law on transboundary harm, to human rights law, and to the law of the sea. That connection was argued about for two decades and is now considerably clearer. Third, they strengthen claimants in the domestic litigation that is now the principal engine of climate law, which includes challenges to the grant of extraction licences, to the approval of new infrastructure, and to the adequacy of national mitigation plans. And fourth, and this is the consequence most relevant to this series, they inform the interpretation of investment treaties. A state defending a claim brought by a fossil fuel investor whose asset has been stranded by climate policy can now point to an authoritative international statement that its conduct was required by law rather than chosen. That is a material change in the strength of the police powers defence, and it will be pleaded in every such case from now on.

### 10. Three Waves of Climate Litigation `bullets`

- First wave: challenges to government policy, alleging that mitigation plans or targets are inadequate.
- Second wave: challenges to specific projects and approvals, alleging that emissions were not properly assessed.
- Third wave: claims against enterprises, seeking to establish corporate liability for contributions to global harm.
- Each wave raises different doctrinal problems: justiciability, standing, causation, and the appropriate remedy.

*On-screen note:* The centre of gravity has moved from the legislature to the project and then to the enterprise.

**Narration**

Before leaving the litigation, I want to give you a map of it, because the field is large and students tend to encounter cases without a framework. There have been, broadly, three waves. The first wave consists of challenges to government policy. Claimants allege that a state's mitigation target, or its plan for achieving it, is inadequate to meet its own commitments or to protect the rights of its citizens, and they seek declaratory and mandatory relief. These cases raise acutely the questions of justiciability and of the separation of powers, since a court is being asked to supervise the adequacy of national policy. Some have succeeded, and the reasoning in the most influential of them turns on the proposition that a decision which is incompatible with the state's own statutory commitments, or which fails to consider a material risk, is reviewable on ordinary administrative law grounds. The second wave consists of challenges to specific projects and approvals. Claimants allege that the environmental assessment of a proposed extraction or infrastructure project failed to consider the emissions that the project will cause, either in construction and operation or in the eventual use of the product. These cases are procedurally more conventional, because a court is being asked to review a specific administrative decision, and they have succeeded in a number of jurisdictions. The third wave consists of claims against enterprises, seeking to establish that a company bears liability for its contribution to global harm. These cases face the hardest doctrinal obstacles, principally causation and the identification of a duty owed, and their progress has been uneven. But notice the direction of travel. The centre of gravity has moved from the legislature, where the claim is political and the remedy is structural, to the project, where the claim is administrative and the remedy is specific, and then to the enterprise, where the claim is civil and the remedy is compensatory. Each move has lowered the doctrinal barriers, and that is why the field is expanding.

### 11. Carbon, Loss and Stranding `section`

**Narration**

Let me now turn to the instruments and the consequences.

### 12. Carbon Pricing, Border Adjustment and Article Six `bullets`

- Emissions trading systems cap total emissions and allow trading of allowances; carbon taxes set a price instead of a quantity.
- Border carbon adjustment charges imports for embodied emissions, to prevent relocation and to protect domestic industry.
- Article Six of the Paris Agreement establishes cooperative approaches and a mechanism for internationally transferred mitigation outcomes.
- The legal difficulties are accounting integrity, the avoidance of double counting, and consistency with trade law.

*On-screen note:* Pricing internalises the externality. Border adjustment exports the internalisation. Both are legally contested.

**Narration**

Let me now survey the instruments through which emissions are actually reduced, beginning with pricing. There are two principal designs. An emissions trading system sets a cap on total emissions within the covered sectors, issues allowances up to that cap, and permits trading, so that the quantity of emissions is fixed and the price emerges from the market. A carbon tax fixes the price and lets the quantity emerge. The choice between them is a choice about which variable to control, and the standard answer is that quantity instruments suit a pollutant with a steeply rising damage curve, while price instruments suit one where the cost of abatement is uncertain. Both are now in widespread use, and several jurisdictions operate both. The second instrument is border carbon adjustment, under which imports are charged for the greenhouse gases embodied in their production. Its purposes are twofold: to prevent the relocation of emissions-intensive production to jurisdictions with weaker constraints, which would defeat the object of the domestic measure; and to protect domestic industry from competitors not subject to an equivalent cost. Its legal difficulty is also twofold: consistency with the trading system, particularly the national treatment obligation and the general exceptions; and compatibility with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, since a measure of this kind bears hardest on exporters in developing states. The third instrument is Article Six of the Paris Agreement, which establishes both cooperative approaches, under which parties may transfer mitigation outcomes internationally, and a centralised mechanism supervised by an international body. The difficulties here are technical and severe: ensuring that a transfer is real, verified and additional; ensuring that it is not counted twice, by both the transferring and the acquiring party; and ensuring that the overall mitigation of global emissions is not reduced by the transaction. Getting that accounting right is the difference between an integrity mechanism and an accounting fiction, and it is being negotiated in detail now.

### 13. Loss and Damage: Compensation Without Responsibility `bullets`

- Loss and damage refers to the adverse effects of climate change that are not, or cannot be, avoided by mitigation or adaptation.
- States most vulnerable to those effects have sought compensation; states with historical emissions have resisted it.
- The outcome has been the establishment of funding arrangements, carefully framed so as not to admit liability.
- The legal distinction between compensation founded on responsibility and finance founded on solidarity is therefore central.

*On-screen note:* The drafting of the loss and damage decisions is studied for what it avoids as much as for what it says.

**Narration**

There is one element of the climate regime that raises the question of state responsibility most directly, and it deserves your attention because it shows how international law handles a claim that is morally powerful and legally difficult. Loss and damage refers to the adverse effects of climate change that are not, and in some cases cannot be, avoided by mitigation or by adaptation: the permanent loss of territory to sea level rise, the destruction of a reef system, the salinisation of a delta, the loss of a water source. The states most exposed to those effects, which are frequently small island developing states and least developed countries, have for years sought compensation for them. The states with the largest historical emissions have consistently resisted, for reasons that are legally understandable: attribution of a particular harm to a particular state's emissions is scientifically and legally formidable, causation is diffuse across more than a century and every jurisdiction, and an admission of liability would carry enormous financial consequence. The outcome, reached after many years of negotiation, has been the establishment of funding arrangements to assist particularly vulnerable developing countries, framed with conspicuous care so as not to admit responsibility or to create any basis for liability. The distinction being maintained is between compensation, which is founded on responsibility for a wrongful act, and finance, which is founded on solidarity and on the differentiated obligations of the regime. That distinction is the reason the decisions on loss and damage are studied for what they avoid as much as for what they say. And it remains to be seen whether the distinction will hold, because the advisory proceedings have made the question of responsibility harder to avoid.

### 14. The Neglected Half: Adaptation and the Duty to Prepare `bullets`

- Mitigation addresses the cause; adaptation addresses the consequences that can no longer be avoided.
- The Paris Agreement establishes a global goal on adaptation and requires parties to engage in adaptation planning and reporting.
- Domestically, adaptation becomes a legal duty through infrastructure standards, planning law, building codes and water allocation.
- The hardest legal questions concern liability for failure to adapt: who is responsible when infrastructure built to old standards fails?

*On-screen note:* Adaptation is where climate law becomes ordinary law: planning, engineering standards, insurance and liability.

**Narration**

I want to say something about the half of this subject that receives far less attention than it deserves, and that is adaptation. Mitigation addresses the cause of climate change; adaptation addresses the consequences that can no longer be avoided, and a substantial measure of warming is now unavoidable whatever is decided about emissions. The Paris Agreement recognises this by establishing a global goal on adaptation, of enhancing adaptive capacity, strengthening resilience and reducing vulnerability, and by requiring parties to engage in adaptation planning processes and to report on them. But the real legal work on adaptation is domestic, and it is done through instruments that have nothing specifically to do with climate. It is done through infrastructure design standards, which determine whether a road, a bridge or a drainage system is built for the rainfall regime of the last century or of this one. It is done through planning law, which determines whether development is permitted in exposed locations. It is done through building codes, through water allocation and abstraction rules, through flood defence obligations, through heat and cooling requirements in workplaces, and through the management of public health emergencies. And it is done, increasingly, through insurance and through the allocation of insurable risk. The hardest legal questions in adaptation are about liability for failure. When infrastructure designed to a superseded standard fails and causes loss, who is responsible: the designer, the owner, the regulator who approved the standard, or no one? When a planning authority permits development in an area that floods, is it liable to those who bought in reliance? And when insurance becomes unavailable in an exposed region, what is the state's obligation? These questions are being answered now, unevenly, and they will occupy a very large share of legal practice in this field over the coming decades.

### 15. The Asset That Cannot Be Produced `case`

*The problem of unburnable carbon and its legal consequences*

If the temperature goal is to be met, a substantial proportion of known fossil fuel reserves cannot be produced and consumed. That proposition, which originated in climate science and economics, has become a legal problem in three directions. First, investors in fossil fuel assets may claim compensation where a state prohibits or restricts production. Second, states and regulators increasingly require disclosure of climate-related financial risk, including the risk that reserves become uneconomic. Third, claimants in domestic litigation have challenged the approval of new extraction projects on the ground that the emissions from the resulting fuel were never assessed.

> **Holding / significance.** The legal significance of unburnable carbon is that it converts a physical constraint into three distinct legal questions: compensation for investors, disclosure to markets, and the scope of environmental assessment.

**Narration**

Our case study for this lecture is not a judgment but a problem, and it is the problem that concentrates everything we have discussed. If the temperature goal is to be met, the quantity of fossil fuel that can be produced and consumed is limited, and that limit is substantially below the quantity of known reserves. A large proportion of the world's coal, oil and gas therefore cannot be produced. That proposition began in climate science, was taken up by economists and financial regulators, and has now become a legal problem in three distinct directions. The first is compensation. Where a state prohibits or restricts extraction in order to meet its climate commitments, the investor whose asset is stranded may bring a claim under an investment treaty, alleging expropriation or breach of fair and equitable treatment. We have seen such claims, and we will examine them in detail in the lectures on investment arbitration and on the Energy Charter Treaty. The state's defence is the right to regulate, reinforced now by the advisory opinions. The second direction is disclosure. Financial regulators and accounting standards bodies increasingly require enterprises to disclose climate-related financial risk, and for a fossil fuel producer the most material such risk is precisely that its reserves become uneconomic. The legal question is whether disclosure of that risk is adequate, and there is active litigation and regulatory scrutiny on the point. The third direction is environmental assessment. Claimants in several jurisdictions have challenged the approval of new extraction projects on the ground that the assessment considered only the emissions from constructing and operating the facility, and not the emissions from the eventual combustion of the fuel it produces. Some courts have accepted that argument. Taken together, these three strands mean that the physical constraint on the atmosphere is being translated into legal constraints on extraction, on disclosure, and on approval. That is the single most important development in the contemporary law of energy.

### 17. What We Have Established `summary`

- Paris binds process tightly and outcome loosely: obligations of conduct, collective goal, self-differentiation.
- Common but differentiated responsibilities survives, but operates now through self-differentiation rather than annexes.
- Three tribunals gave climate advisory opinions in 2024 and 2025, converging on binding obligations and possible responsibility.
- Carbon pricing, border adjustment and Article Six are the operative instruments, each with hard legal difficulties.
- Unburnable carbon converts a physical limit into questions of investor compensation, disclosure and assessment scope.

> **The one thing to remember.** The climate regime is no longer an overlay on energy law. It is becoming the instrument through which energy law is made, and the advisory opinions have made that irreversible.

**Narration**

Let me close. The Paris Agreement binds process tightly and outcome loosely, through obligations of conduct rather than obligations of result, and through self-differentiation rather than annexes. Common but differentiated responsibilities survives as the organising principle, expressed now through the form of each party's own contribution. The advisory opinions delivered by three tribunals in 2024 and 2025 converged on the propositions that states have binding obligations to act, that those obligations are informed by the best available science, and that failure may engage responsibility. Carbon pricing, border adjustment and Article Six are the operative instruments, and each has hard legal difficulties of design. And the problem of unburnable carbon converts a physical limit on the atmosphere into three distinct legal questions, about investor compensation, about disclosure to markets, and about the scope of environmental assessment. In the next lecture we turn to the human dimension of extraction: human rights, indigenous peoples, and the duties that attach to a project on someone else's land.
