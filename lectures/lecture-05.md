### 01. Trade, Investment and Development `bumper`

### 02. Natural Resources in International Economic Law `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Five, which closes the foundations module. We have established what the field governs, where its rules come from, what permanent sovereignty means, and how the global energy order is organised. In this lecture I want to show you the field working as it actually works, and the best way to do that is to put three bodies of law into the same room and let them disagree. The law of the World Trade Organization says one thing about a resource measure. The law of international investment protection frequently says the opposite. And the development case, which is the reason resource-rich states care about these questions at all, says a third thing. A single government decision, for example a requirement that solar panels contain locally manufactured cells, can simultaneously breach the national treatment obligation, support a legitimate industrial policy, and discourage foreign investment. That is not a pathological case. It is Tuesday. Let me show you how to think about it.

### 04. The Trade Layer `section`

**Narration**

Let us begin with trade, and with a preliminary question that determines everything else.

### 05. First Ask: Is It a Good, a Service, or Something Else? `bullets`

- Energy as a good: crude, refined products, coal, gas, electricity in some classifications, and the equipment that produces them.
- Energy as a service: exploration, drilling, engineering, distribution, transmission and retail supply.
- Energy as an investment: a licensed, capital-intensive activity whose value depends on host state conduct.
- The answer determines the applicable regime. The same project is frequently all three at once.

*On-screen note:* Categorisation is not a formality. It decides which treaty applies and which forum hears the case.

**Narration**

The first question in any trade analysis of energy is a question of categorisation, and it is not a formality, because the answer decides which treaty applies and, in practice, which forum hears the case. Energy may be a good: crude oil, refined products, coal, gas, and in a growing number of classifications electricity, are all traded commodities, and the rules that apply to them include the national treatment obligation, the general prohibition on quantitative restrictions, and the rules on subsidies and on customs valuation. Energy may be a service: exploration, drilling, engineering, construction, transmission, distribution and retail supply are all services, and they are governed only to the extent that a member has undertaken commitments under the General Agreement on Trade in Services, which for most members is far less than they have conceded for goods. Energy may be an investment: a licensed, capital-intensive activity whose entire value depends on the conduct of the host state over decades, and which is therefore governed by whatever investment treaties that state has concluded. And here is the difficulty. A single project is frequently all three at once. A liquefied natural gas development involves the sale of a good, a set of engineering and construction services, and an enormous investment protected by treaty. The three characterisations carry three different legal regimes, three different dispute fora, and three different remedial structures. The competent adviser's first move is therefore not to apply the law but to classify the transaction, and to classify it in the knowledge that an opponent will choose whichever characterisation is most favourable to them.

### 06. Export Restrictions, Subsidies and Local Content `bullets`

- Export restrictions: quotas and duties on raw materials are generally prohibited, and the conservation exception is assessed by design.
- Subsidies: clean energy support is challengeable, but proving a benefit requires a properly constructed market benchmark.
- Local content: requirements to use domestic goods or services are the most reliably unlawful instrument in the resource policy toolkit.
- Carbon border adjustment: the newest frontier, testing whether a border measure on embodied carbon can be justified.

*On-screen note:* Every one of these instruments pursues a legitimate objective. Most of them are litigated on design.

**Narration**

There are three instruments through which states pursue resource and industrial policy, and all three are contested, so let me take them in turn and tell you where each one now stands. First, export restrictions. Export quotas and export duties on raw materials are generally inconsistent with the trading system, and additional commitments taken on accession can prohibit export duties outright. A state may seek to justify a restriction on conservation grounds, but as we saw in the rare earths disputes, the exception is assessed by examining the design of the measure rather than the objective the state invokes. Second, subsidies. Support for clean energy, whether through feed-in tariffs, tax credits, contracts for difference or guarantees, may constitute a subsidy and may be challenged. The practical obstacle for complainants, however, is the benefit analysis: establishing that a subsidy confers a benefit requires constructing a market benchmark, and in energy markets, which are heavily regulated and frequently lack a competitive reference price, that is genuinely difficult. Third, local content requirements. A requirement that a project use domestic goods, domestic services or domestic labour is the most reliably unlawful instrument in the entire resource policy toolkit, because it discriminates against imported products in a way that both the goods and the investment rules reach. And then there is the newest frontier: carbon border adjustment, which charges imports for their embodied carbon. Its defenders argue it prevents the relocation of emissions; its critics argue it is protection applied to a state's own industry. Whether it can be justified under the general exceptions is the most important unanswered question in trade law today.

### 07. The Investment Layer `section`

**Narration**

Now let us put the second body of law into the room.

### 08. Why Extractive Industries Dominate Investment Arbitration `bullets`

- The subject matter is immobile: the investment cannot be moved when the dispute arises.
- The capital is sunk, long-lived and exposed to a single sovereign for decades.
- The regulatory surface is enormous: licensing, fiscal, environmental, social and decommissioning rules all bite.
- The quantum is large, which makes the cost of arbitration rational.

*On-screen note:* Immobilised capital plus a single sovereign regulator is the classic recipe for treaty litigation.

**Narration**

Here is a fact about the investment treaty system that explains a great deal about this field. Extractive industries, together with energy supply and generation, account for the largest single share of known investment treaty claims. There are structural reasons for this, and understanding them will make you a better adviser than memorising the case names would. The first reason is immobility. A manufacturing investment can be relocated; a mine, a well or a pipeline cannot. When the relationship with the host state deteriorates, the investor has nowhere to go, and the only remaining remedy is a claim. The second reason is duration and sunkness. Extractive projects involve enormous capital committed years before any revenue, and recovered over decades, which means they are exposed to the conduct of a single sovereign for a period far longer than any political cycle. The third reason is the regulatory surface. An extractive project is exposed to licensing, to the fiscal regime, to environmental regulation, to water rights, to labour and community obligations, to export permissions and to decommissioning liability, and each of those is a site at which a state measure can alter the economics of the investment. The fourth reason is simply quantum. The amounts at stake in these disputes are large enough to make the cost of arbitration rational, which cannot be said of most foreign investments. Put those four together and the pattern is not surprising. Immobilised capital, sunk over decades, facing a single sovereign regulator with a very large surface of regulatory contact, is the classic recipe for treaty litigation.

### 09. The Same Measure, Judged by Two Systems `two-col`

**TRADE LAW ASKS**
- Does the measure discriminate against imported products or foreign service suppliers?
- Is it a prohibited quantitative restriction or a prohibited subsidy?
- If so, does it fall within the general exceptions, and is its design proportionate?
- Remedy: prospective compliance, or retaliation authorised by the organisation.

**INVESTMENT LAW ASKS**
- Has the state's conduct defeated the investor's legitimate expectations?
- Is the measure a direct or indirect expropriation, and was it for a public purpose?
- Does the state's right to regulate, and the police powers doctrine, excuse it?
- Remedy: monetary compensation, sometimes very large, awarded to the investor directly.

**Narration**

Now we come to the collision, and I want you to see it precisely, because it is the core intellectual problem of this lecture. Take a single measure: a state withdraws a subsidy that a foreign-funded renewable project relied upon, or imposes a local content requirement, or bans a method of extraction. Trade law asks one set of questions. Does the measure discriminate against imported products or foreign service suppliers? Is it a prohibited quantitative restriction, or a prohibited subsidy? If so, can it be brought within the general exceptions, and is its design proportionate to the objective claimed? And if it cannot, the remedy is prospective: the state is expected to bring itself into conformity, and in the last resort the complainant may be authorised to suspend concessions. Investment law asks an entirely different set of questions. Has the state's conduct defeated the legitimate expectations that induced the investment? Is the measure an expropriation, direct or indirect, and was it for a public purpose and non-discriminatory? Does the state's right to regulate, expressed through the police powers doctrine, excuse what would otherwise be a breach? And if not, the remedy is retrospective and monetary, and it is paid directly to the investor. Notice how different these are in institutional design. One is state-to-state, prospective, and collective. The other is investor-to-state, retrospective, and individual. One asks whether the measure is lawful going forward. The other asks who pays for the past. A government can comply fully with a trade ruling and still lose an investment claim over the same measure, and that is not a theoretical possibility. It has happened repeatedly, and it is the reason that resource ministries now routinely require both a trade and an investment assessment before acting.

### 10. The Reform Agenda, and What It Would Change `bullets`

- Criticisms converge: inconsistency, lack of an appellate mechanism, cost, and the chilling effect on regulation.
- Responses include express right-to-regulate clauses, narrower definitions of protected investment, and exclusion of purely commercial claims.
- Procedural reforms address arbitrator independence, third-party funding transparency, and mechanisms for early dismissal.
- Structural proposals include a standing investment court with an appeal mechanism, and state-to-state interpretive control.

*On-screen note:* The debate is not whether to reform. It is whether reform can preserve the system's function while addressing its legitimacy deficit.

**Narration**

Before we leave the investment layer I must say something about the reform agenda, because no competent adviser can discuss this field without addressing it, and the criticisms are now well rehearsed. Four of them are prominent. The first is inconsistency: different tribunals have reached materially different conclusions on similar treaty language, and there is no appellate mechanism to resolve the divergence. The second is the absence of appellate review, which means that an error of law is not correctable. The third is cost, both of the proceedings themselves and of the liability that awards impose on public budgets. And the fourth, which is the most serious in my view, is the chilling effect: the concern that governments, and particularly governments in developing states, refrain from regulating in the public interest because they fear a claim. The responses now visible in treaty practice are of two kinds. Substantive provisions include express right-to-regulate clauses, narrower definitions of what qualifies as a protected investment, carve-outs for taxation and for essential security, and provisions excluding claims that could be brought as ordinary commercial disputes. Procedural provisions address arbitrator independence and challenges, transparency and disclosure of third-party funding, mechanisms for the early dismissal of unmeritorious claims, and limits on the quantum recoverable. And there are structural proposals, of which the most developed is a standing investment court with tenured adjudicators and an appeal mechanism, coupled with greater state-to-state control over the interpretation of treaty text. Now, here is the assessment I want you to form for yourselves rather than receive from me. The question is not whether the system requires reform, since it plainly does. The question is whether reform can address the legitimacy deficit without destroying the function the system performs, which is to provide a neutral forum for disputes that would otherwise be resolved by diplomatic protection or not at all. That trade-off is the substance of the debate, and it is being decided now.

### 11. Sovereign Wealth: The Legal Architecture of Saving `bullets`

- The problem: a finite resource converted into a finite stream of revenue, spent against a permanent expectation of public services.
- The instruments are legal: a fiscal rule, a sovereign wealth fund established by statute, and withdrawal and investment mandates.
- Design questions: what may be withdrawn, by whom, on whose approval, and with what transparency obligations?
- The governance failure mode is captured funds, and law is the principal defence against it.

*On-screen note:* A sovereign wealth fund is a constitutional device before it is a financial one.

**Narration**

There is one further instrument of development policy that deserves your attention because it is entirely legal in construction and frequently decisive in effect: the sovereign wealth arrangement. The problem it addresses is straightforward and severe. A finite resource is converted, over perhaps three decades, into a finite stream of revenue. That revenue is spent against expectations of public services that are permanent. Unless part of the revenue is saved and invested, the state faces a fiscal cliff when the resource is exhausted, and it faces it having built public commitments it cannot fund. The instruments through which this is addressed are legal rather than economic. There is a fiscal rule, frequently enacted in legislation, prescribing what proportion of resource revenue may enter the budget and what must be saved. There is a fund, established by statute, with a defined mandate, a governance structure, and an investment policy. There are withdrawal rules, prescribing the circumstances in which the government may draw on the fund and the majority or approvals required to do so. And there are transparency obligations, requiring publication of receipts, of transfers, of holdings and of returns. Now, the design questions that determine whether any of this works are precise and they are all drafting questions. What may be withdrawn, and by whom? Who approves a withdrawal, and on what standard? Are the assets protected from attachment? Are the managers independent of the executive, and for what term? And what must be published, to whom, and when? The characteristic failure mode of these arrangements is capture, in which the fund becomes a source of discretionary finance for the government of the day, insulated from the budget process it was created to discipline. Law is the principal available defence against that outcome, and it is why a lawyer who understands fund design can do more for a resource-rich country than one who merely litigates its disputes.

### 12. Domestic Content and the Limits of Development Justification `case`

*India — Certain Measures Relating to Solar Cells and Solar Modules (WTO, DS456, adopted 2016)*

Under the Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission, India required that solar power developers purchasing electricity under certain contracts use solar cells and modules of domestic manufacture. The United States complained that the domestic content requirements discriminated against imported products. India defended them as measures essential to the acquisition of products in short supply, and as necessary to secure compliance with its laws on sustainable development. The reports found the requirements inconsistent with the national treatment obligation and with the Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures. The exceptions invoked were held not to be available on the facts.

> **Holding / significance.** A development objective does not by itself justify a measure. India had not demonstrated that the products concerned were in short supply, and the measures were not shown to be necessary to secure compliance with its own laws.

**Narration**

Our case is India's solar cells and solar modules dispute, decided at the World Trade Organization in 2016, and I have chosen it because it puts the development argument at the centre of the frame. India's National Solar Mission was a genuinely ambitious programme, designed to expand generating capacity, to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels, and to build a domestic manufacturing industry in a sector in which India had almost no position. To do the latter, it required developers who contracted to sell solar power to use cells and modules manufactured in India. The United States complained. India defended the measures on two principal grounds: that they were essential to the acquisition or distribution of products in general or local short supply, and that they were necessary to secure compliance with India's own laws and obligations concerning sustainable development. The reports found against India. The requirements were inconsistent with the national treatment obligation, because they discriminated against imported products in favour of domestic ones, and inconsistent with the Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures, which prohibits investment measures that breach national treatment. And the exceptions failed on the evidence: India had not demonstrated that the products in question were in short supply, and it had not established that the measures were necessary to secure compliance with its own law. I want to draw out three lessons, and the first is uncomfortable. A development objective, however genuine and however important, does not by itself justify a measure under the trading system; the justification must be established under the specific terms of the exception, with evidence. The second lesson is that the design of the measure failed where a differently designed measure might have succeeded, because subsidies that support domestic manufacturing without conditioning market access on domestic content are treated quite differently. And the third lesson is the strategic one. The states most affected by these decisions have responded by designing policy differently and by pressing for reform, not by abandoning industrial policy. If you advise a developing state, that is the terrain you will work on.

### 13. The Case for Policy Space, Stated Properly `bullets`

- The resource curse literature identifies a correlation between resource dependence and poor developmental outcomes, including conflict and institutional decay.
- The mechanisms are contested: volatility, Dutch disease, rent-seeking, weak institutions, and the structure of the fiscal bargain.
- The policy response is linkage: local content, technology transfer, beneficiation and revenue management.
- The legal question is which of those instruments survive the disciplines described in this lecture.

*On-screen note:* Policy space is not a slogan. It is a claim about which instruments remain available to a state that wishes to develop its own resources.

**Narration**

I do not want to leave you with the impression that the development case is merely a rhetorical resource for states defending unlawful measures, because that would be a serious misunderstanding and a common one. There is a substantial body of economic and political-science literature on what is called the resource curse: the observed correlation between dependence on resource exports and poor developmental outcomes, including slower growth, greater inequality, weaker institutions and, in the worst cases, civil conflict. The mechanisms are contested, and you should be sceptical of any single explanation. The candidate explanations include price volatility, which makes fiscal planning extremely difficult; the crowding out of other tradable sectors through exchange rate effects; rent-seeking and corruption, which are encouraged when large revenues arrive without corresponding taxation of citizens; and the structure of the fiscal bargain itself, under which governments financed by resource rents are less accountable to the populations they govern. Whatever the weighting, the policy conclusions converge on a set of instruments: capture more of the rent, manage it transparently, save rather than consume part of it, and use the resource to build productive capacity elsewhere in the economy. That is what local content requirements, technology transfer obligations, beneficiation policies and sovereign wealth arrangements are for. And here is the legal question that this field exists to answer. Which of those instruments survive the disciplines I have described in this lecture? Which can be redesigned to survive them? And where the answer is none, is the law itself defensible? That is not a rhetorical question, and I would encourage you to take it seriously rather than to assume that the trading system has already resolved it.

### 14. Due Diligence: Regulating the Chain Rather Than the Site `bullets`

- A newer technique: rather than regulate the mine, regulate the importer who buys from it.
- Obligations typically cover human rights, labour conditions, conflict financing and environmental performance.
- Compliance is evidenced through audit, traceability and disclosure rather than through inspection by the state.
- The legal questions concern extraterritoriality, the burden placed on small suppliers, and the measure of due diligence required.

*On-screen note:* Regulation follows the product rather than the territory. This is the most significant jurisdictional innovation in the field.

**Narration**

I want to close with the instrument that has grown fastest in this field and that raises the most interesting jurisdictional questions, which is mandatory supply-chain due diligence. The technique is a departure from everything we have considered so far, and it is worth understanding precisely why. The traditional approach to regulating an extractive project is to regulate the site: to impose conditions on the licence, to inspect the operation, and to sanction the operator. Due diligence regulation does something different. It regulates the importer, the manufacturer or the retailer at the consuming end of the chain, requiring that entity to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for adverse impacts occurring far upstream, in a jurisdiction where the regulating state has no enforcement presence at all. The obligations typically extend to human rights, to labour conditions, to the financing of conflict, to corruption, and to environmental performance. Compliance is evidenced not by state inspection but by the regulated entity's own processes: by mapping the supply chain, by contractual cascading of standards, by audit, by traceability systems, and by public disclosure. Now, this instrument generates genuinely hard legal questions, and I commend all three of them to you. The first is jurisdictional: on what basis may a state prescribe standards for conduct occurring in another state, and does the answer differ where the regulated entity is within its own territory? The second is distributive: due diligence obligations are effectively fixed costs, and they fall hardest on the smallest suppliers, who are least able to bear them and most likely to be excluded from chains as a result, which can harm precisely the communities the instrument is intended to assist. And the third concerns standard: what, exactly, does due diligence require, and can an obligation expressed in such open-textured terms be complied with in a way that is auditable and defensible? Those questions are being worked out now, in litigation and in guidance, and they will be settled by your generation of lawyers.

### 15. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**
- Build a three-column table for any resource measure: what trade law says, what investment law says, what development policy wants.
- Learn the difference between prospective and retrospective remedies, and be able to say who receives each.
- Take one local content requirement and try to redesign it so that it pursues the same objective lawfully.
- Practise classifying a transaction as good, service or investment, and justify the classification in one sentence.

**Postgraduate level**
- Assess whether the resource curse literature supports the policy instruments actually adopted in its name.
- Evaluate the coherence of a system in which the same measure is lawful under one regime and compensable under another.
- Analyse the benefit analysis in subsidy disputes as the principal obstacle to challenging energy support schemes.
- Critique the reform agenda from the perspective of a capital-importing state rather than a capital-exporting one.

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want one habit. Whenever you meet a resource measure, build a three-column table: what the trade rules say about it, what the investment rules say about it, and what development policy wants it to achieve. You will find that the columns rarely agree, and learning to hold that disagreement is most of what this field requires. Learn the difference between prospective and retrospective remedies, and be able to say who receives each, because that distinction explains the politics of the reform debate better than any amount of doctrine. Take one local content requirement and attempt to redesign it so that it pursues the same objective lawfully, which is the exercise a government lawyer actually performs. And practise classifying transactions as goods, services or investments, and justifying the classification in a single sentence. At postgraduate level, I want critique. Assess honestly whether the resource curse literature supports the particular instruments adopted in its name, because the connection is frequently asserted rather than demonstrated, and the instruments sometimes serve domestic constituencies rather than development. Evaluate the coherence of a legal order in which the same measure can be lawful under one regime and compensable under another, and say whether you regard that as fragmentation, pluralism, or simply incoherence. Analyse the benefit requirement in subsidy disputes as the principal practical obstacle to challenging energy support, and consider whether that obstacle is a defect or a sensible limit. And critique the reform agenda from the perspective of a capital-importing state, rather than from the perspective that dominates the literature, which is that of the capital exporters and their advisers.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Categorisation decides the regime: the same resource transaction may be a good, a service and an investment at once.
- Export restrictions, subsidies and local content are the three contested instruments of resource policy.
- Extractive industries dominate investment arbitration for structural reasons: immobility, sunkness, regulatory surface and quantum.
- Trade law is state-to-state and prospective; investment law is investor-to-state and retrospective. They collide routinely.
- The development case for policy space is serious, but it must be made through instruments that survive legal scrutiny.

> **The one thing to remember.** A resource measure is almost never examined by one body of law. It is examined by several, which disagree, and the lawyer's task is to anticipate the collision before the government acts.

**Narration**

Let me close the foundations module. Categorisation decides the regime, and a single resource transaction may be simultaneously a good, a service and an investment. Three instruments carry most resource policy: export restrictions, subsidies and local content requirements, and all three are legally contested. Extractive industries dominate investment arbitration for structural reasons that are unlikely to change: immobile assets, sunk capital, an enormous regulatory surface, and claims large enough to fund. The trade and investment layers are designed differently, one state-to-state and prospective, the other investor-to-state and retrospective, and they collide over the same measures with sufficient regularity that governments now plan for the collision. And the development case for policy space is serious, evidenced, and must nonetheless be pursued through instruments capable of surviving scrutiny, which is a design problem rather than a rhetorical one. In our case, India's solar programme, we saw an ambitious and defensible industrial policy fail because the instrument chosen could not be justified on the evidence. That is the most common way for a state to lose in this field. In the next lecture we leave the foundations and begin the substantive regimes, starting with the law of international petroleum.
