## Lecture Script

### 01. Human Rights, Indigenous Peoples and Extraction `bumper`

### 02. Human Rights, Indigenous Peoples and Resource Extraction `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Fourteen. Let me begin with a structural fact rather than a legal rule, because once you see it you will not be able to look at this field in the same way. An extractive project is immovable. It can only be built where the resource is. And the place where the resource is, is almost always somewhere that somebody already lives, farms, fishes, grazes, worships, or draws water from. That is not an accident of bad planning; it is the defining condition of the industry. It follows that extraction generates human rights questions structurally and not incidentally, because the project necessarily comes into conflict with rights that are already held by people who were there first. Everything else in this lecture follows from that. The question is not whether human rights are engaged by extraction. It is who owes what to whom, and what happens when those duties are not met.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. First, to explain why resource extraction generates human rights questions structurally rather than incidentally, and to derive the legal consequences of that. Second, to identify the principal international sources of the rights engaged by extractive projects, and to distinguish those that bind states from those that address enterprises. Fifth, to assess the content and the legal status of the duty to obtain free, prior and informed consent, which is the most contested standard in the field. And sixth, to evaluate the argument that human rights law now constrains extraction more effectively than resource law itself does, which is a claim I want you to test rather than accept.

### 04. Sources and Duties `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the sources, and with a distinction that organises the whole subject.

### 05. Two Bodies of Law, Two Kinds of Duty `two-col`

**STATE OBLIGATIONS**
- The International Covenants guarantee the right of all peoples freely to dispose of their natural wealth and resources.
- Civil and political rights: life, liberty, fair trial, freedom of expression, freedom of assembly.
- Economic, social and cultural rights: health, water, housing, food, and the benefits of scientific progress.
- Regional charters add collective rights, including the right of peoples to dispose of their wealth and resources.

**ADDRESSING ENTERPRISES**
- The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights: a state duty to protect, a corporate responsibility to respect, and access to remedy.
- The OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, with National Contact Points handling complaints.
- Mandatory human rights and environmental due diligence legislation in a growing number of jurisdictions.
- Litigation against parent companies in their home courts, relying on ordinary tort principles.

**Narration**

The most important distinction in this subject is between the body of law that binds states and the body of standards that addresses enterprises, because the two operate through entirely different mechanisms and students routinely conflate them. On the state side, the obligations are treaty-based and enforceable. Both International Covenants provide, in a common article, that all peoples may, for their own ends, freely dispose of their natural wealth and resources, and that in no case may a people be deprived of its own means of subsistence. Beyond that specific provision, the whole range of rights is engaged by an extractive project: civil and political rights, including the right to life, to liberty, to a fair trial, to freedom of expression and to freedom of assembly, since protests against projects are frequently met with force; and economic, social and cultural rights, including the right to health, to water, to housing and to food, since contamination and displacement affect all of them. Regional charters add a further dimension: the African Charter, as we shall see in our case, guarantees the right of peoples to freely dispose of their wealth and natural resources and to a general satisfactory environment, which is an unusually direct textual hook. On the enterprise side, the instruments are different in kind. The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights rest on three pillars: the state's existing duty to protect against human rights abuses by third parties, including business; the corporate responsibility to respect human rights, which is a responsibility rather than a treaty obligation; and the need for effective access to remedy. The OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises are recommendations addressed by governments to enterprises, backed by National Contact Points which receive complaints. And mandatory human rights and environmental due diligence legislation, now adopted in several jurisdictions and at various stages of development, converts what was voluntary into a legal obligation with sanctions. Notice what has happened. The state duty has been constant; the enterprise-facing standards have been hardening into law.

### 06. Free, Prior and Informed Consent: Content and Status `bullets`

- Free: no coercion, intimidation or manipulation, and no artificial deadline.
- Prior: sought sufficiently in advance of authorisation, and before any activity begins.
- Informed: full disclosure of the nature, scale, reversibility and scope of the project, and of its risks and benefits.
- Consent: the contested element — is it agreement, or is it consultation in good faith aimed at agreement?

*On-screen note:* The first three elements are now relatively settled. The fourth is where the entire argument sits.

**Narration**

Let me now take apart the standard that dominates this field, because it is invoked constantly and defined rarely, and because its legal status is genuinely contested. The standard has four elements. Free means that consent must be given without coercion, intimidation or manipulation, and without artificial deadlines imposed to engineer agreement. Prior means that it must be sought sufficiently in advance of any authorisation or commencement of activity, with adequate time for the community's own decision-making processes, which are frequently slower than a corporate timetable allows. Informed means full and understandable disclosure of the nature, scale, pace, reversibility and scope of the project, of the reasons for it, of its duration, of the areas affected, of the preliminary assessment of likely economic, social, cultural and environmental impact, of the personnel likely to be involved, and of the procedures available for redress. And then there is consent itself, and this is where the entire argument sits. On one reading, consent means agreement: a project may not proceed without the community's approval, which amounts in substance to a veto. On another reading, it means consultation conducted in good faith, through culturally appropriate processes, and directed towards obtaining agreement, but not requiring it. Now, let me tell you the honest state of the law. The Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples is not a treaty and does not bind as such. Its provisions on consent appear in the context of measures affecting indigenous lands and resources. Several states have adopted the weaker reading, accepting a duty to consult but not a duty to obtain agreement. International human rights bodies have moved steadily toward the stronger reading, particularly where a project would have a major impact or would involve relocation. And the commercial reality has outrun the legal debate, because lenders, insurers and purchasers now require adherence to the stronger standard regardless of its formal legal status. The practical advice is therefore unchanged from what I said in the mining lecture: establish the standard applicable in your jurisdiction, and then exceed it.

### 07. The State Must Protect Against the Company `case`

*Social and Economic Rights Action Center and Center for Economic and Social Rights v. Nigeria, African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, Communication 155/96 (2001)*

The communication concerned the Ogoni people of the Niger Delta. It alleged that oil exploration and production, conducted by a consortium in which the state oil company participated, had caused severe environmental degradation and health problems, and that government security forces had carried out killings, destruction of property and other abuses during protests, culminating in the trial and execution of Ogoni leaders in 1995. The African Commission found violations of the rights to life, to health, to property, to family life, to a general satisfactory environment, and of the right of peoples to freely dispose of their wealth and natural resources. Crucially, it held that the state's obligation is not merely to refrain from violating rights itself, but to protect its population from damaging acts committed by third parties, including corporations, and that failure to monitor and regulate the operations of those corporations engages the state's responsibility.

> **Holding / significance.** A state's human rights obligations include a positive duty to protect its population from harmful acts by private actors. Regulatory failure in the face of known harm is a violation.

**Narration**

Our case is one of the most important decisions in the entire field, and it comes from the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights rather than from a court, which is itself a point worth noting. It is the Ogoni case, decided in 2001. The communication was brought by two non-governmental organisations against Nigeria, and it concerned the consequences of oil extraction in Ogoniland in the Niger Delta. The allegations were comprehensive. They concerned environmental degradation from oil operations carried out by a consortium in which the state oil company participated; the contamination of water, soil and air and the resulting effects on health and on the ability of the Ogoni people to farm and to fish; the displacement of communities and the destruction of homes; the conduct of government security forces, which were alleged to have carried out killings, beatings, rapes and the destruction of property during protests against the industry; and the trial and execution of Ogoni leaders in 1995 following proceedings that were alleged to fall far short of international fair trial standards. The Commission found violations across a remarkable range of the African Charter: the right to life, the right to health, the right to property, the right to family life, the right to a general satisfactory environment favourable to development, and the right of peoples to freely dispose of their wealth and natural resources. It also found that the trials violated the right to a fair trial. Now, the holding that matters most, and the reason this decision is studied everywhere, is the Commission's treatment of the state's duty. It held that the state's obligation is not discharged by refraining from violating rights itself. The state must protect its population from damaging acts committed by third parties, including corporations. A government that knows of harm, has the power to prevent it, and does not act, is responsible for it. That principle has since shaped the entire business and human rights field, and if you take one proposition from this lecture, take that one.

### 08. Indigenous and Tribal Peoples: The Specific Regimes `bullets`

- Indigenous and tribal peoples hold a distinct legal position, resting on attachment to territory rather than on citizenship alone.
- ILO Convention No. 169 requires consultation through appropriate procedures and, in appropriate cases, good faith studies of impact.
- The Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples addresses relocation, storage of hazardous materials and redress.
- Regional courts have derived concrete duties: delimitation, effective participation, prior impact assessment, benefit sharing.

*On-screen note:* The distinctive claim is not to a share of the resource. It is to the territory, and therefore to the decision about it.

**Narration**

Let me now separate out the position of indigenous and tribal peoples, because it is legally distinct and because the distinction is frequently blurred in general writing on this subject. The distinct position rests on a particular fact: the relationship of these peoples to their territory is constitutive of their identity, their culture and their survival, and it is not reducible to a property interest held by an individual citizen. The law reflects that in several instruments. ILO Convention No. 169, which is the principal binding treaty on the subject, requires governments to consult the peoples concerned, through appropriate procedures and in particular through their representative institutions, whenever legislative or administrative measures may affect them directly; it requires those consultations to be undertaken with the objective of achieving agreement or consent; and it provides that rights to natural resources pertaining to their lands shall be specially safeguarded, including the right to participate in the use, management and conservation of those resources. The Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, which is not a treaty but which has shaped practice profoundly, addresses relocation, requiring free and informed consent and just and fair compensation with wherever possible the option of return; the storage and disposal of hazardous materials on indigenous lands, which requires consent; and redress for lands taken without consent. And the regional human rights courts have converted these general provisions into concrete duties. The Inter-American Court, in its leading judgment on the point, held that where a state proposes a concession or a large-scale development project on indigenous territory, it must delimit and demarcate that territory, ensure the effective participation of the community, carry out prior environmental and social impact assessments, and ensure that the community receives a reasonable benefit; and that where the project would have a major impact, the state must obtain consent. Notice what the underlying claim is. It is not a claim to a share of the resource. It is a claim to the territory, and therefore to the decision about it.

### 09. Remedy `section`

**Narration**

Let me now turn to the question that determines whether any of this is real: what remedy is available, and to whom?

### 10. Defenders, Dissent, and the Shrinking Space for Objection `bullets`

- Those who oppose extractive projects are among the most exposed people in the world, and the exposure is documented.
- The rights engaged are the rights to life, to liberty, to freedom of expression, association and peaceful assembly.
- The state's duty includes a duty to protect, to investigate, and to ensure accountability for attacks on defenders.
- Criminalisation is the subtler instrument: defamation, unlawful assembly, and offences specific to protest.

*On-screen note:* A right to be consulted that carries a risk of violence is not a right at all. The two questions must be asked together.

**Narration**

There is an aspect of this subject that sits at the boundary between law and physical safety, and it would be a serious omission to leave it out. People who oppose extractive projects, and who organise opposition to them, are among the most exposed people in the world. That is not advocacy; it is a documented finding of the United Nations special procedures, of regional human rights bodies and of non-governmental organisations working in the field, and the pattern is consistent across regions and commodities. The rights engaged are elementary: the right to life, to liberty and security of person, and the rights to freedom of expression, association and peaceful assembly. The state's duty in respect of them is threefold and is not merely negative. It must protect, by taking reasonable measures to prevent attacks of which it knows or ought to know. It must investigate, by conducting an effective, prompt and impartial investigation into any attack, since impunity is the principal reason the pattern persists. And it must ensure accountability, through prosecution or other appropriate means. Now, alongside violence there is a subtler and more widespread phenomenon, which is criminalisation. Those who oppose projects are charged with defamation, with unlawful assembly, with public order offences, and in several jurisdictions with offences specific to protests against infrastructure. Strategic litigation against public participation, in which a company sues its critics, has the same effect through the civil courts. The legal response is uncertain but developing: several jurisdictions have enacted protections for environmental defenders, and the regional human rights bodies have been firm that criminalising peaceful protest against a project is inconsistent with the guarantees of the conventions they supervise. I want to leave you with the point that ties this to the rest of the lecture. A right to be consulted is meaningless if exercising it carries a risk of violence or prosecution. The two questions must always be asked together: whether the community was consulted, and whether it was safe to object.

### 11. The Remedy Gap, and the Routes Around It `bullets`

- Host state courts: the forum of principle, but frequently inaccessible, slow, or subject to pressure.
- Home state courts: claims against the parent company, relying on a duty of care or on participation in the wrong.
- Non-judicial mechanisms: National Contact Points under the OECD Guidelines, and the grievance mechanisms of lenders and of projects.
- The obstacles recur: jurisdiction, limitation periods, corporate separateness, cost, and the difficulty of disclosure.

*On-screen note:* The right exists on paper far more often than the remedy exists in practice. Closing that gap is the field's central task.

**Narration**

The most important failing of this field is not the absence of standards. It is the absence of remedy, and I want you to understand the gap precisely. A community harmed by an extractive project has, in principle, several routes. There is the host state's own courts, which are the forum of principle and frequently the forum of last resort in practice; they may be inaccessible in the relevant language and geography, slow, expensive, ill-resourced, or subject to political pressure where the state is itself a party to the project. There are the courts of the home state of the parent company, and this is the route that has produced the most significant developments: claimants sue the parent, in its own jurisdiction, alleging that it owed a duty of care to those affected by its subsidiary's operations, or that it participated in the wrong. That litigation has produced important decisions on jurisdiction, on the scope of a parent's duty, and on the disclosure of internal documents. There are non-judicial mechanisms: the National Contact Points established under the OECD Guidelines, which receive complaints and may offer mediation; the accountability mechanisms of multilateral lenders; and project-level grievance procedures, which are now standard requirements. And there are the mandatory due diligence regimes, which increasingly provide for administrative enforcement. But the obstacles recur in case after case, and they are formidable. There is jurisdiction, and the forum disputes that precede any hearing on the merits. There are limitation periods, which may extinguish claims arising from events decades earlier. There is the principle of corporate separateness, which shields a parent for the acts of a subsidiary. There is cost, and the asymmetry of resources between a community and a multinational enterprise. And there is disclosure, since the documents that would prove the case are held by the defendant. The right, in short, exists on paper far more often than the remedy exists in practice, and closing that gap is the central unfinished task of this field.

### 12. Resettlement: Compensation Is Not Restoration `bullets`

- Displacement is rarely merely physical: it removes access to land, water, grazing, fishing, burial grounds and livelihood.
- Compensation calculated on a market valuation of land systematically undervalues subsistence and common property.
- The better standards require restoration of livelihoods, replacement housing of adequate quality, and a transition period.
- The legal questions concern the timing of payment, the definition of eligibility, and the treatment of informal and customary rights.

*On-screen note:* The test of a resettlement plan is not whether people were paid. It is whether their standard of living was restored.

**Narration**

Let me take one further practical subject, because it arises in nearly every extractive project and because it is where the gap between legal form and lived outcome is widest. That subject is resettlement. When a project requires land, people are moved. The legal difficulties begin immediately, and they begin with the characterisation of what is being lost. Displacement is rarely merely physical. It removes access to land, but also to water sources, to grazing and to fishing grounds, to forest products, to burial grounds and places of worship, and to the social structures and networks through which a livelihood is actually conducted. A compensation calculation based on a market valuation of the land area, which is the default in many legal systems, systematically undervalues all of that, and undervalues it most severely for the poorest, whose holdings are frequently held under customary or informal rights that a formal valuation does not recognise. The better standards, which are now embedded in the requirements of most multilateral lenders and of many national frameworks, proceed differently. They require that livelihoods and standards of living be restored, and preferably improved; that replacement housing be of adequate quality, security of tenure and access to services; that vulnerable groups be identified and specifically provided for; that a transition period and a grievance mechanism be established; and that the resettlement be supervised and its completion independently verified. The legal questions within that are precise, and each of them is a drafting point. What is the eligibility date, and how is it established and communicated, so as to prevent both the influx of opportunists and the exclusion of legitimate claimants? When must payment be made, relative to the taking of possession? How are informal and customary rights to be evidenced and valued? And what happens if restoration fails, since a plan that has not delivered cannot be undone? The test of a resettlement plan is not whether people were paid. It is whether their standard of living was restored, and whether they would consent again knowing what they now know.

### 13. The Shift to Mandatory Due Diligence `bullets`

- The Guiding Principles were deliberately non-binding, and their author regarded enforcement as the crucial gap.
- Jurisdictions have since legislated: supply chain acts, corporate sustainability due diligence regimes, and import controls.
- The obligations are typically to identify, prevent, mitigate, cease and account for adverse impacts across the value chain.
- Enforcement is administrative and civil rather than criminal, and the debate is now about whether it is effective.

*On-screen note:* Voluntary standards produced reporting. Mandatory standards were legislated because reporting was not enough.

**Narration**

Let me trace the most important regulatory development in this field, because it happened quickly and its significance is not always appreciated. The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, endorsed in 2011, were deliberately constructed as a framework rather than as a treaty. Their author, Professor John Ruggie, was explicit that the crucial gap in the field was not the identification of standards but the enforcement of them, and he observed that voluntary initiatives produce a great deal of reporting and rather less change on the ground. That observation has proved influential, and a number of jurisdictions have since legislated. The instruments take several forms. There are supply chain acts, which require large enterprises to exercise due diligence over human rights and environmental impacts in their supply chains, and to report and to provide a complaints mechanism. There are corporate sustainability due diligence regimes, which impose obligations extending through the chain of activities and, in some formulations, a civil liability for failure. There are import controls, which prohibit the entry of goods produced with specified practices, including forced labour. And there are disclosure regimes, which require reporting on modern slavery, on non-financial matters, or on climate-related risk. The obligations they impose follow a common architecture: identify actual and potential adverse impacts; prevent and mitigate those impacts; bring actual impacts to an end; track implementation and results; and communicate how impacts are addressed. Enforcement is administrative and civil rather than criminal, which is a deliberate choice, and there is now a vigorous debate about whether the regimes will prove effective or will generate compliance documentation rather than changed practice. That debate is worth joining, and I would point you to a specific question within it: whether the regimes reach the small and informal enterprises at the base of most mineral supply chains, where the worst abuses occur and where due diligence is hardest to perform.

### 14. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**
- Be able to state why extraction produces human rights questions structurally rather than accidentally.
- Distinguish the state duty to protect from the corporate responsibility to respect, and say which binds.
- Take SERAC v. Nigeria and identify each right found to have been violated, and the reasoning on the state's positive duty.

**Postgraduate level**
- Assess whether consent should operate as a veto, and defend your position against the strongest contrary case.
- Analyse the parent company litigation: does it provide meaningful accountability or merely relocate the dispute?
- Evaluate whether mandatory due diligence will change conduct at the base of supply chains, or only in the middle.
- Critique the remedy gap: is the solution procedural reform, a treaty, or something else?

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want four things. First, the ability to state why extraction produces human rights questions structurally rather than accidentally, and that answer should be about immovability: the project must go where the resource is, and people are already there. Second, a clean distinction between the state duty to protect, which is a legal obligation, and the corporate responsibility to respect, which under the Guiding Principles is not, together with an explanation of how mandatory due diligence legislation is changing that. Third, a close reading of our case, in which you identify each right found to have been violated and you can articulate the Commission's reasoning on the state's positive duty in your own words. And fourth, the four elements of free, prior and informed consent, with particular attention to the fourth. At postgraduate level, I want defended positions. Assess whether consent should operate as a veto, and make the strongest case you can for whichever position you reject, because the argument is genuinely balanced. Analyse the parent company litigation and give a view on whether it delivers accountability or simply relocates disputes into a forum where the claimants can afford to litigate but where the underlying operations are unaffected. Evaluate whether mandatory due diligence will change conduct at the base of supply chains, where the worst abuses occur, or only in the middle, where enterprises have the capacity to comply. And critique the remedy gap directly: is the answer procedural reform in home state courts, a binding treaty on business and human rights, or something the field has not yet tried?

### 15. What We Have Established `summary`

- Extraction generates human rights questions structurally, because the project must go where the resource is and people are already there.
- State obligations are treaty-based; enterprise-facing standards have been hardening into mandatory law.
- Free, prior and informed consent is settled on three elements and contested on the fourth.
- SERAC v. Nigeria established that a state must protect its population from harm by private actors, including corporations.
- The remedy gap, not the standard gap, is the field's central failure.

> **The one thing to remember.** The decisive principle is that a state does not discharge its human rights obligations by standing aside. Regulatory failure in the face of known harm is a violation.

**Narration**

Let me close. Extraction generates human rights questions structurally, because a project must be built where the resource is and people are already living there. State obligations under the human rights treaties are well established and bind directly, while the standards addressed to enterprises have been hardening from voluntary guidance into mandatory due diligence legislation across a growing number of jurisdictions. Free, prior and informed consent is settled as to three of its four elements and genuinely contested as to the fourth, and the commercial requirements have outrun the legal position in any event. In the Ogoni case we saw the African Commission establish the principle that now underpins the entire business and human rights field: a state does not discharge its obligations by standing aside, and regulatory failure in the face of known harm is itself a violation. And the central failure of the field remains the remedy gap rather than the standard gap, since rights exist on paper far more often than remedies exist in practice. In the next lecture we turn to the environmental dimension proper: biodiversity, water, and the regulation of the extractive sector's impact on the living world.
