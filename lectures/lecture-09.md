## Lecture Script

### 01. The Law of the Sea and Offshore Energy `bumper`

### 02. The Law of the Sea and Offshore Energy Resources `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Nine. We now leave the shore. A large and growing share of the world's oil and gas is produced offshore, and the offshore is where the law of the sea and the law of energy meet directly. I want to begin with a proposition that will organise the whole lecture. Before any question of licensing, contracting or investment arises in the offshore, a prior question must be answered: in whose maritime zone does this prospect lie? Until that question is answered, nobody knows which state may license the block, which fiscal regime applies, and which courts or tribunals have jurisdiction. Everything downstream depends on it. And the answer is supplied not by energy law at all but by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which is the constitutional instrument of the oceans and, incidentally, one of the most successful treaties ever concluded. Let me show you how it allocates the resource base.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. First, to describe the maritime zones created by the Convention and the precise resource rights each one confers, because the rights differ by zone and the differences matter. Second, to explain the legal definition and the outer limits of the continental shelf, including the extended continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles and the role of the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf. Third, to analyse the methodology of maritime delimitation, and the line of cases that established it, since a great deal of offshore acreage is contested. Fourth, to explain how states manage shared offshore resources, both pending delimitation and afterwards, through provisional arrangements and joint development zones. Fifth, to describe the regime of the Area, the seabed beyond national jurisdiction, and the current state of the regulation of deep seabed mining. And sixth, to apply all of this to a disputed offshore prospect, which is the practical test.

### 04. Zones and Rights `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the zones, and with the resource rights attached to each.

### 05. Six Zones, and a Different Resource Right in Each `bullets`

- Internal waters and territorial sea: sovereignty, subject to innocent passage; the coastal state's powers are at their fullest.
- Exclusive economic zone: sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing natural resources, living and non-living.
- Continental shelf: sovereign rights over the mineral and other non-living resources of the seabed and subsoil, which exist ipso jure.
- The Area: the seabed beyond national jurisdiction, held as the common heritage of mankind and administered by the International Seabed Authority.
- High seas: the water column beyond the exclusive economic zone, governed by the freedom of the high seas and by conservation duties.
- The distinction between the seabed and the water column is fundamental: they can be allocated to different states or to different regimes.

*On-screen note:* Sovereignty in the territorial sea, sovereign rights in the EEZ and on the shelf, and neither in the Area.

**Narration**

The Convention creates a zonal framework, and the resource rights differ at each step, so let me be precise. In internal waters and the territorial sea, the coastal state has sovereignty, subject to the right of innocent passage and to other limitations; its powers over resources are at their fullest here. Beyond and adjacent to the territorial sea lies the exclusive economic zone, which may extend to two hundred nautical miles from the baselines. Here the coastal state does not have sovereignty but sovereign rights, and they are functional rather than territorial: sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, whether living or non-living, of the waters superjacent to the seabed and of the seabed and its subsoil. Then there is the continental shelf, and here I want you to notice something that surprises students. The rights of the coastal state over its continental shelf exist ipso jure: they do not depend on occupation, on effective control, or on any proclamation. They exist because the state is the coastal state. What the coastal state has on the shelf is sovereign rights over the mineral and other non-living resources of the seabed and subsoil, together with living organisms belonging to sedentary species. Beyond national jurisdiction lies the Area: the seabed and ocean floor and subsoil, declared the common heritage of mankind, in respect of which no state may claim or exercise sovereignty, and which is administered by the International Seabed Authority on behalf of mankind as a whole. And above the Area lies the water column of the high seas, governed by the freedoms of the high seas subject to conservation and cooperation duties. The distinction between the seabed and the water column is fundamental and frequently overlooked: the two can be, and sometimes are, allocated to different states or subjected to different regimes.

### 06. Article 76: How Far Does the Shelf Go? `bullets`

- The shelf extends to the outer edge of the continental margin, or to two hundred nautical miles where the margin does not reach that far.
- Beyond two hundred miles, the outer limits are fixed by complex formulae and constraint lines set out in Article 76.
- A coastal state making a claim beyond two hundred miles must submit information to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf.
- The Commission makes recommendations; the coastal state then establishes the final and binding limits on the basis of those recommendations.

*On-screen note:* Within two hundred miles, entitlement is automatic. Beyond it, entitlement must be demonstrated geologically and approved.

**Narration**

The single most technically demanding provision in the Convention is Article 76, which defines the continental shelf and its outer limits, and I will give you the essentials because offshore energy increasingly depends on it. The shelf comprises the seabed and subsoil of the submarine areas that extend beyond the territorial sea throughout the natural prolongation of the land territory to the outer edge of the continental margin, or to a distance of two hundred nautical miles from the baselines where the outer edge of the margin does not extend that far. That second limb is what makes the shelf legally independent of geology up to two hundred miles: every coastal state has a shelf of at least that distance, whether or not its geology supports it. Beyond two hundred miles, entitlement depends on demonstrating the natural prolongation, and the outer limits must be fixed by reference to a set of formulae and constraint lines laid down in the article. These involve the determination of the foot of the continental slope, the application of a sediment thickness formula and a distance formula, and the application of two constraint lines. It is, in short, applied geology married to law. A coastal state that wishes to establish limits beyond two hundred miles must submit the relevant scientific and technical information to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, a body of specialists in geology, geophysics and hydrography. The Commission examines the submission and makes recommendations, and the coastal state then establishes the outer limits of its shelf on the basis of those recommendations, and those limits, once established, are final and binding. The practical consequence for the energy lawyer is straightforward but important. Within two hundred miles, entitlement is automatic. Beyond it, entitlement must be demonstrated and approved, and until it is, the legal status of the seabed is unsettled.

### 07. The rights of the coastal State over the continental shelf exist ipso jure and a `quote`

> The rights of the coastal State over the continental shelf exist ipso jure and ab initio, by virtue of its sovereignty over the land, and as an extension of it in an exercise of sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring the seabed and exploiting its natural resources.
>
> — North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (Federal Republic of Germany v Denmark; Federal Republic of Germany v Netherlands), I.C.J. Reports 1969, p. 3, at para. 19

**Narration**

Let me read you a sentence from the North Sea Continental Shelf cases of 1969, because it is the foundation stone of everything that follows. The Court held that the rights of the coastal state over the continental shelf exist ipso jure and ab initio, by virtue of its sovereignty over the land, and as an extension of it, in an exercise of sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring the seabed and exploiting its natural resources. Three things are packed into that sentence. The rights exist ipso jure, which means by operation of law and not by occupation or proclamation. They exist ab initio, which means from the beginning and not from the moment the state asserts them. And they exist as an extension of the state's sovereignty over its land territory, which is the doctrinal basis for the shelf's connection to natural prolongation. That holding, from a judgment delivered in 1969, was subsequently codified in the Convention and remains the law. Notice also what it removes. A state does not need to drill a well, plant a flag, or pass legislation in order to have rights over its shelf. This is one of the very few places in international law where title arises without any act of possession, and it is why the shelf, rather than the water column, is the dominant legal concept in offshore energy.

### 08. Delimitation and Shared Resources `section`

**Narration**

Now we come to the hard case: what happens where the entitlements of two states overlap?

### 09. The Three-Stage Approach to Maritime Delimitation `bullets`

- Stage one: draw a provisional equidistance line, unless there are compelling reasons to use another method.
- Stage two: consider whether any relevant circumstances require the line to be adjusted to achieve an equitable result.
- Stage three: test for disproportionality, comparing the ratio of coastal lengths to the ratio of maritime areas allocated.
- The result is not a discretionary equitable decision; it is a structured method with equity as its objective rather than its method.

*On-screen note:* Equidistance is the method. Equitable result is the objective. The cases insist on the difference.

**Narration**

Where the entitlements of two adjacent or opposite states overlap, the Convention requires delimitation by agreement on the basis of international law in order to achieve an equitable solution. The cases have turned that rather open-textured instruction into a structured methodology, and it now has three stages, which I want you to know precisely. At the first stage, the tribunal draws a provisional equidistance line, unless there are compelling reasons making that method inappropriate in the particular case. At the second stage, the tribunal considers whether there are relevant circumstances which require the provisional line to be adjusted or shifted in order to achieve an equitable result. Relevant circumstances have included the concavity or convexity of a coast, the presence of islands, the disparity in the lengths of the relevant coasts, the conduct of the parties, and considerations of navigational or resource-related access. At the third stage, the tribunal applies a disproportionality test: it compares the ratio of the lengths of the relevant coasts of the parties with the ratio of the maritime areas allocated to each by the line, and satisfies itself that the result is not so disproportionate as to be inequitable. Now, here is the point about this methodology that matters most, and it is a point that separates the informed from the merely well-read. Equity is the objective of the exercise, not the method. The tribunal does not simply decide what is fair; it applies a structured method whose purpose is to produce an equitable result. That distinction was hard-won over decades of jurisprudence, and it is why the case-law is now reasonably predictable, which in turn is why most delimitation disputes are settled rather than litigated.

### 10. Provisional Arrangements and Joint Development `two-col`

**PENDING DELIMITATION**
- States are obliged to make every effort to enter into provisional arrangements of a practical nature.
- Such arrangements must not jeopardise or hamper the reaching of a final agreement.
- Typical forms: a moratorium on new licensing, a standstill, or a notification and consultation mechanism.
- Unilateral activity in disputed waters carries legal risk and can give rise to a claim.

**AFTER OR ALONGSIDE DELIMITATION**
- Joint development zones allow a single geological structure to be exploited under one regime across the boundary.
- Unitisation agreements apportion production between the two states by reference to reserves.
- Institutions range from a simple treaty committee to a joint authority with regulatory powers.
- Examples include the Timor Sea arrangements, the Nigeria–São Tomé zone, and the Malaysia–Thailand joint authority.

**Narration**

Overlapping claims are managed in two quite different ways, and I want you to distinguish them clearly. Pending delimitation, the Convention imposes a specific obligation: the states concerned must make every effort, in a spirit of understanding and cooperation, to enter into provisional arrangements of a practical nature and, during this transitional period, not to jeopardise or hamper the reaching of a final agreement. That is a real obligation, and it has been held to constrain unilateral activity in disputed waters. The typical provisional arrangements are a moratorium on new licensing in the disputed area, a standstill on existing activity, or a notification and consultation mechanism. A state that drills unilaterally in genuinely disputed waters takes legal risk, and the case-law shows that such activity can give rise to a claim and, in some circumstances, to provisional measures. Separately, and frequently after delimitation, states manage shared resources through joint development. Where a single geological structure straddles a delimited boundary, the efficient solution is a joint development zone, within which the resource is exploited under a single regulatory and fiscal regime, with revenues shared according to an agreed ratio. The institutional forms vary considerably: some arrangements are governed by nothing more than a treaty committee, while others, such as the Malaysia–Thailand Joint Authority, create a body with genuine regulatory powers over the zone. The Timor Sea arrangements and the Nigeria–São Tomé joint development zone are further examples. And where the field straddles the line but the parties prefer not to create a zone, unitisation performs a similar function by apportioning production rather than by pooling the area. The choice between these instruments is a choice about how much sovereignty each state is willing to suspend, and that is a political decision expressed in legal form.

### 11. ITLOS Delimits a Boundary, and Invents a Solution for the Grey Area `case`

*Dispute concerning Delimitation of the Maritime Boundary between Bangladesh and Myanmar in the Bay of Bengal (Bangladesh v. Myanmar), ITLOS Case No. 16, Judgment of 14 March 2012*

This was the first maritime delimitation decided by the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea. Bangladesh's coast is markedly concave, and Bangladesh argued that a strict equidistance line would produce a manifestly inequitable result by cutting off its seaward access. The Tribunal applied the three-stage methodology. It drew a provisional equidistance line, found that the concavity of the Bangladeshi coast was a relevant circumstance requiring adjustment, and adjusted the line accordingly. It then addressed the area lying beyond two hundred nautical miles from Bangladesh but within two hundred from Myanmar, and held that the delimitation line it drew continued into that area, with the result that Myanmar's continental shelf rights were subject to Bangladesh's rights in the water column above.

> **Holding / significance.** Concavity is a relevant circumstance capable of requiring adjustment of a provisional equidistance line. And where shelf and water column entitlements diverge, the Convention does not leave the resulting grey area unresolved.

**Narration**

Our case is Bangladesh against Myanmar, decided by the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in 2012, and it is a landmark for two reasons. First, it was the first maritime delimitation decided by that Tribunal, which established that the Tribunal is a genuine forum for boundary disputes and not merely for prompt release and provisional measures. Second, and more interestingly for our purposes, it produced a genuinely creative solution to a problem the Convention does not expressly address. Let me set out the facts. Bangladesh's coast is markedly concave, and Bangladesh argued that a strict equidistance line in the Bay of Bengal would produce a manifestly inequitable result, effectively cutting off its seaward access. Myanmar argued for equidistance and for the stability of the method. The Tribunal applied the three-stage methodology. It drew a provisional equidistance line. It then held that the concavity of the Bangladeshi coast was a relevant circumstance of a kind that could produce a cut-off effect, and that the line required adjustment to avoid it. It adjusted the line accordingly, and finally applied the disproportionality test. That is the delimitation reasoning, and it is a textbook application of the method. The more interesting part concerned what is called the grey area. Because Bangladesh's entitlement beyond two hundred miles depended on its extended continental shelf claim, while Myanmar's two-hundred-mile entitlement was automatic, there existed an area lying beyond two hundred miles from Bangladesh but within two hundred miles from Myanmar. The Tribunal held that its delimitation line continued into that area, with the consequence that Myanmar held the continental shelf rights while Bangladesh held rights in the superjacent water column. That is a split of the seabed from the water column, and it is a striking illustration of the point I made at the beginning of this lecture: the two are legally distinct, and they can be allocated differently. For the energy lawyer the lesson is concrete. Where extended shelf claims are unsettled, the status of the seabed is uncertain, and no sane investor will commit capital to it until the limits are final.

### 12. The Area and Deep Seabed Mining `section`

**Narration**

Finally, let me take you beyond national jurisdiction altogether.

### 13. The Area: Common Heritage, and a Regulatory Mandate Under Strain `bullets`

- The Area and its resources are the common heritage of mankind; no state or person may claim sovereignty or property over them.
- The International Seabed Authority organises, regulates and controls activities in the Area on behalf of mankind as a whole.
- Exploration contracts for polymetallic nodules, crusts and sulphides have been granted, and the exploitation regulations have been under prolonged negotiation.
- The tension is between the mandate to develop the resources and the obligation to protect the marine environment.

*On-screen note:* The deepest legal question about the Area is not ownership. It is whether the mandate to exploit can be reconciled with the duty to protect.

**Narration**

Beyond national jurisdiction lies the Area, and it is governed by a regime unlike any other in the law of the sea. The Area and its resources are declared the common heritage of mankind. No state may claim or exercise sovereignty or sovereign rights over any part of it, no state or natural or juridical person may appropriate any part of it, and activities in the Area are to be carried out for the benefit of mankind as a whole, with particular consideration for the interests and needs of developing states. The International Seabed Authority, established by the Convention, is the organisation through which states parties organise, regulate and control activities in the Area, and it has granted a substantial number of exploration contracts for polymetallic nodules, for polymetallic sulphides and for cobalt-rich crusts. The exploitation regulations, the so-called Mining Code, have been under negotiation for a considerable period, and the process was given a sharp procedural jolt when a sponsoring state invoked a provision requiring the Authority to complete the adoption of the rules within two years of a request. That triggered a deadline, and the resulting debate has been among the most consequential in the contemporary law of the sea. The tension at the heart of it is this. The Authority has a mandate to facilitate the development of the resources of the Area, and at the same time an obligation under the Convention to ensure effective protection of the marine environment from harmful effects arising from activities in the Area. Those two instructions pull in opposite directions, and the scientific uncertainty about the consequences of deep seabed mining for poorly understood ecosystems makes the reconciliation genuinely difficult. A number of states have called for a moratorium or a precautionary pause, while others argue that the nodules are needed for the energy transition and that delaying is itself a decision with costs. Whatever the outcome, and it may well be settled by the time you are in practice, the deep seabed is the clearest contemporary test of whether the concept of the common heritage of mankind has operational content. Let me add one caution, which is that the institution is being asked to do something unusually difficult. It must complete a regulatory code for an industry that has never operated commercially at scale, in an environment that is very poorly understood, while its own membership is divided, and while it simultaneously holds a mandate to protect that environment and to facilitate the exploitation of it. There are few examples in international law of an organisation asked to regulate a novel industry prospectively rather than retrospectively, and the Authority's handling of the task will be studied for that reason whatever the outcome. For the practitioner, the practical advice is simple. Watch the exploitation regulations, because they will become the template for environmental and liability standards in deep-water extractive activity generally, and because the sponsoring state obligations they contain are likely to be litigated within the decade.

### 14. Protecting the Marine Environment from Offshore Operations `bullets`

- States have a general obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment, and sovereign rights over resources must be exercised with due regard to it.
- Article 206 requires an environmental impact assessment where there are reasonable grounds for believing that planned activities may cause substantial pollution or significant and harmful changes.
- Regional seas conventions, national environmental regulation and offshore-specific safety regimes layer on top of the Convention.
- The Advisory Opinion of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in 2024 addressed the application of these duties to greenhouse gas emissions.

*On-screen note:* Sovereign rights over offshore resources are not unqualified. They are exercisable with due regard to the marine environment.

**Narration**

I want to add an element that is sometimes treated as separable from resource law and is not, which is the environmental duty. The Convention imposes on all states a general obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment, and it qualifies the exercise of resource rights accordingly: the sovereign rights of the coastal state in the exclusive economic zone must be exercised with due regard to the rights and duties of other states, and activities in the Area must be carried out with necessary measures for the effective protection of the marine environment. The operative provision for our purposes is Article 206, which requires that where states have reasonable grounds for believing that planned activities under their jurisdiction or control may cause substantial pollution of or significant and harmful changes to the marine environment, they shall, as far as practicable, assess the potential effects of such activities and communicate reports of the results. That is the treaty basis for environmental impact assessment in the offshore, and it has been held to be a requirement of customary international law applying to activities that may affect the marine environment of another state or of areas beyond national jurisdiction. On top of the Convention sit regional seas conventions, national environmental regulation, and offshore-specific safety and integrity regimes, which in most producing states now impose detailed requirements on well integrity, on discharges, on decommissioning and on emergency response. And in 2024 the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, in an advisory opinion of considerable significance, addressed the application of these duties to greenhouse gas emissions, treating anthropogenic emissions as pollution of the marine environment for the purposes of the Convention. The practical consequence for the offshore energy lawyer is straightforward and should be stated plainly. Sovereign rights over offshore resources are real, but they are not unqualified. They are exercisable with due regard to the marine environment, and that qualification is increasingly enforceable.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- The Convention creates zones, and the resource right differs in each: sovereignty, sovereign rights, and the common heritage.
- Shelf rights exist ipso jure and ab initio as an extension of the land territory of the coastal state.
- Delimitation follows a three-stage method: equidistance, relevant circumstances, and a disproportionality test.
- Overlaps are managed by provisional arrangements pending delimitation, and by joint development or unitisation thereafter.
- The Area is the common heritage of mankind, and the Authority must reconcile a development mandate with a protection duty.

> **The one thing to remember.** Before you ask who may license an offshore block, ask in whose zone it lies. Everything else in offshore energy is downstream of that question.

**Narration**

Let me close. The Convention creates a zonal framework in which the resource right differs at every step: sovereignty in the territorial sea, sovereign rights in the exclusive economic zone and on the continental shelf, and the common heritage of mankind in the Area. Rights over the shelf exist ipso jure and ab initio, as an extension of the coastal state's land territory, which is why the shelf rather than the water column is the dominant concept in offshore energy. Delimitation of overlapping entitlements now follows a structured three-stage method, with equity as the objective rather than the method. Overlaps are managed before delimitation through provisional arrangements, and afterwards through joint development zones or unitisation. And beyond national jurisdiction, the Area is held as the common heritage of mankind, administered by an Authority required to reconcile a mandate to develop with a duty to protect, which is the hardest institutional problem in the contemporary law of the sea. In Bangladesh against Myanmar we saw both the methodology applied and a creative solution to a gap the Convention left open. In the next lecture we return to land and examine mining and critical minerals in international law. And let me leave you with this thought: the ocean is the last large space on the planet whose resources are governed by a treaty that allocates them to all humanity rather than to whoever can reach them first, and the tension between that principle and the commercial reality of extraction is only beginning to be tested.
