### 01. Investment Arbitration and the ECT `bumper`

### 02. Investment Arbitration and the Energy Charter Treaty `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Sixteen, and to the fourth module of this series, in which we examine how disputes in this field are actually resolved. I want to begin with a fact that will surprise you if you have not studied the field. The energy sector generates more investor-state arbitration than any other sector of the world economy, by a considerable margin, and it has done so consistently since the system expanded in the late nineteen nineties. There are reasons for that, and they are structural rather than accidental. Energy projects are the largest capital commitments made by private enterprise. They are immovable, so the investor cannot leave. They are politically salient, so governments are tempted to intervene. And their returns depend on a regulatory regime that governments have both the power and, when prices move, the incentive to change. Put those four things together and you have a recipe for disputes. Let me take you through the standards, then the treaty that has produced most of them, then the retreat from it.

### 04. The Standards `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the substantive protections, since everything else in this field is procedure attached to them.

### 05. What an Investment Treaty Actually Promises `bullets`

- Fair and equitable treatment: protection of legitimate expectations, due process, non-arbitrariness, and freedom from coercion.
- Expropriation: no nationalisation or equivalent measure unless for a public purpose, non-discriminatory, under due process, and against compensation.
- Full protection and security: a standard of due diligence in the physical and legal protection of the investment.
- Umbrella clause: an undertaking to observe any obligation entered into with the investor, which may elevate a contractual breach to a treaty breach.

*On-screen note:* Fair and equitable treatment is the most pleaded standard, the most awarded on, and the least precisely defined.

**Narration**

Let me set out the standards, and let me be precise about what each one requires, because these phrases are used loosely even in professional writing. The most frequently pleaded is fair and equitable treatment, and its content has been elaborated by tribunals into a set of recurring elements. There is the protection of the investor's legitimate expectations, which is the element that does most of the work in regulatory change cases and which was central to the Spanish renewables claims we examined. There is the requirement of due process and the prohibition of a denial of justice. There is the prohibition of arbitrary, discriminatory or unreasonable conduct. And there is freedom from coercion and harassment. The standard is deliberately open-textured, which is precisely why it is both the most useful and the most criticised provision in the system. The second standard is protection against expropriation. A taking must be for a public purpose, must be non-discriminatory, must be carried out under due process of law, and must be accompanied by the prompt, adequate and effective compensation. The difficult cases are those of indirect expropriation, where title has not been taken but the value of the investment has been destroyed, and of creeping expropriation, where a series of measures produces that effect over time. The principal test is one of effect: whether the measures deprived the investor of the substantial value or the effective control of the investment. The third standard is full protection and security, which is a standard of due diligence requiring the state to exercise reasonable care in protecting the investment, and which has been held to extend to legal as well as physical protection. And the fourth is the umbrella clause, which provides that each party shall observe any obligation it has entered into with an investment of an investor of the other party. Its effect, where a tribunal accepts it, is to elevate a breach of a contract with the state into a breach of the treaty, which is a very substantial expansion of jurisdiction and has been the subject of divided authority.

### 06. The Machinery: ICSID, Ad Hoc, and Enforcement `two-col`

**INSTITUTIONS**
- The ICSID Convention establishes a self-contained regime: a registry, panels, awards subject only to internal annulment.
- Annulment under the Convention is not an appeal: grounds are limited to excess of powers, corruption, serious procedural departure and failure to state reasons.
- Ad hoc arbitration, frequently under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, has no administering institution and its awards are subject to the courts of the seat.
- Arbitral awards may be registered and enforced under the ICSID Convention, and non-ICSID awards under the New York Convention.

**ENFORCEMENT**
- The ICSID Convention requires each member state to recognise an award as binding and enforce the pecuniary obligations as if a final judgment of its own courts.
- The New York Convention requires recognition of foreign arbitral awards, subject to narrow and exhaustively listed grounds for refusal.
- Sovereign immunity is the principal obstacle in practice, distinguishing assets used for commercial purposes from assets used for sovereign purposes.
- The absence of a central enforcement mechanism means an award is only worth what the claimant can reach.

**Narration**

Let me now describe the machinery, because the distinction between the two systems is not procedural pedantry but determines what happens to an award afterwards. The Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States, which established the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes, creates a self-contained regime. There is a registry, there are panels of arbitrators and conciliators, and there is an internal mechanism for annulment. The critical feature is that annulment is not an appeal. The grounds are limited and they are: that the tribunal was not properly constituted, that it manifestly exceeded its powers, that there was corruption on the part of a member, that there was a serious departure from a fundamental rule of procedure, and that the award failed to state the reasons on which it is based. An error of law, or an error of fact, is not a ground. By contrast, arbitration conducted ad hoc, usually under the Arbitration Rules of the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law, has no administering institution, and its awards are subject to the supervisory jurisdiction of the courts of the seat, which in most jurisdictions may set an award aside on grounds that are somewhat wider. Now, on enforcement, there are two distinct pathways. Awards made under the ICSID Convention must be recognised by every contracting state as binding, and the pecuniary obligations enforced as if they were a final judgment of that state's own courts, with no review on the merits and no public policy exception of the kind found elsewhere. Other arbitral awards are enforced under the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, which requires recognition subject to a closed list of grounds for refusal, including incapacity, invalid agreement, want of proper notice, excess of jurisdiction, irregular composition of the tribunal, and non-arbitral awards subject to annulment at the seat. The principal obstacle in practice is sovereign immunity, since a successful claimant must find assets that are used for commercial rather than sovereign purposes, and that enquiry has produced some of the most protracted and expensive satellite litigation in the field.

### 07. The Energy Charter Treaty `section`

**Narration**

Let me turn to the treaty that has produced more energy arbitration than any other instrument.

### 08. The ECT: An Unusually Ambitious Sectoral Treaty `bullets`

- It covers investment, trade in energy materials and products, transit, and energy efficiency and environmental aspects.
- Part III contains the investment protections, including fair and equitable treatment and protection against expropriation.
- It applies to the making of investments throughout their life, and contains no general carve-out for environmental or climate measures.
- It has more than fifty signatories and contracting parties, and provisional application has produced difficult jurisdictional questions.

*On-screen note:* The ECT is the only multilateral treaty whose principal function is investment protection in a single economic sector.

**Narration**

Let me describe the treaty, because it is unlike anything else in the field. The Energy Charter Treaty grew out of a political initiative of the early nineteen nineties, launched with the object of integrating the energy sectors of the former Soviet Union and Eastern Europe into the wider European and global economy. It was signed in 1994 and entered into force in 1998. Its scope is unusually broad for a sectoral instrument. It addresses four subject areas: the protection and promotion of foreign investment in the energy sector; trade in energy materials and products on non-discriminatory terms; freedom of energy transit through pipelines, grids and other means; and the promotion of energy efficiency together with the minimisation of environmental impact. Two features deserve particular attention. The first is that Part III, which contains the investment protections, applies to investments made by investors of one contracting party in the area of another, and applies throughout the life of the investment. There is no general exception clause permitting measures necessary to protect the environment or to address climate change, and the absence of such a clause has become the central objection to the treaty. The second feature is provisional application. Article 45 provides that each signatory agrees to apply the treaty provisionally pending its entry into force for that signatory, to the extent that such provisional application is not inconsistent with its constitution, laws or regulations. That provision has generated a substantial and contested body of jurisdictional decisions, because several significant states signed but never ratified, and tribunals have had to determine whether provisional application extends to the dispute settlement provisions. The treaty's membership extends across Europe, Central Asia and Japan, and it remains the only multilateral instrument whose principal function is the protection of investment in a single economic sector.

### 09. Contraction: The Withdrawal of Contracting Parties `timeline`

- **2016** — Italy withdraws from the treaty, the first contracting party to do so.
- **2018** — The Court of Justice of the European Union holds in Achmea that investor-state arbitration under an intra-EU bilateral investment treaty is incompatible with EU law.
- **2021** — The Court of Justice extends that reasoning in Komstroy to arbitration under the Energy Charter Treaty in an intra-EU context.
- **2022-2024** — A series of European states announce withdrawal, including some of the treaty's original supporters, following the failure of the modernisation process to deliver an agreement.

**Narration**

Now let me take you through the contraction, because the trajectory of this treaty over the last decade is one of the most significant developments in the field and it is not always well understood. In 2016, Italy withdrew, becoming the first contracting party to leave, and it did so principally on fiscal grounds. In 2018, the Court of Justice of the European Union gave judgment in Achmea, holding that the arbitration clause in a bilateral investment treaty between two member states is incompatible with the autonomy of the European Union legal order, because it removes disputes that may concern the interpretation and application of Union law from the judicial system established by the treaties. In 2021, in Komstroy, that reasoning was extended to the Energy Charter Treaty, so that an investor of one member state may not invoke the treaty's arbitration provisions against another member state. And then, between 2022 and 2024, a substantial number of European states announced their withdrawal, including several that had been among the treaty's strongest supporters, following a modernisation process that had been under way since 2018 and that failed to deliver an agreed text capable of adoption. The European Union itself, and its member states, proposed to withdraw on a coordinated basis. Let me be precise about what withdrawal does and does not achieve. The treaty contains a survival clause, under which its investment protections continue to apply to investments made before the withdrawal takes effect for a period of twenty years. That means the disputes arising from this treaty will continue for a generation after the last contracting party has left it. And it means that the practical consequence of withdrawal is to prevent new investments from acquiring protection, rather than to terminate the liability that has already accrued.

### 10. Damages: What an Expropriated Investment Is Worth `bullets`

- The standard for lawful expropriation is compensation for the value of the investment immediately before the taking became known.
- The standard for unlawful expropriation is more generous, and may permit a higher valuation date reflecting subsequent increase in value.
- The principal methods are the discounted cash flow method, comparable transactions, and the net book or replacement value.
- Discounting is where the case is won: the discount rate and the treatment of political risk move the number more than the method does.

*On-screen note:* Two experts applying the same method to the same facts can differ by a factor of three. Almost all of the difference is in the assumptions.

**Narration**

Let me take a detour into quantum, because students consistently neglect it and because it is where the outcome is decided once liability has been established. The starting point is that the standard differs according to whether the expropriation was lawful. Where a taking is lawful, because it satisfies the requirements of public purpose, non-discrimination and due process, the compensation due is the value of the investment at a date immediately before the taking, or before the impending taking became public knowledge. Where a taking is unlawful, the applicable standard is more generous to the claimant, and tribunals have held that the valuation date may be later, reflecting the fact that an unlawful act should not deprive the claimant of the increase in value that occurred afterwards. That distinction is why claimants nearly always plead unlawfulness, and why the characterisation finding is worth more than it appears. Now, the methods. There is the discounted cash flow method, which forecasts the net cash the asset would have generated and discounts it to a present value, and which is the dominant method for income-producing energy assets. There are comparable transactions, which look at what comparable assets have actually sold for, and which are useful only where there is genuine comparability, which in this sector there frequently is not. And there is the net book or replacement value, which is a floor rather than a measure and is used where there is no reliable income stream. But the method matters less than the assumptions, and this is the point I want you to take. The discount rate, the forecast of commodity prices, the probability of success in exploration, the treatment of political risk, the treatment of tax, and the terminal value assumption each move the result substantially, and they are all contestable. Two reputable experts applying the same method to the same facts can readily produce valuations differing by a factor of three. That is why valuation hearings are long, why tribunals frequently split the difference, and why a party that wins on liability can still lose the case.

### 11. Creeping Expropriation Disguised as Taxation `case`

*Yukos Universal Limited (Isle of Man) v. The Russian Federation, PCA Case No. AA 227; Final Awards of 18 July 2014*

The claimants were majority shareholders in Yukos Oil Company, once the largest oil producer in Russia. Following the arrest of its principal shareholder in 2003, Russian authorities raised very large tax assessments, froze assets, and enforced the sale of Yukos's principal production subsidiary at auction to an entity subsequently acquired by a state-controlled company. Yukos was declared bankrupt and liquidated. The claimants brought proceedings under the Energy Charter Treaty, relying on its provisional application. The tribunal held that Russia was bound by provisional application, including of the dispute settlement provisions, and that the Russian measures, though formally taxation, were not bona fide. It found that the object and effect of the measures was to destroy Yukos and to transfer its assets to state-controlled entities for reasons that included the political objectives of the state, and that this constituted unlawful expropriation. Damages were assessed at over fifty thousand million United States dollars, the largest award in the history of arbitration.

> **Holding / significance.** Measures formally within a state's taxing power may constitute expropriation where their object and effect is the destruction of the enterprise and the transfer of its assets to the state.

**Narration**

Our case is Yukos against the Russian Federation, and it is worth studying for three reasons: the size of the award, the characterisation of the measures, and what happened to the award afterwards. The claimants were majority shareholders in Yukos Oil Company, which in the early two thousands was the largest oil producer in Russia. The sequence of events began with the arrest of its principal shareholder in 2003 on criminal charges. It continued with very large tax assessments for earlier years, the freezing of assets, and the enforcement of those assessments through the seizure and auction, in December 2004, of Yukos's principal production subsidiary, which was acquired by an entity that was itself acquired almost immediately by a state-controlled oil company. Yukos was declared bankrupt and liquidated. The claimants brought proceedings under the Energy Charter Treaty, and the first hurdle was jurisdiction, since Russia had signed the treaty but never ratified it. The tribunal held that Russia was bound by provisional application under Article 45, and that provisional application extended to the arbitration provisions, since nothing in Russian law made that inconsistent. On the merits, the tribunal's central finding was a characterisation. It accepted that the levying of tax is an exercise of sovereign power and that a mere error in the assessment of tax would not engage the treaty. But it found that the measures were not bona fide taxation. Their object and effect, on the evidence, was to destroy Yukos and to transfer its assets to state-controlled entities, and the reasons included the political objectives of the state in respect of its principal shareholder. That, the tribunal held, was expropriation, and it was not accompanied by compensation. Damages were assessed at over fifty thousand million dollars. Now, what happened afterwards is the part that students usually omit. The Russian Federation applied to the courts of the seat, and in 2016 the District Court of The Hague set the awards aside on jurisdictional grounds, holding that provisional application did not extend to the arbitration provisions. In 2020 the Court of Appeal of The Hague reversed that judgment and reinstated the awards, and in 2021 the Supreme Court of the Netherlands upheld the Court of Appeal's decision on the limited grounds available to it. Enforcement proceedings have since continued in several jurisdictions, and they have been complicated at every turn by claims of state immunity. The lesson is blunt: an award is worth what you can reach, and no more.

### 12. The Right to Regulate and the Police Powers Doctrine `bullets`

- A state does not promise that its regulation will never change; it promises that change will not be arbitrary or confiscatory.
- The police powers doctrine holds that non-discriminatory measures adopted in the public interest do not constitute expropriation.
- Its scope is contested: does it exclude the taking from compensation altogether, or only from unlawfulness?
- Newer treaties express the doctrine textually, which removes the argument in future cases but not in pending ones.

*On-screen note:* The distinction is between regulation that reduces the value of an investment and regulation that transfers its value to the state.

**Narration**

Let me address the question that lies behind every case in this field, which is how far a state may go before it owes compensation. The starting proposition is the one I want you to internalise: an investor is not promised that the regulatory framework will never change. No state could make such a promise, and no tribunal has held that fair and equitable treatment requires it. What the investor is promised is that change will not be arbitrary, discriminatory, disproportionate or confiscatory, and that it will not be used as an instrument to take the value of the investment. The doctrine through which that limit is expressed is the police powers doctrine, and it derives from general international law. It holds that a state which adopts a non-discriminatory measure in the exercise of its bona fide powers, for a legitimate public purpose such as the protection of public health, safety, morals or the environment, does not thereby expropriate, and owes no compensation for the resulting loss of value. Now, the scope of the doctrine is contested, and there are two readings of it. On the narrower reading, the doctrine goes to lawfulness: a measure within the police powers is not an unlawful expropriation, but if it destroys the value of the investment entirely it may still be a lawful expropriation requiring compensation. On the broader reading, the doctrine goes to the existence of a taking at all: a measure properly within the police powers is simply not an expropriation, and no compensation is due regardless of its effect. Tribunals have divided on this, and the distinction is decisive in cases where regulation has destroyed the whole value of an asset. The useful practical test, which recurs in the reasoning, is to ask whether the measure merely reduced the value of the investment or transferred that value to the state. A ban on a product reduces value. The transfer of a licence to a state entity transfers it. And there is one further development you must know: the newer generation of treaties states this expressly, providing that non-discriminatory measures designed and applied to achieve legitimate public welfare objectives do not constitute indirect expropriation except in rare circumstances. That resolves the argument for future cases, and it leaves the pending ones, which were brought under older instruments, to be resolved by tribunals on the contested reading.

### 13. Reform `section`

**Narration**

Let me close with the reform of the system, which is now the most active area of development in the field.

### 14. The Reform of Investor-State Dispute Settlement `bullets`

- Concerns identified by states: consistency, correctness, cost, duration, arbitrator independence, and the chilling effect on regulation.
- Options under consideration range from incremental reform of arbitration to a standing multilateral investment court.
- Work proceeds in a working group of the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law, and in the procedural rules of the Centre.
- Newer treaties specify the content of the standards, preserve the right to regulate expressly, and restrict or omit arbitration altogether.

*On-screen note:* The direction of travel is clear even where the destination is not: more precision in the standards, more control over the adjudicators.

**Narration**

Let me close with reform, because the system is under sustained pressure and it is being reshaped rapidly. The concerns articulated by states are consistent and they are serious. There is the problem of consistency, since differently constituted ad hoc tribunals have reached materially different conclusions on similar facts and on similarly worded provisions. There is the problem of correctness, since there is no appellate mechanism to correct an award. There is the problem of cost and duration, since these proceedings are extremely expensive and routinely take years. There is the problem of the adjudicators, since they are appointed by the parties, they sit in shifting combinations, they are paid by the hour, and some of them act as counsel in other cases, which gives rise to questions of independence and impartiality. And there is the problem of regulatory chill, which is the allegation that the prospect of very large claims deters governments from adopting legitimate public welfare regulation. The options under consideration range from the incremental to the structural. Incremental reform includes a code of conduct for adjudicators, requirements of transparency and third-party participation, mechanisms for the early dismissal of unmeritorious claims, and a standing advisory centre to assist states, particularly developing states, with the cost of defence. Structural reform includes an appellate mechanism and, at the furthest end, a permanent standing investment court with tenured judges appointed by states rather than by parties, which has been proposed and which has been included in some recent treaties between the European Union and its partners. Work on all of this proceeds principally in a working group of the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law, which has produced codes of conduct and draft provisions on procedural and cross-cutting reform, while the Centre has amended its own arbitration rules to require disclosure of third-party funding, to provide for early dismissal, and to permit greater transparency. And meanwhile, at the treaty level, the most consequential change is being made quietly in the drafting room: newer instruments define fair and equitable treatment by a closed list, state expressly that non-discriminatory measures designed to achieve legitimate public welfare objectives do not constitute expropriation, and preserve the right to regulate in unambiguous terms.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Energy generates more investment arbitration than any other sector, for four structural reasons: scale, immovability, salience and regulatory dependence.
- The core standards are fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation, full protection and security, and the umbrella clause.
- The ECT is the only multilateral treaty whose principal function is investment protection in a single sector, and it has contracted sharply.
- Withdrawal does not extinguish liability: the survival clause extends protection for twenty years after a withdrawal takes effect.
- Reform is proceeding on two tracks: procedural changes to arbitration, and substantive redrafting of the standards themselves.

> **The one thing to remember.** The system is being reformed rather than abolished, and the most consequential reform is occurring in the drafting of new treaties rather than in the institutions.

**Narration**

Let me close. Energy generates more investment arbitration than any other sector, for four structural reasons: the scale of the capital committed, the immovability of the investment, the political salience of the industry, and the dependence of returns on a regulatory regime the state can change. The core standards are fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation, full protection and security, and the umbrella clause, and the first of these is the most pleaded and the least defined. The Energy Charter Treaty is the only multilateral instrument whose principal function is the protection of investment in a single economic sector, and it has contracted sharply in recent years following the European Court's decisions and the failure of its modernisation. Withdrawal does not extinguish liability, because the survival clause continues protection for twenty years after it takes effect. And reform is proceeding on two tracks simultaneously: procedural changes to arbitration, and a substantive redrafting of the standards themselves in new treaties, which is where I would direct your attention. In the next lecture we examine the other side of the coin, and turn to corruption, illicit financial flows and the governance of resource revenues.
