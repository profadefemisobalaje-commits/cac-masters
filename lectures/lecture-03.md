## Lecture Script

### 01. Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources `bumper`

### 02. Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Three. We now turn to the doctrine at the very centre of this field, and I want to begin by warning you against a misunderstanding that costs students years. Permanent sovereignty over natural resources is often presented as a slogan: the state owns its oil, its minerals, its forests, and foreigners must ask permission. That is not wrong, but it is thin to the point of being misleading. The doctrine is in fact a densely argued settlement about four separate questions. Who holds the right? What is its content? What duties qualify it? And what happens when the right is exercised in a way that harms the people on whose behalf it is supposedly held? These were contested bitterly in the United Nations from the nineteen fifties onward, and the contest has not ended. What follows is therefore not a settled rule that you can memorise. It is a live argument, and by the end of this lecture you should be able to take a position in it and defend it.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. First, to trace the emergence of permanent sovereignty from the decolonisation era to the present day, and to understand why the doctrine took the particular shape it did. Second, to explain the content of the right and, crucially, the duties that qualify it, because the duties are where the modern litigation is. Third, to distinguish the competing standards of compensation for nationalisation, and to give an honest assessment of which one now prevails. Fourth, to analyse the relationship between sovereignty over resources and the right of peoples to self-determination, and to explain why the word peoples, rather than states, appears in the foundational text. Fifth, to evaluate the claim, increasingly made, that sovereignty over resources is held in trust for present and future generations. And sixth, to apply all of this to a contemporary dispute over fiscal renegotiation, which is the form in which the doctrine most often reaches a lawyer's desk today.

### 04. The Making of a Doctrine `section`

**Narration**

Let us begin with history, because this doctrine is unintelligible without it.

### 05. A Doctrine Built in the General Assembly `timeline`

- **1952** — General Assembly resolution affirms the right of peoples freely to use and exploit their natural wealth and resources.
- **1962** — Resolution 1803 (XVII): permanent sovereignty, exercised in the interest of national development and the well-being of the people.
- **1966** — The two International Covenants embed the right of all peoples to dispose freely of their natural wealth and resources.
- **1974** — The Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States asserts the fullest permanent sovereignty and the right to nationalise.
- **1974** — Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order is adopted in the same session.
- **1990s** — Bilateral investment treaties proliferate; the doctrine is argued against treaty standards rather than custom alone.

**Narration**

The chronology matters, so let me walk you through it. In 1952 the General Assembly, then a body of sixty member states in which the decolonising world was still a minority, adopted a resolution affirming the right of peoples freely to use and exploit their natural wealth and resources. It was cautious, and it linked that right to the flow of capital on mutually agreeable terms. A decade later, in 1962, Resolution 1803 was adopted by a very different Assembly, and its language is the language we still use: permanent sovereignty, to be exercised in the interest of national development and of the well-being of the people of the state concerned. In 1966 the two International Covenants, one on civil and political rights and one on economic, social and cultural rights, each included a common article providing that all peoples may, for their own ends, freely dispose of their natural wealth and resources. That move is significant: it placed the resource question inside the human rights corpus, where it has remained. Then came 1974, the high-water mark, with the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States and the Declaration on a New International Economic Order, both asserting the fullest permanent sovereignty over natural resources, including the right to nationalise, expropriate or transfer ownership, and to regulate foreign investment. And then, from the nineteen nineties, the ground shifted again. The bilateral investment treaty network expanded from a few hundred instruments to well over two thousand, and the doctrine began to be argued not against customary international law alone but against express treaty standards: fair and equitable treatment, indirect expropriation, full protection and security. What I want you to notice is the arc. The doctrine was built in a political forum, hardened into human rights language, radicalised in the nineteen seventies, and then, in the last three decades, increasingly litigated in a forum it was never designed for.

### 06. What Permanent Sovereignty Actually Entitles a State to Do `bullets`

- To possess, use and dispose of the natural resources within its territory and its maritime zones.
- To regulate and control the activities of foreign investors, including the terms of entry and the fiscal regime.
- To nationalise, expropriate or require the transfer of property, subject to the conditions of international law.
- To determine the pace and pattern of exploitation, including conservation and the protection of the environment.
- To recover, and to renegotiate, a share of resource rents for the national community.

*On-screen note:* The powers are broad. The controversy has always been about their limits.

**Narration**

Now, what does the right actually entitle a state to do? Five things, and I want you to hear all five rather than only the third, because students tend to remember the nationalisations and forget the rest. First, to possess, use and dispose of the natural resources found within its territory and within the maritime zones over which it holds sovereign rights. Second, to regulate and control the activities of foreign investors within its jurisdiction, including the terms on which they enter and the fiscal regime that applies to them. Third, to nationalise, expropriate, or require the transfer of property, subject to the conditions that international law imposes. Fourth, to determine the pace and pattern of exploitation, which includes the power to conserve, to leave a deposit in the ground, and to protect the environment against the consequences of extraction. And fifth, to recover and renegotiate a share of the resource rent for the national community, which is the power most frequently exercised today and the one about which the law is least clear. Notice that the powers are genuinely broad. Nobody seriously disputes them in the abstract. What has always been contested, from 1962 to the present, is where their limits lie: what conditions attach to nationalisation, how much deference a state owes an existing investor, and whether the exercise of the right can ever be reviewed for its reasonableness. Those are the questions on which cases turn, and they are the questions that occupy the remainder of this lecture.

### 07. The exploration, development and disposition of such resources, as well as the i `quote`

> The exploration, development and disposition of such resources, as well as the import of the foreign capital required for these purposes, should be in conformity with the rules and conditions which the peoples and nations freely consider to be necessary or desirable with regard to the authorization, restriction or prohibition of such activities.
>
> — G.A. Res. 1803 (XVII), 14 December 1962, operative paragraph 2

**Narration**

Read the second operative paragraph of Resolution 1803 with me, because it is the paragraph that lawyers forget and the paragraph that decides cases. It provides that the exploration, development and disposition of natural resources, together with the import of the foreign capital required for those purposes, should be in conformity with the rules and conditions which peoples and nations freely consider to be necessary or desirable with regard to the authorisation, restriction or prohibition of such activities. The phrase to notice is freely consider. It is the textual foothold for the modern right to regulate, and it is the sentence that states now invoke when they defend a change of fiscal regime against an investment claim. Notice what it does. It does not merely permit regulation. It locates the judgement about the necessity or desirability of regulation in the people and the nation themselves, not in an external standard of reasonableness applied after the fact. Now, capital-exporting states objected to this paragraph in 1962 and have objected to it ever since, and the awards have never given it the weight its text might suggest. But it is not an empty sentence. It is the doctrinal ancestor of the police powers argument, of the margin of appreciation, and of the modern insistence that a state's principled decision about how its own resources are developed deserves deference.

### 08. Four Waves, and a Fifth That Is Not Called Nationalisation `bullets`

- 1930s: Mexico in 1938 and Bolivia in 1937 expropriate foreign oil assets, and the doctrine is born in practice before it is named.
- 1950s and 1960s: Iran in 1951, Iraq, and the restructuring of concession terms across the Middle East.
- 1970s: the high-water mark — Libya, Algeria, Venezuela and others nationalise, and national oil companies are created to operate what the state has taken.
- 2000s: resource nationalism returns, but increasingly through fiscal instruments rather than outright seizure.
- Present: the frontier has moved to critical minerals, export bans, local content and renegotiation.

*On-screen note:* Direct taking has largely been replaced by measures that achieve similar effects through taxation and regulation.

**Narration**

The doctrine is best understood through the practice that produced it, and that practice comes in waves. In the nineteen thirties, Bolivia in 1937 and then Mexico in 1938 expropriated foreign oil assets, and the argument about compensation that followed produced the classical formulation I described a moment ago; the doctrine therefore existed in practice before it was named in a resolution. In the nineteen fifties and sixties came Iran's nationalisation of 1951, the later restructuring of concession terms across the Middle East, and the growing insistence by producing states on participation in the equity of the operating company rather than mere royalty. In the nineteen seventies came the high-water mark: Libya, Algeria, Venezuela and others nationalised, and national oil companies were created expressly to operate what the state had taken, which created the institutional landscape we still inhabit. Then, after a long interlude in which policy moved toward liberalisation and privatisation, resource nationalism returned in the two thousands, but in a different form. States rarely seized assets outright. Instead they raised royalties and taxes, imposed local content requirements, required renegotiation of existing agreements, and in several cases required the conversion of concessions into service contracts. And today the frontier has moved again, toward critical minerals, export restrictions, beneficiation requirements and the renegotiation of fiscal terms in the battery and magnet supply chains. I want you to notice what this history does to the law. Direct expropriation, the taking that the compensation debate was designed for, has become rare. What has replaced it is the creeping measure: a tax change, a royalty increase, a regulation that renders a project uneconomic. That shift has moved the entire litigation burden from the law on expropriation, which is comparatively settled, to the law on indirect expropriation and fair and equitable treatment, which is not. Anyone who tells you the compensation debate is over is looking at the wrong cases.

### 09. The Contest Today Is Over Rent, Not Ownership `bullets`

- The instruments are fiscal: royalties, resource taxes, production sharing, progressive profit taxes and state participation.
- Stability is negotiated: fiscal stability clauses, economic equilibrium clauses, and express renegotiation clauses.
- The state's answer is the right to regulate, the police powers doctrine, and changed circumstances.
- The legal question is therefore not 'was there a taking?' but 'was this change one the investor had a right to expect would not occur?'

*On-screen note:* Read the stability clause last, and read it against the state's constitutional powers.

**Narration**

This brings me to the form in which the doctrine now most often reaches a lawyer's desk, which is not a seizure but a fiscal renegotiation. The modern contest is over rent rather than ownership, and the instruments of that contest are fiscal. There is the royalty, typically calculated on production or on value. There is the resource rent or additional profits tax, which is designed to capture super-normal returns. There is production sharing, under which the state takes title to production and the contractor recovers its costs before splitting the remainder. There is progressive profit taxation, which raises the state's share as the project becomes more profitable. There is state participation, through a national company holding a carried or a paying interest. And there are signature, discovery and production bonuses, which are payable regardless of profitability and which shift risk onto the investor at the front end of the project. Stability in that arrangement is negotiated rather than assumed. There is the fiscal stability clause, which freezes the regime. There is the economic equilibrium clause, which does not freeze the law but requires compensation sufficient to restore the investor's economic position if the law changes. And there is the express renegotiation clause, which concedes the possibility of change and prescribes a procedure for adjusting to it. Against all of these, the state's answer is the right to regulate: the police powers doctrine, the reserved powers of the constitution, and in some systems a doctrine of changed circumstances. The legal question that reaches a tribunal is therefore almost never whether there has been a taking in the classical sense. It is whether the change was one the investor had a legitimate expectation would not occur, and whether the investor's expectation was reasonable in the light of the state's constitutional order and its developmental needs. That is a question about the bargain rather than about the taking, and it is why the modern lawyer must read contracts, constitutions and awards together.

### 10. Nationalisation and Compensation `section`

**Narration**

Let us now turn to the hardest question the doctrine generates: what happens when a state takes foreign property?

### 11. Two Standards of Compensation, and an Unresolved Argument `two-col`

**CAPITAL-EXPORTING POSITION**
- The classical standard: prompt, adequate and effective compensation, associated with Secretary of State Cordell Hull.
- Treats an established customary rule as requiring full reparation for a lawful taking.
- Valuation by reference to fair market value of the going concern, not book value.
- Widely reflected in bilateral investment treaties and in the awards of the 1970s onward.

**CAPITAL-IMPORTING POSITION**
- Appropriate compensation, determined by the state taking the measure in the light of all circumstances.
- Reflects Resolution 1803 and the 1974 Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States.
- Capacity to pay, the history of exploitation and the purposes of the measure are all relevant.
- Argues that full market value would make the right to nationalise economically meaningless.

**Narration**

The contest over compensation is the most litigated question in the history of this field, and the two positions are worth stating carefully because each of them is more reasonable than its opponents usually allow. On the capital-exporting side stands the classical formula, associated with the United States Secretary of State Cordell Hull: prompt, adequate and effective compensation. Its logic is straightforward. If the right to nationalise is conceded, but compensation is set by the state that takes, at a level the state chooses, then the right to nationalise has swallowed the right to property entirely. Full reparation, measured by the fair market value of the going concern rather than by book value, is said to hold the balance. On the capital-importing side stands the formula of appropriate compensation, drawn from Resolution 1803 and the 1974 Charter, which would have the amount determined by the state taking the measure, in the light of all relevant circumstances and with regard to its needs. Its logic is equally straightforward. A state that has been dispossessed of its resources for decades, under concessions negotiated by a colonial administration, is not obliged to buy back at full market value that which it argues was never lawfully transferred, and to require it to do so would make the right to nationalise economically meaningless. I want to give you my honest assessment of where this now stands, and I will put it in three sentences. First, the classical formula has been dominant in awards and in treaty practice for half a century, and it is the working assumption of most tribunals. Second, the appropriate compensation formula has never been extinguished and continues to appear in constitutions and in the pleadings of respondent states. Third, and this is the point most textbooks miss, the practical gap between the two has narrowed, because the real argument in modern cases is not about the standard but about the valuation method, and a valuation dispute can move the number further than any choice of formula.

### 12. Sovereignty Conceded, Remedy Contested `case`

*Aminoil — Government of the State of Kuwait v. American Independent Oil Company (1982), 21 I.L.M. 976*

Kuwait had granted Aminoil a concession in 1948 and amended it in 1961. Following the restructuring of the industry and a series of negotiated adjustments to the fiscal regime, Kuwait terminated the concession in 1977. The tribunal held that the termination was a lawful exercise of Kuwait's right to nationalise, and that the stabilisation clause in the 1961 agreement did not freeze the state's right to take. It then assessed what lawful nationalisation required by way of compensation, and adopted a standard of appropriate compensation, taking account of Aminoil's legitimate expectations, its own conduct, and the long history of the fiscal settlement between the parties.

> **Holding / significance.** A stabilisation clause does not remove a state's right to nationalise; it shapes the compensation. And 'legitimate expectations', assessed on the whole history of the relationship, can be as important as the nominal standard.

**Narration**

Our case is Aminoil against Kuwait, decided in 1982, and I have chosen it because it shows the doctrine working in the way it actually works, rather than the way it is usually summarised. The concession was granted in 1948 and amended in 1961. Over the following decade and a half the industry was restructured, the fiscal regime was adjusted repeatedly by negotiation, and the company's return changed considerably. Kuwait then terminated the concession in 1977. The tribunal's reasoning proceeds in three steps, and each of them repays study. First, it held that the termination was a lawful exercise of Kuwait's right to nationalise. That was a significant concession to the sovereignty position, and it removed the lawfulness question from the case. Second, it held that the stabilisation clause in the 1961 agreement did not freeze Kuwait's right to nationalise. That was a significant concession in the other direction: a stabilisation clause, on this view, restricts the circumstances in which a taking may occur and shapes what must be paid for it, but it does not remove the sovereign power. Third, and this is the step that matters most, the tribunal assessed compensation by reference to what it called the legitimate expectations of the parties, considered over the entire history of their relationship rather than at a single moment. It took account of the company's own conduct, of the negotiated adjustments it had accepted, and of the fact that it had been compensated in substance over many years. The resulting award was a fraction of what a going-concern valuation would have produced. Now, what should you learn from this? That the compensation question is decided less by the choice of formula than by the tribunal's assessment of the parties' history. That a stabilisation clause is a shield against conduct, not an insurance policy against termination. And that in this field, as in few others, the facts of the relationship displace the abstractions of doctrine.

### 13. Whose Sovereignty? Peoples, Trust and the Future `section`

**Narration**

There remains a deeper question, which the classical accounts of this doctrine tend to treat as rhetorical and which I want to treat as real. Whose sovereignty is it?

### 14. The Text Says Peoples. That Is Not an Accident. `bullets`

- Resolution 1803 speaks of the right of peoples and nations, and both International Covenants speak of all peoples.
- The right is therefore not simply an attribute of statehood held against other states; it has an internal dimension.
- It follows that a government may hold the right as a trustee rather than as an owner, and may breach it against its own people.
- This internal dimension is the foundation of the modern resource governance and transparency agenda.

*On-screen note:* A right held for the benefit of a people can be violated by the government that invokes it.

**Narration**

Here is the question that the textbooks treat as rhetorical and that I want you to treat as real. The foundational text speaks of the right of peoples and nations. The common article of the two International Covenants speaks of all peoples. Neither text says governments. That drafting choice was deliberate, and it carries a consequence that is radical if taken seriously. If the right belongs to peoples and nations rather than to governments, then it is not merely an attribute of statehood held horizontally against other states, as sovereignty classically is. It has an internal dimension. And if it has an internal dimension, then a government may hold the right in the manner of a trustee rather than in the manner of an owner, which means that a government can violate permanent sovereignty by the very act of invoking it. Consider what follows. A government that grants away its country's resource wealth on terms that impoverish the population, or that dissipates the rents through corruption, or that mortgages future production to present creditors, is arguably not exercising permanent sovereignty at all. It is breaching it. That argument is the foundation of the entire modern resource governance agenda: of contract transparency, of the publication of payments, of the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, of constitutional requirements that resource revenues be deposited in a consolidated fund, and of the growing constitutional jurisprudence in several states on the duties of the executive in awarding resource rights. It is also the foundation of the argument I shall make in the final part of this lecture. If sovereignty is held for a people, it is held for a people across time, and that means it is held for generations who are not yet born and cannot vote.

### 15. Sovereignty as a Trust Across Time `bullets`

- Exhaustible resources present the paradigm case: a decision to deplete now is a decision about what is left later.
- The trust idea supports obligations of conservation, of revenue saving and of reinvestment rather than consumption.
- Climate change makes the argument concrete: the atmosphere is a sink, and its capacity is a resource being allocated.
- Several constitutions now express this directly, requiring resource wealth to be safeguarded for future generations.

*On-screen note:* The most difficult question in the field is not who owns the resource. It is for whom it is held.

**Narration**

Let me press the trust idea a little further, because it is where the doctrine is being reconstructed today. Exhaustible resources present the paradigm case. A decision to extract and sell a finite deposit now is necessarily a decision about what remains for those who come afterwards, and no theory of sovereignty that ignores that fact can be adequate. The trust idea therefore supports three kinds of obligation. An obligation of conservation, so that the rate of depletion is defensible rather than merely profitable. An obligation of revenue saving, so that a finite stock is converted into a durable asset, whether financial, physical or human, rather than consumed as it is realised. And an obligation of reinvestment, so that the wealth generated by depletion is available to a generation that will not have the resource. Climate change makes this concrete in a way that the framers of 1962 could not have anticipated. The atmosphere's capacity to absorb greenhouse gases is itself a scarce resource, and the decision to use it is a decision about the allocation of a commons among generations. That is why the advisory proceedings before the International Court of Justice, before the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, and before the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, all of which culminated in 2024 and 2025, are best understood not as environmental litigation but as litigation about the allocation of a resource over time. Several national constitutions now state the point directly, providing that mineral and petroleum wealth is the property of the nation and must be safeguarded and managed for the benefit of present and future generations. Those provisions are not decorative. They are being litigated, and they are being litigated by citizens against their own governments, which is exactly what an internal dimension of permanent sovereignty predicts.

### 16. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**
- Learn the chronology from 1952 to 1974 and be able to recite what each instrument did.
- Practise stating the Hull formula and the appropriate compensation formula without caricaturing either.
- Take one national constitution with a resource clause and identify what duties it imposes on the executive.
- Ask of every nationalisation story: who decided, who benefited, and who was compensated?

**Postgraduate level**
- Assess whether permanent sovereignty has customary status, and what evidence would settle it either way.
- Analyse the internal dimension: can a right owed to a people be enforced against the government that holds it, and by whom?
- Critique the trust argument as a solution to intergenerational allocation, including its democratic difficulties.
- Evaluate whether the 1974 Charter ever acquired normative force, and what its failure or success tells us about law-making in this field.

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want the chronology secure: 1952, 1962, 1966, 1974, and then the treaty era, and I want you able to say what each instrument did rather than merely when it happened. Practise stating both compensation formulas accurately, without caricaturing the one you find less attractive, because the ability to state the opposing case fairly is a professional skill and not a rhetorical trick. Take one national constitution that contains a resource clause and identify the duties it imposes on the executive, because these provisions are now common and they are being enforced. And whenever you read a nationalisation story, and you will read many, ask three questions: who decided, who benefited, and who was compensated. At postgraduate level, I want the harder questions. Assess whether permanent sovereignty has acquired the status of customary international law, and be explicit about what evidence would settle the question either way, including the awkward fact that the states most associated with the doctrine have not consistently behaved as though it were binding. Analyse the internal dimension seriously: can a right said to be owed to a people be enforced against the government that holds it, by whom, in what forum, and with what legitimacy. Critique the trust argument as a solution to intergenerational allocation, and do not neglect its democratic difficulty, which is that a trust constrains the choices of a living electorate in the name of a constituency that cannot be consulted. And evaluate whether the 1974 Charter ever acquired normative force, and what the answer tells us about how law is actually made in this field, which is a question about power as much as about doctrine.

### 17. What We Have Established `summary`

- Permanent sovereignty was built politically in the General Assembly from 1952 to 1974 and is now argued in arbitral fora.
- Its content is broad, but the controversy has always concerned its limits rather than its existence.
- The compensation contest is unresolved in principle, though valuation practice has narrowed the gap in practice.
- The texts speak of peoples, which gives the right an internal dimension enforceable against governments.
- Understood as a trust, the doctrine generates obligations of conservation, saving and reinvestment across generations.

> **The one thing to remember.** Permanent sovereignty is not a licence for governments. It is a right held by peoples, and the hardest cases are those in which a government invokes it against the very people it is held for.

**Narration**

Let me close. Permanent sovereignty over natural resources was built politically, in the General Assembly, across a quarter century in which the membership of the United Nations was transformed. Its content is broad: to possess, to regulate, to nationalise, to determine the pace of exploitation, and to renegotiate the rent. The controversy has never been about whether the right exists, but about where its limits lie. On compensation, the contest between prompt, adequate and effective and appropriate compensation remains unresolved in principle, though the practical difference has narrowed because valuation method now does more work than the choice of formula. In Aminoil we saw a tribunal concede the right to nationalise, refuse to let a stabilisation clause extinguish it, and then decide the amount by reference to the whole history of the relationship. And in the final part we saw the question that will dominate the next generation of this field. The texts say peoples. If that word means what it says, then sovereignty is held in trust, it has an internal dimension enforceable against governments, and it runs across generations. In the next lecture we leave doctrine for structure and ask how the global energy order is actually organised: who produces, who consumes, who sets the price, and what law holds that arrangement together.
