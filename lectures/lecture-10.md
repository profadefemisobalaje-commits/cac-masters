### 02. Mining and Critical Minerals in International Law `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Ten. We move now from hydrocarbons to hard minerals, and I want to begin with an observation about scale and speed. A decade ago, mining was the least fashionable part of this field: a mature industry, governed by national mining codes, of interest principally to specialists. It is now among the most dynamic, for a reason that has nothing to do with mining and everything to do with the energy transition. The technologies of decarbonisation are materially intensive. Electric vehicles, batteries, wind turbines, solar panels, electrolysers and grids all require copper, nickel, lithium, cobalt, graphite, manganese and rare earth elements in quantities that the world has never previously extracted. That single fact has converted mining from a mature industry into the strategic centre of industrial policy, and it has generated a body of law that is being written now. Let me show you its structure.

### 04. Tenure and Allocation `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with tenure, which is the foundation of the whole subject.

### 05. How Mining Tenure Differs from Petroleum Licensing `bullets`

- In most systems the state owns the minerals in situ, whether or not it owns the surface, and grants rights to exploit them.
- The typical sequence runs from a reconnaissance or prospecting permit, through an exploration licence, to a mining lease or exploitation permit.
- Allocation is frequently first-in-time and administrative rather than discretionary and negotiated.
- The instrument is therefore a title issued under a code rather than a contract negotiated with a government.

*On-screen note:* Petroleum is usually negotiated. Mining is usually applied for. That difference shapes everything.

**Narration**

The first thing to understand about mining is that its legal architecture is quite different from petroleum, and the difference is not merely terminological. In petroleum, as we saw, the state commonly grants a large area under a negotiated instrument containing detailed fiscal and work terms, and the relationship is contractual in character. In mining, the typical arrangement is quite different. In most legal systems the state owns the minerals in situ, whether or not it owns the surface, and it grants rights to search for and exploit them under a legislative code. The sequence of titles is standard: a reconnaissance or prospecting permit, which confers no exclusive right; an exploration licence, which confers exclusivity over a defined area for a defined term and is subject to expenditure obligations and to progressive relinquishment; and a mining lease or exploitation permit, which confers the right to produce and is of long duration. Now, the critical distinction is in how those rights are allocated. Petroleum rights in most jurisdictions are awarded through a discretionary process, frequently by competitive tender, and the terms are negotiated. Mining rights in most jurisdictions are applied for, and where the applicant meets the prescribed criteria and the ground is available, the title must be granted. That is the first-in-time principle, and it is administrative rather than discretionary. The consequence is that the primary instrument in mining is a title issued under a code, not a contract negotiated with a government. That has an important effect on the legal analysis. Where a petroleum dispute is about what the contract promised, a mining dispute is frequently about whether the administering authority acted lawfully in granting, refusing, suspending or cancelling a title. The first is a contractual question; the second is a question of administrative law, with all the review and remedy structure that implies.

### 06. What Makes a Mineral 'Critical'? `bullets`

- Criticality is not a geological category. It is a policy judgement about economic importance combined with supply risk.
- Lists are maintained unilaterally by states and regional organisations, they differ from one another, and they are revised periodically.
- The binding constraint is rarely the ore body. It is processing, refining and separation capacity.
- That is why policy targets the midstream: export restrictions on raw ore, beneficiation requirements, and subsidies for domestic processing.

*On-screen note:* Scarcity is rarely the problem. Concentration is almost always the problem.

**Narration**

Let me now address the concept that has reorganised this field, and I want to begin by removing a common misunderstanding. Criticality is not a geological category. A mineral is not critical because it is rare, and several of the minerals on the principal critical lists are in fact abundant in the earth's crust. Criticality is a policy judgement, made unilaterally by a state or a regional organisation, combining two variables: the economic importance of the material to that state's economy, and the risk that its supply will be disrupted. Lists are therefore political instruments. They are maintained by individual states and by regional organisations, they differ from one another in their contents, and they are revised as circumstances and industrial policy change. Now, the second and more important point is about where the constraint actually lies. For most of these materials, the binding constraint is not the ore body. Deposits of lithium, nickel, cobalt and the rare earth elements are distributed across many countries. The constraint is processing: the capacity to refine, to separate, to produce battery-grade chemicals, and to manufacture magnets, cathodes and anodes. That capacity is far more concentrated than extraction, and it is concentrated for reasons of industrial history, capital intensity and accumulated technical skill. This is why contemporary minerals policy almost always targets the midstream rather than the mine. It takes the form of export restrictions on raw ore, designed to force processing to occur domestically; of beneficiation and local content requirements; of subsidies and tax credits conditioned on domestic or allied processing; and of direct investment in processing capacity abroad. Scarcity, in short, is rarely the problem. Concentration is almost always the problem, and it is concentration at a specific stage of the chain that policy is trying to break.

### 07. The Instruments Available to a Mineral-Rich State `two-col`

**RESTRICTING OUTFLOW**
- Export duties and quotas on unprocessed ore, often combined with a scheduled phase-out.
- Outright export bans on specified raw materials, conditioned on domestic processing capacity being built.
- Minimum export prices and export licensing requirements.
- Trade law constrains each of these, as the rare earths disputes demonstrated.

**CAPTURING VALUE**
- Royalties ad valorem or on gross value, and increasingly differentiated by commodity and by stage of processing.
- Progressive profit taxes, windfall taxes and variable royalty rates keyed to price.
- Free carried equity for the state, and options to acquire a participating interest.
- Local content, procurement and employment obligations, and obligations to train and to transfer technology.

**Narration**

Let me now survey the instruments available to a state that wishes to capture more value from its minerals, because the toolkit is larger than most students suppose. On the side of restricting outflow, there are export duties and quotas on unprocessed ore, frequently coupled with a published schedule for their progressive elimination as domestic processing capacity is built, which is the design that survives trade law scrutiny better than a bare prohibition. There are outright export bans, which are the most aggressive instrument and the most likely to be challenged. There are minimum export prices and export licensing requirements. And each of these is constrained by the trading system, as we saw in the rare earths disputes, where the measures failed on design rather than on objective. On the side of capturing value within the fiscal system, there are royalties, calculated either ad valorem on the value of production or as a percentage of gross revenue, and increasingly differentiated by commodity and by whether the product is raw or processed. There are progressive profit taxes and windfall taxes, which raise the state's share when prices are high, and variable royalty rates keyed to the price. There is free carried equity, under which the state acquires a specified interest without contributing to cost, and there are options to acquire a participating interest at defined milestones. And there are the obligations that attach to operation: local content and procurement requirements, employment and training obligations, technology transfer commitments, and obligations to process domestically. Now, the strategic question for a mineral-rich state is whether to exercise these instruments. Export restrictions can be effective in forcing investment in processing, but they can also simply reduce investment in extraction, because a mine that cannot sell its product freely is worth less. That trade-off is the central policy calculation in the sector, and getting it wrong has cost several states a great deal of revenue.

### 08. People and Chains `section`

**Narration**

I want now to turn to the human dimension of this industry, which is where its most serious legal problems are found.

### 09. Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining: The Formalisation Problem `bullets`

- Artisanal and small-scale mining provides livelihoods for a very large number of people, frequently in the poorest regions.
- It is often illegal in fact but tolerated in practice, which produces neither protection nor control.
- Formalisation requires accessible title, a simplified regime, a legal route to market, and services rather than enforcement alone.
- The hardest problems are child labour, mercury use, dangerous working conditions, and the involvement of armed actors.

*On-screen note:* A regime that offers no lawful route will produce an unlawful one, and will criminalise the poor in doing so.

**Narration**

There is an aspect of mining that the international literature neglected for a very long time and that is now central, and it is artisanal and small-scale mining. This is mining conducted with minimal mechanisation, frequently by individuals and small groups, in many cases without formal title, and it provides livelihoods for a very large number of people, disproportionately in the poorest regions of the world. In several important producing countries it accounts for a meaningful share of output of gold, of cobalt, of tantalum, of tin and of tungsten. Its legal status is usually ambiguous: it is often unlawful as conducted, because the operators hold no title and comply with no environmental or safety standard, while being tolerated in practice because it supports communities that have no alternative. That combination produces the worst of both worlds. There is no protection for the miners, no environmental control, no reliable route to market, and no tax revenue; but there is also no enforcement, because enforcement would deprive entire communities of their living. The formalisation agenda attempts to break this deadlock, and its elements are well understood. There must be an accessible form of title, at a scale and a cost that an artisanal operator can actually obtain. There must be a simplified regulatory regime, with obligations proportionate to the scale of the operation. There must be a lawful route to market, which is the element most often omitted and the one that matters most, because a miner who cannot lawfully sell has no reason to formalise. And there must be services: geological support, training, access to finance, and help with safer technology. The hardest problems, which formalisation alone does not solve, are child labour, the use of mercury in gold processing, unsafe workings, and in some regions the involvement of armed groups and the financing of conflict. A regime that offers no lawful route will produce an unlawful one, and will criminalise poor people in the process.

### 10. The right of indigenous peoples to self-determination includes the right to dete `quote`

> The right of indigenous peoples to self-determination includes the right to determine their own priorities for development, and to require that free, prior and informed consent be obtained before the approval of any project affecting their lands or territories and other resources.
>
> — Principles expressed in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, and in the jurisprudence of regional human rights bodies

**Narration**

Let me read you a principle that has become the central contested standard in this field, and then tell you honestly how uncertain its legal status is. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples provides that indigenous peoples have the right to self-determination, and that by virtue of it they may freely determine their political status and freely pursue their economic, social and cultural development. It provides further that states shall consult and cooperate in good faith with indigenous peoples through their own representative institutions in order to obtain their free, prior and informed consent before adopting and implementing legislative or administrative measures that may affect them, and specifically before the approval of any project affecting their lands or territories and other resources. Regional human rights bodies have developed this into enforceable jurisprudence, requiring consultation that is informed, in good faith, culturally appropriate and aimed at obtaining agreement. Now, the honest position. The Declaration is not a treaty and is not, as such, binding. The content of the consent standard varies between jurisdictions, between treaties, and between the human rights systems. Several states have adopted a weaker position, accepting a duty of consultation but not a duty to obtain consent. Investors, lenders and insurers now treat the standard as a commercial requirement regardless of its strict legal status, because the cost of a project that loses its social licence is very high. And the litigation is moving steadily in the direction of the stronger standard. If you advise on a project affecting indigenous territory, the correct advice is not to ask whether consent is legally required in the abstract. It is to establish the standard applicable in that jurisdiction, and then to exceed it, because the legal question will be decided years after the events and in a forum whose views on the matter will have moved further.

### 11. Due Diligence: A Real Mechanism with Real Limits `bullets`

- The OECD Due Diligence Guidance is the operative standard, structured around identifying, preventing, mitigating and accounting for adverse impacts.
- Implementation is by the enterprise itself, through chain mapping, risk assessment, mitigation, independent audit and public reporting.
- Market access frequently depends on it: exchanges, lenders and manufacturers require adherence as a condition of doing business.
- The limits are real: audit fatigue, the exclusion of small producers, and the difficulty of verifying conditions at the point of extraction.

*On-screen note:* Due diligence is a process obligation, not an outcome guarantee. Know which one your client is buying.

**Narration**

I want to describe the mechanism through which the human rights and conflict problems of mineral supply chains are actually addressed, because it is a distinctive instrument and its limits are as important as its operation. The operative standard is the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas, which is supplemented by guidance for the wider extractive sector. Its structure follows a five-step framework: establish strong company management systems; identify and assess risk in the supply chain; design and implement a strategy to respond to identified risks; carry out independent third-party audit of the due diligence practices at identified points in the chain; and report annually on supply chain due diligence. Notice what kind of obligation this is. It is a process obligation, not an outcome guarantee. A company complies by having a defensible system of identification, assessment, mitigation, audit and disclosure, even if adverse impacts are later found to persist. That is both the strength of the instrument, since an outcome guarantee would be unachievable, and its principal limitation. Now, the mechanism is enforced commercially rather than legally. Adherence is a condition of listing on major metals exchanges, of obtaining finance from responsible lenders, and of supplying manufacturers who must themselves report. That commercial enforcement is, in practice, more effective than most regulation. But three limits should be stated plainly. The first is audit fatigue, and the disproportionate burden it places on smaller producers, who may simply be excluded from chains they cannot afford to certify. The second is the difficulty of verification at the point of extraction, particularly where artisanal production is aggregated from many sources. And the third is the risk of de-risking, under which companies withdraw from high-risk jurisdictions altogether, which removes the leverage that engagement would provide and harms precisely the communities the instrument was designed to assist. A lawyer advising in this area should know all three, because a client who has bought a certificate rather than a process will discover the difference at the worst possible moment.

### 12. When Social Opposition Becomes an Expropriation Claim `case`

*Bear Creek Mining Corporation v. Republic of Peru (ICSID Case No. ARB/14/21, Award of 30 November 2017)*

The claimant acquired the Santa Ana silver project in the Puno region of Peru. The project encountered sustained and at times violent opposition from local Aymara communities. Following the unrest, Peru issued a supreme decree that had the effect of depriving the claimant of its ability to hold and operate the concessions. The claimant alleged indirect expropriation and breach of fair and equitable treatment under the Canada–Peru Free Trade Agreement. The majority found that Peru had indirectly expropriated the investment and awarded substantial damages. The dissenting arbitrator, Professor Philippe Sands, concluded that the measure was a legitimate exercise of the state's police powers in response to serious social unrest, and placed weight on the human right to water and on the interests of the affected indigenous communities. The award was subsequently the subject of a partial annulment as to quantum.

> **Holding / significance.** The case exposes the deepest unresolved question in the field: when a state responds to community opposition by withdrawing a project authorisation, is that an expropriation requiring compensation, or a non-compensable exercise of regulatory authority?

**Narration**

Our case is Bear Creek Mining against Peru, decided in 2017, and I have chosen it because it is the clearest illustration of the single most difficult question in the whole of mining law. The claimant acquired the Santa Ana silver project in the Puno region of Peru. The project met sustained and at times violent opposition from local Aymara communities, whose concerns included the effect of the project on water. Following that unrest, Peru issued a supreme decree which had the effect of depriving the claimant of the ability to hold and operate its concessions. The claimant brought a claim under the Canada-Peru Free Trade Agreement, alleging indirect expropriation and breach of fair and equitable treatment. The majority of the tribunal found that Peru had indirectly expropriated the investment and awarded substantial damages. And then there is the dissent, which is the reason this case is taught. Professor Philippe Sands dissented, and his reasoning deserves your close attention. He concluded that the measure was a legitimate exercise of the state's police powers, taken in response to serious social unrest and in protection of the fundamental rights of the affected communities, including the human right to water. He placed weight on the state's obligations to those communities under Peruvian law and under international human rights law, and he regarded the withdrawal of the authorisation as a proportionate response rather than a taking. The award was later the subject of a partial annulment as to quantum. Now, what should you take from this? Three things. First, that the boundary between compensable expropriation and non-compensable regulation is most contested precisely where community opposition is most acute. Second, that the tribunal's composition may determine the outcome in a case of this kind, which is a legitimacy problem and not merely an observation about luck. And third, and most practically, that the project which fails to secure and maintain its social licence will ultimately present its investor with a claim, and its host state with a liability, that careful community engagement would have avoided at a fraction of the cost.

### 13. The Shape of the Regime `section`

**Narration**

Let me close by considering what kind of legal regime this sector is developing.

### 14. Four Features of the Emerging Minerals Regime `bullets`

- Policy is industrial rather than merely fiscal: the objective is to capture the midstream, not only the rent.
- Regulation reaches downstream, through due diligence, product standards and market access conditions set by consumers.
- Community rights are no longer peripheral, and consent standards are hardening through litigation and lender requirements.
- The regime is being written now, in national law, in standards bodies, and in contract, rather than in a treaty.

*On-screen note:* There is no comprehensive international minerals convention, and there is unlikely to be one. That is the defining feature.

**Narration**

Let me conclude with four observations about the regime that is emerging, because this sector is being constructed while you study it, and you should be able to describe its shape. First, minerals policy has become industrial policy rather than merely fiscal policy. The objective is no longer simply to tax the rent from extraction; it is to capture the processing, refining and manufacturing stages that follow, and every instrument, from export restrictions to local content to subsidy, is directed at that objective. Second, regulation reaches downstream. The most effective instruments in this field are not imposed by the producing state at the mine. They are imposed by consuming markets on importers, manufacturers and exchanges, through due diligence requirements, product standards, recycled content mandates and market access conditions. That is a profound change in the geography of regulation. Third, community rights have moved from the periphery to the centre. Consent standards are hardening, through litigation, through lender requirements, through insurance conditions, and through the constitutional jurisprudence of producing states, and no project can now be advised on the assumption that community relations are a public relations matter. And fourth, and this is the structural point, the regime is being written in national law, in standards bodies, in lender policies and in contracts rather than in any treaty. There is no comprehensive international convention on minerals, and there is unlikely to be one, because the producing and consuming states have interests too divergent to codify. That is the defining feature of this field and the reason it rewards the lawyer who can work across public regulation, private standards and commercial contracting simultaneously. I will add one prediction, which you should test against events rather than accept. Because the instruments are national, contractual and standard-based rather than treaty-based, the lawyers who shape this sector over the next two decades will be those who can draft a mining code, negotiate a mineral development agreement, and design a compliance architecture at the same time. The traditional separation between the public lawyer, the transactional lawyer and the compliance lawyer does not survive contact with this subject, and a curriculum that keeps them apart is preparing its students for a profession that is disappearing.

**Undergraduate level**

**Postgraduate level**

- Analyse Bear Creek alongside the police powers doctrine, and defend a position on where the boundary should lie.
- Critique due diligence as a regulatory technique: does a process obligation produce better outcomes than an outcome obligation?
- Evaluate whether a comprehensive international minerals convention is desirable, and what it would have to contain.

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want four things. First, the sequence of mining titles secure: prospecting permit, exploration licence, mining lease, and be able to contrast that with petroleum licensing, where the instrument is negotiated rather than applied for. Second, the ability to define criticality accurately and to say plainly why it is a policy category rather than a geological one, because that distinction is the key to the whole policy debate. Third, take one critical mineral and map its chain from the mine to the end product, identifying at which stage concentration actually bites, because the answer will frequently surprise you. And fourth, the ability to explain the difference between a duty to consult and a duty to obtain consent, and to say which applies in a given jurisdiction. At postgraduate level, I want assessment. Assess whether export restrictions on raw ore have actually succeeded in building domestic processing capacity, and be specific about the evidence you would require, including the counterfactual of what would have happened otherwise. Analyse Bear Creek alongside the police powers doctrine, and take a defended position on where the boundary between compensable and non-compensable regulation should lie when the state's reason for acting is community opposition rather than policy change. Critique due diligence as a regulatory technique, and answer the question directly: can a process obligation produce outcomes, or does it primarily produce documentation? And evaluate whether a comprehensive international minerals convention would be desirable, what it would have to contain, and why the states concerned have not concluded one.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Mining tenure is applied for under a code; petroleum rights are negotiated under a contract. The difference shapes the disputes.
- Criticality is a policy judgement combining economic importance with supply risk, and concentration lies in processing rather than extraction.
- States pursue value through export restrictions and fiscal capture, and trade law constrains the design of each.
- Artisanal mining, community rights and due diligence are now central rather than peripheral to the sector.
- The regime is being written in national law, standards and contracts, not in a treaty, and there is unlikely to be one.

> **The one thing to remember.** Mining law is where the energy transition meets the ground. It is the most rapidly rewritten area of this field, and it is being written without a treaty.

**Narration**

Let me close. Mining tenure is applied for under a code, whereas petroleum rights are negotiated under a contract, and that difference shapes the character of the disputes in each sector. Criticality is a policy judgement combining economic importance with supply risk, and the concentration that matters lies in processing rather than extraction. States pursue value through two families of instrument, restricting outflow and capturing the rent, and the trading system constrains the design of both. Artisanal mining, community rights and supply chain due diligence have moved from the periphery to the centre of the sector, as the Bear Creek case and the consent standard both demonstrate. And the regime is being written now, in national law, in standards bodies and in contracts, rather than in any treaty, which is the defining structural feature of the field. In the next lecture we move to the most heavily regulated energy technology of all, and examine nuclear energy law: non-proliferation, safety, and liability. And let me leave you with the observation that should endure from this lecture: the field is moving, and it is moving away from the settlement of disputes after the event and towards the regulation of conduct before it, through due diligence, through certification, and through the conditions attached to market access.
