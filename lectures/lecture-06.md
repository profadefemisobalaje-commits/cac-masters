### 04. The Contract Family `section`

**Narration**

### 05. Concession, Production Sharing, Service, Joint Venture `diagram`

- Concession or licence: the company holds title to the produced petroleum and pays royalty and tax. The classic form, still dominant in North America and much of Europe.
- Production sharing contract: the state retains title; the contractor recovers agreed costs out of production, and the remainder is split between them.
- Service contract: the contractor is paid a fee, in cash or in kind, for services rendered, and takes no share of the production itself.
- Joint venture or participation: the state or its national company holds an equity or participating interest alongside the contractor in a joint operating structure.

**Narration**

There are four basic forms of petroleum contract, and it is worth being clear at the outset that the categories are not watertight, because modern instruments combine them freely. Under a concession, or licence, the company holds title to the petroleum it produces and pays a royalty and corporate taxes to the state. This is the classical form, the form against which the nationalisations of the twentieth century were directed, and it remains the dominant instrument in North America and in much of Europe. Under a production sharing contract, the state retains title to the petroleum throughout. The contractor bears the exploration risk and funds development, and in return it is permitted to recover agreed costs out of a defined share of production, conventionally called cost oil, while the remainder, profit oil, is divided between the state and the contractor according to a negotiated formula. Under a service contract, the contractor is paid a fee, either in cash or in kind, for the services it performs, and does not take title to or a share of production; the pure form is rare, but the risk service variant, under which the fee is exposed to production and price outcomes, is used in a small number of important jurisdictions. And under a joint venture or participation arrangement, the state or its national oil company takes an equity or participating interest alongside the contractor, becoming a co-venturer rather than merely a regulator. Most modern instruments combine two or more of these: a production sharing contract will very often contain a state participation option and will always be administered through a joint operating agreement.

### 06. Ask of Every Petroleum Contract: Title, Control, Risk, Reward `bullets`

- Title: who owns the petroleum in the ground, at the wellhead, and after processing?
- Control: who decides whether to explore, to appraise, to develop, and to cease production?
- Risk: who bears exploration risk, development risk, price risk, and political risk?
- Reward: how is the economic rent divided, and how does that division change as the project matures?

*On-screen note:* These four questions cut across every contractual form. Answer them and you understand the bargain.

**Narration**

Rather than memorising the contract types, I want you to use four questions that cut across all of them, because these are the questions that determine where value goes. The first is title. Who owns the petroleum in the ground, who owns it once it is produced, and who owns it after processing? In some systems the state owns the resource in situ and transfers title at the wellhead; in others the holder of the licence acquires title on production; and under a production sharing contract the state's retention of title is the organising principle of the whole instrument. The second question is control. Who decides whether to explore, whether to drill an appraisal well, whether to commit to a development, and whether to cease production? Control is frequently vested in a management committee on which the state or national company sits, and the voting rules of that committee are among the most consequential provisions in the document. The third question is risk. Who bears exploration risk, which is the risk of finding nothing? Who bears development risk, which is the risk that construction cost overruns? Who bears price risk, which is the risk that the commodity price falls below the level at which the project is economic? And who bears political risk, which is the risk that the state changes the terms? The fourth question is reward. How is the economic rent divided, and, critically, how does that division change as the project matures and as the investor recovers its costs? Progressive fiscal regimes are designed precisely to increase the state's share over the life of the field. Answer those four questions about any petroleum contract and you will understand the bargain, regardless of what the document calls itself.

### 07. Concession and Production Sharing, Side by Side `two-col`

**CONCESSION / LICENCE**
- The company owns the produced petroleum and sells it.
- State revenue comes from royalty, corporate income tax and supplementary charges.
- The company carries the whole of the commercial risk and keeps the whole of the reward.
- Politically salient: the visible spectacle of a foreign company owning national oil.
- Administratively simpler, because the general tax law applies rather than a bespoke regime.

**PRODUCTION SHARING**
- The state owns the petroleum; the contractor receives a share of production.
- State revenue comes from cost oil limits, the profit split, and any taxes levied on the contractor's share.
- The contractor recovers costs first, so early production is weighted toward the investor.
- Politically durable, because the state can say that the resource was never alienated.
- Administratively demanding, because cost recovery must be audited and ring-fenced in detail.

**Narration**

Let me now put the two dominant forms side by side, because the choice between them is the single most consequential decision a resource ministry makes. Under a concession, the company owns what it produces and sells it, and the state takes its share through royalty, corporate income tax and any supplementary charge. The company carries the whole of the commercial risk and, in principle, keeps the whole of the reward after tax. The instrument is administratively simpler, because the general tax law applies rather than a bespoke regime, which means the revenue authority can administer it with existing capacity. Its political weakness is equally clear: it produces the visible spectacle of a foreign company owning and exporting the nation's oil, which is precisely the image that the nationalisation movements of the twentieth century were mobilised against. Under a production sharing contract, the state owns the petroleum throughout, and the contractor receives a share of production rather than title. Revenue reaches the state through several channels: through the limit set on cost recovery, through the split of profit oil, and through whatever taxes are levied on the contractor's share. The contractor recovers its costs first, which means that early production is weighted toward the investor and later production toward the state, and that is not an accident but the purpose of the design. Its political strength is that a government can say truthfully that the resource was never alienated. Its administrative burden is severe, because every claim to cost recovery must be defined, audited, ring-fenced and, in practice, litigated. A state with a weak revenue authority may therefore do better with a simple concession and a strong tax regime than with a sophisticated production sharing contract it lacks the capacity to audit.

### 08. Stabilisation, Renegotiation, and the Allocation of Regulatory Risk `bullets`

- Fiscal stability clauses freeze the fiscal regime as at the date of the contract, either for the whole term or for a defined period.
- Economic equilibrium clauses accept that law may change but require restoration of the investor's economic position.
- Express renegotiation clauses concede the possibility of change and prescribe a procedure and a standard for adjusting to it.
- The state's answer is the right to regulate, and in several systems a constitutional prohibition on contracting away legislative power.

*On-screen note:* Read the three clauses together. Individually, each one misleads.

**Narration**

There is no set of clauses in a petroleum agreement more consequential, or more frequently misunderstood, than the three that allocate regulatory risk, so let me take them together, because read individually each one misleads. The fiscal stability clause freezes the fiscal regime as it stands at the date of the contract, either for the whole term of the agreement or for a defined stabilisation period. It is the strongest protection available to the investor and the most politically visible concession a government can make. The economic equilibrium clause takes a different approach. It accepts that the law may change, and requires instead that if a change causes the investor a specified economic detriment, the parties will adjust the contract, or the state will compensate, so as to restore the investor to the economic position it would otherwise have occupied. The express renegotiation clause concedes change openly, prescribes a procedure for invoking renegotiation, identifies the matters that may be reopened, and frequently sets a standard, such as the restoration of overall economic balance, by which the outcome is to be judged. Now, against all three, the state has an answer, and it is not merely rhetorical. The state invokes its right to regulate, expressed through the police powers doctrine. And in a number of jurisdictions there is a constitutional difficulty that counsel for investors repeatedly underestimate: a provision of the constitution may forbid the executive from contracting away the legislative power of the state, which means that a stabilisation clause, however carefully drafted, may be void or unenforceable in the very forum whose courts would have to enforce it. If you advise an investor, your first task is therefore not to negotiate the strongest possible stabilisation clause. It is to establish whether the host state's constitution permits any stabilisation at all.

### 10. The Life of a Production Sharing Contract `bullets`

- Exploration period with mandatory work obligations and staged relinquishment of acreage.
- Declaration of commercial discovery, submission of a development plan, and approval by the state.
- Development and production period, with cost recovery capped as a percentage of gross production.
- Split of profit oil according to a formula that commonly varies with rate of return or cumulative production.
- Domestic market obligations, licensing, assignment and change of control restrictions.
- Decommissioning, abandonment security and the transfer of assets to the state at the end.

*On-screen note:* Relinquishment is the clause that keeps an exploration licence honest. Read it first.

**Narration**

A production sharing contract is a machine with six moving parts, and I want to walk you through them in the order in which they operate. First, the exploration period. The contractor is granted an area for a defined term, subject to mandatory work obligations, typically expressed as a commitment to acquire seismic and to drill a specified number of wells. Critically, the contract requires staged relinquishment: at the end of each phase, the contractor must surrender a percentage of the original area, keeping only what it wishes to develop. Relinquishment is the clause that keeps an exploration licence honest, because without it a company could hold vast acreage indefinitely at almost no cost. Second, discovery and development. If a commercial discovery is declared, the contractor submits a development plan for approval by the state or the national company, and approval is a genuine decision point at which the state can influence the pace and method of development. Third, production and cost recovery. Once production begins, the contractor recovers its operating and capital costs out of a capped percentage of gross production, and any costs that exceed the cap in a given year are carried forward. Fourth, the split of profit oil, which is the remainder after cost recovery and royalty. That split is usually not flat: it commonly varies with the contractor's achieved rate of return, with cumulative production, or with the price, which is what makes the fiscal regime progressive. Fifth, the operational obligations: domestic market obligations requiring a share of production to be sold locally at a prescribed price, restrictions on assignment and change of control, and local content and technology transfer commitments. And sixth, the end: decommissioning, the security that must be provided for it, and the transfer of assets to the state. That sixth element is the one that was neglected for a century and is now, I would suggest, the fastest growing area of petroleum practice.

### 11. Unitisation: When the Reservoir Ignores the Border `bullets`

- A single geological structure may extend across a maritime boundary, a land frontier, or both.
- Uncoordinated extraction allows one side to drain the shared pool, which produces a race to produce.
- The legal response is unitisation: treat the field as a single unit, operate it jointly, and apportion production.
- Apportionment is agreed by reference to the estimated recoverable reserves on each side of the line, and is periodically redetermined.

*On-screen note:* Geology does not respect jurisdiction. The law must either coordinate extraction or tolerate a race.

**Narration**

Here is a problem that brings geology, treaty and contract into direct collision, and it is one of the most interesting in the whole field. A petroleum reservoir is a physical fact. It does not respect a maritime boundary, a land frontier, or a licence boundary, and it is entirely common for a single geological structure to extend across a line drawn by treaty or by a colonial administrator. The consequence is stark. If each side extracts without coordination, each has an incentive to produce as fast as possible, because petroleum that is not extracted will migrate across the line and be extracted by the neighbour. That is a race to produce, and it is both economically wasteful and physically damaging, because rapid, uncoordinated extraction reduces the total recovery from the field. The legal response is unitisation. The parties agree to treat the entire field as a single unit, to appoint a single operator, to develop it according to a single plan, and to apportion the resulting production between them. Now, notice what unitisation requires. It requires a treaty or an agreement between the two states to permit it. It requires a boundary that is settled, or at least defined well enough to know which side of it the reserves lie. It requires a unitisation agreement between the states, and a unit operating agreement between the licence holders on each side. And it requires an apportionment formula, based on the estimated recoverable reserves attributable to each side, which must be redetermined periodically as more is learned about the field, and which is therefore a permanent source of technical dispute. Some jurisdictions have made unitisation compulsory where a straddling field is found, and I would commend that approach to you as the sounder policy. Where it is voluntary, the parties must negotiate while drilling already proceeds, which is the worst possible posture from which to bargain.

### 12. Decommissioning: Who Pays to Take It All Away? `bullets`

- The obligation is now standard: remove the installation, plug and abandon the wells, remediate the site, and leave the seabed clear.
- The difficulty is timing: the obligation crystallises decades after the revenue has been received and the corporate structure has changed.
- The responses are security instruments: abandonment trusts, letters of credit, parent guarantees, and decommissioning security agreements.
- The residual question is what happens when the security proves insufficient, and the answer is often that the state and the public bear the loss.

*On-screen note:* Decommissioning is where the longest-lived risk in the industry finally comes due.

**Narration**

I want to close this part of the lecture on the subject that the industry neglected for a century and that now occupies a rapidly growing share of petroleum practice, which is decommissioning. The obligation is now standard in most jurisdictions: at the end of the field's life, the licensee must remove the installation, plug and abandon the wells, remediate the site, and in most cases leave the seabed substantially clear. That is an obligation to spend money, and frequently a great deal of it, at exactly the point at which the asset has ceased to generate any revenue at all. The difficulty is one of timing and of corporate structure. The obligation crystallises decades after the investment decision, frequently after the original licensee has assigned its interest, been acquired, restructured, or dissolved, and after the individuals who negotiated the agreement have retired. The law's responses are security instruments, and you should know all four. There is the abandonment trust or escrow, into which contributions are made during the producing life of the field and from which decommissioning is funded. There is the letter of credit or on-demand guarantee, which provides a liquid source of recourse but must be renewed and can be difficult to obtain from a parent with a deteriorating credit position. There is the parent company guarantee, which is only as good as the parent, and which corporate restructuring frequently leaves behind. And there is the decommissioning security agreement, which sets out the calculation of the required amount, the timing of provision, and the triggers for calling on it. The residual question, which is the one that should concern a student of this field, is what happens when the security proves insufficient. The answer, with uncomfortable frequency, is that the state and the public bear the loss. That is a failure of legal design, and it is one your generation will have to remedy.

### 13. Restitution Refused: The Limits of Undoing a Sovereign Act `case`

*British Petroleum Exploration Company (Libya) Ltd v. Government of the Libyan Arab Republic, Award on the Merits, 10 October 1973 (sole arbitrator, Judge Gunnar Lagergren)*

Following Libya's nationalisation of BP's concession interests in December 1971, the sole arbitrator considered both liability and remedy. He held the taking to be unlawful, reasoning in particular that the measure was directed at the claimant specifically rather than forming part of a general programme of nationalisation, and was therefore not for a public purpose within the meaning of the concession and of international law. On remedy, however, he declined to order restitutio in integrum. He reasoned that the nationalisation had an irreversible character, that specific performance against a sovereign act was impracticable, and that damages were the appropriate remedy.

> **Holding / significance.** Unlawful termination and restitution are separate questions. A tribunal may find the taking wrongful and still hold that restoration of performance is not a remedy it should order.

**Narration**

Our case is British Petroleum against Libya, decided in 1973 by a sole arbitrator, Judge Gunnar Lagergren, and I have chosen it deliberately as a companion to the Texaco award we examined in Lecture Two, because the two decisions reach opposite conclusions on the same question and both were decided within four years of each other. The facts are similar in outline. Libya nationalised BP's concession interests in December 1971. On liability, Lagergren held the taking unlawful. His reasoning turned on a finding that the measure was directed at BP specifically, rather than forming part of a general programme of nationalisation affecting the industry as a whole, and that it was therefore not for a public purpose as the concession and international law required. On remedy, however, he reached a conclusion very different from the one Dupuy would reach in Texaco. He declined to order restitutio in integrum. His reasoning was that a nationalisation, once effected by a sovereign, has an irreversible character; that ordering a state to restore an investor to operational control of a nationalised industry is impracticable as well as undesirable; and that damages are therefore the appropriate remedy for a wrongful taking of this kind. Now, I want you to hold that against Texaco, where a different sole arbitrator ordered restoration of performance in substantially similar circumstances. What does the divergence show? Three things. First, that in this field the remedy question is frequently more consequential than the liability question, and the two are genuinely separate decisions requiring separate reasoning. Second, that a sole arbitrator's view of the proper limits of the office can determine an outcome worth hundreds of millions of dollars. And third, that the scepticism about investor-state arbitration which has grown so sharply in recent years has its origins not in the modern treaty cases but here, in the divergent and largely unreviewable remedial choices of the classical awards.

### 14. Assignment, Change of Control, and the Movement of Liability `bullets`

- Petroleum interests change hands constantly: through farm-ins, asset sales, corporate acquisitions and internal reorganisations.
- Assignment clauses require state consent, and states use that consent as a lever to reopen fiscal and operational terms.
- Change of control provisions attempt to capture indirect transfers, and are among the most litigated clauses in the industry.
- Decommissioning liability is the principal reason transactions now include security for past obligations as well as future ones.

*On-screen note:* A state that cannot prevent the transfer can still ensure that the liability does not travel with it.

**Narration**

One further element of petroleum practice deserves attention because it generates a great deal of work and a surprising amount of litigation, and that is the transfer of interests. Petroleum interests change hands constantly: through farm-in arrangements under which one company earns an interest by funding another's work, through direct asset sales, through corporate acquisitions at a level above the licence holder, and through internal reorganisations within a group. Now, the state's interest in all of this is considerable, because the entity with which it contracted may be replaced by one with less technical competence, less financial capacity, or a different nationality. The legal response has two principal parts. First, assignment clauses require the consent of the state or the minister before a licence interest may be transferred, and states frequently use that consent as a lever, extracting revised fiscal terms, additional work commitments, or the resolution of outstanding disputes as the price of approval. Second, change of control provisions attempt to capture transfers effected at a level above the licence holder, so that a company cannot achieve indirectly what it could not do directly. Those provisions are among the most heavily litigated in the industry, because they turn on what constitutes control, on whether an internal reorganisation is a transfer at all, and on whether consent was unreasonably withheld. And there is a third element which has become dominant in recent years. Because decommissioning liability survives the asset, and because the industry has seen the sale of mature assets to entities with limited balance sheets, transactions now routinely include security for obligations already accrued, not merely for those to be incurred. A state that cannot prevent a transfer can at least ensure that the liability does not quietly travel with it.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Four contract forms dominate: concession, production sharing, service and joint venture, and most instruments combine them.
- Four questions cut across every form: title, control, risk and reward.
- A production sharing contract runs from exploration and relinquishment through cost recovery, profit split and abandonment.
- Unitisation converts a race to drain a shared reservoir into a coordinated development with an apportionment formula.
- Liability and remedy are separate decisions: a tribunal may find a taking unlawful and still refuse restitution.

> **The one thing to remember.** Petroleum law is not learned from doctrine but from documents. Read the clauses in the order they operate, and the doctrine will explain itself.

**Narration**

Let me close. Four contract forms dominate petroleum practice, and most modern instruments combine them. Four questions cut across all of them: who has title, who has control, who bears risk, and how reward is divided. A production sharing contract is a machine with six moving parts, running from exploration and mandatory relinquishment, through discovery and development, into cost recovery and a profit split that varies with the project's success, and finally into decommissioning and abandonment. Where the reservoir crosses a jurisdictional line, unitisation converts a race to drain into a coordinated development, at the cost of a permanent technical argument about apportionment. And in BP against Libya we saw that liability and remedy are separate decisions, so that a tribunal may hold a taking unlawful and still refuse to order restitution, in direct contrast to the Texaco award. In the next lecture we stay with hydrocarbons but move to gas, and examine a commodity whose legal problems are quite different, because unlike oil, gas must usually reach its market through a pipe.
