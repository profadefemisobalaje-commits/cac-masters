## Lecture Script

### 01. Electricity, Power Pools and Cross-Border Trade `bumper`

### 02. Electricity, Regional Power Pools and Cross-Border Energy Trade `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Eight. We now move from molecules to electrons, and in doing so we enter the part of this field where the physical characteristics of the commodity most directly determine its legal treatment. Electricity is unlike oil and unlike gas in one respect that governs everything else: it cannot, at scale, be stored. It must be generated at the very instant it is consumed, and the quantity consumed must match the quantity generated within very tight tolerances, or the frequency of the entire system departs from its nominal value and equipment begins to fail. That single physical constraint is the reason electricity is regulated more intensively than any other energy commodity, the reason a system operator exists, the reason market rules are minute and technical, and the reason cross-border trade in electricity is a legal problem of quite exceptional difficulty. Let me show you how the physics becomes law.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`

### 04. The Physics Becomes Law `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the physics, because the law follows from it in a way that is unusually direct.

### 05. Three Facts, and Three Institutions That Follow From Them `bullets`

- Supply must equal demand instantaneously. Therefore: a system operator with authority to instruct plant.
- Electricity flows according to the impedance of the network, not according to any contract. Therefore: grid codes and congestion management.
- The network is a natural monopoly requiring coordinated investment. Therefore: regulated transmission tariffs and planning obligations.

*On-screen note:* In electricity, the physics is not background. It is the reason the institutions exist.

**Narration**

Three physical facts, and three institutions. Let me take them in turn. The first is that supply must equal demand instantaneously, on a second-by-second basis, across an entire synchronous area. The legal consequence is the existence of the system operator, an entity with the authority to instruct generators to increase or decrease output, to procure reserve and frequency response, and in the last resort to disconnect load. That authority is not contractual in origin; it is regulatory, and it is exercised under licence and under a grid code rather than under any agreement between the parties. The second fact is that electricity flows through a network according to its physical impedance and not according to any contract, commercial arrangement or political wish. A generator in one country selling to a customer in another will in fact cause flows across every parallel path in the intervening network, including through systems that are not party to the transaction at all. The legal consequence is the grid code, which sets the technical rules of connection and operation, and the whole apparatus of congestion management, which allocates scarce transmission capacity when the physical network cannot accommodate the commercial trades that have been agreed. The third fact is that the network is a natural monopoly whose development must be coordinated, because an investment decision in one jurisdiction affects the adequacy of the network in its neighbours. The legal consequence is regulated transmission tariffs, which recover the cost of the network on a permitted revenue basis, and transmission planning obligations that require anticipatory investment. Notice what is absent from all of this. There is no warehouse, no cargo, no vessel and no storage contract. There is a continuously balanced physical system, and the law exists to keep it balanced.

### 06. The Regulatory Anatomy of a Liberalised Power Sector `diagram`

- Generation: licensed, and in liberalised markets usually competitive. Governed by licences, connection agreements and, historically, power purchase agreements.
- Transmission: a regulated monopoly. Governed by a transmission licence, a grid code, a connection agreement and an approved tariff methodology.
- System operation: real-time balancing and security of supply. Governed by the system operator's licence, the balancing and settlement code, and market rules.
- Distribution and retail supply: local monopoly network, competitive supply function. Governed by distribution licences, supply licences and consumer protection rules.

**Narration**

Let me now set out the regulatory anatomy of a liberalised power sector, because the vocabulary is used constantly and often imprecisely. Generation is the production of electricity, and in a liberalised market it is treated as competitive, although in practice it is frequently anything but. It is governed by generation licences, by connection agreements with the network, and, in many developing markets, by long-term power purchase agreements with a state utility, which remain the instrument on which project finance depends. Transmission is the high-voltage network, and it is a regulated monopoly: it is governed by a transmission licence, by a grid code setting the technical rules, by connection agreements, and by an approved tariff methodology that determines how the owner recovers its costs. System operation is the real-time function of keeping the system balanced and secure, and it is governed by the system operator's licence, by the balancing and settlement code which determines how deviations are priced, and by market rules which govern dispatch. Distribution is the local network, again a regulated monopoly, and retail supply is the competitive function of selling to customers, governed by supply licences and by consumer protection regulation. Now, the crucial point, and this is where students most often go wrong: the legal instruments differ at each level because the market structure differs. Generation is governed largely by contract, because it is competitive. Transmission and distribution are governed largely by regulation, because they are monopolies. And system operation is governed by codes, because it must be coordinated in real time. If you know which level you are at, you know which kind of law applies.

### 07. The Power Purchase Agreement: The Document That Finances Everything `bullets`

- A generating plant with no long-term offtake contract is generally unfinanceable on a project finance basis.
- Capacity payments and energy payments allocate volume risk and price risk differently between offtaker and generator.
- The sovereign or utility counterparty's credit is the central risk, and security packages are built around it.
- Termination provisions, change in law, force majeure and political force majeure determine who absorbs a shock.

*On-screen note:* The commercial contract does the work that the treaty cannot. Read it before you read anything else.

**Narration**

Before we turn to regional integration I must deal with the instrument on which the entire independent power sector rests, and that is the power purchase agreement. A generating plant is a capital-intensive asset with no alternative use, and a lender asked to finance it on a non-recourse basis will look first and last at whether there is a credible long-term commitment to buy the output. Without one, the project is generally unfinanceable, which is why the power purchase agreement, and not the licence or the treaty, is the document that determines whether the plant gets built. Let me identify the elements that matter. First, the structure of payment. A capacity payment remunerates the availability of the plant, whether or not it is dispatched, and thereby transfers volume risk to the offtaker. An energy payment remunerates the electricity actually delivered, and thereby leaves volume risk with the generator. Most agreements combine the two, and the balance between them is the central commercial negotiation. Second, the credit of the counterparty. In most developing markets the offtaker is a state-owned utility, and the central risk in the transaction is therefore not regulatory but sovereign. The security package is built around that risk: through government guarantees, through letters of credit, through escrow arrangements over receivables, through tariff stabilization provisions, and in some structures through partial risk guarantees from multilateral institutions. Third, the allocation of shocks. The change-in-law clause determines who bears the cost of new regulation. The force majeure clause and, in some instruments, a distinct political force majeure provision determine what happens when performance is prevented by events beyond the parties' control. And the termination provisions determine what the generator receives if the agreement is terminated for the offtaker's default, which is the number on which the lender's recovery ultimately depends. The general lesson is worth stating plainly. In the independent power sector, the commercial contract does the work that public law cannot.

### 08. Regional Integration `section`

**Narration**

This brings us to regional integration, which is the most distinctive institutional feature of the electricity sector.

### 09. Coordination Without Supranationality `bullets`

- A power pool coordinates the operation and development of national systems across several states.
- The instruments are intergovernmental memoranda of understanding, inter-utility agreements, and harmonised market rules and grid codes.
- The institutions are typically a secretariat, a coordination centre, and committees of regulators and utilities, with limited compulsory powers.
- The hard problems are payment risk, the enforceability of regional rules within national law, and the asymmetry of system sizes.

*On-screen note:* Almost every power pool can coordinate. Almost none can compel.

**Narration**

A regional power pool is an arrangement by which several states coordinate the operation and development of their electricity systems, and there are now such arrangements in most regions of the world: in West Africa, in Southern Africa, in East Africa, in Central America, in Southeast Asia, in the Gulf, in Central and South America, and of course the internal electricity market of the European Union, which is the most deeply integrated of them all. The instruments by which they operate are a familiar stack. There is an intergovernmental memorandum of understanding or treaty, which establishes the pool and sets out its objectives. There are inter-utility agreements, which govern the commercial relationships between the national utilities. There are harmonised grid codes and market rules, which are the technical substrate of integration and which require each member to amend its own national rules. And there are the institutions themselves: a secretariat, a coordination centre, committees of regulators and of utilities. Now, I want you to notice the characteristic limitation. Almost every power pool can coordinate, and almost none can compel. Decisions are typically taken by consensus, there is no supranational regulator with the power to bind a member state, and the enforcement of regional rules depends on their incorporation into national law. That produces three persistent problems. The first is payment risk: a utility in one country selling into a pool must be paid, and if the counterparty is a state-owned utility with weak finances, the mechanisms for enforcing payment across borders are frequently inadequate. The second is the enforceability of regional rules in national law, which is where the absence of supranational authority bites hardest. And the third is asymmetry: where one system in a pool is far larger than all the others, integration can look to the smaller members like absorption.

### 10. Bilateral Coordination and Competitive Regional Markets `two-col`

**COORDINATION MODEL**
- Members trade principally through long-term bilateral contracts between utilities.
- The pool coordinates planning, reliability standards and information exchange.
- Spare capacity is shared on an opportunity basis through short-term energy markets.
- Lower institutional demand: does not require full regulatory harmonisation.
- Common in regions where several members face capacity shortages simultaneously.

**COMPETITIVE MARKET MODEL**
- A regional day-ahead market matches bids and offers across borders.
- Transmission capacity is allocated explicitly, by auction or implicitly through market coupling.
- Requires harmonised market rules, grid codes, settlement and a common trading platform.
- Higher institutional demand, and a genuine transfer of authority from national regulators.
- Approached most fully in the European internal electricity market.

**Narration**

Regional electricity integration comes in two principal models, and the choice between them is a choice about how much authority states are willing to transfer. Under the coordination model, members trade principally through long-term bilateral contracts between utilities. The pool coordinates planning, agrees common reliability standards, exchanges information, and operates a short-term energy market through which members with spare capacity sell to members in deficit. Its great advantage is institutional modesty: it does not require the full harmonisation of market rules, and it can function while national regulatory regimes remain quite different. Its limitation is that it does not produce efficient prices, and it does not, in itself, attract the investment that a deep market would. This model has been characteristic of several pools in the developing world, and it is a rational response to a situation in which almost every member faces shortage. Under the competitive market model, there is a regional day-ahead market in which bids and offers from all participating systems are matched, and transmission capacity across borders is allocated either explicitly by auction or implicitly through market coupling, under which the allocation of cross-border capacity and the matching of bids happen simultaneously in a single algorithm. This model produces efficient prices and sends the right signals for investment, but it demands an enormous institutional apparatus: harmonised market rules, harmonised grid codes, common settlement arrangements, a shared trading platform, and, above all, a willingness on the part of national regulators to be bound by decisions taken at regional level. It has been approached most fully in the European Union, and even there the process took decades. When you are asked to advise on regional integration, the question to ask first is not which model is better. It is how much authority the member states will actually concede.

### 11. Interconnectors, Capacity Allocation and Market Coupling `bullets`

- An interconnector is a physical asset, usually jointly owned, governed by an interconnection agreement and two sets of national rules.
- Capacity allocation determines who may use the scarce cross-border capability: by explicit auction, or implicitly through coupling.
- Congestion rent arises whenever the price difference across a constrained border exceeds zero, and someone must receive it.
- Market coupling simultaneously clears energy and allocates capacity, and is the most efficient mechanism yet devised for this problem.

*On-screen note:* Whoever receives the congestion rent has an incentive to keep the border constrained. Design accordingly.

**Narration**

Let me take you one level deeper into the mechanics, because this is the technical heart of cross-border electricity trade and it is where the legal design problems are most acute. An interconnector is a physical asset, usually a high-voltage cable or a back-to-back converter station, frequently jointly owned by transmission companies in two countries. It is governed by an interconnection agreement, by two sets of national rules, and by whatever regional arrangements apply. Now, the cross-border capacity of that interconnector is scarce and frequently insufficient to accommodate all the trades the market would like to make. So a mechanism is needed to decide who may use it. Under explicit allocation, capacity is auctioned separately from energy: a trader buys the right to use the interconnector, and separately buys and sells the energy. Under implicit allocation, or market coupling, the two decisions are made together: a single algorithm simultaneously matches bids and offers across the whole coupled region and allocates cross-border capacity to the trades that value it most. Implicit allocation is more efficient, and it is now the standard approach wherever the institutional conditions permit. And then there is the question of congestion rent, which is one of the most instructive problems in the field. Whenever the wholesale price on one side of a constrained interconnector exceeds the price on the other, a rent arises from the price difference on the power that flows. Someone must receive that rent. If it accrues to the owner of the interconnector, that owner has a commercial incentive to keep the border constrained, which is perverse. Sound design therefore requires the rent to be applied to reducing network charges, or to be reinvested in additional interconnection, or otherwise neutralised, and the rules governing this are among the most consequential in regional market design.

### 12. The Payment Problem in Regional Power Trade `bullets`

- The characteristic failure of regional power trade is not technical or legal. It is non-payment.
- A state-owned utility facing political pressure to hold tariffs below cost cannot pay its regional suppliers in full.
- The consequences cascade: arrears, curtailment of cross-border flows, withdrawal of capacity, and the collapse of the market.
- The instruments of mitigation include payment security, escrow, partial risk guarantees, and regional clearing arrangements with consequences for default.

*On-screen note:* A regional market is only as strong as the weakest utility's willingness to pay its invoices.

**Narration**

I want to name the single most common cause of failure in regional power trade, because it is not the failure that lawyers are trained to anticipate, and because it explains why several pools have underperformed their promise. It is not a defect in the grid code. It is not an unresolved boundary dispute. It is non-payment. The mechanism is depressingly consistent. A state-owned utility buys power from a generator or a trader in a neighbouring country under a regional arrangement. At home, it is required by its government to hold retail tariffs below the cost of supply, or it suffers high technical and commercial losses, or its own receivables from domestic customers are uncollected. It therefore cannot pay its regional suppliers in full. Arrears accumulate. The supplier, facing its own obligations, curtails or withdraws capacity from the regional market. The market thins, liquidity falls, and the arrangements that took a decade to build begin to unwind. The reason this is a legal problem, and not merely a commercial one, is that the remedies are inadequate. Suing a state-owned utility in its own courts is slow and frequently ineffective. Suing across borders is slower. And the regional institution, as we established earlier, cannot compel payment. The instruments of mitigation are therefore structural rather than remedial. There is payment security, in the form of guarantees or letters of credit provided in advance. There is escrow, under which receivables are held and applied according to a waterfall. There are partial risk guarantees from multilateral and bilateral institutions, which cover defined sovereign risks and are the single most effective instrument yet devised for this problem. And there are regional clearing and settlement arrangements, which is the most important of all, provided that they carry real consequences for a member that defaults. A regional market, in the end, is only as strong as the weakest utility's willingness to pay its invoices, and the lawyer's contribution is to build a structure in which that willingness is not the only thing standing between the market and collapse.

### 13. The Right to Regulate Prevails `case`

*AES Summit Generation Limited and AES-Tisza Erőmű Kft v. Republic of Hungary (ICSID Case No. ARB/07/22, Award of 23 September 2010)*

The claimants invested in Hungarian power generation following the liberalisation of the sector and the privatisation of assets previously held by the state utility. Hungary subsequently reintroduced administrative pricing for electricity sold under long-term power purchase agreements, acting in reliance on a decision of the national competition authority, and later terminated those agreements in the context of European Union liberalisation. The claimants alleged breach of fair and equitable treatment and unlawful expropriation under the Energy Charter Treaty. The tribunal dismissed the claims, finding that the adoption of a regulatory regime, even one that reduces an investor's profitability, is a legitimate exercise of state authority where it is not arbitrary, discriminatory or in bad faith.

> **Holding / significance.** Not every adverse regulatory change breaches a treaty standard. Where a state acts through its institutions, for a public purpose, and without discrimination, the fact that the investor's expectations are disappointed is not sufficient to establish liability.

**Narration**

Our case for this lecture is AES Summit Generation against Hungary, decided in 2010 under the Energy Charter Treaty, and I have chosen it because it states, more clearly than most, the boundary between compensable and non-compensable regulation. The background is the liberalisation and privatisation of the Hungarian power sector. Investors acquired generation assets, and the commercial arrangements on which those acquisitions were financed included long-term power purchase agreements. Hungary subsequently reintroduced administrative pricing for electricity sold under those agreements, acting on the basis of a decision of its competition authority, and later terminated the agreements altogether in the context of its accession to the European Union and the consequent liberalisation of the market. The claimants alleged breach of fair and equitable treatment and unlawful expropriation. The tribunal dismissed the claim. Its reasoning is the part I want you to study. It held that the adoption of a regulatory regime, even one that substantially reduces an investor's profitability, is a legitimate exercise of state authority where the state acts through its institutions, for a public purpose, without discrimination and in good faith. It held further that an investor's expectation that the regulatory framework would remain unchanged is not, by itself, a legitimate expectation protected by the treaty, because a state is entitled and indeed obliged to adapt its regulation to changing circumstances. Now, I want to draw out three points. First, this decision is the clearest judicial statement of the right to regulate in the energy sector, and it has been relied on repeatedly since. Second, it establishes that the investor's remedy for regulatory risk is, in the first instance, the contract: if a change of law is to be compensated, the place to provide for it is the change-in-law clause. And third, it demonstrates why the drafting of stabilisation and change-in-law provisions is the single most valuable skill an energy lawyer can offer a client.

### 14. Capacity, Storage and Demand Response `bullets`

- A system with a high share of variable generation must secure adequacy by a different route: not energy alone but firm capacity.
- Capacity mechanisms pay for availability, through auctions, strategic reserves or reliability options, and each has legal difficulties.
- Storage converts a timing problem into a tradable service, but its legal classification is unsettled: is it generation, network, or both?
- Demand response pays consumers to reduce consumption, and requires aggregation rules, baselines and measurement standards.

*On-screen note:* The variable-generation system is not regulated on the same principles as the dispatchable one. Learn the new ones.

**Narration**

One further development is transforming electricity law, and it is worth a lecture of its own, so let me at least give you its outline. A system dominated by dispatchable generation was kept adequate by ensuring that there was enough plant to meet peak demand, and that plant was paid for the energy it produced. A system with a high share of wind and solar cannot be regulated that way, because the output is variable and, in the case of solar, absent precisely when demand peaks on some systems. The system must therefore secure adequacy by a different route, and the instruments are three. The first is the capacity mechanism, which pays for availability rather than for energy. The forms are a capacity auction, under which providers bid to commit capacity for a future delivery year; a strategic reserve, held outside the market and dispatched only in scarcity; and a reliability option, under which the provider must deliver in scarcity or pay the difference. Each of these has legal difficulties: capacity mechanisms must be designed so as not to distort the energy market, and they must be compatible with competition and state aid rules where those apply. The second instrument is storage, which converts a timing problem into a tradable service. But storage has an unsettled legal classification: it consumes when charging, produces when discharging, and provides network services in both states, so the law must decide whether it is generation, network, or a distinct category, and that classification determines its licensing, its charges and its tax treatment. The third instrument is demand response, under which consumers are paid to reduce consumption at times of system stress. That requires aggregation rules permitting a third party to bid a portfolio of small consumers into the market, together with baselines and measurement standards to establish what the consumer would otherwise have consumed. All three instruments are, in substance, attempts to buy flexibility, and the lawyer's skill in this area lies in defining precisely what is being bought and how it is to be measured.

**Narration**

Let me bring this down to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want four things. ### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- Electricity cannot be stored at scale, must be balanced instantaneously, and follows physical paths rather than contractual ones.
- Those facts produce the system operator, the grid code, congestion management and regulated network monopolies.
- Power pools coordinate across states, usually without supranational authority, and enforcement depends on national incorporation.
- Cross-border trade requires capacity allocation; market coupling is the most efficient mechanism yet devised.
- Adverse regulation is not compensable merely because it disappoints an investor; the remedy belongs in the contract.

> **The one thing to remember.** In electricity more than anywhere else, the law is downstream of the physics. Learn what the network must do, and the regulation becomes legible.

**Narration**

Let me close. Electricity cannot be stored at scale, must be balanced instantaneously, and flows according to the physics of the network rather than according to any contract. Those three facts produce, respectively, the system operator with authority to instruct plant, the grid code and the apparatus of congestion management, and the regulated network monopoly with its tariff methodology and planning obligations. Regional power pools coordinate across states, but almost always without supranational authority, so that enforcement of regional rules depends on their incorporation into national law and the three persistent problems are payment risk, enforceability and asymmetry. Cross-border trade requires a mechanism for allocating scarce capacity, and market coupling, which clears energy and allocates capacity simultaneously, is the most efficient mechanism yet devised for the problem. And in AES against Hungary we saw the clearest statement of the boundary: adverse regulation is not compensable merely because it disappoints an investor, and the place to provide for regulatory risk is the change-in-law clause.
