## Lecture Script

### 01. Renewables and the Energy Transition `bumper`

### 02. Renewable Energy and the International Law of the Energy Transition `title`

**Narration**

Welcome to Lecture Twelve, and to the third module of this series, in which we examine the themes that cut across every commodity regime. I want to begin by correcting a mental picture that most students bring to this subject. The energy transition is commonly imagined as a substitution: replace a coal plant with a wind farm, replace a petrol car with an electric one, and the problem is solved. That picture is wrong in ways that matter legally. Wind and solar are variable, so the system must be balanced differently. They are capital-intensive and have almost no running cost, so the market must be designed differently. They are diffuse rather than concentrated, so the geography of production, and therefore the geography of dependence, changes. And they are material-intensive, so the dependency on fuel is replaced by a dependency on minerals and on manufacturing capacity. What is being built is not the same system with a different fuel. It is a different system. Let me show you the legal questions it raises.

### 03. What This Lecture Establishes `objectives`


**Narration**

Six outcomes. First, to explain why the transition is a restructuring of the energy system rather than a substitution of fuel, and to connect each structural change to a legal consequence. Second, to describe the legal design of renewable support schemes, and their evolution from feed-in tariffs through quota systems to competitive auctions and contracts for difference. Third, to analyse the legal problems of grid integration, including connection queues, curtailment and constraint, which are now among the most commercially significant issues in the sector. Fourth, to explain the legal architecture of renewable electricity tracking, guarantees of origin and corporate power purchase agreements, because that is where a great deal of the world's renewable capacity is now contracted. Fifth, to assess the emerging legal framework for hydrogen as a new energy carrier, including classification, certification and safety. And sixth, to evaluate the international law of technology transfer, climate finance and just transition for developing states, which is where the equity questions in this field are most acute.

### 04. Supporting the Transition `section`

**Narration**

Let me begin with the instruments through which renewable generation has been brought into existence.

### 05. From Feed-In Tariffs to Contracts for Difference `bullets`

- Feed-in tariffs: a guaranteed price per kilowatt hour for a defined period, with a grid connection obligation.
- Quota or certificate schemes: an obligation on suppliers, with tradable certificates evidencing compliance.
- Competitive auctions: capacity awarded by price competition, with the winner receiving a contract at its bid price.
- Contracts for difference: the generator sells into the market and receives or pays the difference against a strike price.

*On-screen note:* Each generation of scheme solved the previous one's problem and created a new one. That pattern is worth noticing.

**Narration**

Renewable generation was brought into existence by law rather than by market forces, and the evolution of the instruments used is one of the most instructive policy sequences in the whole field. The first generation was the feed-in tariff: a legally guaranteed price, per unit of electricity generated, payable for a defined period, usually coupled with an obligation on the network to connect and to purchase. It worked, in the sense that it produced deployment at scale, and it worked because it removed both price risk and volume risk from the investor. Its weaknesses were equally clear: it gave the investor no incentive to generate when the system needed electricity rather than when the wind happened to blow, and it allocated the cost of the subsidy to consumers without any mechanism for controlling the total. The second generation was the quota or certificate scheme, under which the obligation is placed on suppliers to source a defined proportion of their supply from renewable sources, and compliance is evidenced by tradable certificates. That introduced competition among generators and controlled the volume, but it exposed investors to certificate price volatility, which raised the cost of capital. The third generation is the competitive auction, under which a defined volume of capacity is awarded by price competition, and the successful bidder receives a contract at its own bid price. That has driven costs down dramatically and is now the dominant instrument worldwide. And the fourth generation, in markets with a wholesale price, is the contract for difference, under which the generator sells its output into the market and then receives, or pays, the difference between the market price and an agreed strike price. That retains market discipline over dispatch while providing revenue certainty. The pattern is worth noticing. Each generation of instrument solved the central weakness of its predecessor and created a new one, and the new one has always been about the allocation of risk.

### 06. Connection, Curtailment, and Who Pays for the Wait `two-col`

**CONNECTION**
- The queue is the problem: projects wait years for a connection date, and the queue is frequently not ordered by readiness.
- Legal responses include connection offers with enforceable dates, queue management reform, and refundable deposits.
- Shallow and deep connection charges allocate the cost of network reinforcement between generator and network.
- Access regimes must decide whether connection confers a firm right to export or a conditional one.

**CURTAILMENT**
- Constraint arises where the network cannot carry the power generated, and the system operator must instruct a reduction.
- Curtailment without compensation transfers network inadequacy onto the generator and raises the cost of capital.
- Compensation mechanisms range from full compensation to a de minimis allowance before compensation begins.
- The efficient answer is often to pay for flexibility rather than for constraint.

**Narration**

The most commercially significant legal problems in the renewable sector are now rarely about the support scheme. They are about the grid, and there are two of them. The first is connection. In most jurisdictions with rapid renewable deployment, the binding constraint is not capital or turbines but the queue: projects wait years for a connection date, the queue frequently is not ordered by project readiness, and a substantial proportion of the projects in it will never be built. The legal responses include connection offers with enforceable dates and remedies for late delivery, queue management reform that prioritises projects which are genuinely progressed, deposits that are refundable on completion and forfeited on abandonment, and in some jurisdictions the introduction of application fees substantial enough to deter speculative entry. Alongside this sits the question of connection charges: whether the generator pays only for the shallow connection to the nearest point of suitable capacity, or also for the deeper reinforcement of the network that its export requires. The second problem is curtailment. Where the network cannot carry the power being generated, the system operator must instruct some generators to reduce output. If that instruction comes without compensation, the cost of network inadequacy falls on the generator, whose revenue is reduced for reasons entirely outside its control, and the cost of capital for every project rises accordingly. Compensation mechanisms range from full compensation for instructed reductions to a de minimis allowance below which no compensation is paid. The efficient answer, increasingly recognised, is to pay for flexibility rather than for constraint: to procure storage, demand response and flexible connections, which cost less than either reinforcing the network or paying generators not to generate.

### 07. Guarantees of Origin and the Corporate PPA `bullets`

- A guarantee of origin is a certificate evidencing that a defined quantity of electricity was generated from renewable sources.
- It is separable from the energy: the attributes can be traded independently of the physical electricity.
- Corporate power purchase agreements allow a commercial buyer to contract directly with a generator, commonly for additionality and price certainty.
- The legal questions concern double counting, the treatment of attributes in target accounting, and what happens on default.

*On-screen note:* The certificate and the electron are separate legal objects. Most disputes in this area arise from forgetting that.

**Narration**

There is an instrument that now underpins a very large share of corporate renewable procurement and that students consistently misunderstand, which is the guarantee of origin. A guarantee of origin is a certificate, issued by a designated body, evidencing that a defined quantity of electricity was generated from renewable sources, and specifying the source, the installation, the period and the date of commissioning. The critical point, and the one that resolves most confusion, is that the certificate and the electricity are separate legal objects. The attributes of the generation, which is to say the claim that it was renewable, can be traded independently of the physical electricity, which is indistinguishable in the network from any other electron. That separability is what makes the market work and what makes it contentious: a consumer in one country may purchase attributes from a generator in another, and thereby claim to consume renewable electricity that never physically reached it. The instrument through which much of this activity is contracted is the corporate power purchase agreement, under which a commercial buyer contracts directly with a generator to purchase electricity, and usually the associated attributes, over a long term. Buyers enter these agreements for price certainty, for reputational reasons, and increasingly because their own reporting obligations require them to account for their emissions. The legal questions are precise and they are being litigated. There is the question of double counting, where two parties claim the same attributes. There is the treatment of attributes under emissions reporting standards, where the accounting rules determine whether a claim is credible. And there is the question of what happens on default, which is acute where the agreement is structured as a financial settlement against a reference price rather than as a physical supply, because the buyer's exposure is then to a long-dated price difference rather than to a commodity.

### 08. The Prosumer: When the Customer Has a Generator `bullets`

- Rooftop solar, batteries, electric vehicles and heat pumps turn a passive consumer into a participant in the system.
- The legal categories strain: is a household with storage a consumer, a generator, or a market participant?
- Connection, export limits, metering, settlement and network charges must all be re-specified for bidirectional flow.
- Aggregation is the mechanism that makes small resources useful, and it requires a legal framework to exist at all.

*On-screen note:* The system was designed for one-way flow. Almost every rule in it assumes that flow.

**Narration**

There is one further structural change that the law has been slow to absorb, and it is the movement of generation to the edge of the system. A household with rooftop solar and a battery is simultaneously a consumer and a generator, and it can be a source of flexibility to the network as well. The same is true, at larger scale, of a commercial building with storage, of a fleet of electric vehicles capable of managed charging, and of an industrial site with a heat pump and thermal storage. The legal categories strain under this. Is a household with storage a consumer, a generator, or a market participant? Does it require a generation licence? May it export, and on what terms? Who is liable for the safety and the power quality of an export? And how is it to be settled and charged, when the network was designed and is priced on the assumption that electricity flows in one direction? Each of those questions requires an answer, and the answers are being given now, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, in connection rules, in network charging methodologies, in metering and settlement codes, and in the definition of the licence exemptions that permit ordinary customers to generate and to store without becoming licensed suppliers. The mechanism that makes all of this useful is aggregation, under which a third party assembles a portfolio of small resources and offers it into the market or to the system operator as a single dispatchable resource. Aggregation requires a legal framework to exist at all, because without a defined right to aggregate, a defined baseline against which performance is measured, and a defined settlement path, the resource remains inaccessible. This is one of the clearest examples in the whole field of a legal framework creating value rather than merely regulating activity.

### 09. New Carriers and New Questions `section`

**Narration**

Let me now turn to the newest energy carrier in the system, and the legal questions it raises.

### 10. Hydrogen: A Carrier That Does Not Fit the Existing Categories `bullets`

- Hydrogen is an energy carrier rather than a primary source, so its climate value depends entirely on how it was produced.
- Classification is therefore the central legal problem: renewable, low-carbon, and fossil-derived hydrogen must be distinguished.
- Certification schemes, guarantees of origin and carbon intensity thresholds are the instruments being built to do this.
- Safety regulation, blending limits, transport and storage rules, and the repurposing of gas infrastructure all require adaptation.

*On-screen note:* The whole legal framework for hydrogen exists to answer one question: how was this molecule made?

**Narration**

I want to take you through hydrogen, because it is the newest significant subject in this field and because it illustrates something important about how energy law develops. Hydrogen is not a primary energy source. It is a carrier, produced by using energy to split water or to reform methane, and its climate value therefore depends entirely on the energy and the feedstock used to make it. That single fact produces the central legal problem of the subject, which is classification. Hydrogen produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity has a very low carbon footprint. Hydrogen produced by electrolysis using grid electricity may have a higher footprint than the gas it replaces. Hydrogen produced from natural gas with carbon capture occupies an intermediate position, and the answer depends on the methane leakage rate and on the capture efficiency, both of which are contested. Because the climate value is invisible in the product, the entire legal framework for hydrogen exists to answer one question: how was this molecule made? The instruments being built to answer it are a taxonomy distinguishing renewable hydrogen, low-carbon hydrogen and unabated fossil-derived hydrogen; certification schemes, frequently adapting the guarantee of origin architecture from electricity; carbon intensity thresholds that must be met for support or for counting against a target; and in some jurisdictions requirements that support be conditional on additionality, meaning that the renewable electricity used must be new capacity rather than existing supply. Beyond classification, the law must adapt in four further respects. Safety regulation, since hydrogen has different properties from natural gas and different materials requirements. Blending limits, which determine how much hydrogen may be injected into a gas network without endangering appliances or violating gas quality specifications. Transport and storage rules, including for ammonia as a hydrogen carrier. And the repurposing question we touched on in the gas lecture, since the most economical route to a hydrogen network may be the conversion of existing gas infrastructure, with all the regulatory questions that entails.

### 11. The Geography Problem: The Resource Is Not Where the Load Is `bullets`

- Wind and solar resources are frequently remote from centres of demand, which makes transmission the binding constraint.
- Transmission is the slowest element of the transition: lines take longer to consent and build than plant.
- Consenting is the hard legal problem, involving land rights, environmental assessment and the distribution of local disbenefit.
-  Anticipatory investment, community benefit and cross-border cost allocation are the principal adaptive instruments.

*On-screen note:* Generation capacity can be built in two years. Transmission takes a decade. Plan for the slower asset.

**Narration**

Let me identify what is now the binding physical constraint on the transition in most jurisdictions, because it is not generation and it is not capital. It is transmission. Wind resources and high-quality solar resources are frequently remote from the centres of demand: offshore, in upland and coastal areas, or in desert regions. The electricity must therefore be carried, sometimes over very long distances, and the network required to do that does not exist. Now, the difficulty is one of timing as much as of engineering. A utility-scale solar farm can be built in under two years. A high-voltage transmission line of the kind required to connect a remote resource typically takes a decade or more, and frequently longer where it is contested. That asymmetry means that generation queues behind network availability, and it means that any credible transition plan must lead with the network rather than with the plant. The hard legal problem within that is consenting. A transmission line requires land rights across a long corridor, an environmental assessment, often a public inquiry, and a decision that imposes concentrated local disbenefit for diffuse national benefit. Communities in the path of a line bear the visual, construction and property effects, while the beneficiaries are distant. The law responds in three ways, and you should know all three. There is anticipatory investment, under which the network is reinforced in advance of demand rather than in response to it, which requires a regulator willing to allowRecovery of the cost of assets that are not yet fully utilised. There is community benefit, whether through direct payments, through funds, or through participation arrangements, which converts some of the local disbenefit into local benefit. And there is cross-border cost allocation, where a line serves more than one system, which requires agreement between regulators on how the cost is to be shared. Each of these is a drafting problem as much as a policy one.

### 12. When a State Withdraws the Support It Gave `case`

*Eiser Infrastructure Limited and Energía Solar Luxembourg S.à r.l. v. Kingdom of Spain (ICSID Case No. ARB/13/36, Award of 4 May 2017)*

The claimants invested in concentrating solar power plants in Spain in reliance on a regulatory regime established by legislation, which provided a tariff for a defined period. Spain subsequently repealed that regime and replaced it with a new one based on a reasonable return on standard assets over the whole regulatory life of an installation, applied retroactively. The claimants alleged breach of fair and equitable treatment and expropriation under the Energy Charter Treaty. The tribunal found that Spain had breached the fair and equitable treatment standard, holding that the measures were abrupt, discontinuous and deprived the claimants of essentially the entire value of their investment. It rejected Spain's defence that it had been obliged to act by economic circumstances, and it dismissed the expropriation claim as absorbed by its finding on fair and equitable treatment.

> **Holding / significance.** Retroactive replacement of a support regime on which investment was made can breach the fair and equitable treatment standard, even where the state faces fiscal pressure. But the measure was not necessarily an expropriation.

**Narration**

Our case is Eiser against Spain, decided in 2017, and it is one of a series of claims arising from the reform of Spain's renewable support regime, a series that has produced some of the most consequential awards in the modern law of energy investment. The facts are as follows. Spain established a support regime by legislation, providing a tariff for renewable generation over a defined period. Investors, including the claimants, committed very substantial capital to concentrating solar plants in reliance on it. Spain then repealed that regime and replaced it with a new methodology based on a reasonable return on standard assets, calculated over the whole regulatory life of an installation, and applied it retroactively to plants already built. The tribunal found a breach of the fair and equitable treatment standard. Its reasoning turned on characterisation: the measures were, in its view, abrupt and discontinuous, they contradicted the specific assurances on which the investment had been made, and they deprived the claimants of essentially the entire value of their investment. It rejected Spain's defence that fiscal circumstances had left it no choice, reasoning that a state facing economic difficulty is not thereby released from its obligations to investors. And it dismissed the expropriation claim, treating it as absorbed by the finding on fair and equitable treatment. Now, I want you to take three things from this. First, that the protection for an investor in a regulated sector lies substantially in the stability of the specific regime on which it relied, and not in any general expectation that regulation will never change. Second, that the characterisation of the measure, as abrupt, as retroactive, and as total in its effect, does more work in these cases than any doctrinal test. And third, and this is the uncomfortable lesson for governments, that a support scheme designed without an exit route creates a liability that the state will have to discharge one way or another. If you advise a government designing a renewable auction, build the adjustment mechanism into the instrument.

### 13. Equity in the Transition `section`

**Narration**

Let me close with the question that determines whether the transition is politically sustainable.

### 14. Technology, Finance and the Equity Question `bullets`

- Technology transfer is an express obligation under the climate regime and has been one of the least performed.
- Climate finance commitments have been reframed: the parties agreed a new collective quantified goal at COP29 in 2024.
- Just transition now appears in treaty text, in national legislation and in the governance of enterprises.
- The hardest cases are states whose fiscal capacity depends on revenues from the very fuels being displaced.

*On-screen note:* A transition perceived as imposed will be resisted. That is a legal problem as much as a political one.

**Narration**

Let me finish with the question that will ultimately determine whether the transition succeeds, and it is a question of equity rather than of engineering. Three elements. The first is technology transfer. The climate regime has from the outset contained an obligation on developed country parties to take all practicable steps to promote, facilitate and finance the transfer of, or access to, environmentally sound technologies to developing country parties. That obligation is express; it is also, by common consent, among the least performed in the entire treaty. The reasons are worth understanding: much of the relevant technology is privately held, so that a state obligation to facilitate transfer cannot be discharged simply by instructing a company; and the intellectual property regime, which we examined in the trade lecture, operates in significant tension with it. The second element is finance. The long-standing commitment by developed countries to mobilise a defined annual sum for climate action in developing countries was replaced at the twenty-ninth session of the Conference of the Parties in 2024 by a new collective quantified goal, with a substantially larger core figure and a broader contributor base, and that negotiation is itself an excellent case study in how these commitments are made and contested. The third element is the just transition, which now appears in treaty text, in national framework legislation, in the governance expectations applied to enterprises, and in the conditions attached to transition finance. Its content is contested but its core is stable: the costs of the transition should not fall on workers and communities whose livelihoods depend on the industries being displaced, and the benefits should not accrue only to those who were already advantaged. The hardest cases, which I want you to think about, are states whose entire fiscal capacity depends on revenue from the very fuels being displaced, and which have neither the capital nor the time to build an alternative. A transition perceived as imposed will be resisted, and that is a legal problem as much as a political one.

### 15. Why This Matters at Your Level `levels`

**Undergraduate level**

- Explain why curtailment compensation matters to the cost of capital.
- State what a guarantee of origin certifies, and why it is separable from the electricity.

**Postgraduate level**
- Assess whether Eiser and the wider Spanish saga correctly balance the right to regulate against investor expectations.
- Analyse whether contracts for difference allocate price risk efficiently, and at what point they become subsidies.
- Evaluate whether the new collective quantified goal is adequate, against a defensible measure of adequacy.
- Critique the additionality requirement for renewable hydrogen: does it delay deployment or ensure integrity?

**Narration**

Let me bring this to your programme. At undergraduate level, I want four things. First, the four generations of support scheme, with the specific weakness each was designed to address: feed-in tariffs, quota and certificate schemes, auctions, and contracts for difference. Second, an explanation of why compensation for curtailment matters to the cost of capital, since that is the argument that wins the policy case. Third, a precise statement of what a guarantee of origin certifies and why it is legally separable from the electricity, because most errors in this area come from conflating the two. And fourth, one sentence on why classification is the central legal problem for hydrogen, and that sentence should be about the invisibility of production method in the product. At postgraduate level, I want evaluation. Assess whether Eiser and the wider Spanish line of cases strike the right balance between the right to regulate and the protection of investor expectations, bearing in mind that the awards have been criticised by states and by commentators as an undue constraint on policy. Analyse whether contracts for difference allocate price risk efficiently, and identify the point at which a contract of that kind becomes a subsidy for the purposes of trade law. Evaluate whether the new collective quantified goal agreed in 2024 is adequate, and be explicit about the benchmark against which adequacy should be judged, because an assessment without a benchmark is merely an opinion. And critique the additionality requirement for renewable hydrogen, weighing the integrity it secures against the deployment it delays.

### 16. What We Have Established `summary`

- The transition restructures the system rather than substituting a fuel: variability, capital intensity and geography all change.
- Support schemes have evolved through four generations, each solving the previous scheme's weakness and creating a new one.
- Connection queues and curtailment are now the commercially decisive problems, and both are allocation questions.
- Hydrogen requires a new legal taxonomy, because its climate value is invisible in the product itself.
- Technology transfer, finance and just transition remain the least performed and most consequential obligations in the regime.

> **The one thing to remember.** Renewable energy law is not about supporting generation. It is about allocating the costs and risks of a system being rebuilt, and doing so in a way that remains politically durable.

**Narration**

Let me close. The transition restructures the system rather than substituting a fuel, and that restructuring changes the nature of variability, of capital intensity, of geography and of material dependence. Support schemes have evolved through four generations, each solving the central weakness of its predecessor and creating a new one, and the recurring theme is the allocation of risk. Connection queues and curtailment are now the commercially decisive problems in the sector, and both are, at bottom, questions about who bears the cost of network inadequacy. Hydrogen requires an entirely new legal taxonomy, because the climate value of the molecule is invisible in the product and must be established by certification. And technology transfer, finance and just transition remain the least performed and most consequential obligations in the climate regime, as the Eiser award and the Spanish saga illustrate from the investor's side. In the next lecture we take up the largest question in the field, and examine climate change law and the future of fossil fuels. Before we do, let me leave you with the observation that I think best summarises this lecture. Every instrument we have examined, from the feed-in tariff to the hydrogen certification scheme to the transmission consenting process, is a device for answering the same three questions. Who pays for the transition? Who bears the risk that it costs more or takes longer than planned? And who decides? Lawyers in this field spend their careers on the drafting that allocates those three things, and the allocation is rarely revisited once made. That is why the choices being taken now, in instruments most people will never read, will shape the distribution of wealth and opportunity for the remainder of the century.
