# The Constructing Child: Piaget from Rattle to Reversibility {.unit num="3" week="3" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Explain** Piaget's mechanism — schema, assimilation, accommodation, equilibration — and why it makes the child an active constructor rather than a recipient.
2. **Describe** the sensorimotor and preoperational achievements and limits: object permanence and the A-not-B error, symbolic and pretend play, egocentrism, centration, animism, conservation failure.
3. **Administer and interpret** two conservation tasks and one class-inclusion task, recording the child's reasoning rather than only the verdict.
4. **State** the modern critiques — competence versus performance, horizontal décalage, cultural and schooling effects, Siegler's overlapping waves — and what survives them.
5. **Use** the stage account in counselling: matching explanation, task and play to the child's current construction of the world.
:::

### Where this week sits

Piaget changed the question from "how much does the child know?" to "how does the child's knowing work?" His stages are now taught with their corrections attached, but the corrections only sharpen his central claim: children do not think less than adults, they think **differently**, coherently, by rules of their own. For the counsellor this is liberation: the child who says the sky is crying is not wrong, they are operating a system — and a counselling method that ignores the system will talk past the child all session.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Equilibration}
Piaget's engine: the child assimilates experience into existing schemas until the mismatch forces accommodation, and the cycle of the two — equilibration — is development itself. In the room: why a child's "wrong" answer is data about their current system, and why contradiction, gently held, is the teaching moment.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Object_permanence}
The discovery that things exist unseen, built across the sensorimotor years and visible in its failures: the A-not-B error, where the infant who found the toy at A reaches to A again after seeing it hidden at B — knowing and acting not yet reconciled. In the room: the infant separation game: out of sight is not yet fully out of existence, and by twelve months it very much is.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Centration_and_conservation}
The preoperational child centres on one salient dimension — the height of the glass — and therefore judges the poured water to be more; conservation is the discovery that quantity survives transformation, and it arrives dimension by dimension, not all at once. In the room: the reason a five-year-old's grief at the "smaller" cake is logic, not manipulation.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Horizontal_decalage}
The unevenness within a stage: a child reasons at one level with familiar material and a level below with strange material, conserving number before volume and volume before weight. In the room: the reason stage labels should be used as adjectives, never as nouns, about a child.
:::

### Lecture I — The staircase and what each step can hold

![Piaget's four stages as a staircase with ages and signature achievements: object permanence, symbol and pretend play, conservation and reversibility, hypothetical-deductive reasoning; with the caveat of horizontal décalage. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-03-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. The sensorimotor child knows by acting.** From reflex to intention, from circular reactions to the first mental representation: the infant who pulls a cloth to reach a rattle upon it has invented means–ends; the infant who searches for the hidden rattle has invented existence. Pretend play appears at the top of this stair — the child who feeds a doll is operating a symbol, and symbol is the currency of every later therapy hour.

**Proposition 2. The preoperational child represents but centres.** Language explodes, play becomes sociodramatic, and the world is organised by perception and by the child's own position: egocentrism is not selfishness but the genuine difficulty of computing another viewpoint (the three-mountains task), and centration makes quantity hostage to appearance. Animism — the sun follows me because it wants to — is the same system applied to causality. These are coherent rules, and a counsellor who knows them hears a four-year-old's guilt after a parent's death ("I was angry at her") as developmental logic requiring careful release, not as pathology.

**Proposition 3. Concrete operations are reversible operations.** Conservation, class inclusion, seriation: the child can now run a transformation backwards in mind and hold two dimensions at once — but tied to the tangible and the present. Formal operations add the hypothetical: the adolescent who reasons about justice, about the future, about who they might be — and who can therefore be counselled with hypothesis-testing (volume 1, week 6) in a way the eight-year-old cannot.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Match the method to the stair: with the preoperational child, play, draw and enact rather than talk about; externalise in objects (volume 1, week 11) because objects are their grammar; with the concrete-operational child, use tangible scales, cards and charts; reserve pure verbal insight work for formal operations, and even then check the stair under stress, because anxiety drops every child a step.
:::

### Lecture II — What the critiques changed, and what survived

**Competence versus performance.** Simplified tasks show abilities Piaget dated later: infants violate-of-expectation paradigms look like object knowledge in the first year; three-year-olds pass false-belief tasks in some cultures and versions years before the classic schedule (week 5). Piaget's stages are therefore best read as the ages at which competences become **reliable in standard tasks**, not the ages at which they appear.

**Horizontal décalage and overlapping waves.** Conservation arrives dimension by dimension; and Siegler showed that at any age a child holds several strategies at once, choosing among them by cost and success — development as changing mixtures, not replacing stages. The staircase survives as a map of dominant strategies, not as a lift with locked floors.

**Culture and schooling.** Conservation and class-inclusion performance shifts with schooling and with the practices of a community; some cultures' children pass certain tasks earlier and others later, and formal-operational reasoning is exercised where the culture exercises it. Piaget universalised the schoolchild; the correction is week 1's fourth question, applied.

**What survived.** Constructivism; the active child; the sequence of sensorimotor achievements; the qualitative difference of preoperational thought; the clinical interview as a method — Piaget's greatest gift to counselling, the habit of asking "how did you know?" and listening to the reasoning rather than scoring the answer.

::: safeguard
Stage knowledge cuts both ways in safeguarding: a child's concrete-operational logic means a nine-year-old may genuinely not generalise a safety rule to a new setting ("stranger" meant the man in the car, not the neighbour), so safety teaching must be rehearsed across settings rather than stated once; and a child's preoperational egocentrism means they may believe their anger caused a death or a divorce, so every bereavement and separation conversation with a child under seven includes the explicit, repeated release of that belief.
:::

### Lecture III — The clinical interview as method: listening for the child's rule

**Proposition 1. Piaget's greatest gift to counselling is not the stages but the interview.** The habit of asking "how did you know?" and treating the answer as data about a system rather than as right or wrong is directly transferable: the child who says "the sky is crying because it is sad" is stating a causal rule, and the rule is the door into their world.

**Proposition 2. The counter-suggestion tests the system, not the obedience.** Offering a gentle opposite ("but my friend says the tall glass has more") distinguishes a constructed judgement from a social guess; the child who holds their rule against a polite contradiction owns it, and the child who abandons it at the first breath of disagreement was answering the adult, not the question.

**Proposition 3. Emotional statements carry rules too.** "Nobody likes me" at six is often a centred judgement on one salient event, not a self-concept; the interview habit — "what made you say nobody? tell me the one who does" — locates the rule and its exception without arguing, and the exception is where the work begins.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** In every session with a child under nine, ask one "how did you know?" question and record the answer verbatim. Ten such answers are a portrait of a mind that no rating scale can draw.
:::

### Worked example 3.1 — Conservation with Ife, recorded properly

**Task.** Ife (3 years 8 months) is given two identical beakers of equal juice and agrees they are "the same". One is poured into a tall thin glass. Record and interpret.

**Solution.** Ife points to the tall glass: "that one plenty." Asked how she knows: "look! e long." The record must keep her words and her reason — centration on height, justification by perception — and must also record the counter-suggestion test: when the juice is poured back and she again says "same", the interpretation is stable preoperational centration, not confusion or compliance. Two cautions for the record: first, the question "which has more?" can be heard by a young child as "which did I make you change?", so a third-party puppet who "thinks they're the same" is introduced and Ife's disagreement with the puppet noted — the difference between a logical judgement and a social guess. Second, the verdict is not "Ife fails conservation"; it is "Ife, at 3:8, centres on height with familiar material, justifies by perception, and is stable across transformations" — a description that will be comparable with the same tasks at five and at seven.

### Worked example 3.2 — When the stage is the intervention

**Task.** Kunle (7) will not begin written work he might not finish perfectly; he tears the page. His teacher calls it pride. Design the counsellor's response using this week's theory.

**Solution.** Kunle is at the concrete-operational threshold: he can reverse operations in mind — which is exactly the trap, because he can now imagine the finished page and compare it with the present one, and the mismatch is intolerable. The intervention is staged, not verbal: (i) externalise the standard into an object — a "first-draft box" into which imperfect pages go by rule, making imperfection a location rather than a verdict; (ii) use reversibility as the medicine — show the torn page restored by tape and the page rewritten, and have Kunle order photographs of a page from bad to good to see that states are transformations, not identities; (iii) give the teacher the décalage sentence: Kunle's reasoning about numbers runs a step ahead of his reasoning about his own work, so praise his number logic openly and his drafts by rule, never by adjective. "Pride" dissolves into a developmental task: the discovery that a page, like water, remains a page through transformation.

::: skills
**Practical 3 — the clinical interview, Piaget's method.** In pairs with a supplied child (or recording): administer number conservation, liquid conservation and a class-inclusion question ("In this box there are eight roses and two daisies; are there more roses or more flowers?"). Record verbatim the child's justifications. Then write 250 words: the child's dominant strategy, the evidence of décalage if any, and one sentence of what you would teach next and why. The mark is for the recording of reasoning, not the verdicts.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A conservation demonstration log: your two recorded tasks with justifications, plus 500 words placing the child on the staircase with evidence, naming one critique that limits your inference and how you honoured it.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Tasks administered cleanly, third-party control used | 25% |
| Child's reasoning recorded verbatim and interpreted as strategy | 35% |
| Stage placement argued with décalage in view | 20% |
| Critique named and its limit honoured | 20% |

**Formative.** One paragraph: the preoperational sentence a child once said to you (or in this week's recording) that is logic, not error — and the rule it obeys.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Piaget's child constructs the world by acting on it, through schemas that assimilate until they must accommodate; the staircase from rattle to reversibility remains the best map of dominant strategies, read with décalage, culture and overlapping waves in view — and its clinical gift is the interview that listens for the child's reasoning, because counselling that ignores the child's system talks past the child.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. A child says the poured glass has more, then pours it back and says "same again". Is this inconsistency? What would consistency even mean in an overlapping-waves account?
2. Which of Piaget's claims would you defend most strongly today, and which would you retire? Give evidence for each choice.
3. Why does egocentrism make bereavement work with the under-sevens a matter of explicit sentence-making?
4. If formal operations are exercised only where a culture exercises them, what should a secondary curriculum owe every student?
5. Play is Piaget's symbol made visible. What does that make the playroom, epistemologically?
