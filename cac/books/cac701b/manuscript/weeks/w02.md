# Foundations Before Birth: Genes, Teratogens and Early Adversity {.unit num="2" week="2" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Outline** the germinal, embryonic and fetal periods and the principle that teratogenic risk is a function of timing, dose, genetic susceptibility and combination.
2. **Explain** probabilistic epigenesis: how experience regulates gene expression and why neither "in the genes" nor "in the upbringing" is a complete sentence.
3. **Describe** the developmental consequences of undernutrition, malaria in pregnancy, prematurity and early adversity, and the counsel each implies.
4. **Compute** corrected age for a child born preterm and use it to set fair expectations to at least two years.
5. **Counsel** without blame: hold a teratogen or adversity history with a mother without converting it into her guilt.
:::

### Where this week sits

Development begins before anyone meets the child. This week is the counsellor's version of embryology: not a memorised timetable but a set of principles about timing and susceptibility, an honest account of what genes do and do not do, and the public-health realities — undernutrition, malaria, prematurity, adversity — that shape the children this programme will serve. It ends where counselling always begins: with a parent who is afraid it was something they did.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Teratogen}
An agent that disrupts development before birth. Its effect depends on **timing** (the sensitivity window of the organ then forming), **dose**, the **genotype** of mother and fetus, and **combination** with other agents. In the room: the reason "was it the tablet I took?" rarely has a one-word answer, and the reason the answer must still be sought.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Probabilistic_epigenesis}
Gottlieb's account: genes are one participant in a developmental system, expressed or not expressed depending on internal and external experience, which they in turn shape. Development is probabilistic, not preformed. In the room: the sentence that ends both genetic fatalism and parental omnipotence.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Experience-expectant_and_experience-dependent}
Two kinds of learning the brain is built for: circuits that **expect** species-typical input in a window (patterned light for vision, language sound for phonology, responsive care for stress regulation) and circuits that record **whatever** a particular child meets (this language, this compound, this grandmother's songs). In the room: the distinction between what every child needs and what makes this child theirs.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Corrected_age}
For a child born preterm, age counted from the due date rather than the birth date; the fair yardstick for developmental expectations roughly to two years, and beyond that for the most preterm. In the room: the arithmetic that keeps a twenty-month-old ex-premie out of a disability register.
:::

### Lecture I — Three periods and the logic of the window

![Prenatal periods and the sensitivity windows of five organ systems across weeks from conception; the brain's window opens early and stays open, which is why adversity and toxins reach it longest. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-02-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Timing is the first principle.** The embryonic weeks (roughly two to eight from conception) are when organs lay themselves down and when teratogens do their greatest structural damage; the fetal period is when systems grow and refine, and when agents act on function and growth rather than structure. Figure 2.1 shows the windows: the heart's is brief and early, the brain's opens early and never fully closes — which is why the brain is the organ most reachable by later adversity, and the reason week 10's ACEs literature exists.

**Proposition 2. Dose, genotype and combination modify everything.** Alcohol has no known safe threshold in pregnancy and produces a spectrum — growth, face, and central nervous system, in combinations and degrees — rather than an all-or-none syndrome; valproate in pregnancy raises the risk of neural-tube defects and later neurodevelopmental difficulty; malaria in pregnancy and maternal undernutrition act through the placenta on growth and on the brain's fuel. And the same exposure in two pregnancies yields two outcomes, because susceptibility is partly genetic: the honest clinical sentence is "this raises risk; it does not write the sentence".

**Proposition 3. Genes are participants, not authors.** Probabilistic epigenesis replaces the blueprint metaphor: gene expression is regulated by experience — by care, by nutrition, by stress hormones — from the womb outward. The practical consequence is symmetry: a genetic vulnerability can be softened by environment (week 6's goodness of fit), and an environmental risk can be buffered by relationship (week 10's resilience literature). Neither half of nature–nurture is a complete sentence.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Take a prenatal history on every child, once, calmly, as part of the ordinary map: birth weight and weeks, complications, maternal health and medication, alcohol and other substances, and the household's circumstances in that year. Record it as history, not verdict; and record what is unknown as unknown.
:::

### Lecture II — Adversity before and around birth, in our context

**Undernutrition and stunting.** Maternal undernutrition, anaemia and adolescent pregnancy constrain fetal growth; after birth, stunting in the first two years travels with delayed cognitive and motor development at group level, and the mechanism is transactional: a child with less energy explores less, is responded to less, and receives less of the experience-dependent input the brain expects. The counsel is not despair but sequence: nutrition and responsive care together, because either alone underdelivers (the Lancet early-childhood series).

**Malaria, infection and inflammation.** Malaria in pregnancy contributes to low birth weight; severe early infection and its inflammation reach the developing brain. Public-health measures — nets, intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy, prompt care — are developmental interventions, and the counsellor who knows this advocates differently.

**Prematurity.** The preterm infant's brain completes its most vulnerable growth outside the womb, in a world of light, sound and handling it did not expect. Outcomes range from entirely typical to significant disability, and the predictors are weeks at birth, complications, and — again — the environment after discharge. Corrected age is the fair yardstick: a child born at thirty-two weeks is, at four months of age, developmentally expected to behave like a two-month-old, and the expectation should be corrected roughly to two years, longer for the most preterm.

**Early adversity and care.** Institutional care without a stable responsive adult alters stress regulation, attachment and cognitive trajectories, and the earlier the stable care arrives the better the recovery — the strongest natural experiment in the whole of developmental science, and the argument behind every foster and adoption standard. Family poverty acts not mainly through want of things but through want of dependable, unhurried, responsive care: stress taxes the caregiver, and the caregiver is the child's environment.

::: safeguard
A prenatal history includes matters that are, in some places, crimes or sins: alcohol use, undisclosed medication, an unwanted pregnancy. The counsellor's frame is protective, not prosecutorial: information given in a session is used to help the child and to support the mother, and where a current pregnancy is at risk the duty is to the unborn child's care, discharged through support and referral, never through blame. Blame is not only unkind; it is ineffective — it is the single most reliable way to ensure the next mother tells you nothing.
:::

### Worked example 2.1 — The question every counsellor dreads

**Task.** A mother of a four-year-old with language and attention difficulties asks, crying: "Was it the malaria tablets in my first pregnancy? It was my fault." Respond in the register this volume requires: accurate about mechanism, honest about uncertainty, and protective of the relationship.

**Solution.** Three moves, in order. First, the relationship: "You have carried that question for four years. Thank you for trusting me with it." Second, the mechanism, honestly: intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy is given **because** malaria itself damages the pregnancy; the medicines used in national programmes are chosen for safety in pregnancy, and no one can attribute an individual child's difficulties to a tablet taken years ago — the honest answer to "was it the tablets?" is "we cannot know, and the evidence does not point that way". Third, the turn to the usable: what is known is this child, now — her language sample (week 7), her attention in play (week 5), the interactions that will move her from here (weeks 4 and 6). The sentence that closes: "Whatever began it, what helps it now is in this room, and most of it is you." Blame is refused not by reassurance but by redirection to mechanism and to work.

### Worked example 2.2 — Corrected age, worked

**Task.** Tobi was born at thirty-two weeks' gestation and is now twenty months old by the calendar. His nursery compares him with the twenty-month group and proposes a "developmental delay" note. Compute and counsel.

**Solution.** Thirty-two weeks is eight weeks early; corrected age is twenty minus eight, or twelve months. Against twelve-month expectations — first words emerging, pulling to stand, joint attention well established — Tobi's reported skills (walking with a held hand, four words, pointing) are within the ordinary range of his corrected age, and the "delay" is an arithmetic artefact. The counsel to the nursery: record corrected age beside chronological age on every review to at least two years; compare him with twelve-month expectations; and watch, because ex-preterm children remain a group worth monitoring for attention, learning and growth — monitoring, not labelling. The counsel to the parents: the number that matters this year is not the calendar but the due date he never got.

::: skills
**Practical 2 — the non-blaming history.** In threes: mother, counsellor, observer. The counsellor takes a full prenatal and birth history from a "mother" who carries one hidden fear (supplied on a card: alcohol, a tablet, a fall, a wished-for termination). Requirements: ask every necessary question including the feared one; use the three moves of worked example 2.1 when the fear surfaces; end with the turn to the usable. The observer records one sentence: the moment blame was refused or the moment it was accepted. Rotate twice.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Before birth, timing governs risk and experience governs expression: teratogens act in windows, genes act as participants in a probabilistic system, and adversity acts mostly through the caregiver — so the counsellor takes a calm prenatal history, corrects the age of the preterm, and refuses blame while turning to what can now be done.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why does the brain's sensitivity window make early adversity more consequential than adversity at nine — and what does "more consequential" not mean?
2. Corrected age protects a child from a label. What protects the same child from the label's opposite, the missed referral?
3. "Genes load the gun, environment pulls the trigger" is popular and wrong. Where exactly does the metaphor fail?
4. What in your own culture names a child's difficulty as the mother's sin, and how will you counsel inside that belief without endorsing it?
5. If probabilistic epigenesis is true, what does a prognosis actually claim?
