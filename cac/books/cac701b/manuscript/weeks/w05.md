# Processing and Executive Function: The Working Mind and Other Minds {.unit num="5" week="5" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Outline** the information-processing account: attention, working memory, processing speed and strategy development across childhood.
2. **Describe** the three components of executive function and their developmental trajectory, and state why EF is a better school-readiness predictor than many expect.
3. **Administer and score** a standard false-belief task, and interpret a pass and a fail without over- or under-reading either.
4. **Explain** theory of mind's consequences for counselling: perspective-taking, understanding of lie and secret, and the credibility of a child's testimony.
5. **Recognise** EF demands in ordinary classroom failure, and design one accommodation that lowers demand without lowering expectation.

### Where this week sits

Two revolutions meet in this week. The information-processing tradition treats the mind as a working system with bottlenecks — and much of what adults call disobedience is a bottleneck. The theory-of-mind tradition discovers that at around four to five the child grasps that minds **represent** rather than copy the world — the single cognitive event beneath every later empathy, every lie detected, and every therapeutic conversation about what someone else was thinking. Both are measurable, both vary with language and context, and both are routinely mistaken for character.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Working_memory}
Holding and using information in mind over seconds; capacity grows steadily through childhood and is severely limited at any age — which is why a three-step instruction loses its third step somewhere in a six-year-old's hallway. In the room: the cheapest, most missed accommodation in education and in therapy.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Executive_function}
The coordinating system: inhibitory control (stopping the prepotent response), working memory, and cognitive flexibility (shifting set). Develops steeply across the preschool and primary years, vulnerable to stress, sleep loss and adversity — and trainable in the ordinary sense that use and scaffolding train it. In the room: the dimension on which "could if he tried" is usually false.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=False-belief_task}
The test of representational mind: the child who understands that Sally will look where she **believes** the marble is, not where it is, has grasped that minds can be wrong — the foundation of deception detection, of empathy as cognitive act, and of understanding that the counsellor has a mind too. In the room: the four-to-five watershed, shifted months by language, conversation and siblings.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Metacognition}
Thinking about one's own thinking: knowing what one knows, monitoring comprehension, choosing a strategy. Emerges visibly in the primary years and is the engine of independent learning — and of the reflective work therapy later asks for. In the room: the reason "think about why you did that" is an impossible instruction at five and a fair one at eleven.
:::

### Lecture I — The working system and its bottlenecks

![The three components of executive function converging on the coordinating system, above a theory-of-mind timeline from desire psychology at two to second-order belief in the primary years; language and conversation shift the timing. After Diamond (2013) and Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001). *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-05-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Much of childhood "misbehaviour" is capacity, not choice.** The child who answers before the question ends (inhibition), who loses the third step (working memory), who cannot switch from play to pack-up (shifting) is displaying the ordinary limits of a developing system. The diagnostic habit this week installs: before any moral reading of a failure, ask which component the task demanded and whether the child's system, today, under this stress, has it.

**Proposition 2. EF is developmentally honest and socially unequal.** EF develops steeply between three and seven and into adolescence; it is depressed by chronic stress, poor sleep and adversity — the mechanisms behind week 10's adversity findings — and it predicts early literacy and numeracy in several cohorts beyond general ability (Diamond, 2013). A classroom that knows this teaches EF in the grain of the day: routines that externalise memory (lists, pictures, songs), games that train stopping and shifting, and transitions announced before they happen.

**Proposition 3. Strategy development is the visible face of processing change.** Young children do not spontaneously rehearse, organise or elaborate; they must be taught the strategy and then taught **when** to use it — utilisation deficits are the rule. Metacognition follows: the eight-year-old who knows they did not understand the paragraph is operating a monitor the five-year-old lacks.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Audit your own instructions this week: count the steps in each thing you ask a child to do. Then halve them, or write them down, or sing them. The accommodation costs nothing and rescues more children than any referral.
:::

### Lecture II — The discovery of other minds

Before three, children operate a desire psychology: people act to get what they want. Belief enters slowly; at three, children grasp that minds can be about things, but a mind that is **wrong** still defeats them — in the standard false-belief task they send Sally to the true location. Between four and five, across cultures and tasks, the majority cross: minds represent, representations can be false, and action follows the representation (Wellman, Cross & Watson, 2001). Language, conversations about minds, and siblings shift the timing by months; deaf children of hearing parents, deprived of that conversation, cross late — the cleanest evidence that the discovery is made in dialogue.

**Consequences for the counselling room, in four.** *Perspective-taking:* before the watershed, "how do you think she felt?" asks a question the child's system cannot yet compute; use enactment and objects instead. *Lie and secret:* a child who understands false belief understands that a speaker can plant a false mind — the cognitive basis of lying, of being lied to, and of keeping a secret someone planted; safeguarding interviews must therefore date themselves against this competence. *Testimony:* a pre-representational child's account is not less honest but differently suggestible — they answer from the world, not from a monitored representation of it, so leading questions rewrite rather than retrieve. *Therapy itself:* every interpretation you offer is a claim about the child's mind held in your mind; the child who cannot yet hold minds-in-minds experiences interpretation as fact or as nonsense, never as hypothesis — one more reason volume 1's work with the young is play and enactment, not insight.

::: safeguard
Where a child's disclosure is evidence, this week's science is law-adjacent: a child below the representational watershed is highly answerable to leading questions, not because they lie but because their memory is reconstructive and social. Practitioners do not interview such children investigatively; they record what the child says spontaneously, in the child's words, and refer to trained forensic interviewers. The counsellor's room stays the child's room.
:::

### Worked example 5.1 — False belief with Ife: protocol, score, caution

**Task.** Administer a Sally–Anne style task to Ife (3:8) and write the record.

**Solution.** Protocol: enact with two dolls and a visible transfer; control questions first (where is the marble really? where was it at first?) — a child who fails a control question has failed comprehension, not theory of mind, and the trial is void. Test question: where will Sally look? Ife answers the true location (the basket Anne moved it to). Record: fails false belief with controls passed; desire vocabulary present ("Sally want her ball"); belief talk absent. Interpretation, carefully: this is the expected profile at 3:8 — not a deficit, not a prediction of empathy failure, and not stable: re-test at four-and-a-half with conversation about minds in between, because the watershed is near and language-rich interaction moves it. Caution written into the record: the task was administered in Ife's stronger language; a child tested in a weaker language fails tasks their mind has already passed, which is an assessment error with a child's name on it.

### Worked example 5.2 — The classroom failure that is a bottleneck

**Task.** Kunle (7) "refuses" group work: he dominates or withdraws, and his written instructions lose their endings. Analyse by component and design one accommodation per failure.

**Solution.** Group work demands simultaneous inhibition (wait your turn), working memory (hold the group's plan while holding your part), and shifting (move between speaker roles) — the full EF triad under social load, which for a seven-year-old with a quiet, slow-to-shift profile is the hardest room in the school. Dominance is inhibition failure under load; withdrawal is the child's own sensible rationing. Accommodations that lower demand without lowering expectation: roles made external and visible (a card that says RECORDER, turned over when the role turns), the group plan written in three boxes rather than held in air, and a two-minute warning before role change so shifting is prepared rather than sprung. For the written instructions: numbered steps on the board remain all lesson — the accommodation is environmental memory, and the expectation (follow all steps) is untouched. Review in four weeks: if dominance and withdrawal persist **with** demand lowered, the question changes from capacity to anxiety or to the ADHD dimension of week 11 — and the record of what was tried is what makes that referral credible.

::: skills
**Practical 5 — the EF audit.** Observe (live or recorded) one classroom or nursery session for twenty minutes with a three-column sheet: demand on inhibition / on working memory / on shifting. Record each demand event and each child failure that matches it. Write 250 words: the single demand that produced most failures, and one environmental accommodation for it. Bring the sheet to week 14, where it becomes part of your measurement portfolio.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A false-belief critique of 800 words: administer or observe the task, then critique one popular over-reading (e.g. "fails false belief, therefore cannot empathise" or "passes, therefore can be interviewed investigatively"), using this week's propositions and one empirical caution (language, controls, or timing).

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Task protocol correct, including control questions | 30% |
| Over-reading identified and dismantled with mechanism | 40% |
| Empirical caution applied concretely | 30% |

**Formative.** One paragraph: an instruction you gave a child this month that their working memory could not hold. Rewrite it.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The mind works with bottlenecks and with a coordinating system that develops under stress and scaffolding alike; and around four to five the child discovers that minds represent and can be wrong — the watershed beneath empathy, deception, testimony and therapy itself — so the practitioner audits demands before morals, dates every interview against the watershed, and tests in the child's language or not at all.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. "He could do it if he tried." Under an EF account, what is this sentence usually hiding, and what would the honest version say?
2. Why does a child who fails false belief answer leading questions differently — and what follows for a safeguarding conversation?
3. A child tested in their weaker language fails. Whose error is recorded on the form, and how should the form say so?
4. Is metacognition trainable, or does it arrive? Use the utilisation-deficit evidence to argue.
5. Which of your own professional routines is an external working-memory device you have stopped noticing?
