# Self, Identity and Moral Development {.unit num="8" week="8" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Trace** the development of self-concept from physical and possessory description to psychological and comparative self, and state what self-esteem does across childhood.
2. **Apply** Erikson's childhood crises — trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity — as a formulation instrument, naming what each unresolved balance looks like in the room.
3. **Sequence** moral reasoning from Piaget's realism through Kohlberg's levels, and **state** Gilligan's and social-domain critiques precisely.
4. **Describe** conscience and prosocial development as relationship-built rather than rule-taught.
5. **Interview** a child about a moral dilemma and code the reasoning, not the verdict.
:::

### Where this week sits

A child enters counselling with a working theory of who they are and what they owe — built from being met or not met, trusted or not trusted, able or not able. This week is that theory's development: the self that describes itself, the crises that organise it, and the moral reasoning that justifies it. It closes the volume's first movement; from week 9 the child meets other people.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Self-concept}
The child's working description of themselves: physical and possessory at three ("I have a red cup"), behavioural and comparative in the primary years ("I can run faster than Tobi"), psychological and trait-based from late childhood, and in adolescence an integrated identity — with the school-age dip in which the child discovers that others rank them. In the room: the register in which the child can hear you; praise pitched above the child's self-concept is not heard.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Eriksonian_crisis}
Each childhood period poses a social crisis whose balance is carried forward: trust v. mistrust (hope), autonomy v. shame (will), initiative v. guilt (purpose), industry v. inferiority (competence), identity v. role confusion (fidelity). Not stages that pass but balances that persist as dispositions. In the room: the shortest useful history — which balance did this child mostly meet?
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Moral_reasoning}
Piaget's move from realism (rules as external, outcome-based judgement) to relativism (intent, agreement); Kohlberg's levels — preconventional (consequences and exchange), conventional (relationships and law), postconventional (principle) — as reasoning about justice, scored from justification, never from choice. In the room: the seven-year-old who judges by damage is not immoral; they are seven.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Conscience}
Internalised self-regulation with an emotional core: guilt that repairs rather than shame that hides, built in mutually responsive relationships and through induction ("look at her face — she is crying because…") rather than through power assertion. In the room: the difference between a child who stops because someone might see and a child who stops because someone else hurts.
:::

### Lecture I — The self, built in mirrors that are people

![Erikson's childhood crises with their ages and the strength each leaves behind: hope, will, purpose, competence, fidelity; each balance is carried forward into the next crisis and into the counselling room. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-08-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. The self is social before it is personal.** The infant's sense of agency (I make the rattle move), the mirror and rouge recognitions of the second year, the possession-language of the third ("mine!") that adults misread as selfishness when it is selfhood: each is built in interaction, and each can be read back from it. The school-age child's self-concept is comparative because school is comparative — and the dip in self-esteem around eight to twelve is the child discovering the ranking, not a disorder of confidence.

**Proposition 2. Erikson's balances are the counsellor's shortest anamnesis.** The child who cannot trust the room (mistrust carried), who cannot choose without shame (autonomy defeated), who proposes nothing (initiative punished), who expects to be bad at everything before trying (industry defeated), who is no one in particular and terrified of it (identity diffuse) — five sentences, each with a history, each with a different therapeutic task. Figure 8.1 is a history-taking form wearing a diagram's clothes.

**Proposition 3. Adolescent identity work begins earlier than adolescence.** The primary-years industry crisis is identity's foundation: competence experienced is the material identity is later built from; the child whose industry was defeated arrives at fifteen without raw material, and the identity confusion then looks like laziness to everyone but the developmental reader.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Pitch your language at the child's self-concept: with the under-six, concrete and possessory ("you worked hard on that tower"); with the school-age, behavioural and effort-based, never trait-based ("you kept going" not "you are clever"), because trait praise collapses at the first failure and teaches the child to hide from difficulty.
:::

### Lecture II — Moral reasoning and its critiques

Piaget watched children play marbles and judge stories: the young child's morality is realist — rules are external and unchangeable, and the child who breaks twelve cups by accident is naughtier than the child who broke one stealing jam, because damage is the measure. By roughly ten, intent governs and rules become agreements. Kohlberg generalised the sequence into levels and stages using dilemmas (Heinz and the drug above all), scoring the **reason** given, never the choice made.

**Gilligan's critique**, precisely stated: Kohlberg's instrument prized a justice-and-rights voice and scored a care-and-responsibility voice lower; women and girls in his samples appeared "less developed" by a yardstick built from boys' answers. The correction is not that care replaces justice but that moral maturity has two registers, and an instrument blind to one mis-measures whoever speaks it. **Social-domain theory's critique**, equally important for practice: children do not reason morally about everything at once — they distinguish, from early, the moral (harm, fairness), the conventional (school rules, dress) and the personal (my hair, my friend), and much adult–child conflict is a domain dispute: the adult arguing convention as if it were morality, the child defending the personal as if it were a right.

**Conscience and prosocial development** grow in relationship, not in lecture: induction — drawing the child's attention to the other's feeling and the repair available — builds guilt-that-repairs; power assertion builds compliance-that-vanishes-when-unwatched; and prosocial behaviour follows empathy's development, from reactive crying in infancy to helping that costs the helper something by the primary years (Eisenberg and colleagues). The child who shares because they are watched is at an earlier station, not a worse soul.

::: safeguard
Moral reasoning knowledge protects children in two directions. First, from adult over-reading: a seven-year-old's outcome-based judgement is not callousness, and a child's lie to escape punishment is preconventional reasoning doing its job — punish the lie as character and you teach concealment. Second, from adult under-reading: children distinguish moral from conventional early, so a child who says "that rule is stupid but I follow it" and a child who says "hitting is wrong even if teacher doesn't see" are making different claims, and the second is the conscience you are building on. Records of a child's "immorality" are usually records of their age; write the age.
:::

### Lecture III — Conscience in practice: induction, repair, and the apology that is not shame

**Proposition 1. Induction builds guilt-that-repairs; power assertion builds compliance-that-vanishes.** Drawing the child's attention to the other's feeling and to the repair available ("look at her face; what could make it better?") internalises a standard the child owns; punishment externalises the standard into the punisher's presence, so the behaviour returns when the punisher leaves.

**Proposition 2. The apology is a repair script, not a shame ritual.** "Say sorry" demanded in public teaches performance; the repair sequence — name the harm, ask the harmed what helps, do it — teaches conscience and leaves the child's dignity intact. Where a service insists on the performed apology, the counsellor's work is to convert the ritual into the sequence without open defiance: add the question and the deed to the word.

**Proposition 3. Shame and guilt are different destinations.** Guilt says "I did a bad thing" and moves toward repair; shame says "I am bad" and moves toward hiding, and hiding is the soil of concealment, of lie upon lie, and of the unreportable disclosure. Every moral conversation with a child is therefore also a decision about which of the two the child will carry home.

::: safeguard
A child whose file accumulates moral adjectives ("manipulative", "remorseless") is a child whose future hearings are pre-written; the record carries behaviours and contexts, and the conscience work carries the hope.
:::

### Worked example 8.1 — The cup story with Kunle, coded

**Task.** Tell Kunle (7) the standard pair: child A opens the cupboard for a sweet and knocks fifteen cups down; child B takes a sweet they were told not to and knocks one cup down. Who is naughtier, and why? Code.

**Solution.** Kunle: "A. Him break plenty." Asked whether A meant to: "no, e just open." Asked if that matters: "the cups still break." Code: realism / outcome-based judgement with intent acknowledged but not weighted — the expected profile at seven, sitting at the realism-to-relativism transition. The record's value is the justification, and the follow-up question that locates the zone: "if A was helping mummy and B was hiding from mummy, which one would you tell mummy about?" — Kunle hesitates, then "B… because e hide." The hesitation is the zone: intention is entering the system under social framing. Next teaching step (week 4's scaffold): stories in which intent and outcome are pitted with the child as judge, then as author. And the Erikson line in the same record: Kunle's industry — "I don't want to write because my writing is bad" — names the balance to protect this term; moral work and industry work will run in the same sessions, because a child who expects to be wrong judges himself by outcomes too.

### Worked example 8.2 — The domain dispute in the staffroom

**Task.** A teacher demands that a nine-year-old apologise for refusing to cut his long hair: "it is school rule and he must respect rules". Advise, using social-domain theory.

**Solution.** Name the domains aloud: hair is in the personal domain for the child and, in this school's code, in the conventional domain for the institution — and the conflict is real but not moral; no one has been harmed, and demanding an apology recruits the moral register ("respect", "sorry") for a conventional dispute, which teaches the child that all rules are moral and all disobedience is sin, and buys the school a resentment it will pay for at thirteen. Advise the sequence: (i) uphold the institution's right to a dress code transparently, as convention, with its reason stated ("our code says X because Y"); (ii) withdraw the moral demand — no apology required for a domain claim; (iii) give the child a voice within the convention (a hearing, a review date, an exemption route), which is how conventions earn allegiance; (iv) watch the child's own reasoning — if he argues "my body, my choice", he is making a personal-domain claim worth hearing developmentally, not a disciplinary offence. The teacher leaves with a distinction that will save a dozen relationships: morality is about harm and fairness; convention is about how we run this place; and children can tell them apart long before staffrooms can.

::: skills
**Practical 8 — the moral interview.** Interview two children of different ages with the cup stories and one personal–conventional probe ("Is it wrong to wear red socks here if the school says white? Is it wrong to hit? Why the difference?"). Code each: realism/relativism or level; domain distinctions present or absent; register (justice, care, or both). Write 300 words comparing the two children's reasoning and naming one teaching step for each. The mark is on the coding of justifications.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A moral reasoning interview record with coding, plus 600 words: the child's Eriksonian balances as you read them from the session and the file, and one intervention pitched at the child's self-concept register.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Justifications recorded verbatim and coded correctly | 35% |
| Domain distinctions analysed | 20% |
| Erikson applied as history, not label | 25% |
| Intervention pitched at the child's self-concept | 20% |

**Formative.** One paragraph: which Eriksonian balance was mostly met in your own childhood, and what it costs you to defend it in others.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The self is built in social mirrors and carries its Eriksonian balances forward; moral reasoning moves from outcome to intent across the primary years in two registers, justice and care, inside a mind that distinguishes moral, conventional and personal from early — so the practitioner codes justifications, pitches praise at the child's register, builds conscience by induction and repair, and refuses to moralise a domain dispute or an age.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Trait praise collapses at first failure. What does effort praise do instead, and why does it survive?
2. A child lies to escape punishment. Write the developmental sentence, the moral sentence, and the counsel sentence — and say which order you would speak them in.
3. Gilligan's critique is often summarised as "women are care-oriented". What is wrong with that summary, and what is lost by it?
4. Which domain is a school uniform rule in, and what changes if a school argues it as morality?
5. Which Eriksonian balance do you defend too quickly in the children you meet, and what in your own history is defending it?
