# Language and Communication: From Cooing to Narrative {.unit num="7" week="7" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Sequence** the milestones of prelinguistic and linguistic communication and state the red-flag ages at which absence mandates hearing test and referral.
2. **Analyse** a short language sample: vocabulary breadth, word combinations, and the errors that are normal at a given age.
3. **Explain** what bilingual development is and is not: simultaneous and sequential acquisition, the silent period, and the myths that harm bilingual children.
4. **Describe** the input that builds language — responsive, conversational, book-sharing — and counsel a family on it without class contempt.
5. **Use** language development in counselling: matching the child's grammar, and reading a language history as part of every formulation.
:::

### Where this week sits

Language is the child's first technology and the counsellor's main medium; both facts are easy to forget. This week gives the developmental backbone of that medium — the milestone sequence with its points of no waiting, the social engine of input, and bilingualism described properly, because in the communities this programme serves, a child's strongest language will often not be the language of their school, their tests, or their therapist.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Milestone_sequence}
Cooing, babbling, first words around the first birthday, a fifty-word vocabulary with two-word combinations by roughly two years, telegraphic speech, the grammar explosion of the third year, and the narrative of the preschool years: a sequence robust across cultures, with wide normal variation around it. In the room: the map against which "late talker" becomes either a watchful wait or a referral.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Red_flag}
An absence at an age where presence is near-universal: no babbling or gesture by twelve months, no single word by eighteen, no two-word combinations by twenty-four. Not a diagnosis: a **referral point** — hearing first, always — because waiting is the only irreversible mistake in this field.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Conversational_turns}
The active ingredient of language input: contingent, back-and-forth exchange — the served and returned volley — which predicts language growth more strongly than the number of words a child overhears. In the room: the counsel that costs nothing: talk **with**, not at; follow the child's look; answer the point before the word.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Sequential_bilingualism}
Acquiring a second language after the first is established: with a normal **silent period** of comprehension before production, mixing and interference that are systematic rather than confused, and a temporary gap in the second language that is a sequence, not a disorder. In the room: the difference between a child learning a language and a child referred for not having one.
:::

### Lecture I — The sequence and its points of no waiting

![The language milestone timeline from cooing to narrative across the first sixty months, with the three red-flag ages at which absence mandates hearing assessment and referral rather than waiting. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-07-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Communication precedes language by months.** Joint attention, gaze following, pointing — the declarative point that says "look with me" — are the foundation on which words are laid; a twelve-month-old who does not point or show is a child to assess even if words later come, because the social engine matters as much as the vocabulary it drives.

**Proposition 2. The first lexicon is built by fast mapping and errors that prove the system.** Over-extension ("dog" for every animal) and under-extension are hypothesis-testing; two-word combinations obey a grammar of their own ("mummy sock", "more juice") that no one taught; the third year's morpheme errors ("goed", "foots") are the sound of rule discovery, and correcting them into silence teaches the child that speech is dangerous.

**Proposition 3. Red flags are about absence, and absence has a first cause to exclude: hearing.** Every late language presentation gets a hearing assessment before any other explanation is entertained — including the child who "hears the biscuit tin", because selective hearing of desired sounds coexists with high-frequency loss. The flags of Figure 7.1 are not norms to enforce but ages at which the cost of waiting exceeds the cost of assessing.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Take a language history on every child: first words, combinations, the language of the home and of the church and of the street, who talks with the child and when, and any period of silence and when it lifted. Four minutes that repeatedly dissolve false diagnoses and occasionally catch true ones.
:::

### Lecture II — Bilingualism, described without myths

**Myth one: two languages confuse a child.** They do not. Bilingual children reach the milestones of each language on the ordinary schedule **in that language**, mix languages within a sentence by rule rather than by confusion, and code-switch as competence, choosing the language of the listener when they can. **Myth two: a bilingual child's smaller vocabulary in each language is a delay.** Total conceptual vocabulary across two languages is ordinary; single-language counts understate a bilingual child, and every single-language test administered to them is a test of their exposure, not their capacity. **Myth three: the home language should be dropped for the school language to thrive.** The opposite: a strong first language is the scaffold the second is built on, and the family that abandons its language loses the conversations in which the child's mind grows — and loses the language of comfort, which is the language therapy will one day need.

The **silent period** of the sequential bilingual — weeks or months of comprehension before production — is normative and protective: the child is building the system before risking the performance. The counsel to a school that calls it mutism: date it, support it with comprehension input and gesture, and review at three months; selective mutism (week 12) speaks in some contexts and not others, whereas the silent period speaks at home fluently — the discrimination is one question to a parent.

::: safeguard
Language is a safeguarding instrument: a child's disclosure arrives in their strongest language, and an interpreter who is a family member is not an interpreter — least of all where the family member is implicated. Where a child's language is not the service's, the record must state which language the child was assessed in and by whom; an assessment in a weak language is not evidence about the child.
:::

### Worked example 7.1 — A language sample, analysed kindly

**Task.** Ten minutes of play with Ife (3:8) yields: "mummy go market", "me want red cup", "doggy runned fast", "why the sky crying?", "me no like that man him shout". Analyse.

**Solution.** Combinations of three to four words with subject–verb–object order: within expectation at 3:8. "runned": over-regularisation of the past morpheme — evidence of rule acquisition, to be celebrated in the record, not corrected in the room. "me want": pronoun case error, ordinary at this age and gone by four to five in most children. "why the sky crying": a causal question with animist framing (week 3) — the cognitive system working exactly on schedule. The last utterance is not grammar data at all: it is content — a named man who shouts — and the sample's most important line, to be followed in safeguarding terms rather than linguistic ones. The record therefore carries two conclusions: language development within the expected range for age in her home language, with the note that any school-language assessment will understate her; and a disclosure to be pursued this week. Analysis that hears only the morphemes has missed the child.

### Worked example 7.2 — The red flag and the waiting parent

**Task.** Kunle's classmate (24 months) has no two-word combinations, single words around eight, no pointing at twelve by mother's recall. The mother says the neighbour's child spoke at three and "boys are late". Counsel and act.

**Solution.** Act first, kindly: the twenty-four-month flag is met; the pathway is hearing assessment now, in parallel with a developmental language assessment — not after a waiting period, because the waiting period is the harm. Counsel the mother with the neighbour's child honoured and the population stated: some children do speak late and thrive, and no one can tell at twenty-four months which late talkers those are — which is exactly why the assessment is free of cost to her and the waiting is not free to him. Give her the mechanism sentence: language is built in turns of conversation, and the assessment will tell us whether his ears, his processing or his opportunities are the bottleneck, because each has a different remedy. Give her work for the fortnight that is also treatment: narrate the market, count the peppers, wait five seconds after every question, and let him be wrong aloud without correction. Review date written on the card: hearing result and sample in two weeks, no exceptions.

::: skills
**Practical 7 — the ten-minute sample.** Record ten minutes of child-led play with a child whose first language you share (or use a supplied recording). Transcribe twenty utterances verbatim. Code: word count per utterance, combinations present, one morpheme error, one evidence of rule use, one communicative function beyond requesting (comment, question, protest). Write 200 words: where this child sits against the sequence, and the one sentence of counsel to the caregiver. Bring the transcript to week 14's portfolio.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A language sample analysis: your coded transcript plus 600 words placing the child on the milestone sequence, naming one red flag you checked for and how, and stating the child's languages and which one you assessed.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Transcript verbatim, coding accurate | 30% |
| Placement on sequence with age-appropriate errors read as competence | 30% |
| Red-flag check and hearing logic stated | 20% |
| Languages of the child named and respected | 20% |

**Formative.** Write the three-sentence counsel to a parent advised by family to drop the home language.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Language develops by a robust sequence on a social engine: communication before words, rules discovered through honourable errors, red flags that mandate hearing assessment rather than waiting, and bilingualism that is competence rather than confusion — so the practitioner samples before they judge, assesses in the child's language, and protects the home tongue as the child's instrument of thought and of comfort.
:::
