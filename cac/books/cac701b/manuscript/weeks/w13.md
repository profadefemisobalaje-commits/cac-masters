# Integration: The Developmental Formulation {.unit num="13" week="13" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Write** a developmental formulation of one page: description, developmental history, the three gates (difference, delay, disorder), mechanism across levels, the age-appropriateness verdict, the zone, the plan and its falsifier.
2. **Run** the three gates on a concern in order — difference, then delay, then disorder — and state what each gate excludes.
3. **Place** the age in every clinical sentence, distinguishing what is normative here from what is not.
4. **Integrate** the volume's strands on one child: cognitive, emotional, linguistic and contextual evidence in a single coherent account.
5. **Present** the formulation to a family and to a panel, including what remains unknown and who else must be told.
:::

### Where this week sits

Thirteen weeks of strands become one instrument. The developmental formulation is this volume's counterpart to volume 1's five-P formulation, with one addition that changes everything: the age. A formulation without the age is a formulation of a small adult; the developmental formulation asks, of every sentence, whether it is true of a child of this age in this context — and it carries the semester's running case to its integration.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_three_gates}
Difference (context, culture, language: assess in the child's own language and settings first), delay (timing: corrected age, sequence, range, with watchful waiting that has a date), disorder (pervasive across contexts, impairing, off-course in slope rather than in start). Only what passes all three is a disorder, and it remains a formulation, never a label. In the room: the ordered discipline between a concern and a category.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Age-appropriateness_verdict}
For each presenting behaviour, the explicit sentence: expected here, or not, for this age and context — with the norm and its range named. In the room: the sentence that dissolves half of all referrals and sharpens the other half.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Developmental_zone}
What this child can do next with help, in the domain of the difficulty (week 4): the formulation's target, stated as the assisted level and the next independent step. In the room: the difference between a plan and a wish.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Cascade_link}
The maintenance chain across systems (week 12), drawn as links with the transactions marked, so the plan can aim at the cheapest openable link rather than at the loudest symptom. In the room: the reason the plan has an order.
:::

### Lecture I — The gates, in order

![The three gates between a concern and a disorder: difference (context, culture, language), delay (timing, corrected age, sequence), disorder (pervasiveness, impairment, slope); the gates sort questions, not children. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-13-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Order is protection.** The gates run in a fixed order because each later gate is more expensive and more stigmatising than the one before: a language or context difference misread as delay wastes months; a delay misread as disorder writes a sentence on a child's file that outlives the delay. Gate 1 asks whether the assessment so far has been conducted in the child's own language and settings; gate 2 asks whether the child is simply earlier on an ordinary sequence (with corrected age where relevant); gate 3 asks the disorder questions of week 11 — pervasiveness, impairment, slope.

**Proposition 2. Watchful waiting is an intervention with a date.** Gate 2's outcome is often monitoring, and monitoring without a review date is neglect with paperwork. The dated wait names what will be watched, by whom, with what measure, and what will happen on the date — including referral if nothing has changed, which is the sentence that keeps the waiting honest.

**Proposition 3. The gates do not exclude each other's findings.** A child may pass gate 1 (assessed correctly in her language), pass gate 2 (no delay on sequence) and still not meet gate 3 — a difference without disorder, needing support rather than treatment; or meet gate 3 in one domain while a delay explains another. The formulation records each gate's verdict separately, because services read the verdicts, not the adjectives.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Write the three gate-verdicts as three labelled lines at the top of every developmental formulation. A reader — a school, a medic, a court, the child at twenty — should see in three lines what was excluded and how.
:::

### Lecture II — The one-page instrument

The developmental formulation fits a page because selectivity is its discipline:

1. **Description**, in the family's and child's words first, then the observer's: what brings them, since when, in whose day it bites.
2. **Developmental history**, compressed: prenatal and birth (week 2), milestones with the child's languages (week 7), temperament (week 6), attachments and separations, school and peer course (week 9), adversities audited with buffers (week 10).
3. **Gate verdicts**, three lines.
4. **Age-appropriateness verdicts**, one line per presenting behaviour, with the norm named.
5. **Mechanism across levels**: the child's own systems (attention, language, regulation, cognition), the dyads (the transactions that maintain), the contexts (demands, resources, culture), each with the strand and week it came from.
6. **Cascade links**, drawn, with the cheapest openable link circled.
7. **Zone**: assisted level now, next independent step, in the domain of the difficulty.
8. **Plan in order**, with dates; **falsifier**: the observation, dated, that would show the formulation wrong; **who else must be told**, and what the family has agreed about telling.
9. **What remains unknown**, stated as enquiry rather than as silence.

Nine lines of thinking, one page of writing. The page is complete when a colleague could continue the case from it and a family could correct it.

::: safeguard
The formulation is a travelling record. Three rules from volume 1 apply with developmental force: write the child as a person developing, not as a list of deficits — the page may be read aloud to the child one day; name uncertainty as uncertainty, especially at the gates; and never let a cultural or familial observation become a character verdict in a document that outlives the case. Where the formulation touches possible harm, the safeguarding sentence overrides every other line and says so.
:::

### Lecture III — Ife and Kunle, integrated

The semester's running case closes its developmental movement here. Ife at four-and-a-bit: slow-to-warm temperament in a good-fit redesign (weeks 6 and 9), language within expectation in her home language with the school-language question open (week 7), separation behaviour age-appropriate in duration and falling (weeks 6 and 12), buffers strong and one strain — the pending separation of her parents (week 10). Gate verdicts: difference — school-language contribution assessed in home language first, excluded as sole cause; delay — settling time within range and shortening, no sequence delay; disorder — not met: pervasiveness absent (settles fully at home and with grandmother), impairment falling, slope on-course. Formulation: a temperamental style meeting a normative developmental task (separation-individuation) inside a family transition, maintained briefly by an over-fast goodbye sequence now redesigned; plan: hold the redesign, protect the routine card through the separation, review settling minutes monthly; falsifier: if settling time rises above fifteen minutes for four consecutive weeks after the separation settles, or if regression appears in language or toileting, the formulation returns to gate 2 and the separation work becomes the governing strand.

Kunle at seven-and-a-bit: neglected peer status engineered toward visibility (week 9), industry protected through effort-praise and the figures competence (week 8), inattention questions answered as context-sensitive and sub-threshold with a demands audit attached (week 11), buffers: father's Sunday hour, grandmother, church group (week 10). Gate verdicts: difference — school-language comprehension thin and contributing, support in place; delay — none; disorder — attention dimension elevated in one setting only, impairment mild and external (teacher tolerance), not met this term. Formulation: a quiet, slow-to-shift child whose difficulties are transactions with demanding contexts, with two protective strands to spend carefully and one strain — the same family transition. Plan: the sociometric engineering continues to its six-week review; the demands audit goes to the teacher as two accommodations (written plus oral instructions; a two-minute warning before transitions); the Sunday hour is protected in writing through the separation; falsifier: teacher and home ratings converging upward on inattention at the six-week review opens gate 3 properly, with three settings' evidence and a specialist conversation.

Two children, one family transition, nine lines each. That is the instrument.

### Worked example 13.1 — The gate that was skipped

**Task.** A six-year-old is referred with "speech delay" on the basis of a school-language sample alone; the family's language is not the school's. The file already carries the word "disorder". Reconstruct the correct sequence and the sentences that repair the record.

**Solution.** Gate 1 was skipped: no sample exists in the child's home language, so the "delay" is an artefact of sequential bilingualism's ordinary gap (week 7). The repair has three parts. First, the assessment: a home-language sample with a speaker of that language, hearing screen, and a bilingual vocabulary count across both languages (total conceptual vocabulary, not single-language). Second, the record: an addendum, not a deletion — "the earlier 'disorder' entry rested on a single-language sample; gate 1 (difference) was not assessed; it is now assessed with these results; the gate-2 and gate-3 questions reopen on [date] with [measure]." Records are repaired by addition and dating, never by silent erasure, because the earlier entry travelled and may be quoted. Third, the family conversation: what happened, in plain words, without defending the institution at length — "the first test was in a language that is not hers at home; that was our error; here is what we will do instead" — and the child's experience checked, because a six-year-old who was tested and found wanting in a half-known language carries that hour with her. The falsifier for the repaired position: if the home-language sample shows combination and vocabulary below the home-language expectation for age, gate 2 opens properly and the referral stands on evidence rather than on arithmetic.

### Worked example 13.2 — The ninety-second developmental version

**Task.** Reduce Ife's formulation to ninety spoken seconds for her parents.

**Solution.** "Here is what we see, in plain words. Ife is not anxious in a worrying way; she is a child who needs time and one familiar face to change worlds, and that is her style from birth, not anything anyone did. Her settling at nursery was long because the goodbye was fast and the room was loud, and since we changed those two things the minutes have fallen from twenty to six — that is her development working, not a treatment working. Her speech is right on time in your language; the school language is new to her and we will keep watching it the honest way, in both languages. What we are holding now is her routine through the family change: the same three steps every morning, the same card, the same grandmother at four o'clock. And here is our promise: if the mornings lengthen again for four weeks running, or anything she had learned goes backwards, we will say our picture was wrong and we will look again, together. Nothing about her is a disorder; a lot about her is development, and development is what we are here to protect." Ninety seconds; the gates in plain words; the falsifier as a promise.

::: skills
**Practical 13 — the formulation panel.** In fives on a supplied complex case (full history pack): thirty minutes to write the one-page formulation; five minutes to present gates, mechanism, zone, plan, falsifier; six minutes of panel interrogation using week 15 of volume 1's six question types plus two developmental ones — "what is normal here for this age?" and "which gate's verdict is weakest, and what would strengthen it?" Rotate so every student presents once and panels twice.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** The formulation portfolio piece: a one-page developmental formulation of a supplied case with all nine elements, plus the recorded ninety-second family version, plus 300 words naming the weakest gate verdict and the observation that would strengthen it.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Gate verdicts labelled, ordered, evidence-attached | 25% |
| Age-appropriateness verdicts with norms named | 20% |
| Mechanism across levels with strands cited by week | 20% |
| Zone, plan order, dated falsifier | 20% |
| Family version in their words, uncertainty stated | 15% |

**Formative.** Write the nine element-headings from memory, then check; the ones you forgot are the ones your cases will miss.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The developmental formulation is the volume made instrument: gates of difference, delay and disorder run in protective order; the age placed in every sentence; mechanism gathered across the child's systems, dyads and contexts; the cascade drawn to find the cheapest link; the zone named as the target; the plan ordered with a dated falsifier and a list of who must be told — one page, nine elements, complete when a colleague could continue from it and a family could correct it.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why must gate 1 precede gate 2 and gate 2 precede gate 3? Give a harm produced by each possible inversion.
2. Watchful waiting with a date: what exactly does the date commit the service to, and what does it commit the family to?
3. Which of the nine formulation elements do you most often omit under time pressure, and what does its omission cost the child?
4. A formulation may be read aloud by the child at twenty. Which sentence of yours would you most wish to revise, and how would you write it now?
5. Ife's gates all closed. What would have been different in the room — and in the record — if gate 3 had been run first?
