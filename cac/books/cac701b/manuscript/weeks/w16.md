# Revision, Integration and Examination {.unit num="16" week="16" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Reconstruct** the volume from memory as one map: four strands, the shared ground, and the developmental position.
2. **Answer** the four questions of week 1 — what develops, by what process, with what universality, to what purpose — for any case in the volume.
3. **Use** retrieval practice and spaced revision on a four-week schedule you can keep.
4. **Write** examination answers in the three required formats: the comparative essay, the short developmental answer, and the case-based formulation, each to its mark scheme.
5. **State** the ethical obligations this volume attaches to developmental knowledge: the age in every sentence, the gate before the category, and the child's words in the room.
:::

### Where this week sits

The last week of CAC. 701 Child Development Theories. Nothing new enters; everything is retrieved, examined and closed. The block runs the consolidation, the examination, and the volume's closing argument — which is a single sentence about what a developmental education does to a counsellor's eye.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_four_questions_returned}
What develops (sequences and their ranges); by what process (maturation, learning, transaction); with what universality and variation (which patterns travel, which were local all along); to what purpose (the child in front of you, this week). In the room: the revision key and the examination's hidden mark scheme.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Retrieval_practice}
Attempting recall before re-reading, spaced over days; the fluency of re-reading is the least reliable feeling of knowing. In the room: close the book, draw the map, then open the book and mark in a second colour; the colour of the corrections is the syllabus.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=The_developmental_sentence}
The examination's highest-mark form: "for a child of this age in this context, X is expected / not expected, because Y, and the mechanism that would change it is Z, which we would know is working by W." In the room: age, norm, mechanism, observation — four clauses, most of the marks.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=What_theory_forbids}
Each strand's prohibition: Piaget forbids teaching above the child's construction; Vygotsky forbids help that never fades; the temperament account forbids character adjectives; the ACE literature forbids prognosis; the categories forbid being the child. In the room: the ethical content of a development course.
:::

### Lecture I — The map of the volume

![The course in one map: the thinking child, the feeling child, the speaking child and the situated child, converging on the shared ground of transaction, culture and language, measurement with humility and safeguarding, issuing in the developmental position — a formulation with the age in it. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-16-01.png){width=94%}

**The four strands, one line each.** The thinking child: Piaget's constructor, Vygotsky's apprentice, the processing system with its bottlenecks, the executive functions and the discovery of other minds (weeks 3–5). The feeling child: temperament and goodness of fit, attachment and co-regulation, the self and its Eriksonian balances, moral reasoning in two registers (weeks 6, 8). The speaking child: the milestone sequence with its red flags, bilingualism as competence, the input that builds language (week 7). The situated child: ecology, parenting climates under stress, adversity and ordinary magic, peers and gender, and the atypical courses read developmentally (weeks 1, 9–12).

**The shared ground.** Development as transaction — the child acting and the context answering, each editing the other; culture and language as conditions of every claim, including this volume's; measurement with humility — the number and its worth in one sentence; safeguarding overruling all of it, including the gates, when harm is in the room.

**The developmental position.** A formulation with the age in it: what is normal here, what is next for this child, what would change our mind, and who else must be told. Memorise the sentence; it is the answer to the largest question the examination can ask and to most of the ones your career will.

### Lecture II — Four weeks of revision that works

**Week A: retrieval.** Draw Figure 16.1 from memory; then answer the four questions for Ife, for Kunle and for one case of your own, one page each, unseen. Mark in a second colour; re-attempt in three days.

**Week B: gates and norms.** For each of six presentations (tantrums at two and at seven; no words at eighteen months; inattention at six; low mood at thirteen; parallel play at three; night waking at four), write the age-appropriateness verdict with its norm and range, then the gate you would run first and why. This is the examination's Format 2 in embryo.

**Week C: mechanisms and prohibitions.** For each strand, the three-sentence mechanism answer and the three-sentence prohibition (week 16's fourth keypoint), plus one sentence on whose sample the strand's norms were built on and what you therefore check.

**Week D: timed practice.** One Format 1 essay and one Format 3 formulation under time; then the portfolio's index page re-read, because your own records are the best past paper you will ever sit. Sleep before the examination is part of the syllabus; the memory you are examining was consolidated in it.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** The same four-week cycle is reflective practice under another name: retrieve what you expected, compare with what happened, correct the map, try again. Revision discipline and clinical supervision are one skill at two ages.
:::

### Lecture III — The examination: formats, marks, closing argument

**Format 1 — the comparative essay** (one from two, 45 minutes, 40%). Typical stem: *"Compare Piaget's and Vygotsky's accounts of why a seven-year-old fails a conservation task, and argue which should govern the first three weeks of teaching for a child who has never been to school."* What earns marks: mechanism-level comparison, the strongest form of the rejected account, a governing choice with a dated falsifier, and the schooling caveat of week 3 stated as evidence rather than as piety. What loses marks: biography instead of mechanism, and neutrality.

**Format 2 — the short developmental answer** (six from eight, ten minutes each, 30%). Typical stem: *"State whether night waking at four years is expected, name the norm and its range, and give one mechanism that would maintain it and one observation that would show the mechanism operating."* Four clauses carry the marks: age-verdict, norm, mechanism, observation. Definition without the age-verdict earns the lowest band; the age-verdict without the observation earns the middle; all four earn the top.

**Format 3 — the case-based developmental formulation** (one compulsory, 45 minutes, 30%). A new case with a history pack and two informants' ratings. Required: the nine elements of week 13, the informant variance read as context, the gates in order with the weakest verdict owned, and the ninety-second family version. The examiner reads the gate verdicts first, because their absence is the fastest indicator of an unformed developmental mind.

::: assessment
**Examination.** Three hours, closed book: one comparative essay (40%), six short developmental answers (30%), one case-based formulation (30%). Coursework already assessed: week 1 observation records, week 3 conservation log, week 4 scaffolding exercise, week 5 false-belief critique, week 6 goodness-of-fit analysis, week 7 language sample, week 8 moral interview, week 9 play record, week 10 adversity map, week 11 timeline, week 12 equifinality essay, week 13 formulation portfolio, week 14 screening critique and portfolio index, week 15 practicum.

**Examiner's note.** Candidates are not rewarded for naming theorists or milestones; they are rewarded for placing the age in the sentence, the mechanism under the age, the observation under the mechanism, and the child's own words somewhere on the page. An answer that could have been written without the case supplied has not read the case supplied.
:::

### Worked example 16.1 — A specimen Format 2 answer, marked

**Task.** Stem: *"State whether night waking at four years is expected, name the norm and its range, and give one mechanism that would maintain it and one observation that would show the mechanism operating."* Write the four-clause answer, then mark a weaker version.

**Solution.** Top-band answer: "Night waking at four is within the expected range when it is occasional and self-settling: brief night wakings occur in a substantial minority of preschool children and are normative; what is not expected is waking that requires parental presence every night and recurs most nights. One maintaining mechanism is negative reinforcement: the child's calling brings the parent, and the parent's arrival removes the child's distress, strengthening the calling while starving the self-settling that would otherwise consolidate. The mechanism is shown operating if, on nights when the parent delays entry by agreed minutes, the child's self-settling rises across two weeks while total waking falls — and if it does not, the maintenance hypothesis is wrong and the formulation returns to fear content, sleep hygiene or a medical question." Four clauses: age-verdict with range, mechanism as process, observation that confirms, observation that refutes. Middle-band version marked: "Night waking at four is normal because children wake; it is maintained by habit and shown by the child waking." — the age-verdict lacks the range and the qualifier, the mechanism is a label ("habit") not a process, and the observation restates the symptom; two clauses of four.

### Worked example 16.2 — The case-based formulation in forty-five minutes

**Task.** A new case is supplied with a history pack and two informants' ratings (teacher elevated, parent within range; child silent on self-report). Plan the forty-five minutes and give the skeleton.

**Solution.** Minutes: 5 reading the pack and marking the two ratings' divergence; 5 the description in the family's words from the pack's quotation; 5 the three gate verdicts with the weakest named (gate 1: no home-language evidence in the pack — declared); 8 the age-appropriateness verdicts per presenting behaviour with norms; 7 mechanism across levels, including the variance read as school-located demand sensitivity (week 14); 5 the cascade links with the cheapest circled; 5 zone, plan order, dated falsifier, who must be told; 5 the ninety-second family version drafted aloud; 5 review against the nine elements. Skeleton sentences: "For a child of eight in this context, losing oral instructions is expected under a forty-pupil oral-only regime and not expected as a stable trait, because the home rating and the one-to-one room disagree with the classroom; the mechanism that would change it is a written-plus-oral channel with a two-minute transition warning, which we would know is working by the teacher re-rating falling toward the home level at four weeks; the variance itself is our outcome measure, and no averaging is performed; what we do not know is the home-language reading level, and the sample due [date] will tell us." The examiner's first look is at the gate verdicts; the second is at whether the child's own words appear anywhere — in this skeleton, they do not yet, which is the one mark lost, and the candidate who notices it in review adds the quotation and takes it back.

**The closing argument.** You began this volume with four questions and a three-year-old at a nursery doorway. You end it with the same four questions and a discipline: to ask what is normal here before asking what is wrong; to run the gates in their protective order; to read the variance between informants as the location of the difficulty; to count the buffers as seriously as the adversities; to let categories arrive last and leave first; and to keep, in every formulation, the sentence that says what would change your mind and by when. Development is not a timetable and a child is not a milestone chart. A developmental education, properly taken, does one thing to a counsellor's eye: it makes the eye slow enough to see a course instead of a state — and fast enough, when the red flags rise, to move the same day. The next volumes give you the disorders in full, the counselling methods, the ethics and law, and the supervised hours. Take the four questions, and the age, with you into all of them.

::: skills
**Practical 16 — the last seminar.** In fours, ninety minutes. Unseen, each writes a Format 2 answer to the same stem; the group marks all four against the four-clause scheme, defends the marks, and agrees which to submit. Second half: each states one thing the volume changed in how they will watch a child, and one thing they still cannot do — and the group names where it is taught, in which volume or which placement. No one leaves without both statements made aloud.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The volume reduces to one map and four questions: four strands over a shared ground of transaction, culture, humble measurement and safeguarding, issuing in the developmental position — a formulation with the age in it, gates in order, buffers counted, categories last, falsifier dated, and the child's words in the room. Revise by retrieval, examine by the developmental sentence, and practise by the same discipline.
:::
