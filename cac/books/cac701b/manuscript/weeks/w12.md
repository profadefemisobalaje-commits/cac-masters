# Atypical Development II: The Principles of Developmental Psychopathology {.unit num="12" week="12" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Define** developmental psychopathology and state why its unit of analysis is the developing course rather than the present symptom.
2. **Apply** equifinality and multifinality to cases, and explain why each defeats a one-cause account of a presentation.
3. **Explain** heterotypic continuity — anxiety becoming depression, difficult temperament becoming conduct difficulty — and use it to read a family history forward.
4. **Use** the concepts of developmental cascade and transaction to show how a difficulty recruits its own maintenance across systems.
5. **Judge** whether a presentation is deviant for age and context, using age-appropriateness as the first criterion of abnormality in childhood.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 11 read the categories. This week installs the grammar beneath them. Developmental psychopathology's founding insight is that pathology in childhood is not a set of diseases that happen to occur in small people but a set of deviations from, and variations of, the developing course — so every clinical question is a developmental question: deviant from what, for this age, in this context, on this trajectory? The principles of this week are the ones you will use in every formulation for the rest of your career, including the typical children's.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Equifinality}
Different early paths to the same presentation: conduct problems at nine arriving from harsh unpredictable care, from an unmet language disorder, or from a neurological vulnerability. In the room: the reason the presentation never explains itself, and the reason two children with identical behaviour need different treatments.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Multifinality}
Different outcomes from the same beginning: early adversity eventuating in recovery, in anxiety at eleven, or in conduct difficulty at nine, depending on what intervened. In the room: the scientific death of prognosis-by-history, and the scientific life of protection as a variable.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Heterotypic_continuity}
Continuity of an underlying vulnerability across changing expressions: the inhibited toddler's wariness becoming the seven-year-old's selective silence becoming the thirteen-year-old's social anxiety and low mood; the difficult infant's intensity becoming oppositional conflict under coercive parenting. In the room: the reason a family history of "shyness" belongs in an adolescent depression formulation.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Developmental_cascade}
The sequence by which a difficulty in one domain recruits others across time: reading failure to avoidance to lost practice to widened gap to self-concept wound to school refusal — each step a new maintaining system, so late intervention must open several doors, not one. In the room: the map of why "just start again" fails at year four of a cascade.
:::

### Lecture I — The course is the unit

![Equifinality and multifinality: three different beginnings converging on one presentation, and one beginning diverging into three outcomes; risk is not destiny and the presentation never explains itself. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-12-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Deviance is judged against the developmental norm, and the norm is contextual.** The same behaviour is normative at two and deviant at seven — tantrums, night waking, fear of the dark, parallel play, private speech — so the first clinical question is never "is this bad?" but "is this expected here, at this age, in this context?" A behaviour that is expectable is not a symptom however loud it is; a behaviour that is off-age however quiet it is belongs in the formulation (a nine-year-old's enuresis after a dry year is a sentence, not an accident).

**Proposition 2. Equifinality obliges the differential history.** Three children present with the same conduct difficulty at nine (Figure 12.1, left). The first needs a caregiving intervention, the second a language and communication intervention with the classroom's demands changed, the third a medical and neuropsychological pathway. A service that treats the presentation delivers two wrong treatments out of three, and will blame the children for the failures of its own uniformity.

**Proposition 3. Multifinality obliges the protective audit.** The same early adversity diverges (Figure 12.1, right), and the divergence is not random: it tracks the buffers of week 10 — the committed adult, the routine, the meaning, the school that held. Risk assessment that counts adversities without counting protections is half a science and, in practice, half a cruelty.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Write into every formulation the two principle-sentences: "this presentation could arise from at least these courses…" (equifinality) and "given this history, these outcomes remain open…" (multifinality). The sentences keep the formulation humble and the plan revisable.
:::

### Lecture II — Continuity that changes its clothes

Heterotypic continuity is the reason developmental histories read like family novels: the vulnerability persists while the symptom changes costume with development. The behaviourally inhibited toddler does not become "the same thing" at thirteen; the wariness that at two was clinging becomes at seven the refusal to read aloud, at thirteen the avoided party and the unposted application, and under adolescent self-scrutiny the low mood that arrives when a temperament meets a mirror. The internalising trajectory classically runs anxiety then depression; the externalising trajectory runs difficult temperament then oppositional conflict then conduct difficulty, with the coercive parent–child loop (week 6's transaction) as the engine between stages.

Two clinical duties follow. First, **read the history forward**: a presenting depression at fourteen is interrogated for the earlier costumes — inhibition, selective silence, somatic complaints — because the formulation of the course differs from the formulation of the episode, and the prevention of the next costume differs from both. Second, **read the present backward with restraint**: heterotypic continuity is a group pattern, and most inhibited toddlers do not become depressed adolescents; the principle licenses enquiry, not prophecy, and the counsellor who prophesies has converted a probability into a self-fulfilling expectation in a parent's ear.

### Lecture III — Cascades and transactions: how difficulties recruit the world

The developmental cascade explains maintenance across systems: the child's reading difficulty produces avoidance; avoidance produces lost practice; lost practice widens the gap relative to peers; the widening gap produces the public failures; the public failures produce the self-concept wound (week 8); the wound produces refusal; refusal produces the school's concern and the parent's pressure; the pressure produces the battles that now maintain the refusal independently of the reading. By year three, treating the reading alone opens one door in a locked house.

The transactional model (week 1) is the cascade's engine at the dyad level: the child's difficulty evokes a response that feeds the difficulty — the intense child evokes coercion, the withdrawn child evokes neglect of their needs, the anxious child evokes accommodation that starves them of disconfirmation (volume 1, week 5). Transaction is the reason family-involved work is not an add-on to child treatment but often the treatment: the loop is the target.

**The categories, finally, in their place.** Diagnostic categories are hypotheses with utility: they predict course, recruit evidence, and open services. They are not the child, not the cause and not the plan; and their boundaries in childhood are softer than their numbering suggests, with comorbidity the rule and dimensions beneath every cut-point (week 11). Developmental psychopathology's discipline is to use the category as one sentence inside the formulation — "the pattern meets criteria for X, which predicts Y and responds to Z in these samples" — surrounded by the developmental sentences that make it true or false for this child.

::: safeguard
The principles carry a safeguarding edge in the opposite direction from over-diagnosis: age-appropriateness cuts both ways, and some presentations are never normative at any age — sexualised behaviour beyond the child's developmental knowledge, cruelty to animals, fire-setting with planning, a disclosed secret with an adult's name attached. "It is probably developmental" is a hypothesis to test, and testing it includes asking the questions whose answers would end it.
:::

### Worked example 12.1 — Three nines, one presentation

**Task.** Three children present with the same conduct difficulty: lying, taking things, Explosions when challenged. Histories: (a) care by rotating relatives after a mother's departure at two, discipline erratic and sometimes frightening; (b) late language, still thin comprehension of the school language, episodes clustered after long verbal instructions; (c) uneventful history, high activity from infancy, incidents impulsive and immediately regretted. Formulate each in two sentences and name the treatment each implies.

**Solution.** (a) Equifinality via caregiving: conduct difficulty as an adaptation to unpredictable care — trust tested, control taken because control was never given; treatment begins with a stable committed adult and predictable discipline (week 10), not with consequences for lying, which is the child's only reliable instrument. (b) Equifinality via communication: conduct episodes as the behavioural tail of incomprehension — the child who cannot parse the rule breaks it and, challenged, cannot narrate why; treatment is language support and demand redesign (weeks 7 and 11), with the "lying" re-read as confabulation under verbal pressure. (c) A neurodevelopmental course: impulsivity with immediate regret, pervasive and early; treatment is the attention course with specialist input plus external structure (week 11), and the moral reading withdrawn from the file. Same presentation, three treatments, and a service that delivered the same package to all three would produce three failures with three different true causes — which is the sentence equifinality exists to prevent.

### Worked example 12.2 — Reading the history forward at thirteen

**Task.** Bisi (13) presents with low mood and school refusal. Compose the heterotypic enquiry.

**Solution.** Four questions to the history, in order. *Infancy and preschool:* was she slow to warm, clinging at drop-off, wary of strangers? (the inhibited costume). *Primary years:* selective silences — refusing to read aloud, mute at assemblies — and somatic complaints on performance days? (the second costume). *Late childhood:* friendships narrowing to one or none, parties declined with reasons that change? (the third). *Now:* the mood's content — "everyone saw me shake" — which locates the depression inside a social-evaluation history rather than beside it. The formulation then reads: an inhibited temperamental line, heterotypically continuous through selective silence and social avoidance, now depressed under adolescent self-scrutiny, maintained by avoidance of every evaluating situation and by a school response that removed demands without removing the fear (accommodation, volume 1 week 5). The plan follows the course, not only the episode: behavioural re-entry graded through non-evaluating situations first, the cognitive work on self-evaluation, and — because multifinality keeps other doors open — the protective audit of week 10, because the one friend who remains is the buffer this plan must not spend.

::: skills
**Practical 12 — the cascade map.** Take one case you know or a supplied case with a multi-year history. Draw the cascade as a chain of at least six links across at least three systems (child, family, school, peers), marking at each link the transaction — what the child's difficulty evoked and how the evoked response fed it. Then circle the two links where an intervention this term is cheapest, and say what each would stop. One drawn page plus 200 words.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** An equifinality essay of 1,200 words: for one presentation of your choice (conduct difficulty, school refusal, or low mood), construct three plausible developmental courses with their evidence requirements, state the treatment each implies, and argue which single early observation would best discriminate between two of them.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Three courses genuinely distinct, mechanisms named | 35% |
| Evidence requirements specified per course | 25% |
| Treatments matched to mechanisms | 20% |
| Discriminating observation observable and dated | 20% |

**Formative.** Write one heterotypic sentence about a case you know: the earlier costume, the present one, and the vulnerability you believe connects them — labelled as hypothesis.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Developmental psychopathology makes the course the unit: deviance judged against age and context, equifinality obliging the differential history, multifinality obliging the protective audit, heterotypic continuity reading the vulnerability through its changing costumes, cascades and transactions explaining how difficulties recruit their own maintenance — and the diagnostic category restored to its proper size, one predictive sentence inside a developmental formulation.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. A behaviour is expectable at two and deviant at seven. What is the counsellor's sentence to a parent who believes the reverse in either direction?
2. Equifinality makes uniform treatment unethical in what precise sense? Name the sense.
3. Heterotypic continuity licenses enquiry but not prophecy. Where is the line in a conversation with a worried parent?
