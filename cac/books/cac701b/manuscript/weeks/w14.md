# Observation, Screening and Measurement: The Instruments and Their Honesty {.unit num="14" week="14" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Select** a developmental screening or rating instrument appropriate to age, language and setting, stating its norming population and its limits.
2. **Run** the screening pathway in order: concern recorded, screen in the child's language plus hearing and vision, focused assessment of the zone, then monitor-with-a-date or refer-with-the-record.
3. **Interpret** multi-informant variance (child, parent, teacher) as data about contexts rather than as error to be averaged away.
4. **Critique** one instrument's cultural fairness: norming sample, item content, translation versus adaptation, and what the critique licenses you to say.
5. **Assemble** a personal measurement portfolio from the semester's records, with a review date for each.
:::

### Where this week sits

Every judgement of this volume eventually meets an instrument: a checklist, a schedule, a rating scale, a sample, a screen. Instruments extend the eye and corrupt the judgement in equal measure, and the difference between the two is knowing what the instrument was normed on and what it was never asked about. This week builds the practitioner's measurement practice — and collects the semester's records (weeks 1, 5, 7, 9) into the portfolio you will keep.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Screening_versus_assessment}
Screening sorts quickly among the many (is this concern worth a closer look?); assessment describes slowly among the few (what is this, for this child?). A screen is never a diagnosis, and a negative screen is never a clean bill — it is a date on which to look again if the concern persists. In the room: the sentence that keeps a screened-negative child on the watch list.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Norming_population}
The children on whom an instrument's cut-points were set. Scores are comparisons with that population; a child outside it is being compared with strangers, and the score's meaning changes accordingly. In the room: the first question about any number: "compared with whom?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Multi-informant_variance}
Child, parent and teacher reports of the same child disagree systematically, because children are different people in different rooms; the disagreement locates the difficulty's context (home, school, peer group, or the child's inner world that only self-report reaches). In the room: variance as the finding, not as noise — and the reason averaging informants destroys the information.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Adaptation_versus_translation}
Translating an instrument's words is not adapting its items: "rides a tricycle" presumes a tricycle; "plays pretend games with dolls" presumes the dolls of the norming culture. Adaptation tests whether the item measures the same construct here, and re-norms where it can. In the room: the difference between a fair score and a fluent one.
:::

### Lecture I — The pathway and its order

![The screening pathway: concern recorded in the family's words; screen in the child's language with hearing and vision always; focused assessment of the zone with help; then monitor-with-a-date or refer-with-the-record attached. Parent–teacher disagreement is data about which room the difficulty lives in. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-14-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Hearing and vision precede everything.** A surprising share of "developmental" referrals are auditory or visual, and every language, attention and learning question is unanswerable until the senses are cleared. The screen that skips the audiologist is not a shortcut; it is a gamble with the child's years.

**Proposition 2. Screens in the child's language or not at all.** Parent-completed developmental screens (the ASQ family among the most used) exist in many translations, but translation is not adaptation and neither is norming; where no adapted norm exists, the screen's output is a structured conversation with the parent — valuable, and labelled as such — rather than a score. The label travels with the number into the file.

**Proposition 3. The focused assessment is the zone, not the stock.** After a positive screen, the assessment that changes practice is week 4's dynamic one: what the child does alone, what with help, and what the help profile says. Static re-testing after a screen mostly re-measures the screen; the assisted level measures the next term.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Attach the record to every referral: the screen and its language, the hearing and vision results, the assisted-level profile, the settings' evidence, and the family's own words. A referral without its record invites a re-assessment from zero, and the child pays for the repetition.
:::

### Lecture II — Multi-informant variance as the finding

Parent, teacher and child scores on the same construct correlate modestly at best, and the pattern of disagreement is informative in four canonical shapes: **home-only elevation** (difficulty in the family climate: look at stress, conflict, parenting fit); **school-only elevation** (difficulty in the classroom's demands or peer ecology: look at class size, language of instruction, peer status); **cross-informant elevation with child silence** (the child's inner experience not reaching self-report: common in young children and in stoic cultures — and a reason to use play and projective-adjacent methods with care); **child elevation alone** (the inner world no observer sees: anxiety, low mood, self-criticism — the shape that most often ends in "nothing wrong" files and is the most dangerous).

The discipline: report the variance, never average it. A file that says "scores were inconsistent" has destroyed the data; a file that says "elevated at school, not at home, with the child reporting X" has located the work. Where informants disagree about severity rather than context, the tie-breaker is function: which room is the child losing something they want in?

### Lecture III — Cultural fairness: four questions per instrument

**Norming:** on whom were the cut-points set, and when? A mid-century Western norm applied to today's children in this region compares them with strangers across both space and time. **Items:** what does each item presume — objects, routines, sleeping arrangements, foods, toys — and which items change meaning here? **Translation or adaptation:** was the version in your hand validated here, or merely rendered? **Consequence:** what decisions ride on the score, and who bears the error in each direction — the false positive's label or the false negative's lost years?

The honest conclusion is not instrument-nihilism: adapted screens and ratings still outperform unstructured impression for catching delay early, and early catching is the whole game. The conclusion is labelled use: the score, its population, its language and its limits in one sentence in the file, every time — so that the next reader, and the child at twenty, can see exactly what the number was worth.

::: safeguard
Instruments in safeguarding contexts carry asymmetric error costs: a screen that under-detects neglect because its items presume middle-class homes (clean clothes, regular meals, toys) can miss the neglect it was built to find, while over-detecting poverty as neglect punishes families for being poor. Where neglect is the question, the instrument is a prompt to look, and looking means the home visit, the growth chart, the school attendance record and the child's own account — never the checklist alone.
:::

### Worked example 14.1 — The divergent file, read properly

**Task.** Kunle at eight: teacher rating elevated on inattention; mother rating within range; Kunle's own one-page self-report: "I am fine; the class is too fast." Interpret and act.

**Solution.** The shape is school-only elevation with child attribution to context — not the classic home/school split but a child who locates the difficulty outside himself, which is both information and a defence to hear gently. Read the variance as data: the difficulty lives where demands are fast, oral and single-channel (the classroom), and does not live where demands are slow, written or one-to-one (home, church, your room). The interpretation is therefore not "Kunle overstates" or "mother under-reports" but a demand-context interaction consistent with week 11's formulation: sub-threshold attention sensitivity expressed under high oral load. Action: no new label; the two accommodations continue and are now evidence-based by variance; add one written-channel trial for four weeks (instructions on card plus oral) with the teacher rating repeated at the end — the variance itself becomes the outcome measure: if school elevation falls toward home levels, the demands hypothesis is confirmed by its own instrument; if it does not, gate 3 reopens with three settings' evidence. The file sentence: "informant variance interpreted as context-specific demand sensitivity; written-channel trial to [date]; teacher re-rating due [date]; no averaging performed."

### Worked example 14.2 — A screen and its sentence

**Task.** A nursery asks you to "do the ASQ" on a thirty-month-old whose home language is not the nursery's. Compose the practice and the file sentence.

**Solution.** Practice: administer the adapted home-language version with the parent as respondent, in the parent's language, with you present to observe rather than to score; hearing and vision screened the same week; the tricycle-type items replaced in conversation with local equivalents (what does she ride, climb, carry?) and the substitution noted. Suppose the communication item set flags concern: the focused assessment is then a home-language language sample (week 7) plus a dynamic vocabulary task in both languages (week 4), because the screen's flag may be a true language delay, a bilingual gap, or an item artefact — and only the sample and the zone can tell. The file sentence, one line: "ASQ-3 administered [date] in [language] adapted version, parent respondent; communication area flagged; norming population [X], not this child's population, score treated as structured parental report; hearing and vision clear [date]; home-language sample and two-language dynamic vocabulary assessment due [date]; review date [date]." Every number in the file now carries its worth in writing.

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A screening critique of 900 words: choose one instrument you have used or been given; state its norming population, three item presumptions, its translation/adaptation status, and the error costs in each direction for your setting; then write the file sentence you would attach to any score it produces.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Norming population and its distance from the child stated | 25% |
| Item presumptions concrete and local | 25% |
| Adaptation status verified, not assumed | 20% |
| Error costs in both directions argued | 15% |
| File sentence complete (score, population, language, limits, date) | 15% |

**Formative.** Submit your portfolio index page.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Instruments extend the eye when their limits travel with their numbers: hearing and vision first, screens in the child's language and labelled by norming population, focused assessment as the zone rather than the stock, multi-informant variance read as the location of the difficulty and never averaged away, cultural fairness asked as four questions per instrument — and every score in the file carrying one sentence of what it was worth, with a review date attached.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. "Compared with whom?" Name an instrument used in your setting and answer for it honestly.
2. Why is averaging informants worse than reporting their disagreement? Give a case where the average hid the child.
3. A screen flags concern in a language with no adapted norm. What exactly may you write in the file, and what may you not?
4. False positive versus false negative: which error does your service tolerate more easily, and who pays for that tolerance?
5. Your portfolio: which record in it would most change a decision if it were lost, and what does that say about your practice?
