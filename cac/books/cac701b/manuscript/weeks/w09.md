### Where this week sits

Until now the child has met adults: caregivers, teachers, therapists. This week the child meets equals, and the rules change. Peer experience is not a luxury around development; it is a laboratory in which perspective-taking, negotiation, rejection and repair are practised daily, and peer status in the primary years predicts later adjustment beyond what family variables explain. Play is the medium, and gender one of the first organising principles the child imposes on the peer world.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Play_types}
Parten's sequence — unoccupied, solitary, onlooker, parallel, associative, cooperative — describes the social density of play across the early years. Earlier types persist; development is the changing share of time, not the disappearance of a stage. In the room: the observational grammar of the nursery, and the reassurance that a five-year-old playing alone is choosing, not failing.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Peer_status}
Sociometric positions — popular, rejected, neglected, controversial — measured by nominations rather than by adult impression. Rejected children are actively disliked; neglected children are simply unseen, and the two need opposite interventions. In the room: the difference between a child who needs social skills and a child who needs to be noticed.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Social_information_processing}
Crick and Dodge's steps: encoding cues, interpreting intent, clarifying a goal, generating responses, deciding, enacting. Hostile-attribution bias at the interpretation step is the engine of much reactive aggression — and a trainable step. In the room: the micro-map behind "he just hits".
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Gender_schema}
The cognitive structure that organises the child's world by gender once constancy is achieved: attention, memory and self-evaluation become gender-filtered, so the child seeks the information their schema marks as theirs. In the room: why telling a child "anyone can play" loses to a schema that decides who "anyone" is.
:::

### Lecture I — Play and the peer world

![Parten's play types with their typical ages: unoccupied, solitary, onlooker, parallel, associative and cooperative; earlier types persist and what develops is the share of the child's time. After Parten (1932). *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-09-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Play is the child's peer curriculum.** In parallel play the child learns proximity without conflict; in associative play, exchange of materials; in cooperative play, roles, rules and repair. The nursery that schedules only group activity deprives the child of the solitary and parallel practice that cooperative play is built on; the nursery that never protects solitary time misreads its own best-regulated children.

**Proposition 2. Friendship understanding develops in stages.** From the momentary playmate of the preschool years ("she has a bike"), through the one-way and two-way fair-weather friendships of the primary years, to the intimate, mutually shared friendship of late childhood and adolescence (Selman). A seven-year-old's "best friend" of four days is not fickleness but stage: friendship as reciprocal service has not yet become friendship as mutual understanding, and counselling that moralises the change mislabels development.

**Proposition 3. Peer status is measurable and malleable.** Rejection is predicted less by difference than by the interaction of difference with dysregulation: the inattensive child who interrupts, the intense child who escalates. Neglect — the unseen child — is the status that most resembles Kunle at seven: competent, quiet, neither chosen nor refused, and at risk not of conflict but of absence from the peer record altogether. Interventions differ utterly: the rejected child needs regulation and entry skills rehearsed; the neglected child needs visibility engineered by adults who arrange the dyads.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Ask the peer question on every child over four: who chose them yesterday, and for what? A child's peer position is part of the presenting picture, and often the part the child feels most.
:::

### Lecture II — When peer conflict becomes a processing map

The child who hits after a bump in the corridor has, in two seconds, encoded the cue (contact), interpreted it (hostile), chosen a goal (retaliation or safety), generated responses (hit, tell, walk), decided and enacted. Crick and Dodge's model locates the divergence: some children under-encode (miss the apology), some over-attribute hostility, some generate only one response, some decide by fear of looking weak. Each step has its own intervention — and "anger management" that addresses none of the steps manages nothing.

The benignant counterpart matters as much: prosocial children process the same bump with a benign attribution because their history supplied benign episodes to encode. Processing is history made procedure.

### Lecture III — Gender: four accounts, one child

**Biological** accounts point to prenatal hormonal organisation and to modest average differences in some domains; they explain little of the variance in any individual child and nothing of the content of gender roles, which varies enormously across cultures and decades. **Social learning** accounts show modelling and differential reinforcement: adults respond to the same behaviour differently by perceived sex, and children imitate same-sex models preferentially. **Cognitive-developmental** accounts (Kohlberg's gender constancy) show the child constructing gender as a category: once the child grasps that gender is constant, they actively seek gender-consistent information. **Gender-schema** theory integrates: the schema, once formed, filters attention, memory and self-worth — the child who refuses the "girls' crayons" is not obeying an adult but protecting a self-category.

The counselling consequence is precise: rules and exhortation address social learning; the schema addresses itself through experience that the schema cannot digest — the boy who discovers he loves the cooking corner does not update by instruction but by an episode the schema cannot file. And where gender itself is the child's question rather than the peer world's rule, the child's own account governs the conversation, and the developmental task is safety and self-authorship, not category enforcement.

::: safeguard
Peer work carries two safeguarding edges. First, bullying is a peer-status phenomenon with a power asymmetry; it is never "conflict" between equals, and mediation between a bully and a target re-injures the target — protect first, then work separately. Second, gender non-conformity attracts peer victimisation; the school's obligation is the child's safety and the peer group's conduct, never the child's conformity, and a counselling referral whose implicit aim is conformity is a harm wearing a helping face.
:::

### Worked example 9.1 — Ife at the nursery edge, coded

**Task.** Twenty minutes of observation: Ife (3:8) spends nine minutes in solitary symbolic play with cups, four minutes watching two children build (onlooker, with one comment unanswered), five minutes beside a child filling buckets — same activity, no exchange (parallel), and two minutes in a chase initiated by the other child (associative, brief). Code and counsel.

**Solution.** The profile is normative at 3:8: solitary-symbolic and parallel dominant, associative emerging, cooperative absent — exactly the share-pattern of Figure 9.1. The onlooker episode with the unanswered comment is the clinical grain: Ife bid once and did not repeat, consistent with slow-to-warm style (week 6) rather than with peer deficit. Counsel: no intervention on play type; one environmental arrangement — the key worker places Ife beside (not into) a dyad with materials that require sharing one resource (one tap, two buckets), converting parallel into associative without a demand to perform. Re-observe in four weeks and compare shares, not types. The record states the shares in minutes; minutes are the data, adjectives are not.

### Worked example 9.2 — Kunle, neglected rather than rejected

**Task.** Sociometric picture of Kunle's class: few nominations for "like most", almost none for "like least"; teachers describe him as "no trouble, no friends". Design the intervention.

**Solution.** Kunle is neglected, not rejected: unseen rather than disliked, which is why skills-training would treat a deficit he does not have. The mechanism to change is visibility among equals, engineered by adults: (i) pair tasks with a defined complementary role in which Kunle holds the scarce resource (the ruler, the timer, the answer key) so that contact requires him; (ii) a standing dyad for two weeks — the same partner across tasks, because repeated contact is what converts proximity into acquaintance; (iii) one public competence display in his strength (figures), framed as service ("Kunle will check the totals"), which supplies peers with a reason to approach; (iv) no forced sharing of feelings and no "friendship group", which for a neglected child is exposure without material. Review at six weeks with a second sociometric snapshot: the target is not popularity but a rise in neutral-to-positive nominations and at least one reciprocal choice. The falsifier: if nominations are unchanged at six weeks, the hypothesis (invisibility, not dislike) is wrong, and the assessment turns to social anxiety and to the child's own wishes — some children prefer few companions, and a counsellor must distinguish the neglected child who suffers from the solitary child who does not.

::: skills
**Practical 9 — the play record.** Observe (live or recorded) one child for twenty minutes in free play, sampling every thirty seconds: play type, partner count, bids made, bids answered. Produce the minute-shares table and one paragraph: the child's dominant type against age expectation, one bid sequence transcribed verbatim, and one environmental arrangement you would make. Bring the table to week 14's portfolio.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A play observation record with coded table plus 600 words: peer status hypothesis (rejected / neglected / none) with evidence, one social information-processing step you would target if conflict were present, and the arrangement or intervention that follows.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Sampling and coding accurate, shares in minutes | 30% |
| Status hypothesis distinguished (rejected v. neglected) with evidence | 30% |
| Processing step or arrangement matched to mechanism | 25% |
| Child's own preference considered | 15% |

**Formative.** Recall your own peer position at eight. Name it in sociometric terms, and name one adult arrangement that would have changed it.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Among equals the child practises everything the adult world cannot teach: play types sequence and persist as shares of time, friendship understanding deepens in stages, peer status splits into rejected and neglected with opposite treatments, conflict resolves into processing steps that can each be trained, and gender organises the peer world through schemas that change by indigestible experience rather than by instruction.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why is "neglected" not a milder form of "rejected", and what intervention would be actively wrong for each?
