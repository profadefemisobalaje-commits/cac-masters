# Child Development Theories {.chapter num="1" short="Mapping Development" code="CAC. 701 · First Semester · 1.6 units" semester="First" units="1.6"}

Every counsellor of children carries a theory of development, whether or not it has been examined. It is in the sentence "he should have grown out of it by now", in the worry "she is not like the others", in the quiet judgement on a mother. This course puts those theories on the table, names them, tests them against evidence, and hands you back a set of instruments for watching a child develop — and for noticing, early and accurately, when development has left its ordinary course.

Child development is not one theory but a conversation among several: the maturational and the learning accounts; Piaget's constructing child and Vygotsky's social mind; the information-processing account with its bottlenecks; the attachment and temperament account with its transactions; the linguistic, the moral, the ecological; and, over all of them, the principles of developmental psychopathology that tell us how ordinary variation shades into clinical concern and back again. The course moves through these in sixteen weeks, and it never lets you forget the fourth question of week 1: what does this mean for the child in front of you on Thursday?

Two commitments govern the volume. The first is to evidence and to its limits: where a finding comes from a narrow sample, you will be told; where a classic claim has been revised, you will meet the revision; where the honest answer is "we do not know", the book will say so rather than decorate. The second is to context: the children of this programme grow up in compounds and classrooms, markets and churches, in several languages and in economies that tax their parents' patience. A development science that cannot speak to that world is not the science this course teaches.

A fictitious family — Ife, three years and eight months, and her brother Kunle, seven — accompanies the course from week 1 to week 15, so that each theory meets the same children and you can watch the theories disagree about them.

# Mapping Development: Questions, Methods, and the Child in Context {.unit num="1" week="1" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **State the four questions** of developmental science — what develops, by what processes, with what universality and variation, and to what purpose for the child in front of you — and place any theory on that map.
2. **Compare** longitudinal, cross-sectional and sequential designs, naming what each can and cannot establish about change.
3. **Choose** a method of studying a child — observation, informant report, self-report, task — appropriate to the child's age and the question asked, stating its bias.
4. **Apply** the ecological model to one child, locating factors at micro, meso, exo and macro levels and in time.
5. **Conduct** research and observation with children ethically: consent and assent, power, and the return of findings to families.
:::

### Where this week sits

Volume 1 asked what a theory is for. This volume asks what develops, and how we could possibly know. Child development theory is not a museum of famous names; it is a set of answers to four questions, each answer carrying a method and a method carrying a bias. Every week that follows is one lineage's answer; this week builds the frame you will hang them on, and introduces the child who will accompany us all semester.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_four_questions}
What develops (description and sequence)? By what processes (mechanism: maturation, learning, transaction)? With what universality and variation (which patterns hold across peoples, and which are local)? To what purpose (what does this tell the counsellor in the room)? In the room: the grid on which every theory in this volume is filed.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Development_as_transaction}
The modern consensus: development is neither the unfolding of a programme nor the writing on a blank page, but a continuous transaction between a child who acts and a context that responds, each changing the other (Sameroff's transactional model, developed in week 12). In the room: the reason "what is wrong with this child?" is always the wrong first question.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Sensitive_period}
A window in which an experience organises a system far more powerfully than it later can — language phonology, vision, attachment. Not a door that locks, but a gradient of cost. In the room: the difference between urgency and panic when a child presents late.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Ecological_model}
Bronfenbrenner's nested contexts: microsystem (family, class, peers), mesosystem (the links between them), exosystem (settings the child never enters but which reach the child: a parent's workplace, a school board's decision), macrosystem (culture, law, economy, belief), and chronosystem (time). In the room: the checklist that stops a formulation being a portrait of a child with the world airbrushed out.
:::

### Lecture I — The four questions and the child who carries them

**Proposition 1. Description precedes explanation, and description is harder than it looks.** Before any theory, someone had to watch: to notice that most children walk between nine and eighteen months, that a three-year-old explains rain as the sky crying, that a seven-year-old can hold a rule in mind and a four-year-old mostly cannot. Norms are descriptions with distributions, not deadlines; the counsellor who treats a norm as a deadline pathologises the tails of every curve.

**Proposition 2. Mechanism is where theories actually differ.** Maturation accounts (development as ripening), learning accounts (development as accumulated experience), and transactional accounts (development as a child–context loop) make different predictions about what an intervention can change and how fast. When two therapists argue about a child, they are usually arguing about mechanism while believing they are arguing about the child.

**Proposition 3. Universality is a finding, not an assumption.** Some sequences hold wherever anyone has looked — sitting before walking, babbling before words, desire-psychology before belief-psychology. Others, including several celebrated "stages", shift with schooling, language and the practices of a culture. The discipline of this volume: for every pattern, ask where it was found, in whom, and whether it has been found elsewhere.

**Proposition 4. Purpose governs method.** We study development in order to help particular children, and every method has a price: the laboratory gains precision and loses the child's Tuesday; the home observation gains the Tuesday and loses control; the parent report gains history and loses the present; the child's own report gains the inside and loses the outside. Method choice is therefore an ethical act, not a technical one.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Begin every case with five minutes of ecology before five minutes of child: who is in the microsystem, which links are working and which are broken, which exosystem decision landed on this family this month, which macrosystem belief is naming this child's difficulty, and what changed in time. The child's symptoms sit inside that map, never outside it.
:::

### The running case of this volume

**Ife and Kunle — a fictitious composite.** Ife is three years and eight months old. She speaks in three- and four-word sentences, insists on the same cup, hides behind her mother's wrapper when strangers greet her, and at nursery settles only after twenty minutes of watching from the doorway. Her brother Kunle is seven: quiet in class, quick at figures, called "proud" by his teacher because he does not ask for help. Their mother trades in the market six days a week; their father drives a commercial bus on night routes; their grandmother lives in the same compound and holds the household together. No real family is described here; every detail is invented for teaching, and the details will accumulate week by week as each theory meets them.

### Lecture II — Methods: how we could possibly know

**Longitudinal** designs follow the same children over time and can describe change within a child — the only design that can. Their prices are time, cost, attrition (the families who leave are not a random sample), and practice effects. **Cross-sectional** designs compare ages at one moment: cheap and fast, but they compare cohorts, not development; a difference between today's eight-year-olds and today's twelve-year-olds may be development or may be four years of history, schooling reform, or a pandemic. **Sequential** designs combine both and can separate age, cohort and time — the design behind the best modern claims, and the reason to read a "age effect" carefully.

**Observation**, naturalistic or structured, is the royal road of early development: the Strange Situation (week 6) is a structured observation, and your own practicum records will be naturalistic ones. Observation's bias is the observer: we see what our coding scheme permits, and children behave differently when watched — least of all when the watcher is a stranger with a clipboard.

**Informant reports** (parents, teachers) buy history and context and sell the present: parents are excellent historians of their child and poor calibrated instruments, and teacher reports are framed by class size and by what a teacher has been taught to worry about. **Child self-report** becomes possible in degrees: a four-year-old can report now, an eight-year-old can report yesterday, an adolescent can report a pattern — and each degree has a method that respects it.

**Tasks and experiments** isolate mechanism: the false-belief task (week 5), the conservation task (week 3), the scaffolded teaching session (week 4). Their bias is the strange adult and the strange room, which depress exactly the competences the task measures.

::: safeguard
Research and observation with children carry obligations heavier than with adults: consent from the guardian **and** assent from the child in words the child owns; the child's right to stop, honoured the moment it is signalled, not the moment it is spoken; findings returned to the family in usable form, because a family that lent you their child is owed more than a publication; and special care where the observer is also the counsellor, because a child cannot decline the person who holds their secrets.
:::

### Lecture III — Norms, ranges and the ethics of the curve

**Proposition 1. A norm is a description with a distribution, and the tails are children.** Every milestone curve is built from children who walked at nine and children who walked at eighteen; the curve does not prefer either, and a practice that treats the median as a deadline converts ordinary variation into a queue of worried parents and unnecessary referrals.

**Proposition 2. Norms carry their sample, and most samples are local.** Height, language and even motor milestones shift with nutrition, opportunity and the practices of a culture; a chart built elsewhere describes strangers until it is validated here. The honest use of an imported norm names its population every time it is quoted, which is week 14's discipline arriving early.

**Proposition 3. The range is the clinical object, not the point.** What the counsellor needs is not "the age" but the width: the interval within which development is ordinary, the flags outside which enquiry begins, and the direction and slope of this child's own course through it. A single measurement locates a child on a curve; two measurements, spaced, locate the child on a course — and courses, not points, are what developmental science and developmental counselling both trade in.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When a parent quotes a milestone app, give them the range, the sample and the slope in three sentences, and write the child's own two measurements beside it. Anxiety falls where the distribution becomes visible.
:::

### Worked example 1.1 — Choosing a design, paying its price

**Task.** A college asks: "does attending our crèche from age two make children's language faster at four?" Propose a design, name what it can establish, and name the confound that will be blamed for whatever you find.

**Solution.** A cross-sectional comparison of crèche attendees and non-attendees at four confounds attendance with everything that predicts attendance: mothers who enrol a two-year-old differ in education, income, language exposure and anxiety from mothers who do not, and those differences, not the crèche, will explain the gap in either direction. A longitudinal study from age two, measuring language at two before attendance diverges, and again at three and four, establishes sequence and controls the starting point, but attrition will remove precisely the most chaotic households, inflating every estimate. The strongest feasible design is sequential: two intake cohorts followed with the same measures, which separates cohort from age. The confound that will be blamed regardless: family language environment, which is why any honest report measures conversational turns at home (week 7) and states its limits in the first paragraph, not the last.

::: skills
**Practical 1 — two ways of seeing.** In pairs at the college observation nursery (or from a supplied recording): ten minutes of **narrative record** of one child — everything, in order, without interpretation — followed by ten minutes of **checklist record** of the same child using a supplied twelve-item list. Compare: what the narrative caught that the checklist could not; what the checklist caught that the narrative buried; and which record you would defend in a case conference. Write 300 words on the difference and keep both records: they return in week 14.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Developmental science answers four questions — what develops, by what process, with what universality, to what purpose — using methods whose biases must be named before their findings are believed; and it locates every child in nested contexts and in time, because development has an address.
:::
