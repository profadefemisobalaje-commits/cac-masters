# Atypical Development I: The Neurodevelopmental Presentations {.unit num="11" week="11" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **State** the neurodevelopmental group of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 and the developmental logic that binds it: onsets in the developmental period, deficits in personal, social, school or occupational functioning, and a course rather than an episode.
2. **Describe** ADHD dimensionally — inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity — with its developmental course, its executive-function substrate, and the referral biases by gender and context.
3. **Describe** autism spectrum presentation across ages, the early red flags, and the double empathy problem as a critique of one-sided social deficit accounts.
4. **Describe** specific learning disorders and their relation to language development, and explain why response to teaching is part of the definition.
5. **Build** a developmental timeline for one referred child, separating onset, course, pervasiveness and impairment from the presentation of the moment.
:::

### Where this week sits

Weeks 1 to 10 described the ordinary course and its variation. This week asks what it means when a child's development is not within the range of variation but on a different course — and insists that the question be developmental before it is diagnostic. A diagnosis names a pattern; a counsellor needs the pattern's history, its pervasiveness across rooms, its cost to this child's days, and the child's own account of their difference. The categories are useful; the categories are not the child.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Neurodevelopmental_group}
Intellectual developmental disorders, communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, specific learning disorder, motor disorders: grouped because they begin in the developmental period, affect functioning across contexts, and run as courses rather than episodes. In the room: the reason a neurodevelopmental question is always a history question.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Dimension_and_cut-point}
Attention, activity, social communication and reading skill are dimensions on which all children vary; a disorder is a cut-point on a dimension, placed where impairment begins. Cut-points are decisions, and decisions carry the biases of whoever decides. In the room: why "how much inattention?" precedes "is it ADHD?", and why the same child crosses the line in one classroom and not another.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Pervasiveness_and_impairment}
Two gates between difference and disorder: the pattern appears across contexts and informants (pervasiveness), and it costs the child something they want — friendship, learning, ease (impairment). In the room: the two questions that most referrals have not answered.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Double_empathy_problem}
Milton's critique: the social difficulty in autism is not a one-sided deficit but a mutual mismatch of communicative styles between autistic and non-autistic people; autistic people communicate readily with one another. In the room: the reorientation from "teaching the child to pass" to building a two-way bridge, and the humility it requires of the non-autistic practitioner.
:::

### Lecture I — ADHD as a difference in course

![Two developmental courses against the expected range: a late start that closes (delay) and a different slope whose gap widens with every term (disorder); the distinction is in the course, never in a single point. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-11-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Attention is a dimension; the disorder is the upper tail with impairment.** Inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity vary continuously across children; the diagnostic cut-point sits where the pattern is pervasive, persistent across six months or more, present in two or more settings, and costs the child function. The executive-function account (week 5) explains much of the texture: inhibition, working memory and shifting are the systems whose immaturity produces the lost instructions, the blurted answers, the unfinished page.

**Proposition 2. The presentation travels with context and with sex.** The same child is more impaired in a forty-pupil classroom with three transitions an hour than in a quiet room with one adult; and girls, whose presentation skews to inattention without disruption, are under-referred for years while boys with the same scores are over-referred relative to impairment. A referral rate that tracks disruptiveness rather than impairment is measuring the classroom's tolerance, not the child's difficulty.

**Proposition 3. Course matters more than state.** Onset in the developmental period, persistence across years, and a gap that widens as demands rise (Figure 11.1) distinguish the neurodevelopmental course from a state — an anxious child who cannot concentrate since the separation, a hungry child who cannot sit, a child taught in a language they do not yet hold. Each of those is treatable at cause, and each is mislabelled daily.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before any ADHD conversation, collect three settings' evidence (home, school, and your room or a community setting) and one week of demands: the pattern's pervasiveness is the first gate, and it is closed more often by absence of evidence than by absence of disorder.
:::

### Lecture II — Autism spectrum: red flags, course, and the two-way bridge

Autism spectrum presentation is defined by persistent differences in social communication and social reciprocity, and by restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interest or activity, with onset in the early developmental period and sensory differences threaded through both. The early red flags belong to every counsellor's literacy: no response to name by twelve months with hearing excluded, no declarative pointing, no shared enjoyment shown by bringing or showing, regression in language or social engagement at any age — the last an urgent referral whatever else is true.

Across ages the presentation changes shape: the preschooler's difference in pretend play and peer interest; the school-child's literalness, justice-sensitivity and special interest that is a resource as often as a restriction; the adolescent's exhaustion from camouflage, which presents as anxiety or refusal long before anyone names its cause. Camouflage, most documented in girls and in verbally fluent children, is the reason a diagnosis can arrive a decade after the suffering began.

The double empathy problem reorients the work: if the difficulty is mutual, the intervention is bilateral — the child's own communicative preferences honoured (directness, processing time, written options, the interest as a bridge), and the environment taught the child's language as the child is taught the environment's. A therapy whose goal is indistinguishability from peers has chosen a value and called it a treatment; a therapy whose goal is the child's own communication, comfort and competence has chosen the child.

::: safeguard
Diagnostic labels carry administrative consequences — support, funding, sometimes exclusion — and the counsellor sits at that hinge. Two duties follow: never let a label travel ahead of its evidence (a school's "he is autistic" is a hypothesis to assess, not a fact to file), and never let the absence of a label withhold support that a child's functioning plainly needs. Support follows function, not paperwork.
:::

### Lecture III — Specific learning disorders and the language beneath them

Specific learning disorder names persistent difficulty in acquiring academic skills — reading accuracy and fluency, written expression, mathematics — substantially below expectation for age and schooling, not attributable to intellectual disability, uncorrected sensory problems, or inadequate instruction. The last exclusion is the developmental point: a child who has not been taught to read has not a disorder but a debt, and response to systematic teaching is therefore part of the assessment, not an afterthought.

The reading difficulty sits on the language continuum of week 7: phonological processing — hearing and manipulating the sounds within words — is the substrate, and a child whose language history is thin (late talker, unaddressed hearing loss, or a school language that is not the home language without support) arrives at reading with one hand tied. Assessment must therefore name the child's languages and the language of instruction before any score is interpreted, and the bilingual child's reading difficulty must be assessed in both languages or not claimed at all.

Comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception: reading difficulty with attention difficulty, with language disorder, with the anxiety that years of public failure produce. The developmental cascade runs both ways — the child who cannot read stops reading, and stops reading into a widening gap (Figure 11.1's slope) — which is why the counsellor's first move for a learning difficulty is often protective: rebuild the child's relationship with being taught before the gap becomes identity.

### Worked example 11.1 — The timeline that refuses the label

**Task.** Chidi (6 y 4 m) is referred by his school as "hyperactive and unteachable": leaves his seat, calls out, one unfinished task in the record. Build the developmental timeline before any category.

**Solution.** *Onset:* mother dates the high activity and low settling to infancy; nursery records at three describe "always moving, joyful"; no onset event. *Course:* persistence across four years and three settings (home, nursery, school) — pervasiveness gate passed. *Impairment:* Chidi's own words — "teacher no like me; I no get star" — and the record: no finished task, two friendships strained; impairment gate passed, and the impairment includes the school's response. *Competing states excluded:* hearing and vision screened (clear); sleep history (adequate); language: home language differs from school language, and Chidi's school-language comprehension is thin — a contributor that must be measured, not assumed away; adversity: none reported, buffers strong. *Demands audit:* forty-one pupils, floor seating, three transitions before break, instructions given once, orally, in the school language. The timeline yields a formulation before a diagnosis: a pervasive, impairing, early-onset activity and attention pattern consistent with the ADHD dimension, sitting on top of a school-language comprehension gap and inside a classroom whose demands maximise his difficulty — three targets, one of which (the language gap) is treatable to cause and one of which (the demands) is free. The referral question "is it ADHD?" becomes "which of these three courses are we changing this term, and in what order?" — and the label, when a specialist confirms it, arrives as a description of the first course rather than as an explanation of the child.

### Worked example 11.2 — The girl nobody referred

**Task.** Amaka (9) is described as "quiet, dreamy, helpful"; her books are empty in three subjects; her mother says she "lives in her head". Teachers see no problem. Assess the referral gap.

**Solution.** Inattention without disruption is the classic under-referral: no classroom cost to the teacher, so no referral, while the child's cost accumulates in empty books and in the private conclusion that she is slow. The assessment sequence: three settings' evidence (home: long solitary play, forgets chores, loses materials; your room: warm, talkative once settled, working memory span thin for age on games; school: the empty books) — pervasiveness present; impairment present but internalised (shame rather than disruption); language and hearing checked; teaching adequacy checked (she has been taught; the gap widens despite teaching, Figure 11.1's slope). The formulation names the inattentive presentation of the attention dimension with impairment, plus the secondary self-concept wound of week 8 ("I am slow") that now protects her from trying. The intervention holds both courses: the attention course with the specialist and the school's accommodations (written as well as oral instructions, task chunks, seat placement), and the industry course with evidence-based praise at effort and finished-draft rituals. The safeguard note for the file: the referral gap was systemic, not accidental — record what would have caught her at six, so the next quiet girl is caught.

::: skills
**Practical 11 — the developmental timeline.** For a supplied referred case, draw the timeline page: conception to now, marking onset claims with their informant, setting changes, demands changes, assessments and their languages, and every competing state excluded with its evidence. Beneath it, three lines: pervasiveness verdict, impairment verdict (in the child's words if available), and the competing courses still open. One page, drawn, brought to the tutorial.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A developmental timeline case analysis of 1,000 words: timeline page plus written analysis separating onset, course, pervasiveness and impairment; competing states excluded with evidence; the child's languages and the language of instruction named; and a three-course formulation with the term's order of work.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Timeline complete, informants and dates marked | 25% |
| Pervasiveness and impairment gates argued | 25% |
| Competing states excluded with evidence, languages named | 25% |
| Formulation as courses with an ordered plan | 25% |

**Formative.** One paragraph: a child you know who was mislabelled or missed by a referral system; which gate failed, and whose tolerance the system was measuring.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Neurodevelopmental presentations are differences in developmental course, read through gates of pervasiveness and impairment on dimensions that all children vary along; attention, autism and learning difficulties each carry referral biases — by sex, by disruptiveness, by language — and each carries a critique that reorients practice, from the double empathy problem to the teaching-debt exclusion; so the practitioner builds the timeline first, excludes the states, names the languages, and lets the label arrive last, as a description of one course among the ones being changed.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why is "response to teaching" part of the definition of a learning disorder, and what does that make a school that has not taught?
2. A girl with the same attention scores as a referred boy is never referred. Whose tolerance is the system measuring, and what would catch her at six?
3. The double empathy problem: what in your own communicative style would an autistic child reasonably find difficult, and what will you change first?
4. Camouflage costs. What does a decade of passing buy a child, and what does it cost them?
5. Support follows function, not paperwork. Give one case where waiting for the label withheld the help.
