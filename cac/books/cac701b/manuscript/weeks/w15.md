# The Practicum: Three Ages, One Discipline {.unit num="15" week="15" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Formulate** three cases at three ages — toddler, school-age, early adolescent — using the one-page developmental instrument, under panel conditions.
2. **Defend** each formulation's weakest gate verdict and its falsifier under interrogation.
3. **Adapt** method and language to developmental age: play and parent work with the toddler, concrete external tools with the school-age child, collaborative hypothesis-testing with the adolescent.
4. **State**, for each case, who else must be told and what the family has agreed about telling.
5. **Give and receive** panel feedback in the register of the profession: mechanism, evidence, child.
:::

### Where this week sits

The volume's examination of practice. Three fictitious composites stand for the three doors your work will actually open: the toddler brought by a parent who has been told to wait; the school-age child brought by a school that has stopped waiting; the adolescent who has not been brought at all. Each is formulated in full, presented in five minutes, and defended for six. The discipline is the semester's: gates in order, the age in every sentence, the plan with its falsifier, and the child's own words somewhere in the room.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Age-graded_method}
The same instrument, three deliveries: with the toddler, the parent is the method and play is the language; with the school-age child, external tools — cards, charts, boxes, rituals — carry the work the inner speech cannot yet; with the adolescent, the work is collaborative hypothesis-testing between two people, one of whom is not yet sure they are a person. In the room: method follows development, not preference.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=The_waiting_parent}
The toddler case's true client is often the parent who has been told "wait and see" without a date, and whose trust in services is now the scarce resource. In the room: the dated wait (week 13) as the repair of a broken promise.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=The_referring_system}
The school-age case arrives inside a system that has already formed a verdict; the formulation must answer the system's question while refusing its adjective. In the room: the panel skill of holding two audiences — the child and the institution — without betraying either.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=The_unbrought_adolescent}
The adolescent case tests whether the formulation can hold a client who declines the room: engagement as the first intervention, consent and confidentiality as its materials, and the gate questions run on sparse data with the sparseness declared. In the room: the honest formulation's line, "what we do not know, and how we will earn it".
:::

### Lecture I — The three cases

**Case A — Zara, 2 y 6 m (fictitious composite).** Brought by her mother after a nursery remark that Zara "has no words like the others". Home language differs from the nursery's; no words in either language beyond four; no pointing at eighteen months by recall; hearing never tested. The mother has been told twice to wait, without dates, and asks, quietly, whether the professionals think she is a bad mother.

**Case B — Emeka, 8 y 2 m (fictitious composite).** Referred by his school with "attention and reading problems, possible dyslexia, mother uncooperative". Records: oral instructions lost, reading below class expectation, two unfinished assessments; the mother has missed three meetings. Your first phone call learns that the meetings were scheduled at her market hours and that she believes the school wants her son out.

**Case C — Titi, 13 y 4 m (fictitious composite).** Not referred: self-referred through a school counsellor's open-door hour, then stopped coming. One session attended: spoke of "nothing being worth the stress", of a friendship group that reorganised without her, and of a mother who "would fall apart if she knew anything was wrong". Declines further sessions; agrees to one more if "nothing is written down that my mum will see".

### Lecture II — The panel's questions, and the answers that hold

The panel asks six questions per case, and each has a legitimate shape of answer.

*What is normal here for this age?* — Zara: four words at thirty months is below expectation in any language and is a red-flag territory (week 7), but the absence of pointing at eighteen months is the heavier grain and the hearing test the first door; Titi: friendship reorganisation and mood volatility at thirteen are normative, but "nothing being worth the stress" is not a normative sentence at any age and is screened for risk in the session, not in the file.

*Which gate verdict is weakest?* — Zara: gate 1 (both languages unsampled) and the missing hearing test make every later verdict provisional; declared, not hidden. Emeka: gate 1 again — reading assessed in the school language only, and the "uncooperative mother" is a gate-1 finding about the system, not about the mother. Titi: gate 3 cannot be run at all on one session; the formulation states that its disorder question is open and names the single session that would advance it.

*What maintains this now?* — Zara: the waiting without dates (maintenance by system), plus possible hearing; Emeka: oral-only instruction plus a home–school rupture that removes the one person who could coach reading at night; Titi: avoidance of the reorganised group plus a family rule that the mother must be protected, which makes Titi the family's regulator at thirteen.

*What is the zone?* — Zara: joint-attention and gesture work with the mother as scaffold, assisted level now; Emeka: reading with written-plus-oral input and a phonological task with help; Titi: one more session, on her terms, as the assisted level of engagement itself.

*What is the falsifier, dated?* — Zara: home-language sample and hearing result by [date]; if combinations absent in the home language, gate 2 opens with a named referral. Emeka: four-week written-channel trial with teacher re-rating (week 14's variance method). Titi: if she does not return by [date], the counsellor initiates one low-demand contact (a note she collects, not a summons), and risk screening is repeated at that contact.

*Who else must be told, and what has the family agreed?* — Zara: the nursery, with the mother present when it is said; Emeka: the school's leadership and the mother in one meeting, the adjective withdrawn in the first sentence; Titi: confidentiality and its limits stated in her words at the start of session two, including the one circumstance in which the mother is told the same hour, and Titi's agreement recorded.

::: safeguard
Case C carries the week's sharpest edge: an adolescent's confidentiality is the material of engagement and a right, not a courtesy — and it has one limit the counsellor states before it is needed: risk to life. The sentence is prepared, not improvised: "What you tell me stays with me, except one thing: if I believe your life is in danger, I will tell you first, and we will tell together." A counsellor who cannot say that sentence plainly should not hold an adolescent's secrets.
:::

### Lecture III — Age-graded method: one instrument, three deliveries

![Method graded to development across the three ages of the practicum: with the toddler the parent is the method and play the language; with the school-age child external tools carry the work; with the adolescent the work is collaborative hypothesis-testing under an explicit confidentiality contract. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701b/figures/fig-15-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. With the toddler, the parent is the method.** A two-year-old cannot be treated around the caregiver; the intervention is the caregiver's sensitivity, language and routine, coached in play and in the kitchen rather than in the chair. The child's progress is measured in the dyad's interactions, and the falsifier is an interaction count, not a score.

**Proposition 2. With the school-age child, external tools carry the work.** Cards, charts, boxes, rituals and the fold in the page do the holding the child's inner speech cannot yet do (weeks 4 and 5); the tool is faded as the function internalises, and the fading is the record of success. Concrete operations mean concrete instruments: the eight-year-old's formulation belongs on a card the eight-year-old can hold.

**Proposition 3. With the adolescent, the work is two people testing hypotheses.** The early adolescent can hold minds-in-minds and hypotheses about themselves (volume 1, weeks 3 and 6); the method is therefore collaborative: the formulation is drafted with them, in their words, with its uncertainty visible, and the confidentiality contract stated before the secrets arrive. Engagement is not the prelude to this work; at thirteen, engagement is the work, and every other intervention waits on it.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before any session, ask the age question of the method: what can this child hold in mind today, and what must the room hold for them? The answer chooses the materials before the materials choose the session.
:::

### Worked example 15.1 — The five-minute presentation, Case B

**Task.** Present Emeka's formulation to the panel in five minutes, in the order the instrument requires.

**Solution.** Ninety seconds of description, opening with the mother's sentence ("they want my son out") and the school's ("possible dyslexia, mother uncooperative"), held side by side without adjudication. Thirty seconds of history: birth and milestones unremarkable; language in the home language strong by mother's account and untested by anyone else; reading taught in the school language from year one. Gate verdicts, thirty seconds: gate 1 failed by the system twice — no home-language sample, and the "uncooperative" label is a scheduling artefact now corrected by one phone call; gate 2 open — reading delay may be timing plus teaching-debt in a second language; gate 3 not met on current evidence — pervasiveness untested (home reading unobserved), impairment real but school-located. Mechanism, sixty seconds: oral-only instruction meeting a thin school-language comprehension (week 7), plus a home–school rupture removing the night coach (week 10), plus a class demand structure that maximises his loss (week 11) — three courses, one of which (the rupture) is repairable this month. Zone, thirty seconds: with written-plus-oral input and a phonological game with help, Emeka reads two clauses ahead of his alone level; the next independent step is one paragraph with a card. Plan and falsifier, sixty seconds: repair the alliance first — one meeting at her hours, the adjective withdrawn in the first sentence, the market-day schedule honoured; the written-channel trial four weeks with teacher re-rating; home reading in the home language twice weekly, mother as expert rather than defendant; falsifier dated at the re-rating. Close, thirty seconds, with Emeka's own sentence from the one observation session — "the book is shouting" — and the panel's sixth question answered before it is asked: who must be told is the school leadership and the mother, in one room, and the family has agreed to the telling provided the word "uncooperative" leaves the file by addendum this week.

### Worked example 15.2 — The panel's hardest question

**Task.** The panel asks on Case C: "Your formulation rests on one session. Why should we accept any of it?" Answer in the register of the profession.

**Solution.** "You shouldn't accept it as a formulation of Titi; you should accept it as a formulation of what one session can hold. What it holds: a thirteen-year-old whose mood sentence is not normative at any age and was screened for risk in the room, with a negative screen and a safety plan she co-wrote; a peer ecology that reorganised without her in the term's first month; a family rule that makes her her mother's regulator, which is a maintaining factor I can defend from two observations inside the session and from the literature on parentified children; and a confidentiality condition that is the engagement itself. What it does not hold: any gate-3 verdict, any zone beyond the next contact, and any claim about what she is like when no professional is present. The falsifier is the return date; the method for earning the next sentence is one low-demand contact she controls; and the file says all of this in those words. A formulation that declares its own thinness is not weak practice; it is the only honest practice on one session — and the alternative, a confident formulation built from nothing, is what panels should be failing."

::: skills
**Practical 15 — the practicum, run as panels.** In fives across the full block: each student presents one of the three cases (allocated so all three are presented across the group) for five minutes, defends for six, and panels twice. The fifth member records, per presentation, which of volume 1's five failure modes appeared (technique salad, lineage loyalty, false neutrality, unanswerable hedging, the child absent) and which of this volume's two developmental failure modes: the ageless formulation (no age-appropriateness verdict anywhere) and the gateless formulation (a category without gates). The cohort's three most common modes go on the board for week 16.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** The practicum portfolio: the five-minute recorded presentation of your allocated case, your panel record of two peers' presentations (one page each, in the register of mechanism–evidence–child), and a 300-word reflection naming your own dominant failure mode across the two volumes and its repair.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Instrument complete under time: gates, age verdicts, zone, plan, falsifier | 35% |
| Defence under interrogation: weakest verdict owned, sparseness declared | 25% |
| Age-graded method evident in the plan | 20% |
| Peer records in the professional register | 10% |
| Reflection: failure mode named and repaired | 10% |

**Formative.** Write the confidentiality sentence you will actually use with adolescents, in your counselling language, and rehearse it aloud once.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The practicum tests the volume's discipline at three ages: method graded to development, gates run in order with their weakest verdict owned, the referring system answered without adopting its adjective, the unbrought adolescent held by confidentiality and dated contact, and every formulation declaring its own thinness where it is thin — because the panel, like the family and like reality, trusts the practitioner who can show the work and name its edges.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Which of the three cases would you most dislike to receive on a Friday evening, and what in you makes it so?
2. "Mother uncooperative" is a gate-1 finding about the system. Name three other file adjectives that are really findings about services.
3. An adolescent's confidentiality has one limit. Why must the sentence be prepared before it is needed?
4. The ageless formulation: where in your own writing this semester did an age-appropriateness verdict go missing?
5. What did the panel process teach you about receiving feedback that the receiving of marks never could?
