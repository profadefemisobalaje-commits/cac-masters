# The Behavioural Lineage I: Learning Theory, Functional Analysis, and the Loop That Maintains {.unit num="5" week="5" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **State** the difference between respondent and operant learning, and give one child example of each.
2. **Complete a functional analysis** (ABC) from a supplied narrative, identifying the reinforcer that maintains the behaviour.
3. **Predict** the extinction burst and explain, in parent-friendly language, why behaviour worsens before it improves.
4. **Design** a simple contingency change for one maintaining factor, specifying what will be measured and for how long.
5. **State** the ethical limits on behavioural methods with children, including why aversive procedures are excluded from this programme.

### Where this week sits

Weeks 2 to 4 asked what goes on inside the child. This week asks what goes on around the child, and the change of question is the whole of the behavioural revolution: not "what does the behaviour mean?" but "what does the behaviour accomplish?" The two questions are not enemies. A formulation that ignores meaning is crude; a formulation that ignores consequence is usually ineffective. Daniel, our school-refusing nine-year-old from week 1, returns this week as a behavioural case, and you will see that the behavioural reading is not a denial of his anxiety but a plan for his mornings.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Reinforcement}
Anything that follows a behaviour and makes it more likely. Not a reward, not a treat, not something the adult intends as positive: the child's experience defines it. In the room: the attention a teacher gives to a child who calls out is reinforcement if calling out increases, however much the teacher intends it as a reprimand.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Negative_reinforcement}
The removal of something aversive, which strengthens the behaviour that removed it. The most powerful and least noticed force in child casework. In the room: staying home removes dread, so staying home strengthens; the parent's giving in removes the child's distress and the parent's own, so giving in strengthens. Two loops, one symptom.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Functional_analysis_(ABC)}
The discipline of writing down antecedent, behaviour and consequence, in order, repeatedly, until the pattern that maintains the behaviour is visible. In the room: the chart you complete before you propose any change. A behaviour analysed is half treated; a behaviour assumed about is usually mistreated.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Extinction_burst}
The temporary increase in a behaviour when its reinforcer is first withdrawn — bigger, louder, longer — followed by decline if the withdrawal holds. In the room: the fortnight in which a programme is abandoned, and the child learns that escalation works. Figure 5.2 is the most practically important graph in the course.
:::

### Lecture I — Two kinds of learning, and why the difference matters clinically

**Proposition 1. Respondent learning pairs a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned one until the neutral stimulus alone provokes the response.** The child bitten by a dog flinches at dogs; the child who was humiliated in a classroom feels dread at classroom doors. The response is not chosen and cannot be argued with. The clinical technology that follows is graduated exposure with prevention of avoidance: the conditioned response declines when the stimulus is met without the unconditioned consequence and without escape.

**Proposition 2. Operant learning selects behaviour by its consequences.** What is followed by reinforcement recurs; what is followed by nothing or by punishment declines — the latter with side effects (avoidance of the person who punishes, concealment, aggression) that make punishment a poor clinical instrument and, in aversive form, an excluded one.

**Proposition 3. The same topography of behaviour can serve different functions in different children.** Two children both "have tantrums in the supermarket". For one, the tantrum obtains sweets (positive reinforcement by access); for the other, it ends the shopping trip (negative reinforcement by escape). The identical behaviour requires opposite interventions. This is why functional analysis precedes intervention, and why copying another practitioner's plan for "tantrums" is not practice.

**Proposition 4. Social learning completes the picture: children learn by watching.** Bandura's demonstration that children reproduce modelled aggression — and, more usefully, modelled coping — put the adult's behaviour inside the model. Every parent, teacher and counsellor is a model whether they intend it or not, which is why behavioural work with children is usually work with the adults around them.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before any plan, ask of the behaviour: what does it obtain, and what does it escape? If you cannot answer both from observation, you are not yet ready to intervene; you are ready to observe.
:::

### Worked example 5.1 — Two functions, one topography

**Task.** In a Year 3 classroom, Chinedu shouts out during instruction roughly eight times an hour. The teacher's current response is a verbal reprimand each time, delivered at the whiteboard in front of the class. Over three mornings you record: on 6 of 8 occasions the reprimand is followed by peers looking at or answering Chinedu; on 2 of 8 the teacher sends him to the corridor, where he draws quietly and appears calm. Identify the candidate functions and the intervention each implies.

**Solution.** Candidate function 1: peer attention (positive reinforcement) — supported by the 6 occasions; the teacher's reprimand is the delivery mechanism for the peer audience. Candidate function 2: escape from instruction (negative reinforcement) — supported by the 2 occasions and by his calm in the corridor, which is reinforcing in itself. The two functions require different plans: for attention, deliver attention contingently on raised-hand behaviour and withdraw the audience from shouting (brief, neutral, no reprimand-as-performance); for escape, make the corridor unavailable as an escape route and teach a legitimate way to request a break. Treating only the attention function would leave escape intact and the behaviour would migrate. Note the trap in the teacher's current plan: the reprimand supplies both functions, which is why the behaviour is stable at eight an hour.

### Lecture II — Functional analysis as clinical discipline

Figure 5.1 draws the sequence and the loop. Three rules keep the analysis honest.

**Rule 1: describe, do not interpret.** "Refusal" is not a behaviour; "puts head on desk and does not respond to two prompts" is. Interpretation ("attention-seeking", "manipulative") ends observation and begins prejudice.

**Rule 2: the consequence that matters is the one that reliably follows, not the one the adult intends.** Intent is irrelevant to selection. Write what happened.

**Rule 3: analyse in the setting where the behaviour lives.** A functional analysis conducted in an office about a classroom behaviour is a story about a classroom behaviour. Wherever possible, observe, or obtain records from those present: frequency counts, time samples, or a simple ABC log kept for five school days.

![Functional analysis as an ABC sequence with the maintenance loop: antecedent, behaviour, consequence, and the negative reinforcement that returns the consequence to the antecedent as a strengthened expectation. The loop is the target of intervention. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-05-01.png){width=94%}

::: safeguard
Behavioural methods carry a history that the profession must own: aversive procedures were used on children, including disabled children, and caused harm. This programme excludes them absolutely. Reinforcement-based plans, extinction of reinforcement for harmful behaviour, and teaching of replacement skills are the permissible instruments; any plan whose mechanism is pain, fear or humiliation is not a treatment and is reportable professional misconduct. Where a child's behaviour is dangerous, safety planning precedes functional analysis, not the reverse.
:::

### Lecture III — Changing the consequence, and surviving the burst

If the loop is the target, the intervention is a change in what follows the behaviour, plus teaching of a behaviour that earns the same outcome legitimately. Four design principles follow.

**Match the replacement behaviour to the function.** A child who shouts out for attention must be able to obtain attention by raising a hand, quickly and reliably, or the plan will fail: extinction without replacement is simply removal.

**Make the new contingency immediate and certain.** Children discount delay steeply; a token at Friday's assembly cannot compete with attention now. Early in a plan, reinforce every occurrence; thin the schedule only after the behaviour is established.

**Expect and plan for the burst.** When reinforcement stops, the behaviour escalates before it declines — Figure 5.2. The burst is not evidence of failure; it is evidence that the reinforcer has been correctly identified. What destroys programmes is adult inconsistency during the burst: reinforcing escalation once teaches the child that escalation works, and produces a behaviour more resistant than the original.

**Measure.** A plan without a count is an opinion. Frequency, duration or interval recording, kept simply and kept daily, tells you by week two whether the loop has changed. Where measurement is impossible, the plan is not yet ready.

![The extinction burst: behaviour rate across sessions, with intervention beginning at session eight, a temporary peak as reinforcement is withdrawn, then decline. The annotation marks the point at which programmes are usually abandoned, which teaches the child that escalation works. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-05-02.png){width=94%}

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When you present a behavioural plan to parents, present the burst first. A parent who knows that Tuesday and Wednesday will be worse and that this means the plan is working will hold the line; a parent who meets the burst unprepared will abandon the plan and blame the child.
:::

### Worked example 5.2 — Daniel's mornings, behaviourally

**Task.** Return to Daniel (week 1): eleven days of non-attendance, mother sitting with him each morning, distress falling when he stays home. Write the ABC, name the reinforcer, and specify the first two weeks of a plan including what is measured.

**Solution.** A: morning routine reaches the point of leaving; mother present and available; father absent. B: complaint, distress, refusal. C: stays home; distress falls within the hour; mother's anxious attention is concentrated on him; school demand removed. Reinforcer: negative reinforcement (escape from school-related dread) plus, probably, positive reinforcement (mother's undivided presence). Plan, weeks 1–2: (1) reinstate attendance in graduated form — first hour only, with a named adult to meet him at the gate — so that attendance is achievable and therefore reinforceable; (2) change the morning consequence: mother's attention is given warmly and abundantly at breakfast and at reunion, and is brief, neutral and boring during refusal; (3) school agrees a predictable, non-negotiable welcome and a predictable, non-punitive routine for the first hour; (4) measure: minutes attended per day, recorded by the school, plus a daily one-line parent log of morning duration. Expect a burst in days 3–6: longer protests, possibly somatic complaints. Brief the mother on it in week 1, in writing. What the plan does not yet address is Daniel's catastrophic cognition — that is week 6's contribution, and the two plans will be combined in week 13.

::: skills
**Practical 5 — the five-day ABC.** Obtain (or, if you are not in placement, use the supplied narrative) a five-day record of one child behaviour. Complete an ABC chart for every occurrence. Then write: the behaviour in observable terms; the two candidate functions with the counts that support each; the reinforcer you judge operative; and one sentence of parent-facing explanation of the burst. Swap with a partner and critique: is any cell interpretive rather than descriptive?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A functional analysis and contingency plan of 1,200 words on a supplied case: observable behaviour definition, ABC summary table, function hypothesis with supporting counts, replacement behaviour matched to function, measurement plan, and a parent-facing paragraph that explains the burst.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Behaviour defined observably; no interpretation in the ABC | 25% |
| Function hypothesis supported by the recorded consequences | 25% |
| Replacement behaviour matched to function | 20% |
| Measurement plan feasible in the setting described | 15% |
| Parent-facing paragraph accurate and jargon-free | 15% |

**Formative.** Submit your one-sentence statement of the reinforcer for a case of your choosing (fictitious or fully anonymised) by the end of the week.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Behaviour is selected by its consequences, and the practitioner's first task is to discover what the behaviour accomplishes for this child in this setting; intervention then changes the consequence, teaches a behaviour that earns the same outcome, and survives the burst.
:::
