# The Systemic Lineage II: Solution-Focused and Narrative Practice with Children {.unit num="11" week="11" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Conduct** a scaling conversation, including the coping question and the "what told you it was a 4 and not a 3?" question, and explain why the latter is the intervention.
2. **Externalise** a problem with a child in the child's own naming, and map the problem's tricks and the child's counters.
3. **Identify** unique outcomes and use one to begin a re-authoring conversation with an audience.
4. **State** the evidence position for SFBT and narrative therapy with young people, including where claims outrun data.
5. **Explain** what these approaches share with, and refuse from, the problem-centred lineages of the course.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 10 treated the family as the unit and the problem as real. This week's two approaches make a different wager: that detailed analysis of the problem is not necessary for change, and that the story a child tells about themselves is not a report but a construction that can be re-authored. Both grew from the systemic lineage's discovery that language and interaction constitute the problem; both then went further than Minuchin would follow. Used well they are among the most respectful methods available for work with children; used lazily they are a way of not listening.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_scaling_question}
A 0–10 scale in the child's own metric, used not to measure but to discover: what is already working, what the next small step is, and what evidence the child already has of movement. In the room: "what told you it was a 4 and not a 3?" — because the answer lists the child's own competencies, in the child's words, and lists them as facts rather than compliments.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Externalisation}
The narrative move of separating the child from the problem: the problem becomes an entity with habits, tricks and intentions that can be described, resisted and outwitted. In the room: "the Worry" rather than "your anxiety" — a grammatical change with clinical consequences, because a child can fight a character in a way they cannot fight a trait.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Unique_outcome}
An event that the dominant problem-saturated story cannot account for: the night the Worry came and was not obeyed. In the room: the raw material of the alternative story, and easily missed because it contradicts the narrative everyone has come to believe.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Re-authoring_with_an_audience}
The deliberate recruitment of witnesses to the alternative story, because a story held by one person is a rumour and a story held by several is an identity. In the room: who needs to be told that she outwitted the Worry on Tuesday, and what will they say?
:::

### Lecture I — Solution-focused practice: the problem is not the necessary route

**Proposition 1. Solutions do not require analysis of the problem.** de Shazer and colleagues' radical claim is that knowing the aetiology is not necessary for constructing a solution; what is necessary is amplifying what already works. The corollary methods: the miracle question (what would be the first small sign, tomorrow, that the problem had gone?), exception questions (when was it slightly less bad, and what was different?), scaling, and coping questions (given how hard this is, how have you managed to keep going at all?).

**Proposition 2. The child is the expert on their own scale.** Scaling appears to be measurement and is actually respect: the metric, the anchors and the movement are the child's. Figure 11.1 draws the conversation; the therapeutic action sits in the gap between 4 and 3, which belongs to the child.

![The scaling conversation on a zero-to-ten line: the worst it got, today, and the next small step, with the question 'what told you it was a four and not a three?' whose answer is the intervention already in the child's words. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-11-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 3. Small is the unit.** SFBT works in increments the child can actually take this week, and treats the increment as the whole achievement rather than a step toward a professional goal. With children this is not modesty but accuracy: change in a child's life is made of small things repeated in small rooms.

**Proposition 4. The evidence is real and bounded.** Reviews of SFBT outcome research — Gingerich and Eisengart's frequently cited review among them — find a positive signal across studies of variable quality, with stronger effects in some child and school settings; the method is brief, acceptable and cheap. What the evidence does not support is superiority over active alternatives, nor its use as a substitute for assessment where risk or disorder is present. SFBT is an excellent first conversation and an excellent whole therapy for some presentations; it is not a risk assessment and must not be used as a way of avoiding one.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Resist the professional hunger for the story behind. In a solution-focused frame, asking "why" is not depth; it is a change of method. If you need the history this week, say so and take it elsewhere in the session — do not smuggle it into the scaling conversation.
:::

### Worked example 11.1 — A scaling conversation, transcribed in shape

**Task.** Emeka (10), referred after his parents' separation, rates "the shouting inside" at 3 today; two months ago it was 8. Write the four questions you would ask, in order, and what each is for.

**Solution.** (1) "What told you it's a 3 today and not a 2?" — discovers what is working: he names that he sleeps at his cousin's on Wednesdays and that he has stopped waiting by the phone. These are his competencies and his exceptions, recorded as facts. (2) "What would be the first tiny sign, a week from now, that it had moved to a 4?" — builds the next small step in observable terms: "I'd eat breakfast without my aunt asking twice." (3) "Back when it was an 8 and everything was worse — how did you manage to keep going at all?" — the coping question, which surfaces strength under the worst conditions and dignifies survival: "I drew. And I made sure my little brother wasn't in the room." (4) "Who has noticed that it's a 3? What did they say?" — recruits the audience and tests whether the change is socially real. Note the order: competence, next step, survival strength, witness. Note also what was never asked: why the parents separated, and how he felt about it. That history may matter later; it is not this method's door.

### Lecture II — Narrative practice: the story is the treatment

**Proposition 1. The dominant story organises experience.** A child described and describing themselves as "the bad one" will notice, remember and expect evidence for the story, and the story will recruit adults as its evidence-gatherers. The problem-saturated story is not a distortion to be disputed (week 6) but a narrative to be rivalled.

**Proposition 2. Externalisation creates the fighting position.** "The person is not the problem; the problem is the problem." Once the Worry, the Rage or the Black Dog has habits and intentions, the child can report on it, resist it, and — decisively — be morally repositioned: failures become the problem's victories rather than the child's defects. Figure 11.2 draws the separation and the audience that makes it stick.

![Externalisation as the separation of the child and the problem into two boxes with a describable relationship between them — the problem's tricks, the child's counters — and an audience of witnesses below, because a story held by several people is an identity rather than a rumour. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-11-02.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 3. Unique outcomes are the seed of the alternative story.** The search is deliberate and forensic: across the week, when did the Rage come and lose? Each exception is then thickened — who was there, what did it say about you, what would your grandmother call that? — until the alternative story has events, witnesses and a trajectory.

**Proposition 4. With children, narrative method becomes play and document.** The problem is drawn, given a size and a silly voice, interviewed ("Worry, what do you want for Chidi?"), and outwitted in planned operations. Letters and certificates from the counsellor, and definitional ceremonies in which chosen witnesses declare what they saw, do with paper and ritual what adult narrative therapy does with conversation. The documents are not decoration: for a child, an identity held in a letter in a school bag is durable.

::: safeguard
Externalisation has one strict limit: it must never be applied to responsibility for harm done to others. "The Rage made me hit him" is not an externalisation; it is an evasion wearing the method's clothes. Where a child has harmed, the work separates the child's identity from the problem while holding the child's responsibility for the act plainly — both at once, in the same sentence, without contradiction.
:::

### Lecture III — What the two share, what they refuse, and what the evidence allows

SFBT and narrative practice share a constructionist premise — problems are constituted in language and interaction — and a posture of not-knowing: the child is the expert, the practitioner is a curious editor rather than an interpreter. They refuse diagnosis, developmental tables and the analytic authority of the previous weeks; and they refuse, deliberately, the idea that the practitioner knows the goal better than the child.

The refusals carry costs that this course names plainly. A not-knowing stance in the face of a depressed fourteen-year-old who cannot generate exceptions is not respect but abandonment of clinical responsibility; where disorder, risk or safeguarding is present, the assessment obligations of weeks 3, 4, 9 and 12 govern and the methods of this week resume afterwards. The evidence, too, is asymmetric: SFBT has an outcome literature of mixed but genuine quality; narrative therapy's is thin, consisting largely of single cases and practitioner reports, so its claims should be held as a well-developed practice tradition rather than as an evidence-based treatment in the week 14 sense. Neither deficiency makes the methods worthless; both make honesty about them obligatory.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Borrow freely across lineages but declare the borrowings. A scaling question inside a CBT session is fine; calling the whole session solution-focused when you also ran a risk assessment and a cognitive experiment is not accuracy, it is costume.
:::

### Worked example 11.2 — The child's own name for the problem

**Task.** Funmi (8) calls her nightly terror "the Night Man". Using her naming, sketch the externalising conversation: three questions about the problem's habits, two about her counters, and one audience question. Then state what you would do with the parents.

**Solution.** Habits: "When does the Night Man come — what time, and what does he do first?" "What does he tell you about your mum and dad?" "What does he want you to do, and what happens if you do it?" Counters: "Think of a night he came and you didn't do what he wanted. What did you do instead?" "Who helped you that night, even a little?" Audience: "If the Night Man lost three nights in a row, who would be the first person who'd notice, and what would they say?" With the parents: teach the grammar — the Night Man is the adversary, Funmi is not; their job is to be on her side of the fight and to notice and report victories aloud, because a counter that nobody witnesses does not become identity. The naming is hers and must not be improved; "night terror" is your word and stays in your notes.

::: skills
**Practical 11 — scaling and externalising laboratory.** In triads, rotating. Round A (eight minutes): a full scaling conversation on a supplied card, including coping question and audience question; observer notes whether any "why" slipped in. Round B (eight minutes): externalise the card's problem using the "client's" own naming, map two tricks and two counters, and recruit one audience member. Debrief: which questions produced new information, and which produced politeness?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A recorded ten-minute solution-focused or narrative segment with a peer (your choice of frame), submitted with an 800-word commentary: the frame declared, the questions listed by type, one moment where the problem-saturated story reasserted itself and how you handled it, and one sentence on what the frame cannot see in this case.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Frame declared and held; question types identifiable | 30% |
| Child's (peer's) language and metric used, not the practitioner's | 25% |
| Handling of the problem-saturated reassertion | 20% |
| Honest statement of the frame's blind spot | 15% |
| Prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Bring one example of a child's own name for their problem, and what the naming made possible.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Solution-focused and narrative practices wager that change does not require the problem's full analysis — that competencies can be amplified and stories re-authored in the child's own language — and their discipline is knowing when the wager must yield to assessment, risk work and the other lineages.
:::
