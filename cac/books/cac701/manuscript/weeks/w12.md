### Where this week sits

Eleven weeks of theory, and not once have we asked who the theory was built on, by whom, and at whose expense. That question is not an add-on to the course; it is a condition of using the course honestly. The lineages you have learned were constructed overwhelmingly on, and by, people of European descent, in high-income countries, in clinical and university settings, with assumptions about family, self, emotion and authority that are local rather than universal. This week does not discard them. It teaches you to use them with the knowledge of their provenance, which is the difference between a tool and a prejudice.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Cultural_humility}
The stance of ongoing self-evaluation and curiosity about difference, as distinct from cultural competence conceived as a possessed inventory of facts about groups. In the room: the difference between "I know about Yoruba families" and "I do not know what being Yoruba, Christian, and twelve in this city means to you — teach me the parts that matter here."
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Positionality}
The practitioner's own social location — race, ethnicity, gender, class, faith, language, age, disability, sexuality, migration status — and the power it carries into the room before a word is spoken. In the room: the first thing the child experiences, and the thing least examined by the practitioner.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Intersectionality}
Crenshaw's account that systems of disadvantage do not add but intersect: a poor Black girl in state care is not girl plus Black plus poor but a distinct position produced by their interaction, which single-axis analysis and single-axis services cannot see. In the room: the reason "the family" you keep failing to engage is not one family-shaped problem.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Idiom_of_distress}
The culturally shaped form in which suffering is expressed and recognised: somatic complaint, spiritual experience, behavioural collapse, silence, possession idioms, anger. In the room: the reason a diagnosis can be both accurate and wrong — accurate to the manual, wrong to the person — and the reason referral thresholds misfire across cultures.
:::

### Lecture I — The theories were built on somebody

**Proposition 1. The samples are local.** Attachment research's foundational cohorts were American and Ugandan infants in particular conditions; CBT's trials over-represent white, middle-class, insured or university-adjacent families; the person-centred conditions were formulated in a mid-century American idiom of selfhood; systemic theory generalised from European and North American family forms in which the nuclear household and the speaking-of-feelings are norms. Each claim travels better than its critics allow and worse than its authors assumed.

**Proposition 2. The self the theories address is a particular self.** The autonomous, verbally reflective, individually bounded self who "has" feelings and "holds" beliefs is not the universal human but a cultural product. In many of the worlds your clients inhabit, the self is relational before it is individual: distress is a family event, healing is a communal process, and an intervention that isolates the child for individual insight can be experienced as an amputation. Figure 12.1 sets out the four questions that keep this in view.

**Proposition 3. The developmental tables are cultural artefacts too.** Milestones, "age-appropriate" behaviour, attachment categories and even the concept of adolescence are calibrated on particular populations. A nine-year-old who cooks for siblings, translates for parents, or cares for a grandmother is not parentified by every valid reading; some of those readings are class and cultural prejudice wearing a diagnostic coat.

**Proposition 4. None of this makes the theories useless.** It makes them hypotheses with a provenance. The mechanisms they name — reinforcement, appraisal, regulation, relational expectancy, narrative — are observed across cultures; the forms they take and the meanings they carry are not. The competent practitioner uses the mechanism and investigates the form.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before applying any instrument, manual or milestone to a child outside the population it was built on, ask: on whom was this calibrated, and what would differ here? If you cannot answer, say so in the record rather than implying a universality the evidence does not hold.
:::

### Worked example 12.1 — The accurate-but-wrong diagnosis

**Task.** Mr Osei (13, Ghanaian-born, in the country four years) is referred for "conduct problems": defiance, leaving class, an incident of striking a peer. School records note "no remorse". His mother describes headaches and a burning sensation in his legs, and says he "has been told" by an elder that the family's trouble follows him. Teacher and mother disagree completely about what is wrong. Formulate across the four quadrants.

**Solution.** Identity and language: his strongest emotional vocabulary may be in a home language in which no assessment has been conducted; "no remorse" may be a register of respect (not meeting an accuser's eyes, not speaking in self-defence) misread as callousness. Concepts of distress: somatic and spiritually framed idioms are the family's legitimate grammar of suffering; a formulation that reads them as superstition has already lost the case. Stressors and supports: migration, a father absent through the visa system, a mother working nights, an elder who is both support and authority; the school is a stressor, not a neutral observer. The relationship: a white assessor with a checklist is, to this family, an institution with a history; the mother's disagreement is data about that history, not resistance. The conduct formulation may still be partly right — contingencies maintain the leaving and the striking (week 5) — but it is a description of behaviour without its meaning, and acting on it alone would punish a child for an idiom. The plan: assessment in his language where possible; a cultural formulation interview with the mother; behavioural analysis of the classroom sequence; and a meeting in which the elder's account is heard as an account rather than corrected.

### Lecture II — Bias in assessment, and what a practitioner can do about it

Two biases are documented well enough that ignorance of them is negligence.

**Over-identification in special education and conduct diagnosis.** Black children — and in several systems, Black boys specifically — are over-represented in conduct disorder, emotional-and-behavioural-difficulty categories and exclusion statistics, and under-represented in autism and anxiety identification, at rates not explained by prevalence alone. The mechanism is not usually individual malice but the calibration of norms, the interpretation of affect and register, and the accumulation of small discretionary decisions. What you can do: use multiple informants and settings, use observation in more than one context, record the alternative explanations you considered, and audit your own referrals by category and ethnicity annually. What you cannot do: neutralise the system alone — which is a political and institutional task, and saying so is not an excuse.

**Under-recognition of distress in minority and migrant children.** Where idioms differ, internalising difficulty is missed until it presents as crisis or somatic emergency. What you can do: ask about somatic and spiritual experience directly and respectfully, use idioms-of-distress questioning in the cultural formulation, and treat a parent's framing as a hypothesis to test rather than an obstacle to remove.

Feminist critique adds the gendered layer: the girl whose distress is "attention-seeking" or "dramatic", the boy whose depression is read as anger, the carer roles assigned to girls and then pathologised as parentification, and the historical use of diagnosis to discipline women and girls who refuse. Intersectionality is the instruction to hold these together rather than in separate columns.

::: safeguard
Bias is a safeguarding issue: children are missed and children are mislabelled by the same mechanism, and both errors endure in records that follow them into adulthood. Where you inherit a file whose formulations carry unexamined cultural interpretation — "no remorse", "chaotic family", "poor engagement" — treat those phrases as hypotheses requiring re-evidence, not as findings.
:::

### Lecture III — Evidence-based practice as governance: the critique and what survives

The strongest version of the critique runs thus: what counts as "evidence" is decided by institutions with interests; the randomised trial privileges interventions that can be manualised and counted, which favours brief, individual, symptom-targeted treatments over relational, communal and structural work; the evidence base therefore shapes the services that get funded, the training that gets accredited, and ultimately the kind of suffering that gets recognised — a governance of distress in the language of science. Decolonial critique adds that the global export of these manuals reproduces a colonial pattern in which the North defines the South's minds, and local healing systems are positioned as folklore while imported protocols are positioned as medicine.

Three responses are available, and the honest position holds all three. First, the critique is substantially true about incentives and silences: the evidence base is not a mirror of what works but a map of what has been funded and measured. Second, the critique does not dissolve the obligation to prefer better-evidenced to worse-evidenced practice where a child is in front of you this afternoon; abandoning evidence because evidence is politicised abandons the child to fashion and to power exercised less visibly. Third, the resolution is practice-level and humble: use the evidence, name its provenance and its gaps in the record, measure your own outcomes with this child (week 14), and hold local knowledge as knowledge — which is what the cultural formulation is for.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Write one sentence in every formulation naming what you do not know about this child's world and how you intend to find out. The sentence is the audit trail of humility, and it is what a supervisor can actually check.
:::

### Worked example 12.2 — Positionality in one session

**Task.** Write a positionality statement of three sentences for yourself, then apply it: recall or role-play a first session with a child of a different gender, faith or class from you, and identify the first three moments your position was in the room.

**Solution (model).** Statement: "I am a [your descriptors] practitioner, trained in [lineages], working in [setting], and my accent, credentials and skin place me inside the institutions that have historically decided which children are disturbed. I have never been assessed by someone who could not pronounce my name, and I do not know what that does to a child's trust in the first ten minutes. My theoretical preferences reward verbal reflection, which is a cultural skill as well as a psychological one." Application: moment 1 — the child's greeting calibrated to an authority figure, not to me personally; I read it as guarded. Moment 2 — my opening question ("how does that make you feel?") assumed a feeling vocabulary and a legitimacy of feeling-talk that the child's household may not share; the silence that followed was my question's property, not the child's deficit. Moment 3 — my note afterwards said "poor eye contact", a norm of my culture written as an observation of his. Each moment is small; the file is made of them.

::: skills
**Practical 12 — the adaptation audit.** In pairs, take one manualised protocol from weeks 5–7 (a graded exposure plan, a thought record, a scaling conversation). Task: identify three points at which the protocol assumes a cultural norm (language, family structure, autonomy of the child, concepts of emotion, time and punctuality, authority), and design one adaptation at each point that preserves the mechanism while changing the form. Criteria: the mechanism must survive; the adaptation must not become a different therapy; the child's community must be able to recognise themselves in it. Present one adaptation to the group.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A critical review of 1,200 words: choose one lineage from weeks 2–11 and one population it was not built on. State what in the lineage travels, what does not, and what a practitioner must add; support with at least one empirical source on bias or adaptation and one source from the population's own scholarship where available.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Provenance of the lineage stated accurately | 25% |
| What travels and what does not, argued rather than asserted | 30% |
| Practical additions specified at mechanism level | 25% |
| Sources include scholarship from the population concerned | 10% |
| Prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Submit your three-sentence positionality statement. It is not graded; it is used in supervision.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The critiques do not retire the lineages; they date and place them. The practitioner's competence is to use each mechanism while investigating its form, to audit one's own position and one's own referrals, and to hold the evidence base as the best available map rather than the territory.
:::

1. Is cultural humility a skill or a virtue? What would training each look like, and which can be examined?
