# The Systemic Lineage I: From Mind to System — Bateson, Circularity, and Structural Family Therapy {.unit num="10" week="10" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Explain** the shift from linear to circular causality and reformulate one linear case statement as a loop.
2. **Draw a structural family map** showing subsystems, boundary quality (clear, enmeshed, disengaged) and any cross-generational coalition.
3. **Describe** joining and enactment as Minuchin's two principal techniques, and demonstrate an enactment in role-play.
4. **State** the ethical limits of systemic thinking with children: what it must never be used to say about abuse, and who is responsible for what.
5. **Plan** systemic-informed work when the family will not attend, using whoever is in the room.

### Where this week sits

Every lineage so far has located the problem inside the child — in drives, contingencies, cognitions, conditions of worth, or the meeting of givens. This week moves it into the space between people, and the move is not an addition to the previous weeks but a change of level: the same child can be formulated intrapsychically, behaviourally and systemically at once, and a practitioner who cannot move between levels will keep treating the wrong unit. Daniel returns this week for the third time, and his family will finally be in the room.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Circular_causality}
The claim that behaviour in a family is organised in loops rather than chains: each person's move is a response to the previous move and a stimulus for the next, so "who started it" is usually unanswerable and always unhelpful. In the room: the question "what happens next, and who notices first?" replaces "why did you do that?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Boundaries}
The rules about who is in on what: between parents and children, between siblings, between family and outside. Clear boundaries permit both belonging and separateness; enmeshed ones permit no private position; disengaged ones permit no recourse. In the room: the family map's principal diagnostic, and the target of structural work.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Enactment}
Minuchin's technique of asking the family to do in the session what they do at home — "tell your mother now what you told me" — so that the structure is observable and available for change rather than reported. In the room: the difference between a family described and a family present.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Identified_patient}
The member who carries the symptom and, often, a function: the child whose refusal keeps two parents in contact, whose wetting keeps a grandmother in the house, whose rage keeps everyone from the grief. In the room: the reason treating the child alone so often fails, and the reason systemic language must be used with care — a function is not a blame.
:::

### Lecture I — From the mind to the loop

**Proposition 1. The cybernetic turn made the relationship the unit.** Bateson and colleagues' work on communication — including the double bind hypothesis, whose specific schizophrenia claims did not survive testing but whose insight about contradictory messages in trapped relationships endured — relocated pathology from the individual to the pattern of messages between individuals. What survived is methodological: observe the sequence, not the person.

**Proposition 2. Linear causality produces blame; circular causality produces a map.** "The mother's anxiety makes the child refuse school" is linear and indicting. The loop is different and larger: the child refuses, the mother's anxiety rises and she presses and stays close, the father withdraws from the conflict, the child's refusal deepens to hold the mother's attention and to spare the parents their quarrel, the school exempts, the loop tightens. Nobody is the author; everybody is a participant; the loop is the target. Figure 10.2 draws the generic form.

![Circular causality as a four-node loop: the child withdraws, the parent presses and worries, the child withdraws further, the parent rescues and then reproaches, and the cycle repeats. Each move is a sensible response to the previous one; the loop, not the person, is the problem. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-10-02.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 3. Symptom and system can stand in a stabilising relationship.** Homeostasis is the observation that families resist change, including beneficial change, because the current arrangement solves something: the child's symptom may be regulating distance, detouring conflict, or maintaining a coalition. This is the most dangerous idea in the week, because misused it becomes "the child is doing it on purpose to hold the family together" — which is false, unjust and harmful. The accurate form is: the symptom has been recruited by the organisation, without anyone's intention, and the organisation will push back when the symptom is removed. Prepare for the pushback; do not accuse the child of it.

**Proposition 4. Circular questioning is the assessment instrument.** Asking each member what they observe between two others ("when your parents argue, what does your brother do?") generates the map faster and more safely than asking anyone about themselves, and it begins the intervention, because hearing the loop described is often the first time the family has seen it.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before any family session, write the loop you hypothesise in four or five arrows. If you cannot, you will collect stories in the room rather than observe a system, and the loudest narrator will set your formulation.
:::

### Worked example 10.1 — Reformulating the linear statement

**Task.** A referral states: "Chidera (12) is manipulative and divides her parents; the father is weak and the mother is overbearing." Reformulate as a loop and state what the reformulation changes about the first session.

**Solution.** Loop: the parents disagree about discipline and do not resolve it (their conflict is undiscussable); Chidera's requests and complaints travel between them, and each parent's separate deal with her is a move in their marital conflict (coalition across generations); the father's withdrawal reads to the mother as abandonment, so she intensifies her involvement with Chidera (enmeshment); Chidera's escalating demands are the only channel through which the parents interact (symptom function); each escalation confirms the parents' view of each other (weak/overbearing), tightening the loop. "Manipulative" disappears: she is the conduit, not the author. First session changes: invite all three; do not begin with Chidera's behaviour; ask each what happens between the other two when a rule is disputed; draw the map live on paper where the family can correct it; and protect Chidera from the position of being the family's explanation — which is also the position that keeps her symptomatic.

### Lecture II — Structural family therapy: subsystems, boundaries, enactment

Minuchin's contribution was to make the system treatable in the room rather than merely describable. Three elements carry the method.

**The structural map.** Subsystems (parental, sibling, spousal), boundaries and their quality, and coalitions — especially the cross-generational coalition in which a parent and a child are aligned against or apart from the other parent. Figure 10.1 draws Daniel's family in these terms, and the drawing is the diagnosis: an enmeshed mother–child dyad, a disengaged father, and a school in coalition with the dyad across the generation of professionals.

![A structural family map of Daniel's family: parental subsystem with a disengaged father, an enmeshed mother-child dyad drawn with a double line, and a dashed cross-generational coalition with the school's informal exemption from attendance. Boundaries are the diagnosis; the symptom sits in the child but the organisation that maintains it does not. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-10-01.png){width=94%}

**Joining.** The therapist enters the family's style before challenging it: accommodates to their idiom, affirms each member's position, tracks their language. Joining is not warmth as manner; it is the purchase that makes the next move survivable. Without it, interventions are experienced as invasion and the family closes or leaves.

**Enactment and boundary making.** The therapist asks the family to perform the problematic sequence in the session and then alters it live: "tell your father now what you just told me"; "mother, let them finish without helping"; "father, what would you say to your son about this, if it were only the two of you?" The mechanism is experience: a new interaction, witnessed and repeated, changes the structure more durably than an explanation of it. Unbalancing — siding deliberately with the peripheral member to shift the hierarchy — is the advanced form and requires joined-ness first.

::: safeguard
Systemic formulations must never be used to distribute responsibility for abuse. Where a child is harmed by an adult, the perpetrator is responsible, full stop; circular language applied to violence or sexual abuse ("the family co-constructed the context") is a professional failure with real victims. Systemic thinking explains organisation, maintenance and recovery; it does not explain away harm, and safeguarding duties run to the child individually regardless of any family formulation.
:::

### Lecture III — When the family will not come

The pure model assumes the family in the room. Reality, especially in online and school-based practice, is frequently the child alone, or one parent. Four adaptations preserve the lineage without the full cast.

**Work the loop with whoever attends.** The child can report sequences; the loop can be drawn with the child and corrected; the child's own moves in the loop can change, and a change in one position alters the whole pattern. This is not second-best; it is systemic work at one node.

**Use the telephone and the letter.** A ten-minute call to a non-attending parent to ask a circular question ("what does your wife do when Daniel cries in the morning?") both gathers data and perturbs the system: the question travels home.

**Brief the professionals in the loop.** Teachers and carers are participants; changing the school's response to refusal (week 5) is systemic intervention by other means.

**Hold the absent in the room.** An empty chair, a genogram, a letter written and not sent: the absent member's position is represented so that the child is not the sole carrier of the family's story.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** A child alone in a room is still a member of a system, and your questions can be systemic even when your seating is not. "What happens at home just before it gets worse?" is a systemic question asked of one person.
:::

### Worked example 10.2 — An enactment, scripted

**Task.** In session with Daniel and both parents (father attending for the first time), you hypothesise that the mother answers for Daniel and the father has no position. Write the enactment you would set, the sentence you would use to initiate it, and two things you would watch for.

**Solution.** Initiation, after joining with each: "Daniel, you told me last week what the morning feels like. I'd like you to tell your dad now, here, while your mum and I listen. Dad, your job for the next five minutes is only to listen and then say back what you heard — not to fix it." Watch for: (1) the mother's first intervention — its timing and form are the enmeshment datum, and when it comes you name it gently and return the floor to Daniel ("mum, I know the answer; let's hear his"); (2) the father's capacity to reflect rather than fix or dismiss — his first accurate reflection is the session's change event, and is marked and repeated ("say that again to him; that's the sentence he hasn't heard"). The enactment converts the map into an experience, and the experience — not the map — is what the family takes home.

::: skills
**Practical 10 — map and enactment.** In fours. One supplies a family (card or anonymised experience); the group draws the structural map in ten minutes (subsystems, boundary qualities, coalition) and agrees one hypothesis. Then a fifteen-minute role-play: two as parents, one as child, one as therapist who must join, then set one enactment and intervene once in it. Debrief: was the joining real or performed? did the enactment target the hypothesis? who in the family was protected by the therapist's move?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A structural formulation of 1,200 words on a supplied family vignette: the map drawn and justified from the text, the loop written in arrows, one enactment specified with its initiating sentence and its target, and a paragraph on what the formulation must not be used to say.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Map justified from the material; boundary qualities named | 30% |
| Loop written circularly; no linear blame | 25% |
| Enactment targets the hypothesis and is survivable | 25% |
| Ethical limits paragraph accurate | 10% |
| APA 7th and prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Bring one family in which the identified child was not the problem, with the observation that showed it.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The systemic lineage changes the unit of treatment from the person to the pattern: circular causality replaces blame, boundaries replace traits, and enactment turns the family's structure into an experience that can be altered in the room — with the absolute limit that no formulation distributes responsibility for harm.
:::
