# The Humanistic Lineage II: Existential Counselling and Meaning Work with Adolescents {.unit num="9" week="9" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Name** the four existential givens and give, for each, the form it typically takes in adolescence.
2. **Distinguish** a philosophical meaning question, a depressive cognition, and a statement of suicidal intent, from three near-identical utterances, and state the different obligation each creates.
3. **Conduct** a meaning-in-adversity conversation using at least three of Frankl's routes (creation, experience, attitude) without resorting to reassurance.
4. **Explain** what existential counselling adds to, and refuses from, technique-based practice.
5. **State** the limits of existential method with children below adolescence, and what you would use instead.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 8 located change in the relationship. This week locates the material that relationship must eventually hold: the questions that have no technical answer. Adolescents ask them plainly — *what is the point, why does anyone bother, does it matter that I die?* — and the professional reflex is to treat the question as a symptom. Sometimes it is. Often it is the healthiest thing in the room, and the adolescent's first test of whether an adult can bear reality. This week teaches you to bear it, and to know precisely when the question stops being philosophical and becomes a risk assessment.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_givens}
The conditions no therapy removes: mortality, freedom (and its responsibility), existential isolation, and the absence of guaranteed meaning. In the room: not topics to be resolved but ground to be acknowledged. An adolescent who meets them with company develops; alone with them, they are at risk.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Will_to_meaning}
Frankl's claim that the primary human motivation is not pleasure or power but meaning, and that meaning remains available in suffering through three routes: what we create, what we experience and love, and the attitude we take toward what cannot be changed. In the room: the three doors you can open with a young person whose circumstances will not improve this term.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Tragic_optimism}
Optimism that has looked at the tragedy and stayed: saying yes to life in spite of pain, guilt and death. Not positivity, which is its counterfeit and is detected instantly by adolescents. In the room: the difference between "it will get better" and "this is awful, and you are still here, and what you do with it is still yours".
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Existential_anxiety}
Anxiety in the face of a given, as distinct from neurotic anxiety disproportionate to its object. In the room: the dread before an exam that is about the exam, and the dread before an exam that is about being a self who can fail in front of others for the rest of a finite life. Only the second is existential, and only the first responds to study skills.
:::

### Lecture I — The givens, in adolescent form

**Proposition 1. Adolescence is when the givens arrive.** The child lives in a world whose meanings are supplied; the adolescent discovers that the supply was local and contingent. Death becomes personal ("I will not be here, and the world will continue without me, which means I was optional"); freedom becomes visible ("no one is actually choosing for me, and the choosing does not stop"); isolation becomes literal ("no one can be inside my head, and the people who claim to understand me are guessing"); and meaning becomes a question rather than a background.

**Proposition 2. The questions are developmental, not diagnostic.** A fifteen-year-old who asks whether anything matters is, in the ordinary case, doing the work of the age. Pathologising the question teaches the adolescent that reality-talk is symptomatic, and they will stop bringing reality. The clinical task is first to establish which case you are in — philosophical, depressive, or at-risk — and Figure 9.1 sets the four givens in the idiom in which they actually arrive.

![The four existential givens in adolescent idiom — mortality, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness — arranged around the central adolescent question of who one is to be. The givens are not pathologies; an adolescent meeting them is developing, an adolescent alone with them is at risk. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-09-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 3. Identity is the givens' meeting place.** The question "who am I?" is freedom experienced from the inside: the self as a task rather than a fact. Existential counselling reads identity work not as a stage to be completed but as a stance to be owned — and reads identity foreclosure (the self adopted wholesale from family, faith or gang without examination) as a defence against freedom, to be respected and, in time, questioned.

**Proposition 4. Existential method is a stance, not a toolkit.** There is no technique manual because the position is that no technique reaches the givens; what reaches them is a relationship in which reality can be said aloud. The discipline is therefore negative as much as positive: no reassurance that cancels the given, no interpretation that reduces it, no hurry.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When an adolescent states a given, your first response should acknowledge it as true before anything else happens. "Yes — nobody can be inside your head. That is real, and it is lonely, and you bringing it here is not a symptom." Reassurance first is a refusal of company.
:::

### Worked example 9.1 — Three utterances, three obligations

**Task.** Classify each and state your next action. (a) Tunde (16): "Honestly, does any of it matter? We'll all be dead in a hundred years and nobody will remember the essay." (b) Amara (15): "Nothing matters. Not in a deep way. In the way that getting up is pointless and I'd rather not." (c) Kele (17): "Nothing matters. Which is fine, because it won't be a problem for much longer."

**Solution.** (a) Philosophical: the affect is lively, the framing general, the invitation argumentative. Obligation: take the argument seriously — sit with it, offer company and counter-thought, let it be a conversation between two people who will die. Treating it as symptomatic ends the adolescent's reality-talk. (b) Depressive: the anhedonic register ("I'd rather not"), the loss of appetite for life rather than a question about it. Obligation: assess for depression — sleep, appetite, concentration, hopelessness, duration, function — and treat or refer accordingly; the existential conversation returns when the depression lifts, and can continue alongside it. (c) At risk: "won't be a problem for much longer" is a statement about the future absence of the speaker. Obligation: immediate risk assessment per procedure — intent, plan, means, previous attempts, current safety, who knows — and safety planning and escalation as CAC. 709 and CAC. 703 require. The three sentences differ by a clause; the obligations differ by a world. Practitioners who cannot hear the clause are dangerous.

::: safeguard
Any statement that implies the speaker's future absence is a risk-assessment trigger, whatever its philosophical packaging. Assess directly, in plain words, the same session: are you thinking of killing yourself? Direct questioning does not implant the idea and does not increase risk; evasion does both. Follow your jurisdiction's and placement's procedure for recording, informing and escalating, and remember that confidentiality with a minor has stated limits which the young person should already know.
:::

### Lecture II — Meaning in adversity: the three routes

Frankl's account was written from the camps and must be used without romanticising them; what survives scrutiny is the structure. Where circumstances cannot be changed, meaning remains accessible through three routes, and each translates into conversational moves with a young person.

**Creation — what the child makes and does.** Work, art, care of another, a team, a garden, a channel, a protest. The question is not "what do you enjoy?" but "what is yours to do, that would not be done if you did not do it?" For a young person in a care home or a hospital ward, the answer is smaller and more real than any ambition: the Tuesday group that only happens because they turn up.

**Experience — what the child receives and loves.** Beauty, humour, friendship, a piece of music, being known. The route most attacked by depression and most defended by relationship: the counsellor's job is often to protect one small experience a week from the timetable that would cancel it.

**Attitude — the stance toward what cannot be changed.** The diagnosis that will not reverse, the parent who will not return, the sentence already served. Here the work is neither acceptance-as-resignation nor fighting, but the discovery that a position remains available: how this is borne, whom it is borne for, what it is made to testify to. This route is where existential counselling meets the third wave's acceptance (week 7) and the two lineages can be taught together without confusion: ACT supplies processes, existentialism supplies the seriousness.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Never use the attitude route to flatter a child into bearing what an adult should change. Where adversity is removable — poverty, bullying, an unsafe placement — the meaning conversation is a consolation and the removal is the treatment. Know which you are offering.
:::

### Worked example 9.2 — A meaning conversation that is not reassurance

**Task.** Zainab (16) has sickle cell disease with a third admission this term; she says, "My body is a prison and everyone's just waiting for me to be grateful it isn't worse." Compose the shape of a response using the three routes, and name what you must not say.

**Solution.** Acknowledge first: the prison is real, the gratitude demand is real, and her anger at it is accurate. Creation: what is hers inside the ward and after — the physics she tutors younger patients on WhatsApp, which stops when she stops. Experience: what survives admission — the playlist, the cousin who visits, the night nurse who doesn't do the gratitude speech. Attitude: not "be brave" but her own position — she has already chosen one, refusing to be inspirational, and that refusal is a stance with dignity in it that can be named and owned: "you've decided what this will not be; what have you decided it will be?" Forbidden: "at least it isn't worse", "everything happens for a reason", "you're so strong", and any version of the gratitude demand she has just told you about. The test of the response is whether Zainab's reality is still in the room at the end of it.

### Lecture III — What the lineage refuses, and what it cannot do

Existential counselling refuses three things that the rest of the course supplies, and the refusal is its contribution. It refuses **technique-as-answer**, insisting that some material is met rather than treated. It refuses **normalisation-by-category**, insisting that this adolescent's death-anxiety is not an instance of a disorder but a person meeting a given. It refuses **the tyranny of adjustment**, insisting that a young person at odds with an unjust situation may be accurately at odds, and that the counsellor's loyalty is to the person rather than to the situation's smooth running — a position with obvious kinship to week 12's critique.

What it cannot do is equally clear. It is not a treatment for disorder: depression, psychosis, trauma reactions and anxiety disorders require the methods of weeks 5 to 7 and 15 to 21 of the programme, and existential stance is the ground on which those methods are delivered humanely, not a substitute for them. It is developmentally limited: below early adolescence the givens are not yet available as objects of reflection, and work with children proceeds through play, relationship and changed circumstances. And it is culturally situated: the givens as formulated are a particular (largely European) philosophical inheritance, and other traditions frame death, self and meaning differently — which is precisely why week 12 follows this one.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Keep the lineage in its place: existential counselling is the room's floor, not its furniture. When a technique is needed, use it; when a given is spoken, put the technique down.
:::

::: skills
**Practical 9 — the meaning interview.** In pairs, twenty minutes. One plays an adolescent who has lost something real and unchangeable (cards supplied: a diagnosis, a death, a migration, a conviction in the family). The other conducts a meaning conversation: acknowledge the given without reassurance; explore one creation route, one experience route; invite an attitude position in the young person's own words; close by asking what they want the adult world to stop saying. Observer codes: any reassurance? any interpretation? any hurry? whose words ended the session? Swap and debrief.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A transcript analysis and composed response of 1,000 words: classify three supplied utterances (philosophical, depressive, at-risk) with the linguistic and affective evidence for each, state the obligation each creates, and write the opening of your response to the at-risk utterance including the direct question you would ask.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Classifications justified from the wording and affect | 30% |
| Obligations stated correctly, including procedure for risk | 30% |
| Composed response acknowledges before it does anything else | 20% |
| Direct risk question present and plain | 10% |
| Prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Bring one question of this kind that a young person has asked you, and what you actually said.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The existential lineage teaches that some material is met rather than treated, that the adolescent's big questions are usually developmental rather than diagnostic, and that the practitioner's first competence with them is the capacity to hear exactly which of the three cases — philosophical, depressive, at-risk — is in the room.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why is reassurance a refusal of company? Give the mechanism and one alternative first sentence.
2. A school refers a sixteen-year-old for "morbid preoccupation with death" on the evidence of poetry. What is your position, and what would change it?
3. Where is the line between tragic optimism and collusion with an unchangeable-but-removable adversity? Give a case.
4. The givens are a European inheritance. What is lost and what is gained by teaching them in a multicultural programme?
5. What did you do with the young person's question in your formative task, and what would you do now?
