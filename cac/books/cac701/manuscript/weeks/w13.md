# Integration I: Case Formulation Across Competing Accounts {.unit num="13" week="13" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Write a five-P formulation** (presenting, predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective) of a complex case in one page, with the mechanism of change named.
2. **Hold four readings of one child simultaneously**, stating what each contributes, what each forbids, and which governs this week's plan.
3. **Specify a falsifier** for a formulation — the single observation that would show it wrong — and say what you would do if it appeared.
4. **Sequence interventions** from multiple lineages without contradiction, identifying where two lineages' first moves would cancel each other.
5. **Present a formulation to a family** in ordinary language, including its uncertainty.
:::

### Where this week sits

Twelve weeks of lineages, each claiming a share of the truth. This week is where the course stops being a survey and becomes a practice: the discipline of writing one coherent account of one child that uses several lineages without being owned by any. Integration is not eclecticism. Eclecticism takes techniques; integration takes responsibilities — for the whole child, the whole plan, and the whole of what happens next.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Formulation}
A hypothesis, not a diagnosis: a written account connecting history, maintaining factors, context and protective factors into one argument that implies a treatment and can be wrong. In the room: the paragraph a colleague could continue the work from, and the paragraph you would defend to a panel.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=The_five_Ps}
Presenting, predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective. A skeleton that survives supervision because it forces the two questions practitioners skip: what maintains this now (perpetuating), and what holds this child up (protective). In the room: Figure 13.2 — perpetuating is where treatment lives, protective is where hope lives.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Governing_lineage}
The lineage whose account organises this week's plan, chosen with reasons and revisable. Not the practitioner's favourite: the one whose mechanism best fits the maintaining factors and whose first move is safe and feasible now. In the room: the answer to "why are you doing that, this week?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Falsifier}
The observation, specified in advance, that would show the formulation wrong. In the room: the difference between a hypothesis and a story. A formulation without a falsifier cannot learn, and a practitioner whose formulations cannot be wrong cannot improve.
:::

### Lecture I — Why integration is hard, and what goes wrong without it

**Proposition 1. The lineages' first moves can cancel each other.** A behavioural plan for school refusal requires the parent to stop accommodating; a person-centred stance requires the practitioner to stop directing; a systemic enactment requires the family in the room; an attachment-informed plan requires work with the mother's anxiety first. Done in the same fortnight without sequencing, these interfere: the child experiences contradiction, the parents experience chaos, and each lineage's failure is blamed on the others. Integration is therefore primarily a sequencing discipline.

**Proposition 2. The formulation is the integrating instrument.** Not a summary of everything known but a selective argument: which factors maintain, which mechanism is chosen, which protective factors the plan will use, and what would falsify it. Figure 13.1 draws the convergence and the two outputs — the plan and its falsifier. Selectivity is the virtue: a formulation that lists forty factors explains nothing and commits to nothing.

![Four readings of one child converging on a single formulation — attachment/psychodynamic, behavioural/cognitive, humanistic/existential and systemic/cultural — expressed as predisposing, precipitating, maintaining and protective factors with the mechanism named, and issuing in a plan with its falsifier. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-13-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 3. Evidence informs the choice but does not make it.** Week 14's three-legged stool stands behind this week's paragraph: the research says what tends to work for this presentation; expertise says what this child and family can actually do on a Tuesday; values say what they want a better life to look like. Where the legs disagree, the disagreement is written into the formulation rather than resolved silently — because a hidden disagreement becomes a hidden drift.

**Proposition 4. Formulation quality is measurable, and poor formulation predicts poor outcome.** Studies of case formulation show wide variation in practitioners' formulations of the same case, and better-rated formulations associate with better-rated plans; the reliability problem is real and is addressed by structure (the five Ps), by supervision, and by the habit of the falsifier. The discipline is not talent; it is format and honesty.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Write the formulation before the plan, and write the falsifier before the first session of the plan. If the plan comes first, the formulation will be its defence counsel rather than its author.
:::

### Worked example 13.1 — Sequencing four first moves

**Task.** For Daniel (weeks 1, 5, 10), four first moves are proposed: (a) graded school return with parent contingency change; (b) non-directive child sessions twice weekly; (c) a whole-family enactment session; (d) work with the mother's anxiety and the dyad's regulation. Sequence them across eight weeks and justify the order, naming any pair that would cancel if run together.

**Solution.** Weeks 1–2: (d) first. The mother is the child's sole regulator and the dyad's morning pattern is the engine; without her regulation capacity, every other move is delivered into an unheld child. Weeks 2–3: (c), because the father's position must exist before contingencies can be shared; the enactment also produces the family's agreement to the plan. Weeks 3–6: (a), the graded return with both parents holding the morning contingency and the school's welcome fixed — now deliverable because the regulators and the structure exist. Weeks 4–8: (b) in parallel from week 4, not before: non-directive sessions give Daniel a room of his own once attendance has begun, and its material feeds the cognitive work of week 6's lineage. Cancelling pair: (a) and (b) run together from day one — the contingency plan requires the practitioner to direct the parents while the non-directive frame requires the practitioner not to direct the child; held by one practitioner in one week without declared frames, the child and the parents experience a double message. Declared and separated in time and role, they coexist. The falsifier for the whole sequence: if by week 4 the mother's morning distress is unchanged and Daniel's attendance has not moved at all, the maintenance hypothesis is wrong and the formulation returns to week 10's map rather than pushing harder.

### Lecture II — The five Ps, worked slowly

Figure 13.2 gives the skeleton; the craft is in the contents of two of its cells.

**Perpetuating is where treatment lives.** Not the history, not the trigger: the present-tense loops, beliefs and contexts that keep the difficulty funded. Write them as processes ("escape is reinforced each morning by removal of demand and by mother's concentrated attention"), never as traits ("anxious child"). Every perpetuating factor should map to an intervention or be explicitly accepted as unchangeable this episode.

**Protective is where hope lives, and where plans are built.** The people, skills, meanings, faiths and institutions that hold the child up. Practitioners omit this cell under time pressure and thereby omit the materials of the plan: the grandmother who is the only reliable adult, the football coach, the Qur'anic class, the drawing skill, the friendship that survived the move. A formulation whose protective cell is empty is usually a formulation that has not asked.

**Predisposing and precipitating are for understanding and for the family's story, not for treatment.** They explain why this child and why now; they are where the family's own narrative is honoured and where shame can be dissolved ("this was set in motion before anyone chose it"). They are also where over-investment hides: a formulation that is mostly history is a eulogy, not a plan.

![The five-P formulation skeleton: presenting, predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective. Perpetuating is where treatment lives and protective is where hope lives; a formulation with neither is a complaint rather than a plan. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-13-02.png){width=94%}

::: safeguard
A formulation is a record. It travels: to schools, courts, insurers, future clinicians. Write it as you would wish the child to read it at twenty-five, because they may. Omit speculation dressed as fact; name uncertainty as uncertainty; and never let a systemic or cultural observation become a character assessment of a parent in a document that will outlive the case.
:::

### Lecture III — Presenting the formulation to the family

A formulation kept from the family is a formulation the family cannot correct, and the family holds most of the disconfirming evidence. Four rules govern the presentation.

**Plain language, their words first.** The presentation begins with the child's and the parents' own descriptions, quoted, before any professional sentence. If the family cannot hear themselves in the first paragraph, the rest will not land.

**Uncertainty stated as strength.** "There are two things that might be maintaining this, and our plan tests both" is more trustworthy than false certainty, and it prepares the family for the plan changing — which is the event that otherwise ends engagements.

**The protective cell read aloud.** Families who have been described only by their deficits hear the protective paragraph as the first accurate thing a professional has said. It is also where the plan's allies are recruited.

**The falsifier shared.** "If by the fourth week we see X, our idea was wrong and we will change it" converts the family from subjects of a plan into collaborators in a test. It is the single sentence that most reduces dropout when the burst or the stall arrives.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Rehearse the presentation aloud before the meeting, in ninety seconds, without jargon. If you cannot say it in ninety seconds you do not yet have a formulation; you have notes.
:::

### Worked example 13.2 — The ninety-second version

**Task.** Reduce the Daniel formulation to ninety spoken seconds for his parents.

**Solution.** "Here is what we think, in plain words. Daniel is not refusing school because he is lazy or soft; he is refusing because staying home stops a fear that feels like danger, and every morning he stays, the fear is fed a little and his confidence starves a little. Two things keep that going: the mornings themselves — the longer they go on, the bigger the fear gets — and the fact that he has had to carry it almost alone, because you, mum, have been holding it with him so closely that he never gets to discover he can survive it, and you, dad, have been away from it because the mornings are so hard to watch. That is nobody's fault; it is a loop, and loops can be broken. What we have on our side is considerable: Daniel wants to go back, he has friends who still text him, and the two of you came here, which not all parents do. Our plan is small steps back into school with both of you holding the morning differently, and a room of his own here where nothing is demanded. And here is our promise: if by the fourth week the mornings are no shorter and he is no nearer the gate, we will say our idea was wrong and we will change it together." Ninety seconds; the family's words; the loop; the allies; the falsifier.

::: skills
**Practical 13 — the formulation panel.** In fives. One presents a five-P formulation of a supplied complex case in five minutes, including governing lineage, sequence of first moves, and falsifier. Three panellists interrogate: which perpetuating factor has no intervention? which protective factor is unused? what cancels what in the sequence? what is the falsifier and is it observable? The fifth records whether the presenter said "I don't know" at least once — and if not, asks why. Rotate across two cases.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A written formulation of one page (five Ps, mechanism, governing lineage, eight-week sequence, falsifier) plus a recorded ninety-second family presentation, on a supplied case.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Perpetuating factors written as processes, each mapped to intervention or accepted limit | 25% |
| Protective cell substantive and used in the plan | 15% |
| Sequence justified; cancelling pairs identified | 25% |
| Falsifier observable and dated | 15% |
| Presentation in the family's words, uncertainty stated | 20% |

**Formative.** Submit the falsifier sentence for a case of your own (fictitious or fully anonymised).
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Integration is the discipline of one coherent, selective, falsifiable account of one child, using several lineages under a declared governing choice and a stated sequence — written so that a colleague could continue it, a family could correct it, and reality could refute it.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Two of your lineages prescribe contradictory first moves for a case you know. Sequence them and defend the order in mechanism terms.
2. Is a formulation without a falsifier unethical, or merely weak? Argue from the consequences for the child.
3. Which protective factor in a case you know did you initially miss, and what would have surfaced it?
4. Why does presenting uncertainty to a family increase rather than decrease trust? When might it not?
5. What in your own practice resists being written down, and what does the resistance protect?
