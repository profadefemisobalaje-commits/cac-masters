# The Psychodynamic Lineage III: Mentalisation and the Mind That Holds Minds {.unit num="4" week="4" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Define** mentalisation and distinguish it from empathy, theory of mind and insight.
2. **Describe** the three pre-mentalising modes — psychic equivalence, pretend mode and teleological stance — and identify each in a transcript.
3. **Explain** marked mirroring and state what makes a mirror "marked", using an example.
4. **Apply** the mentalising loop to a session excerpt, locating the point at which the loop broke and whose mind it broke in.
5. **Use** one measure of parental reflective functioning to structure an assessment interview, stating its limits.

### Where this week sits

Weeks 2 to 4 are one argument. Week 2: the child's mind must be met in the child's medium. Week 3: the child's expectations of minds are built in early relationships and are measurable. Week 4: the capacity that those relationships build is the capacity to think about minds at all — your client's, yours, and eventually the child's own. Mentalisation is the mechanism the psychodynamic lineage proposes for its own effectiveness, and it is the bridge by which the lineage joins modern developmental science.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Mentalising}
The imaginative activity of perceiving and interpreting behaviour in terms of mental states — wants, feelings, beliefs, intentions — in oneself and in others. Not empathy (feeling with), not theory of mind (the developmental capacity tested in false-belief tasks), not insight (understanding one's own history). In the room: the difference between "he hit me because he is bad" and "he hit me because he thought I was going to leave him".
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Marked_mirroring}
The caregiver's response that shows the child's feeling accurately but not identically — the face says *your* fear, slightly stylised, contained, and survivable, not the adult's own fear. In the room: the tone you use when a child screams — matching enough to be recognised, modulated enough to be bearable. Unmarked mirroring (the adult's real alarm) teaches the child that the feeling is as dangerous as it feels.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Reflective_function}
The measurable individual difference in the capacity to mentalise, assessed by coding how a person talks about minds — in the Adult Attachment Interview for adults, in the Parent Development Interview for parents about their child. In the room: what you are listening for when a parent says "he knows exactly how to wind me up" (teleological, no mind) versus "I think he provokes me because he's waiting for me to prove I'll stay" (a mind imagining a mind).
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Pre-mentalising_modes}
The ordinary, developmental, and regressed ways of relating to inner reality before or instead of mentalising: **psychic equivalence** (inner is outer; the thought is the thing, so the fear is a fact); **pretend mode** (inner is severed from outer; talk about feelings that changes nothing); **teleological** (only physical outcomes count; only what you *did* proves what you feel). In the room: the three ways a session stalls.
:::

### Lecture I — What mentalisation is and is not

**Proposition 1. Minds are opaque, and everything social rests on an inference.** You cannot observe a child's fear; you observe a face, a posture, a refusal, and you infer. Mentalising is the inference run deliberately, with the knowledge that it is an inference. The non-mentalising error is not the inference but the certainty.

**Proposition 2. The capacity is developmental and state-dependent.** Children acquire mental-state understanding in a predictable sequence through the preschool years, and the capacity is fragile under arousal: a child — or an adult — who can mentalise calmly cannot do so in panic. Most ruptures in sessions with children are not failures of skill but collapses of mentalising under threat, in the child or in you.

**Proposition 3. Mentalising is what the caregiver builds, and play is where it is built.** When a caregiver treats the child as a being with a mind — naming, wondering, getting it wrong and correcting — the child acquires the same stance toward themselves. Fonagy and colleagues' proposal is that the child's sense of self as a thinking being is internalised marked mirroring: the child finds their own mind in the caregiver's face, stylised and survivable.

**Proposition 4. Psychopathology can be read as a collapse into pre-mentalising modes.** In psychic equivalence the anxious child's prediction is a fact and reassurance is a lie; in pretend mode the adolescent's therapy talk is fluent and weightless; in the teleological stance the neglected child reads love only in what is done and bought, and tests you with demands that cannot be satisfied because they ask for proof of a mind. Figure 4.1 draws the loop whose failure produces these states.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When a session stops working, ask first which mode you are both in. Interpreting to a child in psychic equivalence escalates: your interpretation is experienced as an external fact to be fought. The repair is regulation first, mentalising second, interpretation last.

### Worked example 4.1 — Naming the mode

**Task.** Classify each utterance and say what the practitioner's next move should be. (a) Chidi (8), after a nightmare: "The man is in the house. You didn't lock the door properly." (b) Bisi (15), in week six of counselling: "Yeah, I see how my avoidance maintains the anxiety, it's the negative reinforcement cycle," delivered flatly, with no change in any behaviour. (c) Emeka (10), to a foster carer: "If you loved me you would have bought the phone like I asked."

**Solution.** (a) Psychic equivalence: the dream-content is reality; reassurance ("there is no man") will be heard as dishonesty or stupidity. Next move: take the fear seriously as an experience without confirming the fact — "in the dream he was absolutely in the house; that is what fear does, it makes what it fears real" — then regulate, then explore. (b) Pretend mode: the vocabulary is present and the mind is absent; the therapy has become a seminar. Next move: stop teaching the model and ask what happened this week when the cycle actually ran; bring the discussion back to a specific, felt moment. (c) Teleological: love is only what is done; the demand is a test of mind, and satisfying it proves nothing because only the object was offered. Next move: do not buy the phone and do not refuse it coldly; name what is being asked for — "you want to know I would do things for you; the phone can't answer that, but I can tell you what I notice about you" — and survive the disappointment without retaliation.

### The developmental sequence: how a mind learns to read minds

Mentalising is not switched on at a birthday; it is assembled in a sequence, and each rung of the sequence is a rung your clients can fall back down under stress. **Infancy:** the infant's first relevant achievement is contingency detection — the discovery that my signal moves you — and its partner, the detection of **marked** contingency, where your response matches my state but carries the stylisation that says *this is your fear, shown by me, survivable*. Gergely and Watson's work on the difference between perfect imitation and marked display is the micro-foundation of the whole week: a mirror that reflects exactly gives the child your face; a mirror that marks gives the child their own feeling in a form they can hold. **Toddlerhood:** pretence arrives — the banana is a telephone — and with it the first separation of representation from reality, which is also the first risk: pretend mode, in which inner and outer are severed and nothing believed has consequences. **The preschool years:** desire psychology becomes belief psychology; the false-belief task at around four to five marks the child's discovery that a mind can hold the world wrongly, which is the precondition of every later insight — including the insight that *my* view of the case is not the child's. **Middle childhood:** second-order beliefs (what she thinks that he thinks), interpretive understanding (two people can see the same thing differently and both be honest), and the vocabulary of mixed feelings; this is the age at which the child can first use a formulation, and the age at which therapy talk becomes possible rather than imitated. **Adolescence:** the recursive engine overclocks — minds reading minds reading minds, with the self as the most-read text — and the social brain's sensitivity to the audience makes adolescent mentalising brilliant in the cold and unreliable in the heat, which is CAC. 702's dual-systems story told in this lineage's vocabulary.

Two clinical corollaries follow. First, **the mode is age-graded before it is pathological:** psychic equivalence in a four-year-old is development, in a fourteen-year-old a regression, and in a panicked parent of either age a state — so the first diagnostic question about any non-mentalising utterance is whether it is age, state or trait, because the three call for teaching, regulating and treating respectively. Second, **the sequence is the curriculum of parenting work:** a parent who has never been mentalised cannot be handed a mindset; they can be given the sequence in behavioural form first (name the feeling aloud before the demand; wonder out loud once a day; repair the miss the same evening) and the mindset grows out of the behaviours, which is exactly what the reflective-functioning interventions of Lecture III do in trials.

### Lecture II — The loop in the room, and where it breaks

Figure 4.1 is a description of ordinary good care, not a technique. Six steps, run thousands of times: observe; infer the child's state; check your own state (whose fear is this?); respond marked and contingent; the child experiences being seen by a mind; repeat. Four failure points are worth memorising because each has a different repair.

**Failure at observation.** You did not see it — the micro-turn away, the change in play. Repair is ordinary attention and, where possible, video or supervision: practitioners systematically overestimate what they noticed.

**Failure at inference.** You saw it and read it wrong, usually by reading your own state into the child. Repair is the habit of offering the inference as a question and being willing to be corrected by a nine-year-old, which is one of the most mentalising things an adult can do.

**Failure at self-check.** Your own alarm or anger enters the response unmarked, and the child meets your state instead of their own. This is the failure that most often ends placements and therapeutic relationships. Repair is supervision and the discipline of naming your state before you act on it.

**Failure at marking.** The response is accurate but unmodulated — a mirror without a frame — or modulated into falseness, which children detect instantly. Repair is practice: the marked response is a skill with a feel to it, learned by being marked oneself.

::: safeguard
Mentalising failure is a safeguarding concept as well as a clinical one. Caregiving that is frightening, frightened or persistently unmarked — the carer whose face shows their own terror or rage in response to the child's distress — is both a cause of disorganised attachment and an indicator of risk in the home. Where you observe it, it is reportable material under your safeguarding procedure, not merely a formulation.
:::

### Marked mirroring in practice: a training sequence for the marked response

The marked response is the week's only physical skill, and like all physical skills it is learned in progression rather than by definition. Its four components, each separately trainable: **accuracy** — the response names the state the child is actually in, not the state the room would prefer; **modulation** — the intensity is turned down by one notch, enough to be survivable, never by five, which reads as indifference; **contingency** — the response tracks the child's changes rather than repeating the practitioner's formula, which is why scripted empathy ("I hear that you feel...") fails children so reliably: it is a mirror facing itself; and **timing** — the mark arrives while the state is live but not at peak, because at peak the child needs regulation only, and after the peak the mark arrives as commentary.

The drill progression used in this programme's practical classes runs four steps. **Step 1, discrimination:** hear or read twelve responses to the same child statement and sort them into marked, unmarked and false — the ear is trained before the mouth, and the false category is the hardest, because falseness is modulation without accuracy and sounds, in the first month of training, exactly like skill. **Step 2, modulation of the self:** the trainee delivers their own natural response to a high-arousal card while the group rates its intensity against the card's; most trainees discover they run one notch above the child (contagion) or two below (defence), and the discovery is the training. **Step 3, the one-notch rule:** deliver the response at exactly the child's notch minus one, checked by the group; the rule is crude and temporary, a bicycle's training wheel, but it installs the feel of the distance. **Step 4, contingency under interruption:** the child-actor changes state mid-exchange (rage to shame, shame to silliness) and the responder must re-mark within one sentence; this is the step that separates the skill from the manner, because the manner continues its first sentence while the child moves on.

Three learner failures recur and are named in supervision rather than punished. **The flat mark:** accuracy with the modulation turned to zero, usually in trainees whose own childhoods were met with flatness; the repair is being marked oneself, in the triad, repeatedly, until the body recognises the tone. **The florid mark:** modulation as performance, the theatrical "oooh, that sounds SO hard", which children experience as being handled; the repair is video or audio playback, which is unbearable to watch and curative in one sitting. **The early mark:** the marked response delivered at peak arousal, where the child cannot use a mirror because the child cannot see; the repair is the week's order — regulate first, mentalise second — practised until the pause before the mark becomes automatic, because the pause is where the self-check lives.

### Lecture III — Measuring reflective function, and using it without abusing it

Reflective function is coded from interview: the Parent Development Interview asks parents to describe their child and their relationship, and the coding counts evidence that the parent treats the child as a mind — recognising opacity, wondering about states, tracking change, acknowledging the limits of their own knowledge. Parents high in reflective function have, on average, children with better regulation and fewer difficulties; the association holds over and above income and education, and interventions that raise parental reflective function show effects on parenting and on child outcomes in several trials.

Two cautions govern its use in practice. First, the measures are research instruments requiring training; a clinician who "assesses reflective function" by impression is not measuring anything. Second, low reflective function is not moral failure and not a verdict on love: it is often the residue of a childhood in which nobody mentalised the parent, and it is treatable. The clinical use of the concept is not to sort parents but to decide what to do: with a parent in teleological mode, behavioural work and concrete tasks first, mentalising later; with a parent able to wonder, formulation work can begin immediately.

Mentalisation-based work with adolescents — including the adaptations for self-harm and for borderline presentation in adolescence — applies the same loop to the therapeutic relationship: the therapist's job is to keep mentalising when the adolescent cannot, to mark their own reactions, and to treat every rupture as material about minds rather than as misbehaviour. That stance, more than any technique, is what the lineage hands to the rest of this course.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Adopt one habit from this week and keep it for the rest of the programme: before you respond to a child's strongest feeling, name silently (1) what you think the child is feeling, (2) what you are feeling, and (3) one alternative reading of the child's state. Three seconds. It is the cheapest intervention in the course.
:::

### Mentalising in adolescents: the social brain, the audience, and hypermentalising

With adolescents the loop acquires a third mind of unusual power: the audience. The adolescent's mentalising apparatus is at its developmental peak and simultaneously at its most state-dependent — precise about a friend's ambivalence in the evening, certain and wrong about a teacher's contempt by morning — because peer arousal collapses the capacity exactly where the adolescent lives. Two distortions are worth naming because they present as opposites and are cousins. **Hypomentalising** in adolescents looks like concreteness and contempt: "he did it because he is a bastard", no mind anywhere, and it is the mode in which most adolescent violence is explained by its author. **Hypermentalising** — the excess rather than the absence of inference — looks like sophistication and is more dangerous: the adolescent who produces fluent, detailed, unfalsifiable accounts of what everyone intended ("she said good morning like that because she knows I saw the message and wanted me to know she knows"), with certainty where the evidence is silence. Hypermentalising is over-represented in adolescents who self-harm and in borderline presentation in adolescence, and it is a trap for practitioners because it reads as high reflective function in the first ten minutes and reveals itself only when you ask the one question that kills it: **what would you have seen if you were wrong?** A mind-reading that cannot say what would disconfirm it is psychic equivalence wearing a theory of mind.

Mentalisation-based work with adolescents (the MBT-A adaptations) therefore organises the hour around the stance rather than the curriculum: the practitioner's not-knowing held openly ("I do not know why she said it like that; you do not know either; shall we look at what we actually have?"); the relationship's here-and-now as the laboratory ("just now, when I summarised you, your face closed — what happened in your mind about my mind?"); rupture treated as the best material in the week rather than as interference; and the group, where available, as the place where several minds visibly disagree about the same event, which is interpretive understanding taught by experience rather than by lecture. The bridge to CAC. 702 is exact: the imaginary audience is a mentalising distortion under arousal, the personal fable is psychic equivalence about being known, and the divergence-point method of that volume's week 4 is this week's disconfirming question, taught as a skill the adolescent can run alone at two in the morning.

### Worked example 4.2 — The rupture as material

**Task.** In session eight, Funke (12), who has begun to trust you, arrives and announces that the sessions are stupid and she is not coming back. Last session she disclosed something shameful and you responded well, or so you thought. Using the loop, formulate the rupture and plan your response.

**Solution.** Observe: the announcement follows the disclosure; the timing is the datum. Infer: the shame of having been seen has reorganised as contempt for the seer; the prediction inside her is that you now think less of her, and leaving first converts rejection into choice. Check your own state: you feel stung and, underneath, defensive — the pull to point out her progress, which would be your state, not hers. Respond marked: neither collapse ("of course you can stop") nor defence ("after everything we've done"); instead, naming the sequence as a hypothesis about minds — "last week you told me something hard, and today the sessions are stupid; I wonder if being seen here has started to feel dangerous." The loop's repair is the demonstration that your mind survived her feeling and still holds an image of her that includes last week's courage. What you must not do is treat the announcement as a decision to be negotiated; it is not a decision, it is a mental state.

### Mentalising and the body: arousal, interoception and the child who presents somatically

The loop of Figure 4.1 has a bodily first chapter that the literature of this week is only beginning to repay, and it matters enormously in the settings of this programme, where distress arrives at the clinic door as stomach, head, chest and legs rather than as sadness. The infant's earliest "mental states" are bodily states — hunger, colic, the terror of falling — and the caregiver's first mirroring is of the body: the feeding that answers hunger, the rocking that answers alarm, the held hand on the stomach that says *your pain, seen, survivable*. Interoception — the sensing of the body's interior — is therefore the raw material from which feeling-words are later built, and the child whose bodily signals were answered unpredictably or not at all grows up with a self whose interior is poorly labelled: not numb, but unworded, a weather system with no map. Alexithymia is the adult name for part of this; in the child it presents as "I don't know" that is not defiance but an accurate report of an unlabeled interior, and as the somatic complaint that is the interior's only fluent sentence.

Three clinical consequences follow, and they change what you do on Monday with the child whose referral says "recurrent abdominal pain, medical workup negative". **First, the somatic sentence is a mental state in the only available vocabulary,** and treating it as a fabrication ("there is nothing wrong") repeats, at the level of the body, the unmarked mirroring of week 2: the child's real experience denied by the adult's face. The marked response to a stomach is therefore the same response as to a fear — accurate and modulated: "that stomach pain is real; pain is how this worry travels in your body, and bodies are honest even when words are not available" — which validates the body while opening the door to the mind. **Second, regulation of the body precedes mentalising of the mind in these children more than in any others:** breath, warmth, weight, rhythm — the rocking that the infant received — because the loop cannot run its inference step on an interior the child cannot feel clearly; the three-second habit of this week begins, with the somatic child, as a three-second body scan (where is it loudest today?) before any question about feeling. **Third, the family's idioms of the body are the family's mentalising in its own grammar:** a household that says "the belly is complaining because the home is not at peace" is mentalising through the body, and the practitioner who translates it contemptuously into "they think it is witchcraft" has misread a metaphor as a delusion; the workable move is to join the grammar and extend it — "if the belly speaks for the home, then let us listen to what else the home's week is saying" — because the body-idiom is a bridge into the mind-idiom, and bridges are crossed, not corrected.

The bridge runs the other way too, and this is the week's last caution: mentalising language can itself become a defence against the body — the team that explains every stomach as cognition while the child's constipation, migraine, coeliac disease or sickle crisis goes unexamined. The medical screen of CAC. 702 week 13 is not a rival to this week's stance; it is its precondition. A mind holding a mind includes a mind holding a body, and the practitioner who forgets the body has not mentalised the child but only the child's paperwork.

### The three-second habit as service policy

The three-second habit — name the child's state, your own state, and one alternative reading before you respond — is taught this week as a personal discipline, and it deserves a second life as service policy, because the moments in which it matters most are the moments in which services are least organised: the disclosure at the door as the session ends, the phone call at dusk, the incident in the waiting room. Three policy forms make the habit survive the institution rather than the individual. **The pause is scheduled, not stolen:** services that teach the habit give it time — the two minutes between cases that are not paperwork but the silent three lines written on the slip kept in the practitioner's folder (child's state / my state / alternative), which converts the habit from temperament into record, and gives supervision something to read. **The alternative reading is compulsory in high-stakes decisions:** any recommendation that removes something from a child — a placement, a contact, a school place — carries in its document one sentence of the form "an alternative reading of the child's behaviour is ___, and we excluded it because ___", which is the disconfirming discipline of week 1 applied at the level where errors cost years. **The team runs the loop aloud once a week:** the case conference opens not with updates but with one case mentalised in sequence by the room — observation, inference, self-check (what is this team feeling about this family?), marked response — fifteen minutes that reliably surface the team's psychic equivalences ("that mother is not capable") before they harden into decisions, and that train juniors faster than any lecture in this volume, because they hear seniors being corrected and surviving it.

The audit of all three is behavioural and cheap: the slips exist; the alternative-reading sentence exists in the removal documents; the conference agenda shows the fifteen minutes. Where any of the three is absent, the service's mentalising is a claim about its values rather than a property of its paperwork, and the children of the service will meet the loop only on the days when a practitioner happens to be rested. Policy is the loop's habitat; without it, the habit is heroism, and heroism is what burns out by the third year, taking its marked responses with it. The sentence to carry from this week into every placement you will ever hold is therefore smaller than the theory: before you answer the loudest feeling in the room, spend three seconds naming two minds and one alternative — and where the room is a service, insist that the service spends them with you.

### The evidence and its limits: what the mentalising claims rest on

The mentalising programme has what the older psychodynamic claims lacked: measurable constructs and randomised tests. **Parental reflective functioning** predicts child regulation and difficulty over and above socioeconomic variables, and interventions designed to raise it — brief, video-feedback and relationship-focused parenting programmes among them — show effects on parental sensitivity and on child outcomes in several trials, which is the strongest evidence anywhere in this lineage that a psychodynamic construct can be a treatment target. **Mentalisation-based treatment for adolescents who self-harm** has shown, in a randomised comparison against treatment as usual, reductions in self-harm and in borderline features that outlasted the treatment period — one of the few adolescent psychotherapy trials in which a psychodynamically derived method separated from an active comparator. **The stance travels:** mentalising language now appears in nursing, social work, education and forensic settings, and its core habits — not-knowing, curiosity, repair of rupture — are teachable in short courses with measurable change in practitioner behaviour.

The limits are as instructive as the findings. **Measurement requires training:** reflective-function coding is a research skill; impression-based "assessment" of a parent's mentalising is a prejudice with a professional vocabulary, and in custody contexts it is a weapon. **Allegiance and heterogeneity:** several trials come from the groups that developed the method, and "mentalisation-based" packages vary widely in content, so the package effects are less secure than the construct effects. **The construct is not the whole of therapy:** mentalising describes a process common to many methods — the behavioural therapist who asks "what went through your mind just then?" is mentalising without the lineage — so the honest claim is that mentalisation names and trains a mechanism several schools use, not that it owns one. And **the state-dependence finding cuts both ways:** if mentalising collapses under arousal, then its measurement in a calm interview overestimates the capacity that matters at midnight, which is why the discipline of this week is practised in triads under simulated heat rather than read in a handbook.

### Conversations in the room: three adults and other minds

**The parent who says "he does it to annoy me".** — *Answer:* "He may do; children are experts at the buttons. But notice what the sentence costs you: if it is only annoyance, there is nothing to understand and nothing to change except him, and he is eight. Try a longer sentence with me once: 'He does it to annoy me — and I think he does it because when I am annoyed I am at least looking at him, which I am not doing much of these weeks.' Same facts, one mind added. Everything you can do lives in the added part." *Commentary:* the script does not argue with the parent's truth (the button-pressing is real); it prices the short sentence (no leverage) and offers the long one as an experiment, locating the added mind in an observable (looking) rather than in sentiment.

**The teacher who says "she manipulates everyone".** — *Answer:* "Manipulation is a mind-reading: it claims she plans the effect she produces. Watch with me for one week for something smaller and truer: does she seek you out after the episodes, or avoid you? The child who manipulates avoids the witness; the child who is drowning swims to anyone. Which one you see tells us whether we are describing a strategy or a signal, and the two need opposite responses — limits for the first, an adult for the second." *Commentary:* the rewrite replaces a verdict with a one-week observation that discriminates, which converts the teacher from prosecutor into colleague and gives the child the benefit of the only thing a verdict cannot supply: data.

**The adolescent who says "you're just doing this because it's your job".** — *Answer:* "Partly, yes: I am paid to be here at four o'clock, and I would be a liar to say otherwise. But paid people can also be curious, and here is something I noticed that is not in any job description: you corrected me last week about your brother, twice, until I got it right. People who are only doing a job do not care about getting it right. So: job, yes; and also I want to get you right. Both true. Which part do you want to test today?" *Commentary:* the marked response to a teleological challenge is neither denial (false) nor cynical agreement (unmarked); it is holding both truths and handing the adolescent the experiment, because the teleological stance is only ever moved by what is done — including the doing of admitting what is true.

### Common errors and their corrections

1. **Mentalising as jargon.** "We need to mentalise more" said in a team meeting. *Correction:* mentalising is a verb with an object: whose mind, about whose mind, on what evidence? Name the three or say nothing.
2. **The stance as interrogation.** "How do you think that made him feel?" asked of a dysregulated child. *Correction:* regulate first, mentalise second; the question belongs after the arousal falls, and before that it is a demand in costume.
3. **Marking as flatness.** The modulated response delivered deadpan, which children read as indifference with technique. *Correction:* marked means accurate and modulated, not absent; the frame must still show the picture.
4. **Pretend-mode fluency as progress.** The adolescent who learns the vocabulary and changes nothing, recorded as "good insight". *Correction:* insight is behavioural or it is pretend; ask what changed this week in the kitchen, not what was understood in the room.
5. **Reflective function as parent-ranking.** The coded parent filed as "low RF" and treated accordingly. *Correction:* low RF is a treatable residue of an unmentalised childhood; the file carries the level and the plan, never the level alone.

### Worked example 4.3 — The teleological parent, worked behaviourally first

**Task.** Mr Okafor attends under a school referral for his son's aggression. He describes the boy in outcomes only ("he stole, he fought, he lied"), rejects "feelings talk" as "women's work", and asks for "the cane method". Using the loop and the mode, plan the first three sessions.

**Solution.** *Mode:* teleological, stable across the interview, and culturally reinforced — in his grammar, love and discipline are both what is done, and minds are inferred only from deeds. *The error to avoid:* opening with mentalising invitations ("what do you think he feels when...?"), which in teleological mode are heard as accusations of not caring, and will end attendance. *Session 1 — join the teleology:* accept his grammar fully and work inside it: "You want the stealing stopped. So do I. Stepping is what we will count." Install an ABC chart (week 5's instrument) as the shared deed-book; the chart is teleological language — antecedents and consequences are doings — and it is also, unnoticed, the first mind-reading, because antecedents include the boy's situations. *Session 2 — let the chart introduce minds:* when the chart shows the stealing clustered after the father's market absences, offer the pattern as a doing-question first ("what is happening on those days, in the house, before it?") and only later as a state-question ("what is he carrying on those days?"); the mind enters through the calendar, which is his language. *Session 3 — one marked sentence, modelled rather than taught:* the practitioner mentalises the father in front of him, aloud and accurately ("you work the market six days because the school fees do not wait; and the seventh day the boy steals, and it feels like the fees found another way to take from you") — being mentalised is the intervention that teaches mentalising, and it is delivered here before any request that he mentalise the boy. *Falsifier and exit:* if by session four the chart is not being kept, the alliance has not joined his teleology and the plan returns to session 1's work; if the chart is kept and the pattern-sentence is refused outright at session six, the formulation records a teleological organisation of high rigidity and the case is routed to behavioural parent training (week 5) with the relational work deferred, not abandoned.

### Worked example 4.4 — Chidera's hypermentalising, and the disconfirming question

**Task.** Chidera (15) presents after self-harm without suicidal intent. In session two she offers: "My mum didn't text all day because the new baby's father is staying over and she doesn't want me to know how happy they are, and my form tutor saw my sleeves and told the office, which is why the office lady was nice to me at lunch." Assess the mentalising and plan the response.

**Solution.** *Assessment:* the account is rich in minds (mother's motive, tutor's intention, office lady's knowledge) and poor in evidence (no text seen, no conversation overheard, no facial datum); the inferences are chained (each mind reading the next) and each link is asserted with certainty; the affect is flat where the content is catastrophic. This is hypermentalising: high output, low falsifiability, and — note the function — a story in which everyone's mind is occupied with Chidera, which is preferable, at fifteen, to the alternative hypothesis that several minds are simply elsewhere. *The discriminating question,* delivered without challenge in tone: "That is a very complete picture. Help me with one thing: if you were wrong about your mum — if the day had nothing to do with you at all — what would you have seen differently by now?" *The response pattern:* a pause; then, quietly, "nothing. I would have seen nothing. That is the worst part." — the collapse of the hypermentalising edifice into the underlying state (unheld, unthought-about, waiting for a sign), which is the first genuinely mentalised sentence of the case and the place the work begins. *Plan:* the self-harm function-assessment of CAC. 702 week 7 runs alongside; in this room the target is the falsifiability habit — every mind-reading written with its disconfirming line (week 1's discipline, taught to the client), and the alternative hypothesis of ordinary busy-ness held openly beside the beloved terrible one, not to console but to train: two hypotheses, one child, and the week's actual texts and silences as the data. *Repair criterion:* by session five, Chidera brings one mind-reading with its disconfirming line unprompted; if instead the edifice rebuilds with more rooms, arousal is doing the building and the regulation work moves ahead of the mentalising work, in the week's own order.

::: skills
**Practical 4 — the three-second habit and the marked response.** In triads. One plays a child delivering a high-arousal statement (supplied cards: "I hate you", "You're just like my mum", "Nothing works, nothing ever works", "It's not a big deal, stop looking at me like that"). The responder has three seconds to name silently the child's state, their own state, and one alternative reading, then delivers a marked response of one sentence. The third member codes: was the response marked (accurate but modulated), unmarked (the responder's own state), or false (modulated into nothing)? Rotate. Debrief: which statements pulled you into which failure?
:::

### Non-mentalising in teams, reports and care plans

The pre-mentalising modes are not confined to clients; they colonise services, and the weekly documents are where to catch them. **Psychic equivalence in the referral:** "the child is attention-seeking" offered as an observed fact rather than an inference about a mind — and note what the sentence does: it converts a need into a character, and a character into a verdict, and a verdict into a waitlist position. **Teleology in the risk report:** three incidents counted as risk with no mind anywhere in the paragraph — no want, no fear, no intention — so that the report can predict nothing except more counting, and the child's own account of the incidents (the button pressed, the promise broken, the anniversary) never enters the file that decides the placement. **Pretend mode in the care plan:** the plan that is fluent in the vocabulary of this very course — "strengths-based, child-centred, trauma-informed, mentalising stance" — and changes nothing in any kitchen, because no mind was ever imagined: nobody wrote what the child thinks happens when the door slams, or what the carer believes about the child's silence at dinner.

The corrections are small, behavioural and auditable, which is why they work in institutions. **The mind-line in the referral:** every referral and every handover carries one sentence of the form "we infer that the child wants/fears/believes ___, on the evidence of ___, and we may be wrong because ___" — a sentence that cannot be written without a mind in it, and that exposes instantly the referral that has none. **The incident's second column:** incident forms carry, beside what happened, a column for "what we think was in the child's mind, and what we asked the child, and what the child said" — the asking being compulsory, because the column cannot be completed without the child's voice, and the child's voice is the check on the team's certainty. **The care-plan test:** any care-plan objective is read aloud and asked one question — "whose mind does this sentence imagine?" — and an objective that imagines nobody's ("behaviour will improve") is returned until it imagines somebody's ("the carer will name the child's feeling aloud before the consequence, so that the child experiences being read rather than only stopped"). A team that runs these three disciplines is running the loop of Figure 4.1 at institutional scale: observe, infer, check, respond marked — the child's mind held not by one practitioner's attention but by the paperwork itself, which is what survives staff turnover, and staff turnover is the child's fourth placement.

### This week's row in the comparison grid

*View of the child:* a mind in construction, built by being held in another mind; opaque like every mind, and therefore to be inferred about, never read off. *View of what maintains:* collapse into pre-mentalising modes under arousal — the thought as fact, the weightless talk, the love that is only deeds — and the relational patterns those modes enforce. *Mechanism of change:* a mind holding a mind until the child can hold their own: marked, contingent response; rupture survived and repaired; the child's own mind returned to them in survivable form. *Signature method:* the not-knowing stance; the three-second habit; the marked response; rupture analysis; reflective-functioning parenting work where the parent is the lever. *Who is in the room:* the dyad whose loop is broken — child and caregiver — because the capacity is dyadic before it is individual; the practitioner as temporary marked mirror. *What counts as progress:* the modes loosening under heat — a disconfirming line offered unprompted, a feeling named as feeling rather than fact, a rupture survived without flight; and in parents, the longer sentence with one mind added. *What this approach cannot see:* the contingency maintaining the behaviour outside any mind (week 5's territory); the neurological and cognitive constraints on the capacity itself; and the injustice that is not a misunderstanding — a child whose situation is exactly as bad as they think it is, and whose accurate despair must not be mentalised into distortion. The last blindness is the ethical one: not-knowing is a stance toward minds, never toward facts, and a practitioner who wonders aloud about a child's report of harm has left the lineage and abandoned the duty.

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Mentalisation is the psychodynamic lineage's account of its own mechanism — a mind holding a mind until the child can hold their own — and its practical gift is a discipline: under arousal, regulate first, mentalise second, interpret last.
:::

### Context note: mentalising in a culture of certainty and respect

The mentalising stance — not-knowing, curious, corrective — was formulated in cultures where a professional's open uncertainty is read as honesty. In hierarchical settings it can be read otherwise: the counsellor who says "I do not know; what do you think?" to an elder, or to a parent who has come for the expert's sentence, may be heard as incompetence, as evasion, or as disrespect — and the family that hears disrespect does not return, which is the outcome the stance exists to prevent. The adaptation is not abandonment but **grammar**. Wondering is offered with permission and in the third person rather than as naked uncertainty: "Some families I have sat with have found that the boy's stealing speaks for him when the house is busy; I do not know whether that is this house's language — you would know better than I." The proverb and the story carry the same function in many of the communities this programme serves: a saying about the child who steals when the yam store is empty mentalises the child at one remove, letting the parent adopt the mind-reading as their own discovery rather than receive it as a correction — which is, in the lineage's own terms, marked mirroring delivered in the culture's idiom.

Two further adjustments matter in practice. **Certainty about duties is not the same as certainty about minds,** and the practitioner should be visibly certain where certainty is owed — the safeguarding duty, the law, the appointment time, the confidentiality rule — so that the not-knowing about minds is heard as method rather than as drift. And **collective mind-reading** — the community's confident "everyone knows what that family is like" — is psychic equivalence at social scale, and it will enter your room wearing the referral letter; the discipline of this week applies to it exactly as to the child's: an inference is not a fact, and the formulation records what was observed, by whom, under what procedure. Mentalising, translated, is not Western doubt; it is the old courtesy of leaving room in a sentence for the other person's mind, and most of the cultures this programme serves have a name for that courtesy already.

### Seminar questions

### Model answers to two seminar questions

**Question 1 — mentalising versus empathy, coming apart.** They come apart cleanly in the ward scene: the child screaming before a procedure. The empathic practitioner feels the terror and is moved to end it — to cancel, to hold, to promise it will be quick — and the child meets an adult flooded by the same terror, which confirms that the terror is as large as it feels (unmarked mirroring, week 2's mechanism in negative). The mentalising practitioner feels with the child and additionally holds the child's mind in view — "you are not only in pain; you are in pain with nobody telling you what is happening, and the not-knowing is half of it" — and acts on the inference (narrate the next sixty seconds, give the child one choice, keep the promise made). In a crisis the child needs both in order: empathy first as proof of company, mentalising second as proof of a mind that survived the feeling and can therefore hold it; empathy alone drowns with the child, mentalising alone abandons them politely, and the ordered pair is what the lineage means by containment with a mind in it.

**Question 5 — theory or stance?** It is a theory that generates a stance, and what turns on the answer is everything about how it is taught and audited. As theory, mentalisation makes claims that can fail: that marked mirroring builds the self as thinker, that pre-mentalising modes organise psychopathology, that raising reflective function changes parenting — each testable, each tested, some supported (Lecture III) and some still open. As stance, it is a discipline of the practitioner's attention: not-knowing held openly, inference offered as question, rupture as material, regulation before interpretation. The danger of answering "stance" alone is drift without test — a team that is warmly curious and never checks whether anything changed; the danger of answering "theory" alone is technique without the attention — a manual of mentalising questions asked in pretend mode while the child's arousal runs the room. The position this course takes: the stance is the theory's delivery mechanism, and the audit of both is behavioural — the marked response observed in triads, the disconfirming line written in the note, the repair delivered at minute one. A stance that produces no testable sentences has become a manner; a theory that produces no changed attention has become a vocabulary; the loop of Figure 4.1 is the place where the two are the same thing.
