# Fundamentals of Counseling Theories {.chapter num="1" short="What a Theory Is For" code="CAC. 701 · First Semester · 1.6 units" semester="First" units="1.6"}

Every other course in this programme will teach you something to do. This one teaches you why doing it might work, and how to tell whether it did.

A counselling theory is not a belief system and not a loyalty. It is a set of answers to a small number of hard questions: what a child is, what goes wrong, how change happens, and what the counsellor therefore does. Answered well, those questions make practice deliberate. Answered badly or not at all, practice becomes a series of intuitive moves that cannot be evaluated, cannot be taught, and cannot be defended to the parent, the school, the court or the licensing board that asks what you did and why.

The course proceeds in sixteen weeks. This week builds the frame. Weeks 2 to 4 take the psychodynamic and attachment lineage; weeks 5 to 7 the behavioural, cognitive and third-wave lineage; weeks 8 and 9 the humanistic and existential; weeks 10 and 11 the systemic, solution-focused and narrative; week 12 the critiques that keep all of them honest. Weeks 13 and 14 integrate: formulation and evidence. Week 15 runs the whole course over one extended case, and week 16 prepares you for the examination.

Throughout, the material is addressed to work with children and adolescents. That is not a decoration on an adult curriculum. A child cannot free-associate, may lack the vocabulary for a cognitive dispute, does not choose to attend, and is embedded in a family and a school that are themselves part of the problem and part of the solution. Theories built for adults fail in particular, predictable ways in a room with a nine-year-old, and one purpose of this course is to teach you to see the failure coming.

# What a Theory Is For {.unit num="1" week="1" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **State the four questions** a counselling theory must answer, and identify which of the four a given written account of a case leaves unanswered.
2. **Construct a formulation** of a fictitious case that names the presenting problem, the factors maintaining it, and the mechanism by which an intervention would change those factors.
3. **Distinguish a mechanism of change from a technique**, giving one example of each from any two of the four major approaches.
4. **Evaluate a published effectiveness claim** using three tests: what the comparison condition was, who the population was, and what was measured.
5. **Explain a theoretical position to a parent** in ordinary language, without jargon, in under two minutes.

Outcomes 1–3 are examined in this week's assessed task; outcomes 4 and 5 are examined in the practical and recur in weeks 13 and 14.
:::

### Where this week sits

This week supplies the vocabulary that the remaining fifteen argue with. Nothing here is a theory of therapy; it is a specification of what any theory of therapy has to deliver. If you learn the four questions and the distinction between mechanism and technique, you will read weeks 2 to 12 critically rather than memorising them. If you do not, they will read as twelve lists of names. Begin the comparison grid (Figure 1.3) this week and keep it to the end of the course.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Theory_of_change}
The account a practitioner can give of *why* what they do should produce the result they want. In the room: the sentence you could say aloud after a session — "I did that because I expect it to make this difference" — and the observation that would show you were wrong. A practitioner who cannot say the sentence is not being flexible; they are being arbitrary.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Formulation}
A written or spoken account of a particular child's difficulty that connects history, maintaining factors and context into a single argument, and implies a treatment. It is the alternative to a diagnostic label: a label tells you what category the child belongs to, a formulation tells you what to do on Tuesday. In the room: the paragraph you could hand to a colleague who has never met the child, which would let them continue the work.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Mechanism_of_change}
The process inside the client through which improvement is supposed to occur — insight, extinction of a conditioned response, corrective relational experience, cognitive restructuring, altered family structure, new meaning. In the room: what you are actually counting on happening in the child's head or in the child's relationships. Techniques are what you do; the mechanism is what you are relying on the doing to cause.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Fidelity}
The degree to which what was delivered matches what the method specifies. In the room: whether you performed the intervention the manual describes, or a comfortable variation of it. Fidelity matters for two reasons that pull against each other — a method delivered loosely cannot be evaluated, and a method delivered rigidly to a child it was not designed for may be useless or harmful. Holding both thoughts at once is part of the job.
:::

### Lecture I — Theory is not optional when the client is a child

**Proposition 1. A child cannot be relied upon to tell you what is wrong, so theory is the instrument that supplies hypotheses where speech is unavailable.** An adult client can, at least sometimes, report a pattern: *I always leave before I am left.* A seven-year-old with the same pattern cannot. Developmental limitations of vocabulary, memory, abstraction and metacognition mean that the child's account is partial, concrete, and often an account of what adults have said about them. Theory fills the gap by generating testable hypotheses from what is observable — play, drawing, refusal, somatic complaint, behaviour in the session — and by telling you what those observations would mean if the theory were right. This is the deepest reason child counselling is theoretical in a way that adult counselling can afford not to be.

**Proposition 2. Every intervention is already theoretical; the choice is between an examined theory and an unexamined one.** A counsellor who says they are "not theoretical, I just build rapport" has adopted a position with a mechanism (the relationship produces change), a theory of the problem (problems persist where the child does not feel safe), and a method (be warm, be consistent, do not push). It is a person-centred position, descended from Rogers, and it is defensible. What is not defensible is holding it without knowing it, because then it cannot be revised when it fails.

**Proposition 3. Theory is what makes practice evaluable.** If you have stated a mechanism, then non-improvement is informative: either the mechanism did not operate, or it operated and did not produce the predicted change, or your formulation of the problem was wrong. Those are three different findings and they lead to three different next steps. Without a stated mechanism, non-improvement is only disappointment. This is the difference between a practitioner who learns from twenty years of caseload and one who repeats one year twenty times.

**Proposition 4. In work with minors, theory decides who is in the room.** A behavioural theory of a child's refusal to attend school locates the problem in contingencies and will want the parents and the teacher present. A psychodynamic theory locates it in anxiety and internal conflict and may want the child alone. A systemic theory locates it in the family's organisation and will want the whole family. These are not variations in style; they are variations in who you invite, whose account you privilege, whose consent you need, and whose interest you are serving when the child's interest and the parent's interest diverge — which, in work with children, they routinely do.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Before your next case, write one sentence of the form: *I am working from the idea that this child's difficulty is maintained by X, and that Y will change it, which I would expect to see as Z.* If you cannot complete the sentence, you do not yet have a plan; you have a schedule of appointments.
:::

### Worked example 1.1 — Finding the missing question

**Task.** A training brochure describes a method for childhood anxiety in these terms: "Our programme uses structured play sessions in which the child externalises the worry and the counsellor guides the child to re-author the worry story. Sessions are forty-five minutes, weekly, for ten weeks. Families report high satisfaction." Which of the four questions does this account answer, and which does it leave open?

**Solution.** It answers question 4 (method) completely — structure, duration, frequency — and gestures at question 3 (mechanism) with "externalises" and "re-author", which implies a narrative mechanism: changing the story changes the relationship to the problem. It does not answer question 2 (maintenance): nothing is said about what keeps this child's anxiety going now, so the method cannot be matched to the case. It does not answer question 1 (view of the child) at all, so there is no statement about which children the method suits or at what developmental level "re-authoring a story" is possible — a capacity most children acquire only in middle childhood. Two questions open, one half-answered: usable as a technique, not yet as a theory.

### Lecture II — The four questions

Every counselling theory, whatever its vocabulary, answers four questions. Figure 1.1 sets them out around the case they exist to explain.

![The four questions a counselling theory answers, arranged around the case: human nature, maintenance, mechanism, and method. A theory that cannot answer all four is a vocabulary rather than a treatment. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-01-01.png){width=94%}

**Question 1 — Human nature and development.** What is a child? A bundle of drives requiring channelled expression; an organism whose behaviour is shaped by consequence; a person with an actualising tendency that flourishes under the right conditions; a node in a communication network. Each answer determines what counts as health, and therefore what counts as the target of treatment. This question is where developmental theory enters counselling: a theory that assumes a capacity for reflection cannot be used with a child who has not developed it.

**Question 2 — Maintenance.** Not "how did this begin?" but "what keeps it going now?" The distinction is the most practically useful in the whole course. Origins are often unrecoverable and rarely modifiable; maintaining factors are present, observable and available to change. A child's school refusal may have begun with a bullying incident nine months ago, but it is maintained this morning by the negative reinforcement of staying home, the mother's anxious checking, the school's informal exemption from attendance, and the erosion of the child's confidence that returning is survivable. Four maintaining factors, four different intervention points, and only one of them is about the bullying.

**Question 3 — Mechanism of change.** Through what process does the intervention work? Insight and the making conscious of the unconscious; extinction, habituation and new learning; a corrective emotional experience within the therapeutic relationship; restructuring of the family's hierarchy and boundaries; the construction of an alternative story. Mechanism is the question most often skipped and the one that makes a theory falsifiable: if you claim the mechanism is insight, then a treatment that produces change without insight has refuted something you believed.

**Question 4 — Method.** What does the counsellor do, in what order, with whom, for how long? Method is downstream of the other three, and a method adopted without them is a technique performed on a child rather than a treatment offered to one.

Two further questions are not part of every theory but should be part of every practitioner's: **what is the evidence** for this approach with this problem in this population, and **for whom** does it hold — which cultures, families, settings and languages was it developed in and tested on? The second question is taken up in week 12 and in CAC. 710; the first in week 14.

Table 1.1 shows how four approaches answer the four questions about the same presenting problem, a nine-year-old's refusal to attend school.

| Question | Psychodynamic | Behavioural / CBT | Person-centred | Systemic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| View of the child | A subject with unconscious conflict and a developing internal world | A learner whose behaviour is shaped by consequences and cognitions | A person with an actualising tendency blocked by conditions of worth | A member of a system whose behaviour carries a function for it |
| What maintains it | Separation anxiety; unconscious meaning of school; defensive avoidance | Negative reinforcement of staying home; catastrophic cognition; safety behaviours | A relationship in which the child's experience is not accepted | Parental anxiety, enmeshment, unresolved loss, school–family coalition |
| Mechanism | Insight; containment; making the unconscious conscious; a reliable object | Extinction, new learning, cognitive restructuring, exposure to disconfirmation | Congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy producing self-acceptance | Changed structure, boundaries and communication; altered function of the symptom |
| Method | Play, interpretation, the transference relationship, work with parents | Graded exposure, behavioural experiments, parent and teacher contingency work | Non-directive sessions, reflection, no advice, no direction | Family sessions, enactments, reframing, tasks, boundary work |

Table: Four answers to the same case. The differences are not stylistic; each column implies a different person to invite into the room.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When you read any account of a therapy — a manual, a paper, a training brochure — mark which of the four questions it answers. Marketing material for commercial programmes answers question 4 almost exclusively. That is not a reason to reject the programme; it is a reason not to mistake it for a theory.
:::

### Worked example 1.2 — Maintenance versus origin

**Task.** Amara, aged 11, wets the bed roughly twice a week. It began at age 5, shortly after her father left the home; her mother describes those early years as "the bad time". Current facts: Amara sleeps in her grandmother's room; the grandmother wakes her at 2 a.m. "to be safe" and changes the sheets with loud disapproval; Amara has stopped staying overnight with friends and has declined a school camp; the mother, exhausted, has begun to punish missed mornings by removing phone time. List the *maintaining* factors. Then state, for each, which family member's behaviour would have to change.

**Solution.** Origin: the separation at age 5 — unrecoverable and, for treatment purposes, largely irrelevant. Maintaining factors: (1) the 2 a.m. waking, which prevents Amara from learning bladder capacity and from waking to her own signals — change required: grandmother; (2) the disapproval at sheet-changing, which adds shame and anxiety, and anxiety increases enuresis risk — change required: grandmother; (3) avoidance of overnight stays, which protects Amara from discovering that the problem is manageable and from peer-normalising — change required: Amara, supported; (4) punishment of missed mornings, which converts a physiological event into a contingency and raises household stress — change required: mother. Four maintaining factors, four change agents, none of them the father who left six years ago. Note what the analysis has done: it has moved the treatment from "talk about Daddy" to four concrete, observable changes in the household's night.

### Lecture III — Common factors, specific ingredients, and what the evidence actually shows

Suppose two therapies with incompatible theories of the problem and incompatible mechanisms produce the same outcomes. This happens constantly, and it is the most important empirical fact in the field. Three explanations compete.

**The common factors account.** Most of the variance in psychotherapy outcome is not attributable to the specific method. Lambert's widely cited decomposition attributed roughly 40 per cent of change to extratherapeutic events in the client's life, 30 per cent to relationship and expectation, 15 per cent to hope and expectancy effects, and 15 per cent to the model-specific technique; Frank and Frank made a similar argument about the healing qualities shared by all persuasive treatments. Figure 1.2 sets the argument out. The percentages are schematic and contested — they are not measurements — but the direction of the finding has been replicated many times: what the therapist and client do together, and what happens in the client's life, matter more than which school the therapist belongs to.

![A schematic bar showing the approximate share of variance in psychotherapy outcome attributed to extratherapeutic change, relationship and expectation, hope and expectancy, and model-specific technique. Percentages are illustrative of the argument rather than a measurement, and are contested. *Source:* prepared for this edition, after Lambert (1992) and Frank and Frank (1991).](/books/cac701/figures/fig-01-02.png){width=94%}

**The equivalence account.** The "dodo bird verdict" — that all bona fide therapies are roughly equally effective — has been argued from meta-analytic data and disputed from the same data by investigators who find reliable differences for particular disorders. Both sides are partly right, and the practical conclusion is modest: for many common presentations, differences between established therapies are small relative to differences between a competent therapy and no therapy, or between a good alliance and a poor one.

**The specificity account.** Some treatments do outperform others for some problems, and the clearest examples are in child and adolescent work: behavioural parent training for conduct problems, exposure-based treatment for anxiety, and trauma-focused cognitive-behavioural therapy for post-traumatic stress all have evidence that is stronger than the evidence for undifferentiated supportive counselling. Specificity is real; it is simply narrower than advocates claim.

Three consequences follow for a beginning practitioner, and they are the practical content of this lecture.

First, **the relationship is not the soft part of the work**. Alliance, empathy, agreement on goals and tasks, and the credibility of the treatment to the child and the parent are among the strongest predictors of outcome, and they are trainable. In work with children the alliance is triadic — child, parent, counsellor — and it is common for the adult alliance to be good while the child's is not, which is a serious problem that looks like success.

Second, **allegiance and depth of training matter more than choice of school**. Outcomes are better where the practitioner believes in the method and has been properly trained in it. Eclecticism adopted before competence in any single approach tends to produce drift rather than flexibility, and drift is invisible to the person drifting.

Third, **evidence-based practice is a three-legged stool, not a list**. Sackett and colleagues defined it as the integration of best available research with clinical expertise and with the patient's values and circumstances; Kazdin's distinction between evidence-based *treatments* (manualised methods that have been tested) and evidence-based *practice* (the whole process of deciding what to do with this child) is the version that matters here. A list of approved programmes answers the research leg and ignores the other two. Weisz and Kazdin's collection is the standard reference for what has actually been tested in children and adolescents.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When you read an effectiveness claim, ask three questions before you believe it: what was the comparison condition (waiting list, treatment as usual, or an active alternative)? who was the population (age, setting, comorbidity, culture — and was it children, or adults generalised downwards)? and what was measured (a rating scale completed by the parent, the teacher, the child, or an independent observer — all of which disagree more than you would expect)?
:::

### Worked example 1.3 — Reading an effectiveness claim

**Task.** A paper reports: "In a sample of 62 children aged 8–12 referred to a university clinic for anxiety, the new programme produced significantly greater reduction in parent-rated anxiety than a waitlist control at post-treatment (p < .001), maintained at three-month follow-up." Apply the three tests.

**Solution.** Comparison condition: waitlist. Waitlist controls answer "is this better than nothing for three months?" and almost any credible treatment is; they do not answer "is this better than an existing treatment?" Population: university-clinic referrals, which over-represent families with transport, time and confidence in institutions, and under-represent the children you will actually meet in a school or a district clinic. Measurement: parent-rated anxiety only. Parents are not blind to condition, they have an investment in the treatment having worked, and parent and child ratings of child anxiety correlate modestly at best. Verdict: the claim supports "probably better than nothing, for anxious children of engaged families, as their parents see it" — which is not nothing, and is not what the abstract implies.

::: vignette
**Daniel — a fictitious composite.** Daniel is nine. He has not attended school for eleven days. The stated reason changes: stomach ache, then a boy who looks at him, then that the work is too hard, then nothing at all. His mother sits with him each morning; his father works nights and describes Daniel as "fine, just soft". The family moved seven months ago. In the first session Daniel draws a house with no door, says "that's not for you to see", and asks twice whether his mother is still in the waiting room.

*Reading 1 — attachment and internal experience.* The move disrupted a peer network; the father's absence leaves the mother as the sole regulator of anxiety; Daniel's repeated checking of her presence and his doorless house suggest separation anxiety with an inhibited expression of it. The mechanism of change would be the child's experience of a reliable other and the parents' capacity to tolerate his distress. The method would include play, gentle exploration of the separation, and work with the mother's own anxiety.

*Reading 2 — behavioural and cognitive.* Not attending is negatively reinforced every morning: distress falls when Daniel stays home. The mother's presence is part of the reinforcement schedule. The cognition is catastrophic ("something bad will happen, and I could not cope") and is never tested because avoidance prevents disconfirmation. The mechanism would be extinction of avoidance and disconfirmation of the catastrophic belief. The method would be graded return, parent and teacher contingency work, and behavioural experiments.

Both readings fit the facts. They imply different people to invite into the room, different first sessions, and different things to count as progress. A third reading — systemic — would ask what the symptom is doing for the family, and would notice that Daniel's absence keeps a mother at home and a father's night shifts unchallenged. Daniel returns in weeks 5, 10 and 15 as the course's running case; keep your notes on him.
:::

::: skills
**Practical 1 — formulation under constraint.** Four timed steps, alone or in pairs, then debrief.

1. **Five minutes.** Read the vignette once. Write a single sentence: *Daniel's difficulty is maintained by ___, and ___ would change it.* No theory names allowed.
2. **Ten minutes.** Write the same case twice, once from an attachment or psychodynamic position and once from a behavioural position. Each account must name a mechanism and one observation that would support it.
3. **Ten minutes.** Compare. Identify one prediction each account makes about what will happen in the next session that the other does not make. This is the test of whether you have two theories or one theory twice.
4. **Five minutes.** Role-play: explain to Daniel's mother, in ordinary language and in under two minutes, what you think is happening and what you propose to do. No jargon. She is sceptical and says, "So you just want him to talk about his feelings?"

**Debrief.** Which account generated a testable prediction? Which generated a description that no observation could contradict? What did the mother's question expose about your explanation?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A comparative formulation of 1,500 words. You are given a second fictitious case at the end of the teaching week. Write a formulation of it from two specified theoretical positions, state each position's mechanism of change, identify the one observation that would discriminate between them, and recommend which you would act on and why.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Each formulation names a mechanism of change, not only a description | 30% |
| The discriminating observation is genuinely discriminating | 25% |
| Developmental status of the child is used in the argument | 20% |
| The recommendation is justified and its risks acknowledged | 15% |
| APA 7th citation and prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Submit the one-sentence formulation from step 1 of the practical by the end of teaching week 1. Feedback is returned within five working days and is not graded.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** A counselling theory is a set of answers to four questions — what a child is, what maintains the difficulty, by what mechanism change occurs, and what the counsellor therefore does — and the practitioner's task is not to choose a school but to be able to state, for this child, what they are relying on and how they would know it failed.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. A colleague says, "I don't do theory, I do what works." Reconstruct the theory they are actually using — its view of the child, its account of maintenance, its mechanism, its method — and identify one situation in which it would predictably fail.
2. If most of the variance in outcome lies outside the technique, what is the justification for training practitioners in specific models at all? Argue both sides, then take a position and state what would change it.
3. Take a case you have observed or read about and write four formulations of it, one per column of Table 1.1. Which would you act on, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong?
4. Why is the distinction between origin and maintenance ethically important as well as clinically useful, when the family believes the origin is the point?
5. What would you say to a parent who asks, "Which therapy is the best one?" Answer in the terms of this week, in under a minute.
