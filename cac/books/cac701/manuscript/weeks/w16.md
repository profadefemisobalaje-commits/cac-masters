# Revision, Integration and Examination {.unit num="16" week="16" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Reconstruct** the whole volume from memory as one map: four lineages, their mechanisms, the shared ground, and the practitioner's position.
2. **Answer** each of the four questions that organised week 1 — what is wrong, why does it persist, what changes it, for whom — for any case in the volume.
3. **Use** retrieval practice and spaced revision rather than re-reading, on a four-week schedule you can actually keep.
4. **Write** examination answers in the three required formats: the comparative essay, the short mechanism answer, and the case-based formulation, each to its mark scheme.
5. **State** the ethical obligations the course attaches to theory: what a lineage forbids, and what you will do when it forbids something the child needs.
:::

### Where this week sits

This is the last week of CAC. 701. The block is given to consolidation, to the examination, and to the closing argument of the volume. Nothing new is introduced; everything is retrieved. The structure of this week is deliberately the structure of the whole course, so that the map you carry into the examination is the map you already built.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_four_questions}
Week 1's organising frame, used here as the revision key: what is wrong (description and classification), why does it persist (mechanism and maintenance), what changes it (method and evidence), for whom and in what world (development, culture, power). In the room: every case in this volume can be re-derived from these four questions.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Retrieval_practice}
Learning by attempting to recall before re-reading, and by spacing attempts over days. The evidence for testing and spacing as the most effective study techniques is strong, and the feeling of fluency produced by re-reading is the least reliable guide to knowing. In the room: close the book, write the map, then open the book.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Mechanism_answer}
The examination's shortest high-mark form: name the process that maintains, name the process that changes it, name the observation that would show the change has occurred. In the room: three sentences that carry most of the marks in most papers.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=What_theory_forbids}
Each lineage's limits, stated as obligations: what it must not be used for, and what you owe the child when its prohibitions conflict with the child's need. In the room: the ethical content of a theory course.
:::

### Lecture I — The map of the volume

![The course in one map: the four lineages with their mechanisms, converging on the shared ground of relationship, development, culture and power, evidence and measurement, and safeguarding, issuing in the practitioner's position — a formulation with a mechanism, a plan with a falsifier, outcomes measured, and the child's world investigated. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-16-01.png){width=94%}

**The four lineages and their mechanisms, in one line each.** Psychodynamic and attachment: distress that could not be signalled or metabolised is organised around, and change comes through a relationship that survives it (weeks 2–4). Behavioural and cognitive: the difficulty is maintained by consequences and by untested predictions, and change comes through new learning and disconfirmation (weeks 5–7). Humanistic and existential: the child's experience of conditions and of meaning governs, and change comes through being met without conditions and through meaning found in adversity (weeks 8–9). Systemic, narrative and cultural: the child is a node in structures, loops and stories, and change comes through altered structure, re-authored story and examined power (weeks 10–12).

**The shared ground.** The relationship does most of the work in every lineage (week 1's common factors, measured honestly in week 8 and week 14). Development gates every method: what a six-year-old, a ten-year-old and a fifteen-year-old can each do with a thought, a scale or a metaphor (weeks 4 and 6). Culture and power are not a module but a condition of every claim the volume makes, including its evidence (week 12). Measurement keeps all of it honest (week 14). Safeguarding overrides all of it (weeks 1, 9, 10, 11, 15).

**The practitioner's position.** A formulation with a named mechanism, a plan with a dated falsifier, outcomes counted, and the child's world — including the child's own words — investigated. That sentence is worth memorising, because it is the answer to the largest question the examination can ask.

### Lecture II — Four weeks of revision that works

**Week A: retrieval, not re-reading.** Close the book and write the map of Figure 16.1 from memory: four lineages, mechanisms, shared ground. Then write the four questions and answer them for Daniel, Ama, Chidi, Amaka, Ngozi and Halima — one page each, no notes. Open the book and mark your own work in a different colour; the colour of the corrections is your syllabus. Re-attempt the same task three days later. Testing yourself is the study method; re-reading is the comforting one.

**Week B: comparisons.** Rebuild the week 15 grid for three cases, with one cell left blank, and fill the blank only from memory. Write, for each pair of lineages, one cancelling pair of first moves and one sequence that resolves it.

**Week C: mechanisms and limits.** For each lineage, write the three-sentence mechanism answer, then the three-sentence prohibition: what it must not be used for, and what you owe the child when its prohibition bites. Week 12's critique belongs here: for each lineage, one sentence on whose interests its provenance served and what you now check because of it.

**Week D: timed practice.** Two specimen answers under time (below), then one full past-paper attempt if your programme supplies one, then a final retrieval of the map. Sleep before the examination is not optional; the memory you are examining was consolidated in it.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** The same four-week cycle is how you will revise for the rest of your career, only with clients instead of cases: retrieve what you thought, compare it with what happened, correct it, and try again. Revision discipline and reflective practice are one skill.
:::

### Lecture III — The examination: formats, marks, and the closing argument

**Format 1 — the comparative essay** (one from two, 45 minutes, 40% of the paper). Typical stem: *"Compare psychodynamic and behavioural accounts of childhood school refusal, and argue which should govern the first three weeks of intervention for a nine-year-old whose mother is the sole regulator."* What earns marks: a mechanism-level comparison (not a technique list), the strongest form of the reading you reject, a governing choice with a dated falsifier, the child's own words, and a stated positionality. What loses marks: neutrality ("both have merit"), lineage loyalty, technique salad, and any answer without a falsifier.

**Format 2 — the short mechanism answer** (six from eight, ten minutes each, 30%). Typical stem: *"Explain the mechanism by which graded exposure changes avoidant behaviour, and name one observation that shows it is working and one that shows the extinction burst has begun."* Three sentences carry the marks: the maintaining process, the change process, the observation. Definition without mechanism earns the lowest band; mechanism without observation earns the middle; both earn the top.

**Format 3 — the case-based formulation** (one compulsory, 45 minutes, 30%). A new case is supplied with a transcript extract and a five-session chart. Required: the five Ps; a governing lineage with reason; an eight-week sequence; a dated falsifier; the ninety-second family presentation; and one sentence on what you do not know. The marking scheme is the week 15 rubric; the examiner looks first for the falsifier, because its absence is the fastest indicator of an unformed clinical mind.

::: assessment
**Examination.** Three hours, closed book, one comparative essay (40%), six short mechanism answers (30%), one case-based formulation (30%). Coursework already assessed: week 5 functional analysis, week 8 micro-practice recording, week 12 cultural formulation, week 13 formulation and family presentation, week 14 measurement plan, week 15 practicum portfolio.

**Examiner's note.** Candidates are not rewarded for naming theorists; they are rewarded for using mechanisms, for stating uncertainty precisely, and for keeping the child — the actual child, in that family, in that culture — present in the answer. An answer that could have been written without the case supplied is an answer that has not read the case supplied.
:::

### Worked example 16.1 — A specimen mechanism answer, marked

**Task.** Stem: *"Explain the mechanism by which graded exposure changes avoidant behaviour, and name one observation showing it is working and one showing the extinction burst has begun."* Write a three-sentence answer, then identify what separates the top band from the middle.

**Solution.** Middle-band answer: "Graded exposure is when a child faces feared situations in steps until the fear reduces; it works by habituation. It is working when the child goes to school." — correct vocabulary, no process, no discriminating observation. Top-band answer: "Avoidance is maintained by negative reinforcement: escape removes the aversive state, which strengthens the escaping behaviour and leaves the catastrophic prediction untested (weeks 5–6). Graded exposure changes this by producing repeated contact with the feared stimulus while escape is prevented, so the prediction is disconfirmed and the reinforcing value of avoidance falls — a process requiring the adult to hold the step, not the child to be brave. It is working when a step is completed without escape and the child's predicted catastrophe is named as not having happened; the extinction burst has begun when the behaviour temporarily worsens in frequency or intensity in the first sessions after escape is blocked, which is the sign to continue rather than to retreat, provided the parents were warned in advance." The difference is not length but whether a maintaining process, a change process and two distinguishable observations are all present.

### Worked example 16.2 — The case-based formulation in forty-five minutes

**Task.** Case supplied in the examination: Blessing (12 — a fictitious composite) has stopped eating at school since a boy laughed at her lunchbox four months ago; she eats a large meal at home each evening; her mother alternates between fury and tears at mealtimes; the school nurse has recorded weight loss of 1.5 kg; the family's pastor has advised prayer and fasting. Five-session chart supplied: flat. Plan your forty-five minutes and give the skeleton answer.

**Solution.** Minutes: 5 reading the case and marking the transcript; 5 the five Ps; 10 the governing lineage and sequence; 5 the falsifier; 5 the ninety-second family presentation; 5 the "what I do not know" sentence and a medical-safety check; 10 review against the rubric. Skeleton: *Presenting* — food refusal at school with weight loss; *predisposing* — early feeding history, temperament, body commentary at home (ask, do not assume); *precipitating* — the lunchbox incident and the family's move to evening-only eating; *perpetuating* — the evening meal compensating and thereby removing the cost of daytime refusal, the mother's alternating fury and tears making mealtimes high-arousal, the school's anxious monitoring adding audience, and the fasting advice supplying a morally sanctioned route to eating less; *protective* — the friendship group, Blessing's own stated wish to "eat normally again", the pastor's influence if recruited rather than contradicted. *Governing lineage and sequence* — behavioural/medical first because weight loss in a twelve-year-old requires a medical opinion before any psychological plan and because the maintaining structure is a contingency pattern; weeks 1–2 medical review plus a school-meal plan with the mother's role changed from monitor to companion; weeks 3–5 graded return to eating at school with the lunchbox item she chooses, the laughing boy addressed through the school rather than through Blessing; parallel person-centred sessions from week 3 giving her a room with no demand. *Falsifier* — if by the end of week 4 she has not eaten one full school meal and the chart has not moved below its pre-plan mean by a reliable margin, the contingency hypothesis is wrong and the formulation returns to the medical and eating-disorder pathway, which is a referral, not a technique. *Family sentence* — ninety seconds, in her mother's words, ending with the promise to change the plan if the numbers do not move. *What I do not know* — whether an eating disorder is developing; I would know by medical review, by asking Blessing directly about shape and weight preoccupation, and by the chart at week 4. Note that the pastoral advice is engaged, not overridden: it is a cultural resource with a risk, and the plan recruits the pastor's authority for eating rather than arguing with him (week 12).

**The closing argument.** You began this volume with four questions and a nine-year-old who had not been to school for eleven days. You end it with the same four questions and a discipline: to hold several readings of one child at once, to choose between them for reasons, to say what would change your mind, to count whether it worked, and to notice whose world you were standing in while you did all this. Theories are not beliefs. They are instruments with handles, limits and histories, and a good practitioner is not loyal to an instrument — only to the child it was picked up for. The next volumes in this series give you the assessment, the developmental psychopathology, the specific disorders, the play and group methods, the ethics and law, and the supervised hours. Carry the four questions with you into all of them.

::: skills
**Practical 16 — the last seminar.** In fours, ninety minutes. Each writes, unseen by the others, a three-sentence mechanism answer to the same stem (Format 2 above), then the group marks all four against the three-band scheme, defends its marks, and agrees which answer it would put forward and why. The second half: each states one thing the course changed in how they will practise, and one thing they still do not know how to do — and the group says where the second thing is taught, in which volume or which placement. No one leaves the seminar without both statements made aloud.
:::
