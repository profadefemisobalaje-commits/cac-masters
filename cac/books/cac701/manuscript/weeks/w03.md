### Where this week sits

Week 2 ended with the claim that early relationships become structure. This week examines the research programme that tested that claim and made it measurable. Attachment theory is the psychodynamic lineage's most durable gift to practice with children, and also its most over-used word: "attachment" is invoked to explain everything from clinginess to arson. By the end of this week you should be able to use the concept precisely, and to notice when others are not.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Attachment_behaviour}
Behaviour whose predictable outcome is proximity to a particular person under conditions of fear, fatigue or illness — crying, following, clinging, reaching. It is a behavioural system with a function, not a sentiment. In the room: the child who checks whether mother is in the waiting room is running attachment behaviour; the child who does not check may be running it too, in the deactivating form.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Safe_haven_and_secure_base}
Two directions of one relationship. The safe haven is who the child goes to when frightened; the secure base is who the child goes *from* when exploring. In the room: a child who has neither will not play, because play requires confidence that the world can be left and returned to.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Internal_working_model}
The child's accumulated, largely implicit expectations of what happens when need is expressed: whether the signal will be answered, and how. In the room: the reason two children respond identically to your warmth with opposite meanings — one relieved, one suspicious.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Sensitivity}
The caregiver's capacity to notice the child's signal, read it correctly, and respond promptly and appropriately. Not love, not commitment, not resources: accuracy and timing. In the room: the variable you are actually working on in most parenting-focused work.
:::

### Lecture I — From drive to relationship: what Bowlby changed

**Proposition 1. Attachment is not a derivative of feeding.** The psychoanalytic and learning traditions both derived the child's tie to the mother from the satisfaction of hunger. Bowlby's argument, built on ethology and on the observation of separated children, was that the tie is primary: proximity-seeking is its own system with its own function, the protection of the immature organism. Harlow's work with infant monkeys — preference for the cloth surrogate over the wire one that fed them — made the point unanswerable, though its method would not be permitted today and is cited here as history.

**Proposition 2. Separation has an observable sequence.** Observation of young children separated from a parent showed protest, then despair, then detachment — and that detachment, the apparent recovery, is the most worrying phase, because it is the child's solution to an unbearable situation and it generalises. Robertson's films of hospitalised children changed practice in hospitals and, eventually, in courts and care systems.

**Proposition 3. The Strange Situation made the theory measurable.** Ainsworth's procedure — a series of separations and reunions with a stranger in an unfamiliar room — compresses the attachment system into twenty minutes and makes the reunion observable. What is coded is not the separation distress but the *organisation of behaviour at reunion*: how the child resolves the conflict between the wish for comfort and whatever else the relationship has taught.

**Proposition 4. Four organisations, not four temperaments.** In the secure pattern (B) distress is signalled and comfort accepted, and play resumes. In the avoidant pattern (A) the signal is minimised: the child looks fine and is not, physiologically. In the resistant pattern (C) the signal is maximised and comfort does not settle it. In the disorganised pattern (D), added later by Main and Solomon, there is no coherent strategy: the child approaches and freezes, stills, or shows contradictory behaviour, because the person who would be the solution is also, in some way, the source of alarm. Figure 3.1 places the four on the two dimensions the procedure measures.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** A is not "cold", C is not "manipulative", D is not "naughty". Each category is the best organisation the child could build with the caregiver available. When you feel moral judgement rising toward a child's strategy, you have stopped formulating.
:::

### Separation's sequence in clinical work: protest, despair, detachment

Bowlby's separation sequence is the lineage's oldest clinical instrument and remains the most under-used one in services that move children around. **Protest** is the loud phase: crying, searching, refusal of substitutes, the child at the gate, the ward door, the car window. It is exhausting to witness and it is the healthiest phase, because it is the attachment system doing exactly its job — demanding the specific person. Adults routinely punish protest ("stop that noise, you are fine"), and the punishment teaches the sequence to skip ahead. **Despair** is the quiet phase that untrained observers welcome: the child who stops crying, lies down, withdraws into the bed or the corner, refuses play. It is not recovery; it is grief with the motor still running, and it is the phase most often misrecorded as "settled" in hospital notes, placement reports and boarding registers. **Detachment** is the phase that should alarm everyone: the child who becomes pleasant again — sociable with staff, accepting of substitutes, apparently recovered — and who, when the parent finally returns, does not go to her, or goes and then turns away. Detachment is the child's solution to an unbearable situation: if the signal cannot reach the haven, the signal is switched off, and the switching-off generalises — to the next ward, the next placement, the next relationship, and eventually into the child's character as the avoidant organisation of the Strange Situation. The sequence's clinical force is therefore a warning about **what relief means**: a child who becomes easy after a separation has not recovered; the child may have detached, and the cost will be paid later, elsewhere, to someone who never saw the bill arrive.

Four obligations follow in any service that separates children — hospitals, placements, boarding, migration, the week with the father. **Visit and contact are treatment,** not privileges: Robertson's films changed hospital practice by showing that the sequence runs on the absence of the familiar person and is interrupted by their presence, in doses, early. **Prepare the separation in the child's grammar:** who, when, how long, what stays the same; the child's working model updates from exactly these predictions kept or broken. **Record the phase, not the volume:** "quiet since day three" is a red flag written as a compliment, and the file should say which phase the child is in, by name, each week. **Treat the reunion as the assessment:** the first minutes of return — approach, freezing, turning away, clinging that does not settle — are the cheapest attachment assessment any service will ever run, and they are free, repeated, and already happening; the practitioner's job is to be present for one of them and to write down what happened in behavioural language. Where the sequence has run to detachment, the repair is slow and behavioural rather than interpretive: predictability first, promises kept in small denominations, and no demand for affection the child cannot currently feel — because detachment thaws in the order it froze, and a demand for warmth in month one is a second separation wearing a hug.

### The micro-evidence: still-face, rupture and repair, and serve-and-return

If the Strange Situation is the lineage's macro-instrument, three micro-paradigms supply its finest evidence, and each converts directly into practice. In the **still-face paradigm**, a parent interacts normally with an infant and then, on cue, holds the face still and unresponsive for one to two minutes. The infant's sequence is remarkably lawful: orienting and renewed signalling (the smile offered again, louder), then distress, then withdrawal — and at the resumption of normal interaction, most dyads recover within minutes through a visible co-regulatory dance. The paradigm's lesson is not that stillness harms; two minutes do not harm. It is that **the infant's expectation of answer is observable in real time**, that its violation runs a predictable sequence, and that recovery is a dyadic achievement with a measurable duration. Practitioners who have watched the still-face sequence stop describing unresponsive care as "the parent is shy" and start describing it as a signal meeting no answer, because they have seen what the signal does in ninety seconds.

The **rupture-and-repair** literature, descending from Tronick's mutual-regulation work, adds the finding that matters most for imperfect parents and for practitioners: normal dyads are out of sync far more of the time than in it — the majority of moments in observed interaction are mismatches — and what distinguishes healthy dyads is not the absence of rupture but the **probability and speed of repair**. The infant whose missed signal is caught a moment later, repeatedly, installs a different working model from the infant whose missed signal stays missed: not "I am always answered", which is a fantasy no parent can supply, but "misses are recoverable", which is the model that survives real childhoods. This is the empirical floor beneath week 2's good-enough hour and beneath every parenting intervention this programme will teach: the target is repair rate, not perfection, and the sentence to a shamed parent is therefore evidence-based rather than consolatory — "what your child needs is not a parent who never misses; it is a parent who comes back, and you came back".

**Serve-and-return** names the ordinary unit: the child's signal (a look, a syllable, a pointing finger) and the adult's contingent, marked response, thousands of times a day, building the expectation of answer in doses too small to see. Its poverty — not cruelty but absence, the unresponsive care of depression, of exhaustion, of the phone-faced room — is now understood as a mechanism in its own right, which is why interventions aimed at depressed parents' responsiveness are child mental-health interventions and not only adult ones. Three micro-paradigms, one grammar: the signal is real, the answer builds the model, the miss is ordinary, the repair is the medicine. Every segment of the secure-base cycle you will audit in Practical 3 is these ninety-second events accumulated over years, and the practitioner who can see the micro-sequence in a single session — the child's glance, the carer's missed beat, the carer's late catch, the child's settling — is reading the cycle live rather than reconstructing it from report.

### Worked example 3.1 — Reading a reunion

**Task.** A nursery records a reunion on video for a settling-in assessment. Tunde (14 months) continues playing when his mother leaves, does not look up when she returns, and turns his body away while reaching briefly toward her, the reach abandoned. His heart rate, measured in a research context, rose at separation. Classify, and say what the classification implies about the household.

**Solution.** The pattern is avoidant (A): distress at separation (physiological), minimised signalling at reunion, attention turned to objects, an aborted approach. The implication is not that the mother is unloving but that bids for comfort have, repeatedly, not been answered or have been answered with rejection, so the child has organised around not bidding. Intervention targets the caregiver's response to distress signals, not the child's "independence", which is the strategy and not the problem. Note the trap: to an untrained observer Tunde looks settled and easy. Avoidant children are frequently described as "good babies".

### The Strange Situation in detail: procedure, coding, and what it costs

The procedure is eight three-minute episodes, and its elegance is that it installs a standardised dose of the two conditions attachment theory cares about — an unfamiliar room and a familiar person leaving — and then watches what the child does with the conflict at the moment of return. Episode 1: parent and infant enter. Episode 2: parent settles, infant explores; the room is deliberately interesting, because exploration is the baseline the system protects. Episode 3: a stranger enters, first silent, then conversing, then approaching the infant — the first mild challenge. Episode 4: first separation; parent leaves, stranger adapts to the infant. Episode 5: first reunion; parent returns, greets, comforts if needed, and re-engages the infant with the toys before settling again. Episode 6: second separation, shorter or longer by protocol. Episode 7: second reunion. The coding is done on the reunions, on scales of proximity-seeking, contact-maintaining, avoidance, resistance and distance interaction, and the category is assigned from the **organisation** of these at reunion: not how much the child cried, but what the child did with the wish for comfort in the presence of the person who is supposed to answer it.

Two features deserve emphasis because practitioners routinely misstate them. First, the separation episodes exist to activate the system; the reunion episodes exist to reveal its organisation — which is why a child who cried a great deal and was comforted quickly is secure, and a child who cried little and turned to toys may not be. Second, the procedure measures the relationship, not the child: the same infant can be classified differently with mother and with father, because what is coded is the history of answers this dyad has built. This is the reason the categories travel so poorly into personality language, and the reason Lecture III's first correction exists.

The cross-cultural critique is serious and should be learned rather than deflected. The procedure assumes separations of this kind are unusual but bearable; in communities where infants are rarely separated from the caregiver, or are carried through most of the day, the episode is a larger insult than the designers intended, and the distress it provokes may measure the strangeness of the separation rather than the organisation of the bond. The observed distribution shifts are the evidence: German samples, where early independence is valued, show more avoidant classifications; Japanese samples, where separation of this kind is rare, show more resistant classifications and greater distress at the procedure itself. The defence of the procedure is not that it is culture-free — it is not — but that within a culture the categories retain their meaning and their predictions, and that the reunion logic (what the child does with the wish for comfort) is observable in every culture, even where the laboratory is not. The practitioner's conclusion is the useful one: **code the logic, not the laboratory.** In your own room you will never run the Strange Situation; you will run its questions — who is the haven, what happens to the signal, what happens to exploration — from separations and reunions the child's life supplies for free: the school gate, the clinic door, the Monday morning after a weekend elsewhere.

### Lecture II — The secure-base cycle in practice

Figure 3.2 draws the cycle the whole lineage is trying to protect: exploration out, signal when threatened, safe haven, co-regulation, and out again. Three clinical uses follow.

**First, as an assessment frame.** For any child, ask four questions: who is the safe haven, is the haven actually safe, what happens to the signal on the way to the haven, and what happens to exploration afterwards. A child in care may have a haven on paper and none in fact; a child whose haven responds inconsistently will signal loudly (C); a child whose haven punishes signalling will not signal at all (A); a child whose haven is frightening will show no workable strategy (D).

**Second, as a treatment target.** Most parenting-focused interventions — including the behavioural parent training of week 5 and the dyadic work of CAC. 728 — can be described as repairs to one segment of the cycle: helping the caregiver notice the signal (sensitivity), tolerate the distress (co-regulation), or survive the reunion (repair after rupture). Naming the segment tells you which sessions to run.

**Third, as an account of what the counsellor is.** With an avoidant child you will be tested by apparent indifference; with a resistant child by protest that does not settle; with a disorganised child by fear of you. The counsellor's task is to become a predictable segment of the cycle without pretending to replace the caregiver — a temporary secure base whose job is to make the real one usable again.

Disorganised attachment is strongly associated with caregiving that is frightening, frightened or frighteningly absent, and therefore with maltreatment. Where a child shows fear without solution toward a caregiver — freezing, stilling, approach-avoidance in the same moment — treat it as a safeguarding indicator requiring assessment of the caregiving environment, not merely as a treatment consideration. Follow your jurisdiction's and placement's procedure.

### Attachment after infancy: middle childhood and the adolescent haven

The programme you are training in is a programme in children **and adolescents**, and the secure-base cycle does not expire at preschool — it changes register, and practitioners who keep looking for infant behaviour in a fourteen-year-old will conclude, wrongly, that attachment no longer matters. In middle childhood the cycle lengthens: the haven becomes an **availability in expectation** rather than in contact — the child explores for days at a time (school, sleepovers, the market route) carrying the confidence that the haven would answer if signalled, and the observable behaviour shifts from clinging to **communication**: the small report at dusk, the complaint offered on the walk home, the joke at the expense of the day. The volume of signalling falls; its voluntary character rises; and the audit's question becomes not "does the child signal?" but "does the child still **tell**?" — because telling is the school-age haven's fuel, and its cessation (week 8 of CAC. 702 will return to this as knowledge-supply) is the first measurable break in the cycle long before any crisis.

In adolescence the transformation is sharper and more easily misread as detachment. The adolescent's exploration is the whole point of the stage, and a healthy adolescence looks, to an observer wired for infant cues, like avoidance: less contact, more peer orientation, open disagreement. The discriminating observations are three. **First, the return:** the secure adolescent uses the haven under real threat — illness, humiliation, heartbreak — and the signal, when it comes, comes in words at an odd hour; the avoidant organisation shows as no signal even under demonstrated threat. **Second, the internal coherence of the account:** asked about their parents, the secure adolescent gives a textured, believable picture — gratitude and grievance in the same sentence — while the dismissing organisation gives a flat "fine, they are fine, nothing to say" and the preoccupied one gives a flood in which the parent's absence or unfairness is still running the show. **Third, the base function:** whether the adolescent's exploration is actually launched from the household (the plan told, the route shared, the code-message sent) or conducted in secrecy, because secrecy is not autonomy; autonomy that hides is anxiety in costume.

The clinical payoff is exact: with adolescents, the cycle's repairable segment is usually **the household's reception of the telling** — the rankings, the interrogation, the lecture that follows every disclosure and teaches the adolescent the price of the next one — and the intervention is therefore frequently a parenting intervention aimed at one behaviour: what happens in the first ten seconds after the child tells. Warmth in the eleventh second cannot undo a verdict in the first.

### The practitioner's own history: the instrument checks itself

Attachment theory applies to the person holding it, and supervision in this lineage begins there because the practitioner's own organisation is the instrument through which every signal in the room is read. The patterns present in practitioners as in everyone: the **deactivating** practitioner, whose own unanswered childhood signals were solved by not-signalling, experiences the clinging child as demand and the resistant parent as hysteria, and drifts toward tidy, low-affect formulations; the **hyperactivating** practitioner, whose signals were answered unpredictably, experiences the avoidant child as a rescue mission and the steady parent as cold, and drifts toward over-involvement and boundary thinness (week 4 of CAC. 703 will meet this again as the rescue pull); and the practitioner whose history carries fright without solution experiences the disorganised child's stillness as familiar weather and may stay too long in it, or flee it into procedure.

None of this disqualifies anyone; it is the reason the discipline exists. Three practices keep the instrument honest: **name your pattern once, in supervision, in a sentence** — because a named pattern is a variable you can watch, and an unnamed one is a lens you mistake for an eye; **watch your pull per case-type** — the case that feels like your childhood is the case whose formulation you must write twice, once as felt and once as observed; and **use the countertransference line of the process note** (week 2's format) as the attachment audit of the room: what the child cast you as is data about the child, and what you felt in the casting is data about the intersection of the two histories. The lineage's deepest professional claim is that a relationship can be the treatment; its deepest professional warning is that a relationship is the treatment **only when the practitioner's half of it is observed**, and observation of one's own half is trainability's price.

### Recording the cycle audit: the file format

The cycle audit of Practical 3 has a file form, because an audit that is not recorded cannot be reviewed, supervised or defended. Five lines, after the presenting paragraph: **Haven column** — the names, in the child's routing order by kind of distress ("night: grandmother; shame: sister; hunger and money: mother; school trouble: nobody yet"). **Signal line** — how the child signals each haven, and what the signal costs ("signal to mother loud and late; to grandmother early and quiet; to nobody at school"). **Answer line** — what each haven does with the signal, and its predictability ("mother: answers when not exhausted, unpredictable by market day; grandmother: answers always, dies March"). **Exploration line** — what the child stopped doing since the break ("stopped sleepovers; stopped the choir route alone"). **Break line** — the single segment named, with its date and its disconfirming observation ("break = predictability of mother's answer, from March; disconfirm: ritual installed four weeks with no change in gate catastrophes, then reopen at school segment"). The format's virtue is that it forces the audit into the child's routing rather than the household's ideology, and that the break line, like every hypothesis in this course, carries the observation that would retire it. Reviewed at six-week intervals, the five lines also become the case's longitudinal record: havens gained and lost, signals reopened and closed, exploration resumed — the cycle's history in one column of the file, readable by any colleague who inherits the case, which is the final test of any record: that it lets a stranger continue the work without asking the child to begin again from the beginning.

### Lecture III — What attachment theory does not say

Three over-extensions are common in practice and in court reports, and you should not repeat them.

**The category is not a personality.** Infant attachment categories predict group-level outcomes modestly and probabilistically; they are not diagnoses of the child's character, and a B classification at twelve months is not a warranty. The Minnesota longitudinal study shows the predictive power of early care operating largely *through* subsequent care and circumstance: early patterns matter, and later experience keeps mattering.

**"Attachment disorder" is not the same thing as insecure attachment.** The clinical diagnoses of reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder describe rare patterns associated with severe early neglect and institutional care. They are not labels for difficult behaviour in looked-after children generally, and their casual use has real consequences for the child labelled.

**The mother is not the only attachment figure, and attachment is not the only relationship that matters.** Children build hierarchies of attachment figures including fathers, grandparents and older siblings, and their development depends on many relationships besides attachment ones — teaching, friendship, mentorship. A formulation that reduces a child's difficulties to "attachment" has usually stopped looking.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** When a report states that a child has an "attachment problem", ask three questions: on what observation, using what procedure, against what comparison? If the answer is "the child clings to me", the report has described a behaviour and named a theory over it.
:::

### Disorganisation in detail: fright without solution, and what it obliges

Category D was added because some infants could not be classified: at reunion they approached and then froze mid-movement, or lay prone and stilled for seconds, or reached with the body while the face turned away, or showed the sequenced contradictions — a cry that stops without transition into stillness — that Main and Solomon catalogued as indices of disorganisation. The theoretical account is the most compelling sentence in the lineage: **the attachment figure is the simultaneous solution to and source of the child's alarm.** The child's biology runs one programme — approach the haven — while the same person triggers the fear the haven exists to relieve, and the programme stalls in the contradiction. Fright without solution is not a temperament; it is a logical state, and it is observable as exactly the behaviours the catalogue names.

The correlates are strong enough to govern practice. Disorganisation is over-represented where caregiving is frightening (intrusive, threatening), frightened (the parent who flinches from the child's distress), or frighteningly absent (dissociative lapses, unprocessed loss in the parent), and it is markedly over-represented in maltreated samples — which is why the safeguard box above treats fear-without-solution toward a caregiver as an indicator requiring assessment of the caregiving environment. Its sequelae are probabilistic, not destined: disorganisation elevates the risk of later externalising difficulty and of dissociative-style responses under stress, largely in interaction with continuing adversity, and the Minnesota data again show later care mediating much of the early effect.

Three obligations follow for the practitioner. **Assess the environment before treating the child:** a disorganised presentation is a question about the household first, and a therapy hour that ignores it is a therapy hour decorating a burning house. **Work with the parent's fear, not only the parent's skills:** the frightened and frightening behaviours respond to interventions that let the parent watch their own interaction safely — video-feedback methods (such as the sensitivity-oriented programmes tested in randomised trials with foster and at-risk families) change parental behaviour by showing the parent the child's signal slowed down and labelled, and they change it more reliably than instruction does. **Never diagnose D from a single observation:** the indicators are a research catalogue under a procedure; in the room they are a reason to look harder, to ask the safeguarding questions, and to record what was seen in behavioural language — freezing for twenty seconds at the mother's return — rather than in a category the room did not earn.

### Worked example 3.2 — Where the cycle breaks

**Task.** Zainab (4) is in foster care, third placement in fourteen months. In the current placement she is described as "indiscriminately affectionate": she sits on the foster carer's lap within minutes of meeting visitors, calls strangers "mummy", and shows no preference for the carer at reunion after nursery. Name the segment of the cycle that is broken, the hypothesis it supports, and the first thing you would do.

**Solution.** The broken segment is the specificity of the safe haven: the signal is dispatched to anyone, which means it has never been reliably answered by anyone in particular. The hypothesis is not "too much attachment" but the absence of an organised attachment relationship — the pattern associated with disinhibited social engagement, and a pointer to the quality of early care rather than to the current carer's adequacy. The first thing to do is not to work with Zainab's sociability but to stabilise the placement and work with the carer on being predictably, specifically available: the cycle cannot be repaired with a different adult every quarter. Note what the formulation prevents — a misreading of the child's friendliness as health, and a misreading of the carer as unloved and therefore failing.

### The evidence and its limits: what the attachment claims rest on

Attachment theory is the lineage's best-evidenced branch, and even it requires exact sentences. **Prediction is real and modest:** infant classification predicts child and adolescent outcomes at group level with effect sizes that are meaningful for populations and useless for prophecies about one child; the Minnesota longitudinal study's most important finding is not that early care matters but **how** it matters — largely through the continuity or discontinuity of care afterwards, so that early insecurity followed by good later care yields better outcomes than early security followed by chaos, and the formulation must therefore carry the whole care history, not its first chapter. **Intervention evidence exists where the target is sensitivity:** randomised trials of brief, video-feedback and behaviourally specific parenting interventions (the sensitivity-focused programmes for at-risk and foster families among them) show that parental sensitivity can be changed and that changed sensitivity shifts attachment security more reliably than insight-oriented parenting advice does — which is the empirical backbone of Lecture II's second use, naming the broken segment and treating exactly it. **Measurement beyond infancy is a different instrument:** the Strange Situation expires with the preschool years; thereafter the field uses story-stem and narrative measures (the child completing dollhouse separations) and interview measures with adults, and the concordance between infant categories and later narrative measures is positive but far from perfect — another reason the category is not a personality.

The limit that matters most in this programme's settings is the **court-report limit:** attachment language has become rhetorically powerful in custody and care proceedings, where "disorganised attachment" and "attachment disorder" appear as conclusions without procedures, and where the consequence is a decision about a child's home made on a word. The discipline this week installs is therefore civic as well as clinical: a category is an observation under a procedure against a comparison, and a sentence that lacks any of the three is advocacy wearing a laboratory coat. What would strengthen the base: more trials of segment-specific interventions with follow-up into middle childhood, more work on fathers and on multiple-caregiver hierarchies (the literature remains mother-weighted), and more validation of the measures in non-Western samples, where the hierarchy of havens is often wider than the procedure assumes.

### Conversations in the room: three adults and the word "attachment"

**The foster carer told the child has an "attachment disorder".** — *Answer:* "That phrase is doing two jobs and neither of them helps you. What I can tell you is what Zainab does: she takes comfort from anyone, because for fourteen months no one particular was there to take it from. That is not too much love; it is love with no address. What will change it is not a technique with her but a year of you: the same person, predictable, specifically available, answering the small signals before they become big ones. My job is to help you see the small signals, because children like Zainab stopped making them loudly a long time ago." *Commentary:* the script deletes the diagnosis, translates the behaviour into its history, names the carer as the treatment (which is true and empowering), and promises the practitioner's actual service — signal-detection coaching — rather than a cure.

**The teacher of the avoidant child, "independent and easy".** — *Answer:* "He is neither, and I understand exactly why he looks so. He has learned that asking for help gets nothing useful, so he does not ask — which looks like independence and is actually a eight-year-old alone with everything. The cost will not show in behaviour; it will show in a boy who never brings you the problem until it is a crisis, and in a teenager no adult can reach. What I would ask of you is one small unnatural thing: offer help he has not requested, once a day, lightly, without requiring him to admit need. 'I am going through the spellings with the table; you are in it.' He will refuse some days. The offer is the intervention." *Commentary:* the rewrite converts a compliment into a formulation and gives the teacher a behaviour rather than a belief; the daily unrequested offer is a sensitivity intervention aimed precisely at the deactivated signal.

**The parent who hears "insecure attachment" as a verdict on their love.** — *Answer:* "It is not a verdict and it is not about love. It is about signalling: children are born unable to say 'I am frightened', so they show it, and every parent misses signals — I have missed my own. What we are looking at is which signals get missed most often in your house, and what happens when they are missed, because that is repairable and love is not the question. You came here on a Tuesday evening after work. That is not a verdict either; that is the data on your side." *Commentary:* the script separates attachment from love in the first sentence, universalises missed signals to remove the stigma of defect, and converts the construct into an observable, repairable target — sensitivity and repair — which is what the evidence says can actually change.

### Common errors and their corrections

1. **The category as character.** "He is avoidant" said as a personality. *Correction:* the category names a dyadic history under a procedure; say what the child does with the wish for comfort, and with whom.
2. **Clinging equals insecurity.** The child who clings is read as insecure; the resistant pattern is a hypothesis about inconsistent answer, not about quantity of contact. *Correction:* audit the consistency of answers, not the volume of clinging.
3. **"Attachment disorder" as a behaviour label.** *Correction:* the diagnoses are rare, tied to severe early neglect or institutional care, and require assessment; difficult behaviour in a looked-after child is not one.
4. **The counsellor as substitute parent.** The hour offered as the relationship the child never had. *Correction:* the counsellor is a temporary segment of the cycle whose job is to make the real haven usable; substitution builds dependence on the service.
5. **One observation, one category.** A freezing moment recorded as "disorganised". *Correction:* record the behaviour, its duration and its trigger; let the category wait for a procedure or a pattern.

### Worked example 3.3 — Kemi's resistant cycle, segment by segment

**Task.** Kemi (6) is brought for morning catastrophes at the school gate: clinging, crying, accusations that mother "went without saying goodbye", then a day of checking the gate. At home the pattern is the same in miniature: mother's returns from the market are met with fury and clinging at once, and comfort does not settle her. Audit the cycle and write the segment-matched intervention.

**Solution.** *Safe haven:* mother, unambiguously — Kemi signals to her and only her. *Is the haven safe?* Yes; no fear of mother observed. *What happens to the signal on the way?* It is amplified: the signal arrives already at catastrophe volume, and the history supplied by mother confirms inconsistency — some returns are long and devoted, some are brisk and exhausted, depending on the market day; the child cannot predict the answer, and unpredictability is precisely what maximises signalling. *What happens to exploration afterwards?* It is curtailed: the gate-checking day is a day of monitoring the haven rather than using the base. The broken segment is therefore **the predictability of the answer**, not the presence of the haven and not the child's capacity to signal. *Intervention, matched to the segment only:* a goodbye and a hello ritual of fixed form — the same sentence, the same hug length, the same "I will be back after the bell" — rehearsed with mother and installed for four weeks, with a market-day rule that the return is announced before the door (the child hears the greeting before seeing the state of the mother's fatigue); no work on Kemi's "confidence" at the gate, because the gate is not where the cycle breaks. *Falsifier:* if after four weeks of a fixed ritual the gate catastrophes are unchanged in frequency, the maintenance is not answer-predictability and the audit reopens at the school segment (what happens at the gate after mother leaves — the teacher's reception, the first hour's humiliation), which the present material has not yet examined. *What the formulation does not explain:* Kemi's temperament — her reactivity was high from infancy, per mother — which is a contribution the attachment grammar names as background rather than mechanism, and which the parent work must respect by keeping the ritual boring enough to survive a loud child.

### Worked example 3.4 — Repairing a court-report sentence

**Task.** A report in a custody matter states: "The child's challenging behaviour in contact sessions is caused by disorganised attachment to the mother. Contact should therefore be reduced." Rewrite the paragraph in the observation-procedure-comparison form, and state what a defensible version can and cannot support.

**Solution.** *Rewrite:* "In the two contact sessions I observed (dates), the child approached the mother on entry and stopped moving within one metre of her for approximately twenty seconds on each occasion, with gaze averted; on separation the child cried and then stilled without transition. These behaviours resemble the indices of disorganisation catalogued by Main and Solomon under the Strange Situation procedure; no such procedure was administered in this matter, and I therefore record them as observations requiring assessment rather than as a classification. The behaviours are consistent with fear without solution toward the caregiver, which in the literature is associated with frightening or frightened caregiving and warrants assessment of the caregiving environment; they are not, in themselves, evidence about the safety of contact, and the literature on which I rely does not license an inference from these observations to a recommendation about contact frequency. I recommend assessment of the caregiving environment by [procedure/person], and supervised observation of further sessions, before any recommendation on contact is made." *Commentary:* the defensible version keeps every true thing — the observations, their resemblance to a catalogue, the obligation to assess — and refuses the two leaps the original made: from behaviour to category without a procedure, and from category to a disposal without evidence. A report that cannot survive this rewrite should not survive cross-examination either, and the child's home is what stands in the difference.

### This week's row in the comparison grid

*View of the child:* a signal-sender whose earliest relationships install expectations of answer, and whose strategies — even the costly ones — are organisations built from real history. *View of what maintains:* the broken or unpredictable segment of the secure-base cycle, and the internal working model that keeps routing the signal through it. *Mechanism of change:* repaired sensitivity and predictability in the haven; the child's experience of a signal answered, co-regulated and survived; the counsellor as a temporary, transferable segment of the cycle. *Signature method:* the cycle audit; segment-specific parenting work, video-feedback where available; observation of separations and reunions the child's life supplies. *Who is in the room:* the caregiver, always — attachment work without the caregiver is description without leverage; the child for observation and, later, for narrative. *What counts as progress:* the signal's volume and route normalising toward the available haven; exploration resuming after comfort; repair after rupture becoming ordinary. *What this approach cannot see:* the contingency maintaining the symptom outside the dyad (the classroom, the peer layer); the child's temperament as an independent contributor; and any meaning the child attaches that is not relational — the grief, the guilt, the idea about the self that is not about anyone's answer. Attachment explains the relational grammar of the child's distress; the week 1 grid exists so that you notice when a case needs a second grammar beside it.

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Attachment theory gives practice a measurable relational grammar — signal, haven, base, model — and its discipline is as important as its content: a category is an observation under a procedure, never a personality, and "attachment" explains less than it is asked to explain.

### Context note: hierarchies, compounds and the wide haven

The attachment literature was built largely on infants with one or two caregivers in a nuclear household, and its default image — one mother, one hierarchy — misdescribes the childhoods of this programme. In compound and extended-family care the hierarchy of havens is wide: grandmother for nightmares, auntie for hunger, older sister for shame, mother for everything else, and the child's signal is routed by kind of distress rather than sent to one address. Three consequences follow for the cycle audit. **First, width is a strength, not a dilution:** a child with three reliable havens has three chances of an answer, and the audit should map the routing (who for what) before judging any single relationship; the child who signals differently to different havens is displaying competence, not confusion. **Second, the misreading risk runs both ways:** distributed care can look like indiscriminate friendliness to an observer trained on nuclear norms (worked example 3.2's trap in reverse), and a child left behind by migrating parents — cared for well by a grandmother who is the functional haven — can be misread as abandoned in the attachment sense when the hierarchy has in fact reorganised around a present adult; the audit's question is whether **someone particular** answers, not whether it is the parent the form expects. **Third, migration and loss reorganise the hierarchy without announcing it:** the death of a grandmother-haven can remove the child's only reliable answer for night distress while leaving every daytime relationship intact, and the presenting problem (night terror, school refusal on Mondays after weekend visits) will point at the wrong segment unless the audit asks, explicitly, who answered what **before** and who answers it **now**. The secure-base cycle survives all of this unchanged; what changes is the number of names in the haven column, and the practitioner's discipline is to fill that column from the child's routing rather than from the household's ideology about who ought to be in it.

### Model answers to two seminar questions

**Question 1 — why the reunion?** Because attachment is not distress; it is the organisation of distress-relief. Separation tells you that the system activated — which any unfamiliar room with a leaving parent will achieve in almost any dyad — while reunion tells you what this particular history has taught the child to do with the wish for comfort: signal it and use the answer (B), deactivate it because answers were refused (A), maximise it because answers were unpredictable (C), or stall in the contradiction because the answer is also the alarm (D). The choice of the reunion as the coded event therefore assumes that attachment is a **strategy built from answers**, not a feeling inside the child — which is exactly the assumption that lets the theory be measured, and exactly the assumption that personality-language forgets. It also assumes that twenty minutes of laboratory strangeness can surface a strategy built over months of ordinary nights; the cross-cultural critique questions the strangeness, not the strategy, and the practitioner who codes the logic in their own doorway is using the procedure's insight without its insult.

**Question 3 — when should the counsellor become an attachment figure?** *For:* where no reliable haven exists and none can be built in the time the child has — the child in placement chaos, the unaccompanied adolescent — because the cycle's functions (answer, co-regulation, base) are needs, not luxuries, and a service that withholds them on theoretical grounds withholds them from the only child who cannot obtain them elsewhere; the temporary secure base is then not a boundary violation but the service's honest function. *Against my own answer:* the moment the counsellor becomes the haven, the child's attachment portfolio has one entry that is paid, scheduled and terminable; the termination then repeats the loss the work exists to repair, and the child learns the cruelest lesson available — that havens are services and services end. The position that survives both arguments: the counsellor may **perform** the functions of a haven — answer, co-regulate, base — while explicitly and repeatedly building the child's portfolio elsewhere (a named carer, a teacher, an auntie, a mentor), counting the portfolio's growth as the outcome, and dating from the first session the transfer of each function to a person who will still be present at the child's eighteenth birthday. The test of whether the position is being kept is the file: if the haven column still holds only the counsellor's name at review, the work has failed however warm the hour has been.
