# The Humanistic Lineage I: Person-Centred — The Conditions and the Child {.unit num="8" week="8" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **State** Rogers's three conditions and his sufficiency claim, and distinguish the claim from the weaker claim that the conditions help.
2. **Demonstrate** reflective responding at three levels — content, feeling, and meaning — in a timed micro-practice.
3. **Explain** what child-centred play therapy adds to, and removes from, adult person-centred practice.
4. **Evaluate** the evidence for the conditions and for non-directive play therapy, including what the common-factors literature of week 1 does and does not settle.
5. **Identify** two situations in which a non-directive stance is contraindicated or insufficient with a child, and say what you would do instead.
:::

### Where this week sits

Three lineages down, one question has been constant: what does the practitioner actually do? The behavioural lineage answers with contingencies, the cognitive with experiments. This week's lineage answers with a way of being, and makes the strongest claim in the course: that under specifiable relational conditions, the child does the therapeutic work themselves, and the adult's techniques are at best unnecessary and at worst an interference. You are entitled to reject the claim. You are not entitled to skip it, because the conditions it names are the ones every other lineage borrows when its techniques stall.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Congruence}
The adult is internally what they externally appear; no professional mask, no performed calm. Not self-disclosure of everything — the adult's material is used only where it serves the child — but the absence of a facade the child can sense and must manage. In the room: the child who relaxes when you admit, plainly and briefly, that you too found that hard.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Unconditional_positive_regard}
Prizing the child without conditions: not approving every behaviour, not pretending the behaviour has no consequences, but holding the person as of worth while the behaviour is addressed. In the room: the difference between "what you did hurt him and we will deal with that" delivered inside an unbroken relationship, and the same words delivered as withdrawal.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Empathic_understanding}
Sensing the child's private world as the child senses it, and checking that you have — the checking being half of it. Not sympathy (feeling for), not mind-reading (feeling instead of). In the room: the reflection that ends in a question mark the child can correct, and the correction being welcomed as data.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Non-directiveness}
The deliberate refusal to steer: no advice, no interpretation, no curriculum of questions; the child chooses the material and the pace. In the room: the hardest discipline in the course, because helpfulness is the practitioner's favourite vice.
:::

### Lecture I — The claim, precisely stated

**Proposition 1. Rogers's claim is conditional and strong.** If the adult is congruent, holds unconditional positive regard, and understands the child's inner world empathically — and the child perceives at least a minimum of these — then constructive personality change occurs. Not "the conditions help the techniques work"; the conditions, perceived, are sufficient. Figure 8.1 draws the claim and the question mark this course attaches to it.

![The three person-centred conditions as overlapping circles — congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding — meeting in the child's experience of being felt, with the open question of whether the conditions are sufficient for change or its necessary context. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-08-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 2. The mechanism is the reversal of conditions of worth.** The child who learned that regard is conditional — lovable when quiet, when achieving, when not angry — organises the self around the conditions and disowns what they exclude. Therapy supplies the opposite experience: regard that does not move. Under it, the disowned material can re-enter without catastrophe, and the organism's actualising tendency resumes its work. The mechanism is testable in a soft sense: predicted changes in self-description and in the handling of previously denied feeling.

**Proposition 3. With children, the medium changes and the stance survives.** A child cannot sustain reflective dialogue. Child-centred play therapy (Axline's eight principles, formalised by Landreth) keeps the conditions and moves them into play: the room is permissive within firm limits, the child leads, the adult tracks and reflects play and feeling ("you're making him very small"), and does not teach, direct, or hurry. The limits are themselves part of the regard: a room with no limits is not safe, and a child knows it.

**Proposition 4. Reflection is a skill with levels, not a parrot trick.** Reflecting content ("the tower fell") is tracking; reflecting feeling ("you're furious it fell") is empathy at the first level; reflecting meaning ("it always falls just when it's nearly finished") is empathy at the level the child cannot yet reach alone, and is the one that moves therapy. Mis-timed deep reflection is experienced as intrusion; shallow reflection as boredom. Calibration is the craft.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Test your reflections by the child's response, not by your intention. A reflection that lands produces more material or a correction; a reflection that misses produces politeness or silence. Politeness from a child is a missed reflection until proved otherwise.
:::

### Worked example 8.1 — Three levels of reflection

**Task.** Halima (7) is playing with a doll family. She makes the mother doll leave the house carrying a bag, then has the child doll stand at the window for a long time doing nothing. She says nothing. Write one reflection at each level, and state the risk of each.

**Solution.** Content: "She's going away with her bag, and he's watching from the window." Risk: none, but nothing moves; used too long it becomes narration the child must tolerate. Feeling: "He's waiting. It's a long wait." Risk: moderate — you have named an affect the child has not shown; if wrong, she will correct you, and the correction is progress. Meaning: "He waits at the window every time, in case she comes back that way." Risk: high — this is an interpretation wearing reflection's clothes; if the play is not about return, you have put your story in her room. Sequence matters: earn the deep reflection with two that landed. Note what non-directiveness forbids here: asking who the dolls are, asking whether this is like her home, and reassuring her that mummies come back. All three would convert her play into your agenda.

### Lecture II — What the evidence says, and what it cannot say

The conditions are among the best-supported findings in psychotherapy research, though not in the form Rogers stated. Relationship variables — alliance, empathy, goal consensus, positive regard — predict outcome across therapies and across client ages, and the adult alliance in child work predicts outcome modestly while the child's own alliance predicts it better; Norcross and Lambert's summaries are the standard citation. That supports the weaker claim: the conditions matter enormously. It does not settle the sufficiency claim, because no design can isolate perceived conditions from everything else a practitioner does.

For non-directive child-centred play therapy the evidence is mixed: against waitlist it shows effects on internalising difficulties and on self-concept; against active treatments it generally does not show superiority, and several positive studies are small and allegiance-laden. Two honest conclusions follow. First, non-directive play therapy is a legitimate treatment for some children — particularly those whose difficulties are organised around regard, control and expression, and those too young for verbal method. Second, it is not a default, and presenting it as the gentle option for every young child mis-states the evidence.

::: safeguard
Non-directiveness is a therapeutic stance, not a safeguarding stance. Where a child's play or speech discloses risk, the directive duties of the profession override the stance completely: you stop following the child's lead and you act on procedure. A child-centred room is permissive within limits, and the limits include the law. Practitioners who confuse the stance with passivity in the face of risk cause the harm this lineage exists to prevent.
:::

### Lecture III — Where non-directiveness is not enough

Three situations require you to say so plainly and change method.

**Where the difficulty is maintained by contingencies the child cannot change.** Enuresis maintained by night-time waking patterns (week 3), school refusal maintained by morning contingencies (weeks 1 and 5): reflective understanding of the child's experience is necessary and will not move the loop. The plan needs the adults and the contingencies.

**Where the child needs skills they do not have.** Emotion-regulation deficits, social-skill deficits, trauma-processing deficits: acceptance and prizing do not teach distress tolerance or narrative reconstruction. The humanistic stance makes the teaching possible; it is not the teaching.

**Where safety requires direction.** Risk of harm to self or others, disclosure of abuse, acute crisis: the session becomes directive, structured and, where required, reported. The conditions remain — congruence, regard, empathy — inside a frame the child is told about honestly.

The mature position is not eclecticism but sequence and honesty: know what each element of your work is for, which lineage supplies it, and what would show it failing. That is the position week 13 builds.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** If you find yourself unwilling to give advice because the stance forbids it, while the child drowns in a contingency they cannot change, you are serving the theory rather than the child. Name the element you are using and why; the child's interest governs, not the lineage's pride.
:::

### Worked example 8.2 — The parent's question

**Task.** A father asks, after six non-directive sessions with his son (6): "You just sit there and describe what he does. When does the therapy start?" Compose your reply in ordinary language, under ninety seconds, naming the mechanism without jargon.

**Solution.** "What your son is doing in there is the therapy, and my job is harder than it looks. For six years he has learned which parts of himself get approval and which get him into trouble, so the troublesome parts went underground — into the wetting, the rages, the refusal. In that room nothing he feels gets him into trouble with me, and nothing he plays is corrected. That is not nothing: it is the first relationship in which the whole of him is allowed to be present, and children change from the inside outwards when that happens. What you will see first is not insight — he is six — it is the play getting louder and messier before it gets calmer, and the rages at home easing as they stop being necessary. If by week ten we see neither, I will tell you, and we will change the plan together." Note the structure: mechanism (regard without conditions re-admits disowned material), prediction (play intensifies then settles; home behaviour eases), and a falsification date. A parent can trust a plan that can be wrong.

::: skills
**Practical 8 — reflective micro-practice.** In pairs, six minutes each way. One speaks about a mildly difficult real event of their own (not a client); the other reflects only — content, feeling, meaning — with no questions, no advice, no reassurance, no self-disclosure. The speaker marks afterwards: which reflections landed, which missed, which felt like intrusion, and whether the final minute went deeper than the first. Swap. Debrief: what did you want to do that you were forbidden from doing, and what happened when you didn't do it?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A recorded or transcribed eight-minute micro-practice with a peer, submitted with a 600-word commentary: identify three reflections by level, one miss and what the speaker's response showed, one moment where directive pull arose and how it was handled, and a statement of one case type for which you would not use a non-directive method.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Reflections at three levels present and calibrated | 35% |
| No questions, advice or reassurance in the recorded segment | 20% |
| Commentary uses the speaker's feedback as data | 20% |
| Contraindication stated with reasons | 15% |
| Prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Bring one session in which you believe the relationship itself did the work, with the observation that supports the belief.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The person-centred lineage's claim is that perceived congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding are sufficient for change; the evidence secures the weaker and more useful truth that they are the ground on which every other method stands — and non-directiveness is a discipline to be chosen, not a default to hide in.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Rogers claimed sufficiency; the evidence secures importance. What, practically, is lost and gained by holding the weaker claim?
2. Is congruence compatible with professional boundaries? Give a case where disclosure serves the child and one where it serves the adult.
3. A child-centred playroom has firm limits. Reconcile limits with unconditional positive regard in one paragraph a parent could read.
4. Why is politeness from a child evidence of a missed reflection? What else could it be evidence of?
5. Which of this week's three contraindications would be hardest for you personally to act on, and what would you need in order to act?
