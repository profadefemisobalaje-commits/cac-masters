### Where this week sits

Weeks 5 and 6 built a therapy of change: analyse the loop, test the thought, alter the consequence. This week asks what happens when the loop cannot be changed, or when the attempt to change inner experience is itself the loop. The third wave does not replace CBT; it notices that a child fighting their own mind is a child in a fight, and offers a different relationship to inner events as the mechanism.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_control_agenda}
The strategy of treating unwanted thoughts and feelings as things to be eliminated, and measuring success by their absence. In the room: the adolescent who has spent four years trying not to feel panic and has organised a life around the trying. The third wave's first move is to show that the strategy, not the feeling, is the problem.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Defusion}
Changing the relationship to a thought without changing its content: from "I am stupid" to "I am having the thought that I am stupid", and then to "my mind is offering the stupid story again". In the room: the difference between arguing with a thought and watching it arrive.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Psychological_flexibility}
The capacity to stay in contact with the present, with what one feels, while doing what one values. Not symptom removal; the outcome variable of ACT and, Figure 7.1 argues, of much good therapy under another name.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Radical_acceptance}
In DBT, the deliberate stopping of the fight with what is already true — not approval, not resignation, but the end of the extra suffering that fighting adds. In the room: what makes room for change, paradoxically, because a child who is no longer at war with their history can plan a future.
:::

### Lecture I — What the third wave noticed

**Proposition 1. Thought suppression rebounds.** Instruct a person not to think of something and the thought returns with interest; for anxious children, the attempt to control inner events increases their salience and the fear of them. A therapy whose method is control of inner events can therefore manufacture the problem it treats.

**Proposition 2. The content of the thought may matter less than the stance toward it.** Two children hold "I am worthless". One is fused with it — it is simply true, the way the floor is there. The other can watch it: "that's the old tape". Same content, different suffering, different prognosis. Defusion trains the second stance without disputing the content, which is why it works where disputation produced compliance (week 6).

**Proposition 3. Values give the work a direction that symptoms cannot.** A life organised around not feeling anxious shrinks; a life organised around being a friend, a player, a maker expands and carries discomfort with it. Committed action toward a valued direction is the behavioural engine of ACT, and it is measurable in ways feelings are not.

**Proposition 4. Dialectics adds the other pole: change.** DBT's structure is a held tension — acceptance of the child exactly as they are, and relentless teaching of skills to build a life worth living. For adolescents with self-harm and emotional dysregulation, the package includes skills groups (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness), individual therapy, phone coaching at the moment of crisis, and — crucially — a consultation team for the therapists, because the work punishes isolated practitioners.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Ask of any young client: what has this child been trying not to feel, and what has the trying cost? The answer is usually the case formulation, and it is often not the presenting complaint.
:::

### Worked example 7.1 — The cost of control

**Task.** Ngozi (15) presents with "anger problems": outbursts at home, one suspension. History: father left when she was nine; mother works double shifts; Ngozi cares for two younger siblings. She describes the outbursts as "the volcano", and her strategy as counting to ten, squeezing a stress ball, and leaving the room — strategies that "never work". Using the week's frame, formulate.

**Solution.** The control agenda is the organisation of her life: the volcano must not erupt, every tool is aimed at suppression, and every failure of suppression is evidence that she is defective. What she has not been helped to feel is grief and loneliness at nine, and exhaustion and unfairness now. Defusion target: "I am a volcano" (a fused identity, not a thought about a feeling). Values direction, in her words: being the sister her siblings run to, and being someone who finishes things. Committed action candidates: a weekly hour that is hers; telling one person at school one true thing. Acceptance work: the outburst is not first a discipline problem but the only available expression of an unheld feeling; the skills (distress tolerance, opposite action) are taught *after* the feeling has been met, or they become new tools of suppression and will "never work" like the others. Note what the formulation refuses: a diagnosis of conduct problem, and a plan of consequences.

### Lecture II — Methods with children, and the discipline of language

The third wave's vocabulary is adult and abstract; with children it must be translated or it fails. Four translations carry most of the work.

**Defusion as play.** Give the thought a voice (the counsellor's silly voice, a puppet's), sing it, write it on a leaf and watch it float, thank the mind for the suggestion. The mechanism is identical to the adult exercise — the thought becomes an object — and the medium is the child's.

**Acceptance as making room.** The struggle switch: a two-position switch the child can imagine, "fighting it" and "letting it be here while I get on with my day". With a nine-year-old, a physical metaphor works: holding a beach ball under water takes both arms and all attention; letting it float beside you costs nothing and it is still there.

**Values as a compass, not goals.** Goals can be achieved and ticked; values are directions. With adolescents, the values conversation is often the first time anyone has asked what they are for, and it is where engagement is won.

**Mindfulness as attention training, stripped of claims.** Noticing one thing fully (a raisin, a sound, the breath at the nose), noticing the mind wander, returning without comment. The return is the repetition that trains; the wandering is not failure. Secular, brief, and practiceable in a classroom — which is why school-based programmes adopted it, and why the evidence question matters.

::: safeguard
Mindfulness and acceptance exercises can increase arousal and intrusive material in traumatised children: eyes-closed body scans in particular. With any child with a trauma history, use eyes-open, externally anchored practice (sound, object, movement), keep exposures short, and stop at the first sign of flooding. The method is not neutral and its dosage is clinical judgement.
:::

### Lecture III — The evidence, honestly stated

For adolescents with recurrent self-harm and emotional dysregulation, DBT-A has the strongest third-wave evidence: trials show reductions in self-harm frequency and improvements in emotion regulation against comparison conditions, and the package's structure (skills group, individual work, coaching, therapist team) is itself a teaching model for services. For ACT with young people, randomised trials exist across anxiety, depression, pain and chronic illness, with effects generally comparable to CBT and superior to waitlist; the mechanism evidence — that change runs through flexibility — is promising but thinner than the outcome evidence. For mindfulness-based interventions with children and adolescents, early enthusiasm outran the data: school-based universal programmes show small and inconsistent effects on wellbeing and negligible effects on symptoms in the largest well-conducted trials, and clinician-delivered mindfulness-based therapy for selected young people does better. Three conclusions for practice: use DBT-A as a package where it is indicated, not as a skills sheet; use ACT as a full therapy with a formulation, not as metaphors borrowed for other work; and treat universal school mindfulness as a promising public-health experiment rather than a treatment.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Borrowing third-wave techniques into another therapy is common and often useful; claiming third-wave outcomes from borrowed techniques is not. Know which you are doing, and write it in the plan.
:::

### Worked example 7.2 — One excerpt, two stances

**Task.** In session, Ibrahim (13) says: "I tried the breathing thing and I still felt like a failure, so it doesn't work." Classify the stance and write your response in ACT-consistent terms.

**Solution.** The stance is the control agenda applied to the method: the breathing was recruited to eliminate "feeling like a failure", and its failure to eliminate is read as the method's failure. An ACT-consistent response notices the work the thought is doing and returns the method to its actual function: "so the mind offered 'it doesn't work' the moment the feeling stayed. What if the breathing isn't for making the feeling go? What if it's for staying in the room with it, so you can do the next thing?" Then, behaviourally: what was the next thing he wanted to do that afternoon, and did he do it? The outcome variable moves from feeling to action, which is the week's central reorientation.

::: skills
**Practical 7 — defusion laboratory.** In triads. Each member supplies (from a card or their own material) one fused self-statement. The group has five minutes per statement to devise and run a defusion exercise appropriate to (a) an eight-year-old and (b) a fifteen-year-old. Criteria: the content is unchanged; the relationship to it changes; nothing is disputed; the child's dignity is intact. Debrief: which exercises felt like tricks, and why do tricks fail?
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A third-wave formulation of 1,200 words on a supplied adolescent case: the control agenda and its costs, one fused cognition and a defusion plan matched to age, a values direction in the young person's idiom, two committed actions, and a statement of what in the case the third wave does not address.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Control agenda identified with its costs | 25% |
| Defusion plan age-matched and content-preserving | 25% |
| Values direction in the young person's own idiom | 20% |
| Committed actions concrete and valued-directed | 15% |
| Limits of the approach stated | 15% |

**Formative.** Submit one example of a child trying to control an uncontrollable inner event, with what the control has cost.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The third wave's contribution is a change of target: from the presence of difficult inner events to the child's relationship with them and their movement toward what they value — and its discipline is knowing when that is a therapy and when it is a borrowed metaphor.
:::
