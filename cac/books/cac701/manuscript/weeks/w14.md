# Integration II: Evidence-Based Practice and Session-by-Session Outcome Monitoring {.unit num="14" week="14" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Apply** the three-legged model of evidence-based practice to one decision, naming which leg each consideration belongs to and where the legs disagree.
2. **Explain** reliable change and the clinical cut-off, and interpret a session-by-session chart against them.
3. **Select** a brief outcome measure appropriate to a child's age, language and setting, stating its psychometric limits.
4. **Use** feedback-informed practice: what to do when the chart says not responding by session four, and how to say it to the family.
5. **Critique** one published effectiveness claim for a child intervention using the three tests of week 1 and the population question of week 12.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 13 integrated the lineages into a formulation. This week installs the instruments that keep the formulation honest over time: the decision procedure of evidence-based practice, and the measurement that tells you, by session four rather than by month eighteen, whether the plan is working for this child. Measurement is not bureaucracy. It is the falsifier, operationalised — and it is the respect of taking the child's progress seriously enough to count it.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Evidence-based_practice}
The integration of the best available research with clinical expertise and with the child's and family's values and context (Sackett and colleagues' three legs, as adapted by Kazdin for psychotherapy). In the room: not a list of approved programmes but a decision procedure with three inputs and a written reason.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Clinical_cut-off}
The score on an outcome measure that separates the clinical from the non-clinical range, estimated from population norms. In the room: the line the child needs to cross for the change to count as recovery rather than improvement — and the line that keeps practitioners from celebrating movement that leaves the child still in the clinical range.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Reliable_change}
Change greater than the measure's error: the smallest difference that cannot be explained by the instrument's unreliability alone. In the room: the difference between "seems better" and "better beyond measurement error", and the guard against both false celebration and false alarm.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Feedback-informed_practice}
The routine collection of outcome and alliance feedback every session, and the rule that the data change the plan. In the room: two minutes at the start of the session, looked at with the child, not filed unseen.
:::

### Lecture I — The three legs, and the disagreements between them

**Proposition 1. The research leg is narrower than its rhetoric.** For childhood anxiety and some conduct and trauma presentations, the evidence is strong; for many ordinary referrals — comorbid, multiply disadvantaged, culturally distant from the trial samples — the applicable evidence is thin or absent. The research leg's correct use is to set priors, not to dictate: "for this presentation, exposure-led CBT tends to work, in samples unlike this one in these three ways".

**Proposition 2. The expertise leg is formulation and judgement, not seniority.** It answers what this child and family can actually do: the mother's shifts, the father's absence, the school's culture, the transport, the language, the child's development. Expertise also includes knowing one's own competence boundary and the discipline of the measured outcome, which converts judgement from opinion into a testable series.

**Proposition 3. The values leg is not a courtesy.** What the family wants a better life to look like governs the goal, and the goal governs the method: a family whose aim is the child's return to church and school community may prioritise social re-engagement over symptom score, and their aim is legitimate unless it harms the child. Where the family's values conflict with the child's safety or rights, the child's govern and the conflict is named — which is week 4's and week 12's material, not this week's.

**Proposition 4. Write the disagreement.** When the legs disagree — guideline says X, the family wants Y, your formulation suggests Z — the formulation records all three and the chosen course with its reason. Undocumented disagreement is how drift begins and how complaints are won later by people who read the file.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** In every plan, one sentence per leg: what the research says for this presentation; what this family can actually do; what they want. Three sentences, then the decision. A plan that cannot produce them has not been decided, only begun.
:::

### Worked example 14.1 — Three legs in conflict

**Task.** Guideline-recommended treatment for Aisha's (11 — a fictitious composite) anxiety is a twelve-session exposure-led CBT package, weekly, with a parent session each week. Research leg: strong. Expertise leg: the family lives three hours away, the mother works six days, and Aisha's school will not release her. Values leg: the family prefers that "the school stops being difficult" and sees the anxiety as a school problem, not a family one. Decide and write the three sentences.

**Solution.** Research: "Exposure-led CBT with parent involvement has the strongest evidence for this presentation; its trials assume weekly attendance and a participating parent." Expertise: "Weekly clinic attendance is not feasible; school-based delivery with a trained practitioner and telehealth parent sessions is feasible; the school's current accommodation is a maintaining factor (week 5)." Values: "The family understands the problem as the school's; they will engage with a plan that begins by changing the school's response, and would disengage from a plan that begins by locating the problem in Aisha or in them." Decision: begin with the school-contingency work and psychoeducation (which serves the family's frame and the maintenance analysis), introduce graded exposure in school sessions from week 3, and hold parent work in fortnightly telehealth; review at week 6 against measured outcomes. The decision uses all three legs and says so; a guideline-only decision would have produced twelve missed appointments and a closed file.

### Lecture II — Measuring: what, how often, and with what

**What.** A brief, repeated, child-relevant measure of the target difficulty, plus a one-item alliance or session-rating check. For internalising and externalising difficulties in children, short parent- and child-report scales exist and are free or cheap; for adolescents, self-report is primary because parent and teacher ratings diverge sharply from it. Choose one measure per target, in the family's language where a validated version exists; where none exists, say so and use a personally anchored scale (the week 11 scaling question) as an honest substitute, labelled as such.

**How often.** Every session, or every fortnight at minimum, scored before the session and looked at with the child. Measurement that is collected but not reviewed is theatre; the review is the intervention.

**With what standards.** Plot against the clinical cut-off and compute or estimate reliable change. Figure 14.1 shows the two verdicts that matter: crossing into the non-clinical range with change beyond error (recovery), and a flat line above the cut-off (not responding). A third pattern deserves naming: improvement that is reliable but leaves the child in the clinical range — better, not well — which is a legitimate outcome to discuss honestly with the family rather than a success to inflate.

![Session-by-session outcome for two children against the clinical cut-off: one responding and crossing below the cut-off by session seven, one flat above it, with the annotation that a non-responding chart by session four changes the plan rather than the child. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-14-01.png){width=94%}

::: safeguard
Outcome measurement must never become a rationing instrument: a child who is not improving on the chart is not thereby a child whose service should end. The chart's verdict is "change the plan", never "close the case". Where funding bodies use non-response to terminate, the practitioner's obligation is to the child and the record, and the disagreement belongs in writing.
:::

### Lecture III — When the chart says no: the not-responding protocol

By session four, a flat or worsening chart is information, not failure. A five-step protocol follows, in order.

**Step 1 — check the delivery, not the child.** Was the plan actually delivered? Fidelity first: sessions attended, components run, home tasks attempted. Most apparent treatment failures are delivery failures, and the honest question is what the service did, not what the child resisted.

**Step 2 — check the alliance, with the child.** Ask directly, with the measure in front of them: what is not helping? what is worse? what would make this worth coming to? Adolescents will usually say, if asked with the data visible and without defence.

**Step 3 — re-run the formulation.** Which perpetuating factor was missed? Which protective factor was never used? Which lineage's reading was never tested? The falsifier of week 13 is consulted: has it appeared?

**Step 4 — change one thing, measurably.** Not a wholesale restart: one alteration to the governing lineage, the sequence or the context, with the next two sessions as its test. Wholesale restarts destroy the data and the alliance together.

**Step 5 — tell the family, in the same session.** "Our plan is not working yet; here is what the numbers show; here is the one change we propose and how we will know." Families tolerate honest non-response far better than they tolerate discovered concealment, and the conversation is itself alliance work.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Put the chart on the table, physically or on screen, at the start of every session from session three onward. A child who sees their own data is a collaborator; a child whose data is filed unseen is a subject.
:::

### Worked example 14.2 — Reading a flat chart

**Task.** Sessions 1–5 with Segun (13 — a fictitious composite — low mood after exclusion from school): child-rated mood 28, 27, 28, 29, 28 on a 0–40 scale where lower is better and the clinical cut-off is 15. Attendance perfect; alliance rating high; the plan was behavioural activation with a school-reintegration goal. Run the five steps in outline.

**Solution.** Step 1: delivered — five sessions, activity schedule kept, two reintegration meetings held. Not a delivery failure. Step 2: alliance high but the child's own words: "the sessions are fine, nothing's different at home". Step 3: formulation re-run — the perpetuating cell named school exclusion and peer loss, but the protective cell's strongest entry (his cousin's boxing gym, lost when the family moved) was never used, and the home's maintaining factor (father's nightly criticism since the exclusion) was assessed but not addressed because the father never attended. Step 4: one change — recruit the gym as the activation anchor (a valued, social, mastery activity already trusted by the family) and open a father conversation in the format he will accept (a phone call about "the plan for his training", not about feelings). Step 5: told to Segun and his mother in session 6 with the chart visible: "the numbers say the plan hasn't worked yet; here is the one change; we look again in two sessions." The alternative — repeating sessions 6 to 12 as scheduled — would have produced a completed treatment and an unchanged child, which is the failure mode measurement exists to prevent.

::: skills
**Practical 14 — the chart clinic.** In pairs, each brings or is given a fabricated ten-session chart (supplied: recovering; reliable-but-still-clinical; flat; deteriorating after session 6; missing data from session 4). Task: classify the pattern against cut-off and reliable change, run the five-step protocol in outline for the non-responding patterns, and compose the sentence you would say to the family. Debrief: which step do you habitually skip, and what does skipping it protect?
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Evidence-based practice is a three-legged decision procedure, and outcome monitoring is its conscience: brief repeated measurement against cut-off and reliable change, reviewed with the child, with a five-step protocol for the flat chart that changes the plan rather than the child.
:::
