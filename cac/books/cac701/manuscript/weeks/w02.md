# The Psychodynamic Lineage I: Freud, Anna Freud, and the Child Who Cannot Free-Associate {.unit num="2" week="2" hours="4"}

5. **State two limits** of the psychodynamic evidence base with children, and what would strengthen it.

### Where this week sits

Week 1 gave you four questions. This week begins answering them with the oldest lineage in the field, and with the discovery that matters most for our programme: the child is not a small adult, and a method built for an adult's couch fails in a room with a seven-year-old. Everything later in the course — attachment in week 3, mentalisation in week 4 — grows out of the attempt to take the child's actual mind seriously.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=The_unconscious,_operationally}
Not a place but a claim: that some of what a child does is organised by wishes, fears and memories they cannot report and would deny. In the room: the child who agrees to a plan on Monday and destroys it on Tuesday without knowing why. The claim is testable in a weak but real sense — a formulation built on it should predict repetitions.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Defence}
The mind's way of keeping unbearable material at a distance: repression, denial, projection, displacement, reaction formation, and — most visible in children — regression. In the room: the eleven-year-old who answers every question about her parents with a detailed account of her football team. Not resistance to be broken; a solution to be respected and, slowly, made unnecessary.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Play_as_communication}
The child's equivalent of free association. Play is not a reward for cooperating with talk; it is the medium in which the child thinks aloud. In the room: the difference between watching what a child plays and watching *how* the child plays — who is cast in which role, what is not allowed to happen, where the story always breaks.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Containment}
The function by which an adult receives a child's unmanageable feeling, holds it without being destroyed by it or retaliating with it, and returns it in a form the child can use. In the room: what you are doing when a child's rage meets your unchanged face and unchanged schedule. Mechanism, not manner.
:::

### Lecture I — Why the classical method failed with children

**Proposition 1. The classical method required capacities children do not yet have.** Psycho-analysis as Freud developed it for adults depends on free association, on the capacity to observe one's own thoughts, on a sustained verbal exchange, and on a motive to understand oneself that is strong enough to survive the pain of understanding. A child of six has none of these in usable form. The child cannot lie down and say whatever comes to mind; what comes to mind arrives as play, drawing, movement and symptom.

**Proposition 2. The child also arrives without a request for help.** Adults come complaining of their suffering. Children are brought, usually by the person the child's symptom is most organised around. The analyst therefore meets a client who has not asked for treatment and a referrer who has. Anna Freud treated this as the central technical problem of child analysis: the analytic situation must first be made wanted, which means the analyst begins as a provider of something the child values before becoming an interpreter of anything.

**Proposition 3. The child's dependence changes the ethics and the technique together.** An adult can leave. A child cannot, practically or emotionally, and the analyst is always also an adult authority in a world of adult authorities. Anna Freud's conclusion — that analysis with children cannot be neutral in the way analysis with adults aims to be, and must include an educational and protective dimension — was controversial and remains correct. Klein disagreed about almost everything else but not about the gravity of the asymmetry.

**Proposition 4. Two modifications followed, and they define the lineage.** Anna Freud's: meet the child where the child is, build the alliance before interpreting, work closely with the parents, and interpret defence before impulse. Klein's: the child's play *is* association and can be interpreted directly, symbol by symbol, in the transference, from the first session. Between those two positions — alliance-first and interpretation-first — the whole of subsequent child psychotherapy has oscillated.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Notice which modification you instinctively prefer and why. Practitioners who cannot tolerate not-knowing drift to interpretation-first and interpret material that is not yet there. Practitioners who cannot tolerate the child's pain drift to alliance-only and never interpret at all. Both are defences; name yours.
:::

### Defence in children: a working field guide

Defences are the child's solutions to unbearable material, and the field guide below is organised the way supervision uses it: the defence, a room example, what it protects, the error it invites, and the move that respects it. Learn it as a set of recognitions, not a checklist — a defence named to a child too early is a defence strengthened.

**Denial** — the child who, asked about the father's absence, says "he is at work" and holds the sentence against all evidence. It protects the world's stability. The error: confrontation ("but he is not, is he?"), which steals the protection and leaves nothing in its place. The move: let the sentence stand this session and work beside it — the play will state what the mouth cannot, and the denial thins when the material has somewhere else to live.

**Displacement** — the fury at the stepfather delivered to the small sister, or to you, safely, in the football game's fouls. It protects the real relationship from the real feeling. The error: treating the displaced target as the problem (a referral for sibling aggression). The move: honour the discharge and, later, name the direction — "the fouls got hardest in the weeks after he moved in" — as an observation about timing, which the child can accept or refuse.

**Projection** — "my teacher hates me" where the hate is the child's own, unsafely held. It protects the child from owning a feeling that would flood them. The error: reassurance ("I am sure she does not"), which argues with a structure. The move: explore the hate as a reasonable feeling about a reasonable grievance, because once hatred is permitted in small doses it stops needing to be owned by someone else.

**Reaction formation** — the child who is excessively, rigidly kind to the new baby, and whose kindness has the stiffness of a wall. It protects against the wish to harm. The error: praising the kindness, which funds the wall. The move: give the hostile wish a symbolic home (the dog doll, the story's angry sister) and watch the stiffness soften as the wish finds an address.

**Regression** — the nine-year-old's baby voice and wetting in the weeks after the brother's birth. It protects by returning to the station where love was certain. The error: shame, or a behaviour programme alone, which treats the signal as the disease. The move: supply the regressed need in scheduled, dignified doses — ten minutes of being the baby, openly, with a clock — because a need met by appointment stops commandeering the whole day.

**Intellectualisation** — the twelve-year-old who discusses his parents' divorce as a sociologist, flawless and unfelt. It protects by moving from the feeling to the theory of the feeling. The error: admiring the articulation, or rupturing it ("but how do you FEEL?"), which is an ambush. The move: join the intellect and walk it downward one step at a time — "your account is exact; whose side were you on in the kitchen that night?" — because the intellectual child's trust is earned through competence, not through demand.

**Turning passive into active** — the child who was left who now leaves: ending sessions first, abandoning games mid-play, dropping friends before they drop him. It protects by converting what was suffered into what is done. The error: experiencing it as rudeness or instability. The move: name the sequence as skill-in-progress — "you leave first now; that is not nothing, that is a shield you built" — and then, in the alliance, practise the unbearable other order: being stayed with.

### Worked example 2.1 — Interpretation or containment?

**Task.** In a session, Kofi (7) spends twenty minutes building a wall of blocks around a small animal figure, then knocks the wall down, looks at you, and rebuilds it higher. He does this three times. On the fourth attempt he stops, holds the animal, and says nothing. Classify what the following three responses would each be doing: (a) "You keep making sure he's safe, and then it isn't safe enough." (b) Sitting with the silence, matching his stillness, and saying after a while, "It's a lot of work, keeping him safe." (c) Asking, "Who is the animal?"

**Solution.** (a) is an interpretation: it names the repetition and its meaning (safety that never becomes enough) and offers it to the child's understanding. It is well-timed only if the alliance can carry it; in session three it probably cannot, and an early interpretation is experienced as being seen through, not seen. (b) is containment with a marked mirror: the feeling is received, held, and returned in words that are his but calmer. The mechanism is regulation, not insight. (c) is neither; it is an invitation to symbolise verbally, which a seven-year-old may experience as a demand and which collapses the play into a questionnaire. For this session, (b); the interpretation (a) belongs to a later session when the repetition has been shared many times and named by the child first.

### The analytic situation with a child: Anna Freud's technical sequence

Anna Freud's modification was not a softening of method but a re-sequencing of it, and the sequence is still the sanest introduction to any child therapy hour. **First, the preparatory phase:** the analyst begins as a provider — of interest, of help with something concrete, of the room itself — because a child who has not asked for treatment will not lend their mind to a stranger. The alliance is not the preamble to the work; with children it is the first work, and its construction is technical, not decorative: it is built by doing something the child experiences as useful before anything is asked of them. **Second, the analysis of defence before the analysis of impulse:** the child's defences — the football monologue, the compliance, the silliness, the silence — are met and named before the material beneath them is touched, because an interpretation that bypasses the defence is experienced as an invasion and produces the two results invasions produce: withdrawal or counter-attack. **Third, the parent as colleague and as object:** the child's parents are simultaneously the allies without whom nothing happens and, very often, the figures the child's difficulty is organised around; Anna Freud's insistence on working closely with parents was therefore both practical and theoretical, and it is the reason child therapy has never been able to pretend to the privacy of the adult hour.

Two further instruments of hers deserve revival because they are assessment tools rather than theory. The **developmental lines** — from dependency to emotional self-reliance, from irresponsibility to responsibility in body and social care, from egocentrism to companionship, from the body to the toy and from the toy to work — give the practitioner a way of locating a child not against a norm but against the child's own unevenness: a child can be advanced on one line and regressed on another, and it is precisely the **unevenness**, the disharmony between lines, that generates the symptoms adults bring. The nine-year-old who reasons like a twelve-year-old about football and like a five-year-old about separation is not "advanced with a problem"; the disproportion is the problem's address. Used this way, the lines are a formulation instrument any school can borrow: they convert "immature" — which explains nothing — into "advanced here, regressed there, and the symptom sits at the junction".

The sequence closes with a rule that survives every later school: **interpret in the order the child can bear.** What the child can bear is not a fixed quantity; it is a function of the alliance of the week, the regulation of the day, and the presence or absence of the parent in the next room. The practitioner's skill is not knowing the meaning of the play — meanings are cheap and multiply — but knowing whether this child, this hour, can use the meaning without losing the room.

### Lecture II — Play as the analytic instrument

If play is association, then the playroom is a text and the counsellor is a reader. Schaefer and Drewes organise the therapeutic powers of play into four groups: communication, emotional healing, social competence and personal strength. Four are worth learning first because each carries a mechanism.

**Play communicates what cannot be said.** A child's narrative choices — who is powerful, who is helpless, who is absent, what is not allowed to occur — carry material the child cannot report. The mechanism is expression: material that is enacted is material that can be responded to.

**Play regulates affect at a survivable dose.** In play the child controls the distance to the frightening thing. The monster is in the box; the box is closed; the child opens it when ready. The mechanism is graduated exposure under the child's own control, which is why play therapy can be effective for anxiety and post-traumatic material without ever becoming a protocol.

**Play allows the forbidden in symbolic form.** Wishes that would be dangerous to express directly — to destroy the rival, to abandon the parent — can be expressed by a proxy and survive the expression. The mechanism is displacement with safety: the impulse is discharged and observed without consequence.

**Play rehearses mastery.** The child who was helpless becomes, in play, the one who rescues, decides, repairs. The mechanism is enactment of the counter-position: competence practised where failure costs nothing.

### Klein's counter-position: play as direct association, and the cost of reading early

Klein's claim is bolder than a technique: the child's play is not a preparation for association but association itself, and its symbols can be interpreted in the transference from the first session, because the anxieties beneath the play are already active in the room. Where Anna Freud waited, Klein read: the dog that knocks the baby is not merely a story about a stepfather but the child's own destructive impulse experienced as a danger from outside, and the interpretation is delivered to the anxiety, early and directly, on the view that anxiety not interpreted is anxiety repeated. Her account of the mind's earliest positions — the paranoid-schizoid, in which love and destruction are kept apart because together they are unbearable, and the depressive, in which the child discovers that the mother they wished damage is the mother they love, and guilt arrives — remains one of the few theories that takes infant and child inner life seriously at its own intensity, and every later account of envy, reparation and guilt in child work is in debt to it.

The costs are equally real and they are costs of **suggestion**. An interpretation is a powerful adult sentence about a child's private world; delivered early, it teaches the child what their play is supposed to mean, and children — whose suggestibility in interview research is among the most robust findings in applied psychology — will produce the material the room expects. The over-read symbol is therefore not merely an error of scholarship; it is a contamination of the evidence the practitioner will then cite as confirmation. Add the ethical weight of interpreting destructive and sexual material to a child who has not asked to be understood at that depth, and the modern synthesis becomes intelligible: **symbolic expression is real, and the relationship is the instrument through which it can be borne.** Most contemporary psychodynamic child work therefore keeps Klein's seriousness — the play is taken as meaning, from the first session — and Anna Freud's timing — the meaning is offered when the alliance and the child's regulation can carry it, and is tested against what the child does with it rather than assumed from what the adult sees in it.

The practical test of whether an interpretation was timed rightly is what follows it: a well-timed interpretation is followed by new material, a deepening of the play, or a productive silence; an ill-timed one is followed by compliance, by a flattening of the play into performance, or by a child who begins to hand the adult the symbols the adult appears to want. The flattening is the sign to watch, because it looks like progress and it is the opposite: the child has stopped thinking aloud and started feeding the reader.

### Lecture III — The structural and topographic map, and what survives of it

Freud's map of the mind — conscious, preconscious, unconscious; id, ego, superego — is now mostly of historical interest as a model of cognition, and no serious practitioner reads a child's mind through it literally. Three of its claims, however, survive in modern form and are worth keeping.

**First, that much of mental life is not available to report.** Cognitive science has absorbed this without the hydraulics: implicit memory, automatic appraisal, procedural knowledge of relationships. When a psychodynamic clinician says a child's internal working model is unconscious, the claim is close to what attachment research means by implicit relational knowledge.

**Second, that the mind manages conflict by distance.** Defence is now studied as emotion regulation strategy, and the developmental sequence of defences maps recognisably onto the developmental sequence of regulatory strategies.

**Third, that early relationships become structure.** This is the claim that attachment theory operationalised and tested, and it is the bridge to next week.

### Transference and countertransference in the child hour

The child does not meet you; the child meets you **as** someone — the parent who left, the parent who stays but is tired, the teacher who ranks, the older sibling who took the share — and the casting is not a distortion to correct but the most valuable data in the room, because it is the child's relational hypothesis performing live. A child who tests whether you remember what they said last week is testing whether you are the kind who keeps or the kind who loses; the test will be repeated, in variation, until the evidence closes. The practitioner's discipline is twofold: notice the casting (it repeats — repetition is its signature), and answer it in the real rather than in the role — remembering, returning, staying scheduled, not leaving when the child engineers leaving, because it is precisely the non-repetition of the expected ending that makes the hour corrective rather than confirmatory.

Countertransference — what the child's material stirs in you — is the same instrument pointed the other way, and with children it takes three recurrent forms. **The rescue pull:** the wish to save the child from the parents, which feels like advocacy and operates like a seizure of the triadic alliance; its signature is a practitioner who stops telling the parent the truth. **The irritation at the compliant child:** the good child who never troubles the room and whose goodness is a defence (reaction formation, or fear) that bores or irritates the practitioner before it is understood; its signature is a case that drifts to the bottom of the caseload because nothing burns. **The alliance with the child against the world:** seductive in adolescent work especially, where joining the child's contempt for the household feels like joining the child; its signature is confidentiality drifting into conspiracy (week 14 draws the line).

The rule that makes countertransference usable is that it is **evidence about the field, not about you alone**: the rescue pull usually means the child's helplessness is being broadcast at a frequency the room cannot refuse; the irritation usually means the child's defence is working exactly as designed, on you. Supervision's job is to convert the feeling into a hypothesis — "my wish to rescue appeared in the week the mother cancelled twice; is the child's abandonment signal the loudest thing in this case?" — and the practitioner's job is to bring the feeling to supervision before bringing it to the room. A countertransference enacted is a repetition the child ordered; a countertransference understood is a formulation the child gave you for free.

### The Winnicottian interlude: holding, the squiggle, and the good-enough hour

No figure of this lineage speaks to ordinary practice more directly than Winnicott, and none is more often quoted wrongly. The **holding environment** is not warmth as atmosphere but reliability as structure: the room that begins when it said it would, ends when it said it would, remembers what was said, and survives what was brought. Its mechanism is the gradual discovery by the child that the environment does not collapse under their material and does not retaliate with it — which is containment stated at the level of the setting rather than the sentence, and it is why a cancelled session handled badly can undo three good ones: the structure is the message.

The **transitional phenomenon** — the cloth, the blanket, the song that is neither fully the child nor fully the mother but the bridge between — supplies the lineage's deepest insight about symbolisation: the first symbol is not a representation but a bridge object, and the playroom's symbols inherit its function. This is why the kept object matters (context note above), and why confiscating a transitional object "for the child's own good" is a small catastrophe the adult never sees as one. The **squiggle game** is Winnicott's clinical instrument and remains usable in any setting with paper: the practitioner draws an incomplete line, the child makes of it what they will, then roles reverse. It is not a projective test with scores — Winnicott would have hated that — but a structured invitation to think aloud in company, and its yield is exactly the four channels of Figure 2.2 in miniature: what the child makes (content), whether they glance before the dangerous stroke (relationship), what the made thing becomes across a series (symbol), and whether the arousal of the task stays inside the child's window (regulation). Three squiggles at the start of a course of work, kept in the folder, give the practitioner a baseline of the child's symbolic range that no questionnaire supplies.

Two warnings complete the interlude. The **true self / false self** distinction is frequently misused to pathologise compliance: a child's politeness in a hierarchical culture is competence, not falseness, and the distinction earns its keep only where the compliance is rigid, exhaustive and purchased at the price of any spontaneous gesture — the child who has no preference, no greed, no mess anywhere, because wanting once cost too much. And **good-enough** is a standard, not an excuse: the good-enough hour is the hour that fails occasionally and repairs reliably, which is precisely the dose of failure that installs the child's own capacity; the hour that never fails teaches dependence, and the hour that fails without repair teaches the world's old lesson again. Supervision's question here is therefore not "was the session good?" but "what failed, and was the failure repaired in the child's presence?" — because the repair, witnessed, is the intervention.

### Worked example 2.2 — Four channels, four hypotheses

**Task.** Read the following excerpt from a fictitious session with Ama (6), referred after her mother's new partner moved in. Ama plays with a family of dolls. She seats the mother doll and the baby doll together, then places the father doll on a shelf facing the wall. A large dog doll enters and knocks over the baby doll. Ama right the baby, puts the dog outside the house, then brings the dog back in and seats it at the table. She looks at you and says, "He's not bad. He's just big." Produce one hypothesis per channel.

**Solution.** *Content:* the recurring sequence is intrusion, damage, repair, and re-admission — the new partner damages and is nevertheless seated at the table; hypothesis: the child is working out whether the intrusion can be made safe rather than expelled. *Relationship:* Ama looks at you before naming the dog's goodness; hypothesis: she is testing whether you will agree that the big thing can be good, i.e. whether loyalty to the absent father will be punished here. *Symbol:* the father doll faces the wall — excluded but present, watching; hypothesis: the child holds the father as observer of the new arrangement, and guilt about enjoying the new partner may be part of the problem. *Regulation:* arousal rises at the knock and falls at the re-seating; the play recovers without your help; hypothesis: regulatory capacity is intact and the play is mastery rather than overwhelm. Four hypotheses, none a conclusion; next week's sessions test them.

### The evidence and its limits: what the psychodynamic claims rest on

The honest state of the art is Midgley and Kennedy's critical review and the literature that has accumulated around it: for psychodynamic psychotherapy with children there are fewer randomised trials than for behavioural and cognitive methods, the trials that exist have small samples and heterogeneous problems, and a substantial share of outcome studies use measures that privilege the theory — global ratings by therapists or by parents of "insight" and "relationship", which are the theory's own currency. Where better-designed comparisons exist, the pattern resembles the adult literature: psychodynamic child therapy tends to outperform waiting list and no-treatment conditions, and to be statistically indistinguishable from active alternatives at follow-up, with some trials showing slower early change and durable later gain — a pattern that, if real, matters enormously for how services set their expectations and their review dates, and that is not yet established strongly enough to promise to a parent.

Three specific limits should shape how you hold this week. **The control-condition problem:** the active ingredient of a psychodynamic hour is hard to placebo, so trials compare whole packages, and a null difference between packages cannot tell you whether interpretation, containment, or the twice-weekly attention did the work. **The fidelity problem:** psychodynamic practice resists manualisation more than behavioural practice, so "the treatment" varies between practitioners in ways the outcome cannot see. **The population problem:** the trials over-represent referred, verbally able children of engaged families in high-income settings; the six-year-old in a district clinic who presents somatically, with a grandmother who suspects the talking itself, is not in the sample.

What would strengthen the base is known and underway: session-by-session measurement of the processes the theory names (rupture and repair, symbolic expression, reflective functioning), single-case experimental designs that suit low-volume services, and equivalence trials powered for follow-up rather than post-treatment. And one sentence should close this section, because it is true regardless of where the trials land: the phenomena this lineage named — containment, the repetition of relational patterns, the child who communicates in play what they cannot say — are observable in every hour of every school, and every school borrows the vocabulary when the child cries. A lineage can have a thin evidence base and a true observation deck; the discipline is to know which of the two you are using in any given sentence.

### Conversations in the room: three adults and the playing child

**The parent who asks why the child "just plays".** — *Answer:* "Playing is how a six-year-old thinks out loud. When Ama plays the family and the dog, she is showing me the exact problem she cannot put in words — who is inside, who is outside, whether the big new thing can be safe — and she is showing me what she has tried so far to fix it. If I asked her questions instead, I would get the sentences she thinks adults want. In play I get her own thinking, at her own speed, and I can work with that. What I am relying on is this: a child who can put the worry into the play, and change the ending there even once, has started to change it anywhere." *Commentary:* the script names the mechanism (expression and mastery in symbolic form), gives one concrete instance from the child's own session so the parent hears evidence rather than philosophy, and ends on a testable expectation the parent can watch for.

**The teacher who calls play "babyish" for a nine-year-old.** — *Answer:* "At nine the medium changes but the principle does not: he will not think aloud with dolls, so he thinks aloud with football, with drawing, with the game he invents in the last ten minutes when he believes the session is over. I use whatever he chooses, because chosen material is where the thinking is. The babyish part would be me asking a humiliated nine-year-old to lie on a couch and describe his childhood." *Commentary:* the answer concedes the surface (dolls are not the nine-year-old's medium) and keeps the principle (self-chosen medium carries the thinking), and it uses a small humour that lets the teacher retreat from the word "babyish" without losing face.

**The father who asks when you will "start asking him about his mother".** — *Answer:* "I am asking him about his mother every week, in the only language he has for it yet. Last week she was the figure in the play who left and came back twice; he made her leave and he made her return, which is more than he can do in life. When he can hold the story without the toys — and he will tell me when, because children do — I will ask him in words, and he will answer in words, and it will take ten minutes because the play did eleven weeks of the work first. If I ask in words today, I get a shrug, and we both learn that talking is useless." *Commentary:* the script converts the father's complaint into a progress report, gives a dated logic (when, and why not now), and protects the child from an interview he cannot yet survive — which is also, said plainly to the father, the reason the shrug would damage the alliance the father is paying for.

### Common errors and their corrections

1. **The symbol dictionary.** "The snake is always the phallus; the house is always the self." *Correction:* symbols are personal before they are archetypal; the dog in Ama's play is this household's dog-sized problem, and the child's own gloss, when it comes, outranks every dictionary.
2. **Interpretation before alliance.** The meaning delivered in session two because the practitioner cannot bear not-knowing. *Correction:* the timing test — what followed the last interpretation? Compliance and flattening mean wait; new material means proceed.
3. **Containment as passivity.** "I held the space" offered as a description of doing nothing. *Correction:* containment is observable work — receiving, surviving, returning in marked form — and a session note should say what was received and what was returned.
4. **Play as catharsis alone.** The session counted good because feelings were big. *Correction:* discharge without regulation or meaning is arousal, not therapy; look for the dose the child chose and the ending the child made.
5. **The parent forgotten.** The hour spent entirely on the child while the referrer's question goes unanswered for six weeks. *Correction:* the parent is a corner of the triadic alliance (week 1); a scheduled sentence to the parent each week is part of the technique, not an administrative courtesy.

### Worked example 2.3 — Kofi's wall across three sessions

**Task.** Session 3 is the excerpt in 2.1. In session 5 the wall-building returns, but now the animal is left outside the wall for the first two rounds and brought inside only at the end; Kofi says, "he has to learn". In session 7 the game changes: the wall is not built at all; the animal simply sits in the middle of the floor while Kofi watches you watch it. Track the hypotheses and decide, at each session, whether to interpret.

**Solution.** *Session 5:* the sequence has developed — exclusion before inclusion, with a rule stated ("he has to learn") — which converts session 3's hypothesis (safety that never becomes enough) into a sharper one: the child is working on the conditions of re-admission, i.e. on whether the dangerous one can be trained rather than only walled. The regulation channel shows the same recovery without help. Decision: no interpretation; the play is doing its work and naming it now would hand Kofi the adult's theory of his own rule. Record the development: exclusion-then-inclusion is a movement, and movements are what hypotheses are for. *Session 7:* the disappearance of the wall is the most informative event of the three sessions, and it is ambiguous: it may be mastery (the danger no longer needs a wall) or avoidance (the danger is too near to wall). The channel that discriminates is regulation: if Kofi's arousal stays low and his gaze invites shared attention, mastery is the better hypothesis; if arousal is flat with a watchedness that feels like monitoring, avoidance is. In the session as described — he watches you watch it — the relationship channel carries a question: whether the adult will name the danger or pretend not to see it. Decision: interpret at the level of the relationship only, in one marked sentence: "Now we both look at him, and neither of us builds." This is an interpretation of the shared field, not of the child's interior; it is bearable because it is true of the room, and it tests the avoidance hypothesis — if avoidance is running, the child will rebuild or leave; if mastery is running, the child will speak. *The general lesson:* interpretations are dated interventions on a moving hypothesis, and the record must carry the movement, because "the wall game" written identically in sessions 3, 5 and 7 is a record of nothing.

### Worked example 2.4 — Ama's formulation paragraph

**Task.** Write the psychodynamic formulation paragraph for Ama (6) as it would appear in a case file after four sessions: presenting, the maintained-by sentence, the mechanism the work relies on, and one falsifier.

**Solution.** "Ama, six, was referred following her mother's partner moving into the home; she presents with night waking, refusal to sleep alone, and a new clinginess at separation that the school reports as morning distress. Formulation: the intrusion has been experienced as damage to the baby position she still occupies (play sequence: dog knocks baby, mother repairs), and her night refusal maintains proximity at the price of confirming her own helplessness; the father's exclusion — doll faced to the wall, present and watching — adds a loyalty conflict, so that enjoying the new arrangement is itself dangerous, and the conflict is maintained by silence because no adult has named the father's place in the new order. The work relies on symbolic expression under self-chosen dose (the play's re-admission sequence) plus containment of the guilt (the practitioner surviving both the love of the dog and the love of the absent father without choosing between them), expecting that as the play's endings vary, the night's single solution — clinging — will lose its monopoly. Falsifier: if after six sessions the play remains identical in sequence and affect (no variation in ending, no new figure, arousal unchanged) while night waking persists, the symbolic-expression mechanism is not engaging and the formulation shifts to a behavioural maintenance account (night-time reinforcement schedule) with the parent work that account requires." *Commentary:* the paragraph is school-honest and school-humble: it names its mechanism, its expectation, and the observation that would send the case to another lineage — which is exactly what week 1 demanded of every formulation.

### The session note in psychodynamic work: what to record

A lineage whose evidence is sequence demands notes that record sequence, and most notes fail this by recording atmosphere ("good session, strong material") or by recording the practitioner's conclusions without the observations that earned them. The house format for this lineage has five lines, and it is examined in the practicum.

**Line 1 — observable events, in order:** what was chosen, what happened to it, who was cast where, where the story broke, where arousal rose and fell. No adjectives of meaning; verbs and nouns only. ("Wall built around animal; wall knocked; rebuilt higher; x3; fourth round: wall not built, animal held; silence 2 min.") **Line 2 — the child's words verbatim,** however few, in quotation marks; the child's gloss outranks every later reading, and glosses decay in memory within days. **Line 3 — channel hypotheses, dated:** one line per channel actually observed, each marked H and the session number, each carrying its disconfirming observation in brackets. A hypothesis without a disconfirming line is a prejudice with a date on it. **Line 4 — the relationship line:** what the child cast you as this session and the evidence for it; what you felt (countertransference, named plainly — "rescue pull, strong, from minute 20"); what you did with the feeling (brought to supervision / noted / enacted, and the enacted ones are written down most carefully of all, because they are the ones supervision must see). **Line 5 — the next-session intention and its test:** one sentence of plan plus the observation that would show the plan wrong.

Two examples, before and after. *Weak:* "Kofi engaged well with play; defensive material around safety evident; containing stance maintained; continue." *House format:* "1. Blocks: wall around small animal, knocked, rebuilt higher, x3; round 4 no wall, animal held, stillness. 2. Kofi (no words until close): 'He has to learn' (session 5, recorded here for sequence). 3. H-content (s3, continued s5): re-admission under conditions [disconfirm: exclusion permanent, no return sequence]; H-regulation (s5): recovery unaided [disconfirm: escalation requiring co-regulation]. 4. Cast me as witness-not-helper (glance after each knock, no request); felt urge to reassure at round 4, held, noted. 5. Next: no interpretation of the rule; watch whether conditions of re-admission tighten or loosen; if play flattens into performance, interpretation was premature somewhere — review s4 note." The second note is teachable, supervisable and falsifiable; the first is a weather report. The difference, multiplied by forty sessions, is the difference between a case that learns and a case that accumulates.

### Context note: play, space and materials in under-resourced settings

The playroom of the literature — shelves of graded figures, sand tray, one-way glass — is an instrument of wealthy institutions, and most practitioners of this programme will not have one. The good news is that the medium is not the toys; the medium is **sequence under the child's control**, and sequence survives almost any materials. A drawn family on the back of a leaflet, a story told with three seeds and a stone, a game invented with bottle caps, a sand patch outside the clinic wall: each yields the four channels — content (who is drawn, who is missing, what happens to the small one), relationship (who is glanced at before the dangerous part), symbol (the child's own gloss, waited for), regulation (where the breath changes) — because the channels are properties of the child's thinking, not of the shelf.

Three adaptations carry real costs and should be named rather than romanticised. **Space:** where the session happens in a shared room or a classroom corner, confidentiality is partial and the child knows it; the contract of week 14 must therefore be honest about who can overhear, and the play's dangerous material will arrive late or elsewhere — often in the walk between rooms, which the practitioner learns to treat as part of the session. **Materials:** disposable materials change the play's permanence — a drawing can be torn, a sand story cannot be kept — so where continuity matters, a kept object (the leaflet drawn on, folded and kept in the child's folder) supplies the memory the shelf would have held. **Time and group:** where caseloads force short or grouped sessions, the containment function survives in ritual — the same opening sentence, the same closing sentence, the same seat — because ritual is portability, and a child who knows exactly how the twenty minutes begin and end can risk material in the middle. What does not survive any setting is the over-read symbol and the un-named parent; both are errors of discipline, not of resources.

### Model answers to two seminar questions

**Question 2 — mechanism or manner?** Containment is a mechanism, and the observation that distinguishes the two is the child's regulation across the exchange, measured before and after: if the adult's unchanged face and returned words are doing causal work, the child's arousal curve bends within the session and the child's next expression of the same feeling arrives in a smaller, more usable form — the rage returns as a sentence, the panic as a drawing. If containment were merely manner, the adult's calm would be a courtesy with no downstream effect, and the child's regulation would track only the child's own exhaustion. The discriminating observation is therefore longitudinal and behavioural: over three sessions of received-and-returned feeling, does the child's self-regulation improve in the intervals, indicating internalisation of the function, or does each session restart at the same intensity, indicating that nothing was installed? The first pattern is a mechanism at work; the second is a pleasant hour. (The same test, incidentally, is what separates containment from suppression: suppression lowers arousal in the room and raises it at home; containment lowers it in both, slowly.)

**Question 5 — reading versus imposing.** The line is crossed when the adult's sentence closes the material rather than opening it, and the cleanest example of over-reading is the confident symbol: the practitioner who announces that the buried treasure "obviously" means the buried father, and obtains a child's quiet yes, and records confirmation. What was imposed was the gloss; what was obtained was compliance; what was lost was the child's own meaning, which in the recorded case emerged eleven weeks later and was nothing like the gloss — the treasure was the child's own buried birthday, the last one before the separation. The discipline that keeps the line: hypotheses are written with their disconfirmers (week 1); interpretations are offered as questions the play can answer ("I wonder who the treasure is waiting for?") rather than verdicts; and the child's later gloss, whenever it arrives, outranks the practitioner's, in the record and in the room. An over-reading detected is not a shame to hide but a datum: it tells you what you needed the case to be, which is the most useful supervision sentence there is.
