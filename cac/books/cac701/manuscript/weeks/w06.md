# The Behavioural Lineage II: Cognitive Therapy with Children and Adolescents {.unit num="6" week="6" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Draw and explain** the cognitive model (situation–thought–feeling–behaviour) using a child's own example.
2. **Identify** a hot thought and the thinking trap it instantiates, from a verbatim quotation.
3. **Construct** a behavioural experiment with a child that tests a hot thought, specifying the prediction, the test and what would count as disconfirmation.
4. **Adapt** cognitive methods to developmental level, stating what a child of six, ten and fifteen can and cannot yet do.
5. **Evaluate** the evidence for CBT with child anxiety and adolescent depression, naming where it is strong and where it is thin.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 5 located the loop outside the child. This week locates a second loop inside: what the child thinks between the situation and the response, and the way feeling confirms thought so that neither is ever tested. The two loops interlock — avoidance prevents disconfirmation, and undisconfirmed cognition sustains avoidance — which is why cognitive and behavioural methods are one therapy and not two.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Hot_thought}
The cognition that carries the affect: not the catalogue of what the child thinks but the one that, when spoken aloud, moves the feeling. In the room: the sentence you ask the child to say out loud and then rate — "and if that were true, what would it mean about you?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Cognitive_distortion}
A systematic error in inference — catastrophising, mind-reading, all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation, personalisation, emotional reasoning. In the room: a hypothesis about the thinking, not a verdict on the thinker; children detect being corrected and close.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Behavioural_experiment}
A test the child designs and runs: prediction stated, action taken, outcome compared with prediction. The engine of cognitive change, because it lets reality do the arguing. In the room: the difference between "let's think about whether that thought is true" and "let's find out".
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Safety_behaviour}
What the child does to prevent the feared catastrophe — the whispered rehearsal, the gripped pen, the scanned exit. In the room: the reason exposure sometimes fails: the catastrophe is never tested because the safety behaviour is credited with the non-occurrence.
:::

### Lecture I — The model, and why feeling is not evidence

**Proposition 1. Between situation and response there is an appraisal, and the appraisal carries the affect.** Two children called to the board feel differently because they think differently: "they will see me freeze" and "I get to show the sum I know" are different events. The model is not that thoughts cause everything; it is that appraisals are a legitimate, changeable part of the chain. Figure 6.1 draws the chain and the shortcut by which feeling is recruited as evidence for the thought.

![The cognitive model as a cycle: situation, thought, feeling and body, behaviour, and back again, with the maintenance shortcut by which the feeling is taken as confirmation of the thought. Intervention points are the test of the hot thought and the change of behaviour that makes testing possible. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-06-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 2. Avoidance protects the cognition.** A thought that is never tested cannot be disconfirmed, and every act of avoidance is a small experiment whose result is "the danger was real, and I escaped it". This is the cognitive reason the behavioural loop of week 5 is so stable, and the reason exposure must drop safety behaviours to work.

**Proposition 3. Development gates the method.** The cognitive model requires the child to hold a thought as an object of inspection — a metacognitive capacity that develops through middle childhood and is unreliable before about seven, and that adolescence complicates with self-consciousness. With a six-year-old you do not dispute thoughts; you change what happens and let new experience do the teaching, or you externalise the thought as a character ("the worry bully") so it can be talked to. With a ten-year-old you can catch, check and change with concrete materials. With a fifteen-year-old you can work with rules and assumptions ("if I am not first, I am nothing"), which is where the durable change usually lies.

**Proposition 4. The traps are teachable.** Figure 6.2 collects the six most common. Taught as a game of spotting — in stories, in cartoons, in the counsellor's own examples first — they give the child a vocabulary for their own inference errors without shame.

![Six thinking traps common in children and adolescents — catastrophising, mind-reading, all-or-nothing, overgeneralising, personalising and emotional reasoning — each illustrated in a child's idiom. Each trap is a hypothesis to test rather than a fault to correct. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac701/figures/fig-06-02.png){width=94%}

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Never dispute a child's thought directly. Disputation teaches compliance: the child learns to produce the "rational" sentence while keeping the belief. Test it instead, and let the result belong to the child.
:::

### Worked example 6.1 — From verbatim quote to experiment

**Task.** Amaka (10), before a class presentation: "If my voice shakes, everyone will laugh, and then I'll never be able to come back to class." Identify the hot thought, the traps, and design a behavioural experiment she could run this week.

**Solution.** Hot thought: "everyone will laugh" (the voice shaking she accepts; the universal laughter is the catastrophe). Traps: mind-reading (she knows what everyone will think), catastrophising (laughter becomes permanent exclusion), and all-or-nothing in the "never be able to come back". Experiment, co-designed: prediction written in her words and rated 0–100 ("80% will laugh out loud"); test — she asks two classmates afterwards what they noticed, and the counsellor (with consent) observes from the back and counts actual audible laughs; comparison — predicted number versus counted number, and what the classmates said. Disconfirmation criterion agreed in advance: fewer than three audible laughs, or classmates reporting they noticed nothing. Safety behaviours to drop: gripping the lectern, reading without looking up. The follow-up question is the durable one: "if nobody laughed, what does that tell the thought?" — because a single disconfirmation without a revised belief is a lucky day, not a change.

### Lecture II — Methods, in the order a child can use them

**Catch it.** Externalise and slow: the thought is written or drawn, given a voice, a size, a colour. With younger children a "thought bubble" prop is enough; the point is distance.

**Check it.** Not "is it true?" but "how could we find out?", "what's the evidence for and against, in things that actually happened?", "what would you tell a friend who had this thought?" The friend question reliably produces better reasoning than the self question, and the difference is itself data about the child's self-relationship.

**Change it — or change the behaviour.** Sometimes the cognition yields to a better one ("some people might look; looking isn't laughing; and I can finish the sentence even if they look"). Sometimes the useful move is behavioural: do the avoided thing with the thought still present and see what happens. With children, the second route is usually the stronger, because experience is more persuasive than argument and needs less metacognition.

**Consolidate.** A coping card in the child's own words, one sentence, carried into the next test. Not a slogan supplied by the adult: a sentence the child wrote after an experiment that went their way.

::: safeguard
Cognitive work can surface content that is not a distortion but a fact: a child who thinks "my stepfather hits me and nobody stops it" is not catastrophising. The discipline of the week is to test thoughts, and the discipline of the profession is to recognise when the thought is an accurate report. Where a cognition describes harm, the safeguarding procedure governs and the cognitive work pauses. Do not let a method make you deaf.
:::

### Lecture III — What the evidence supports

Cognitive-behavioural therapy is the best-evidenced psychological treatment for childhood anxiety disorders: manualised programmes built on exposure plus cognitive work outperform waitlist and attention controls, with effects that hold at follow-up, and computer-assisted and parent-involved variants extend reach. Kendall's programme is the standard exemplar. For adolescent depression the picture is weaker: CBT outperforms waitlist and sometimes treatment-as-usual, effects are modest, and for moderate depression the combination with careful medical and social assessment is the norm rather than therapy alone. For conduct problems, cognition alone is not the treatment — the evidence sits with the parents and the contingencies (week 5). Three cautions complete the picture: effects are smaller in routine clinics than in trials; dropout and non-response are common, and measuring outcome session by session (week 14) is how you notice; and the "cognitive" ingredient's specific contribution is smaller than the exposure and the relationship, which is an argument for humility about the ingredient and confidence about the package.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Match the method to the disorder and the age: exposure-led for anxiety, contingency-led for conduct, combined and modest for depression, and cognitive language only where the child's development can carry it. A thought record given to a seven-year-old is not treatment; it is paperwork.
:::

### Worked example 6.2 — Adapting down and up

**Task.** The same hot thought — "if I get it wrong, I'm stupid" — appears in Musa (6), Kemi (10) and Tobi (15). Specify the method for each.

**Solution.** Musa (6): no disputation, no record. Change the contingency and the model: errors are celebrated loudly in the room ("mistakes mean your brain is growing"), the adult models their own errors and repair, and success is shaped in small steps so that "wrong" is frequent and safe. The cognition changes by experience, over months. Kemi (10): catch-check-change with materials — the thought on a card, evidence for and against in two columns of real events, one behavioural experiment a week (answer a question she might get wrong, on purpose, and count the consequences), coping card in her words. Tobi (15): work at the rule level: the assumption "worth = performance"; downward arrow to the meaning ("if I'm stupid I'm unlovable"); behavioural experiments against the rule (disclose a failure and observe the relationship); and a values conversation about what he wants a mind for. Same thought, three developments, three methods.

::: skills
**Practical 6 — the experiment clinic.** In pairs, one plays a child supplying a real-sounding hot thought (cards supplied), the other has ten minutes to produce, with the "child", a written experiment: prediction and rating, test, disconfirmation criterion, safety behaviours to drop, and the follow-up question. The observer checks: was the prediction the child's words? was disconfirmation defined before the test? was any safety behaviour left in? Rotate. Debrief on which checks you habitually miss.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A cognitive formulation and experiment design of 1,200 words on a supplied case: the model drawn with the child's own material, hot thought and traps identified from verbatim quotes, one experiment fully specified, and a developmental justification of the methods chosen.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Hot thought is the child's words and carries the affect | 20% |
| Experiment specified (prediction, test, disconfirmation, safety behaviours) | 30% |
| Developmental justification of method | 25% |
| Model drawn correctly with the maintenance shortcut | 15% |
| APA 7th and prose quality | 10% |

**Formative.** Bring one verbatim hot thought you have heard from a child, with the trap you judge it to instantiate.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Cognition is the second loop: appraisal carries affect, avoidance protects appraisal, and the behavioural experiment — reality, borrowed for an afternoon — is the instrument that opens the loop, provided the method matches the child's development and the disorder.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why does direct disputation produce compliance rather than change? Give the mechanism and one clinical sign that it has happened.
2. A child's hot thought is true (they are, in fact, behind in reading). What is the cognitive work, and what is not?
3. Where does the feeling-as-evidence shortcut appear in your own reasoning about cases? Give an example.
4. Is a coping card a cognitive intervention or a self-instruction? Does the distinction matter for how you build it?
5. If the specific cognitive ingredient contributes little beyond exposure and alliance, should programmes still teach cognitive method? Argue both sides.
