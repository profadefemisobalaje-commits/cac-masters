# How to Use This Book {.front}

**Read the outcomes first and last.** Each week opens with what you will be able to do on completing it, written as capabilities that can be observed and examined. Read them before the week and again after it. If you cannot do what they say, you have not finished the week, however much of it you have read.

**Work the examples as you meet them.** Worked examples are set apart and are not optional reading. Cover the solution, attempt it, then compare. The distance between your attempt and the printed one is precisely the material the examination tests.

**Do the practicals.** Every week carries a practical — a timed exercise, a formulation task, a role-play, an observation or an analysis of supplied material — with a debrief. Most of them require another person or a recording of yourself and a clock. The practical is the part of the week most often skipped and the part that most changes practice. A theory you have only read is a theory you cannot use.

**Keep the comparison grid.** Figure 1.3 in week 1 is a blank grid: five questions down the side, four approaches across the top. Complete one column each time the course meets an approach, in your own words, and by week 15 reproduce the whole grid from memory and defend every cell. The grid is the course. Everything else is commentary on it.

**Argue with the lectures.** Each lecture ends in a clinical implication and a seminar question. The seminar questions are not rhetorical; write answers to them. Where a lecture states a contested position, the contest is named, and your task is to hold a position and say what would change it.

**For the tutor.** Each week is one four-hour block: ninety minutes of teaching, ninety minutes of seminar or practical work, the remainder preparation and follow-up. The three lectures are self-contained and may be reordered where a cohort needs the case before the theory. The assessed task printed in each week is the task set, and the criteria printed with it are the criteria used. Substitute local material in the vignette and the practical where a cohort's placements differ, provided the substitute is fictitious and labelled.

**For the supervisor.** The formulation exercises are yours. The question that converts knowledge into judgement is always the same: *which of the approaches in this week would formulate this case differently, and what would that formulation lead you to do on Tuesday?* A trainee who can state a mechanism of change and then say what would falsify it is ready for supervised caseload. A trainee who can only describe a theory is not.

## Conventions

| Convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Week *n* | the *n*th lecture week of the sixteen-week course |
| Lecture I, II, III | the three teaching sequences within a week |
| *Worked example* | a fully solved illustration; attempt it before reading the solution |
| *Practical* | a timed exercise with a debrief; requires a partner, a recording or supplied material |
| *Hypothetical* / *fictitious composite* | case material invented for teaching; no real client is described |
| Illustrative image (AI-generated) | a photograph created for this edition; depicts no identifiable person |
| Jurisdiction-dependent | a legal or reporting duty that must be checked against local law |

Figures are numbered by week: Figure 3.2 is the second figure of week 3. Figure captions appear below the figure and table captions above the table. Every figure carries alternative text, and the file is tagged for screen readers.
