# Emotion, Internalising, and the Conversations That Save Lives {.unit num="7" week="7" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Explain** the internalising gap — depression's and anxiety's emergence and gender divergence in adolescence — as timing, weather and treatment of weather rather than as weakness.
2. **Describe** adolescent emotion regulation: the intensities of week 3's weather with its regulatory instrument still under construction, and the strategies that are adaptive in adolescence.
3. **Assess** depression, anxiety presentations, and self-harm function in an adolescent, distinguishing normative intensity from clinical presentation and naming the red flags.
4. **Conduct** the risk conversation: asking about suicide directly, in plain language, without planting and without flinching, and documenting what is done with the answer.
5. **Build** a safety plan with an adolescent that they can use at two in the morning, and know the limits of your role.
:::

### Where this week sits

Adolescence's most consequential clinical event is the first internalising episode: the majority of lifetime depression and anxiety disorders begin before adulthood, and adolescence is their peak onset window. This week supplies the epidemiology, the regulation science, the assessment and — the reason the week exists — the conversations: the direct risk question, the function-seeking inquiry about self-harm, the safety plan built with rather than for the adolescent, and the family sentences that turn a household from an audience into a support. These are the most teachable hours in the programme, because they are the ones most often not had.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Internalising_gap}
The epidemiological fact of adolescence: depression and anxiety emerge and rise sharply from early adolescence, and from that point prevalence is roughly twice as high among girls as boys — a gap that opens at puberty and tracks timing (earlier transitions), weather (rumination, co-rumination), body scoring and treatment of distress rather than any difference in capacity to feel. In the room: why week 3's storm matters clinically, not just developmentally.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Rumination}
The repetitive, passive focus on distress and its causes and consequences — the style that converts weather into climate, predicted by adolescence's new capacity for sustained self-focus; it is more common among adolescent girls and is a robust predictor of depression's onset and persistence. In the room: the two-a-morning loop of week 3, and the one habit that most reliably converts a bad month into a disorder.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Self-harm_function}
Non-suicidal self-injury's usual functions: regulation of unbearable feeling, punishment of a hated self, communication to an audience, or escape from numbness — functions assessed by asking, not assumed; distinct from suicidal behaviour in intent while sharing risk, with a history of self-harm among the strongest single predictors of later suicide. In the room: the question that turns a scar from a scandal into a symptom with a grammar.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Direct_risk_question}
Asking about suicide plainly and without euphemism — "have you thought about killing yourself?" — which does not plant the idea (evidence consistently finds asking is safe and often relieving) and is the only reliable way to assess it. In the room: the sentence counsellors most fear and that saves the most lives; its absence, not its presence, is the risk.
:::

### Lecture I — The gap: what opens, when, and why

**Proposition 1. Adolescence is the onset window.** The bulk of lifetime depression and anxiety disorders have their first episode before adulthood, with incidence rising steeply from about the early teens; the disorders that will define many adult lives arrive while the brain is still assembling its brakes. This is the clinical sentence behind week 3's construction site: the instrument is plastic, and plastic cuts both ways — it is the window of opportunity and of vulnerability.

**Proposition 2. The gap opens at puberty and its causes are social-developmental, not intrinsic.** From early adolescence, girls' rates of depression and anxiety run roughly twice boys'. The tracks: earlier and more off-timed transitions (week 2's meaning machinery); rumination and co-rumination styles that convert weather into climate; the body's new public scoring (week 6); and a treatment of distress — in girls, talk and analysis; in boys, distraction and suppression — in which the first two can deepen the hole before they drain it. Boys' internalising is under-detected rather than absent: it presents as anger, conduct, substance and absence, and is recorded in other people's categories.

**Proposition 3. Comorbidity and masking are the rule.** Internalising rarely travels alone: anxiety and depression overlap heavily with each other, with trauma reactions, with the sleep debt of week 3, and — in boys especially — with conduct presentations that mask it. The assessment therefore never stops at the presenting register; it asks what lives underneath anger, absence and achievement alike.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** In every adolescent assessment, ask the internalising questions underneath the presenting category — including in the angry boy and the high-achieving girl, whose presentations are the two the gap hides best.
:::

### Lecture II — Regulation, and what helps

**Regulation in adolescence runs on three instruments at unequal maturity:** the behavioural repertoire learned in childhood (distraction, seeking, problem-solving), the new but still-developing cognitive instrument of week 3 (reframing, reappraisal, perspective-taking), and the peer instrument — co-regulation, which adolescents use more than children or adults and which is powerful in both directions: co-rumination deepens, co-action and companionship relieve.

**What helps, in order of leverage:** *sleep first* — the debt of week 3 is not background; irregular and short sleep predicts and maintains adolescent depression, and no regulation strategy works well on a sleep-deprived brain. *Behavioural activation* — the scheduled, valued, mastery-or-connection-bearing activities that depression cancels first and recovery needs first; in adolescence the schedule must recruit the domains the child weights (week 6), not the domains the adult would choose. *Rumination interruption* — the loop broken by doing rather than by arguing: movement, company, a task with hands; argued-against rumination becomes more rumination. *Cognitive work with the new instrument* — the divergence-point analysis of week 4 (what actually happened, what the loop added) now works because formal operations has arrived; before that it is consolation, after it, it is a tool. *The relationships that do not score* — week 6's second lever, which in depression is the load-bearing wall: one adult who knows, one peer who stays.

**What does not help:** advice-giving in the first ten minutes; minimising ("at your age, what is there to be sad about?" — which teaches the child the weather is a verdict); the interrogation of the loop rather than its interruption; and, above all, waiting for it to pass because adolescence is a storm. Some storms are weather; this week exists to teach which are climate.

### Lecture III — The conversations

**The risk conversation, directly.** Ask plainly, without euphemism, in the adolescent's own language once you have it: "when it is at its worst, do you ever think about killing yourself?" Asking does not plant the idea — the evidence is consistent that direct asking is safe and is often met with relief that the question was finally asked. Then assess in the order of lethality: thoughts (how often, how long, how controllable), plans (how specific, how available the means), intent (what stops you — and the answer to "what stops you" is the protective list the safety plan is built from), and history (previous attempts, self-harm, family suicide — the strongest predictors). The answer is documented in the record plainly: what was asked, what was said, what was done. The limit of your role is stated in the service's escalation policy and to the child before it is needed: life governs the room; if the answer requires it, other adults will be involved, how and when will be told to the child first, and the child is never handed over like an object — they are accompanied.

**The self-harm conversation, function-seeking.** "Tell me what it does for you" — asked without shock, after the wound is cared for. Function governs intervention: regulation gets the interruption repertoire and the sleep work; punishment gets the self-relationship work of week 6's levers; communication gets the family conversation the behaviour has been standing in for; escape from numbness gets the trauma assessment of week 13 beneath it. Means restriction is discussed with the household in language that does not shame the child: it is not confiscation of trust but the same principle as the bag on the windowsill in week 4 — heat needs friction removed, and the household removes it together with the child knowing.

**The safety plan, built with.** The adolescent's own plan, in their own words, on a card in their pocket: the early signs (theirs — "when the music stops sounding like anything"); the things that work alone (the loop-breakers: the walk, the cold water, the drill of a song); the people to contact in order (peer, adult, service) with numbers written, not remembered; and the professional contacts including the crisis line, with the one sentence rehearsed aloud in session so that using it at two in the morning is not a first draft. The plan is reviewed and rewritten as the weather changes; a plan that is never revised was never used.

::: safeguard
Where risk is present the record carries: the direct question asked and the answer given; the lethality assessment; the means-restriction conversation and with whom; the safety plan and its review date; the escalation performed and to whom; and the child's knowledge of every step. Where the child is at imminent risk, they are not left alone and the escalation is immediate — the accompaniment principle: the child goes with an adult, not to one.
:::

### Worked example 7.1 — The question Ada has been waiting for

**Task.** Ada's file now carries: six weeks of low mood, the choir loss, sleep to two a.m., one friend remaining, and — disclosed today, quietly, without being asked — "some nights I think it would be easier not to be here." Conduct and document the risk conversation.

**Solution.** *The direct question, in her language:* "When you think it would be easier not to be here — have you thought about killing yourself?" Answer: yes, sometimes, at night; "I would not do it to Bisi" — the protective list arrives in one name, and the plan will be built on it. *Lethality assessment:* thoughts present most nights, controllable "until about two"; no plan ("I have not thought about how"); no intent (Bisi; also, "I want to see SS3"); no attempts; no self-harm history; no family history known — to be checked with the auntie this week, framed as health history. *Function of the nights:* rumination + sleep debt + the looking (week 2) + two losses; the formulation writes climate-on-weather, and the intervention order is sleep first, choir restoration, Blessing re-watered, rumination interruption for the two-a.m. slot (the cold-water walk to the kitchen she already uses — named, scheduled, and the light left on for her). *Safety plan, with her, in her words:* signs ("when the music stops"); alone ("water, kitchen, sing anything quietly"); order ("Blessing first — but at two a.m. she sleeps — so Auntie Bisi, and the card has the service number"); rehearsed sentence said aloud twice in session. *Accompaniment:* the auntie is told this week — with Ada in the room, Ada's words used first ("she told me herself; this is what she said"), means conversation gentle (nothing to restrict; medicines locked as ordinary household practice, said as ordinary), and Ada's knowledge of every step recorded. *Documentation:* question asked, answer given, lethality assessed, plan built, review date in two weeks, auntie informed with Ada present. Falsifier: at two weeks, the nights are counted — if the two-a.m. slot has moved to four a.m., the plan is revised and escalation reviewed; if thoughts persist at six weeks despite sleep and restoration work, week 13 opens the clinical pathway with medical review.

### Worked example 7.2 — Kelvin's scar and its grammar

**Task.** Kelvin, 16, shows the counsellor two healed cuts on his forearm: "everybody does it." Assess function; intervene without scandal.

**Solution.** *Function-seeking, after care and without shock:* "Tell me what it does for you." Answer, eventually: "when the heat is too much, it is something to do with it" — regulation; plus a communication edge: "at least somebody asks about this." The grammar is now known: unbearable feeling with no repertoire, and a household where distress is not spoken. *Intervention, function by function:* regulation — the interruption repertoire built with him from what already works (the pitch, the walk home the long way, cold water, drill — chosen by him, written on the card); sleep and substance audit (week 3's debt and the evenings of week 4 both raise the heat); communication — the family conversation the cuts have been standing in for, prepared in session and held with the auntie present, Kelvin's words first; and the self-relationship lever — one adult in the house who now knows, so the function "somebody asks" is met by a person rather than a wound. *Means:* discussed with the household plainly, without shaming him — the razor is not hunted but the household agrees to the ordinary: medicines and blades stored as stored, and the sentence said to Kelvin aloud: "this is not because we do not trust you; it is because heat needs friction removed, and we are removing it together." *Documentation and limits:* function assessed, plan built with review date, auntie informed with Kelvin present, no current suicidal intent reported — the direct question still asked, and its answer recorded, because "everybody does it" is a sentence that has covered both. Falsifier: fresh marks within eight weeks reopen the assessment at medical review; the plan's use — one card sentence Kelvin repeats back unprompted — is the sign it is working.

::: skills
**Practical 7 — the risk conversation, rehearsed.** In triads, rehearse on the supplied transcripts until the sentences are fluent: the direct question in the adolescent's language; the lethality sequence (thoughts, plan, intent, history); "what stops you" and building the plan's first line from the answer; the function question after a self-harm disclosure; and the accompaniment sentence to the family. Each counsellor rotates through all three roles. 400-word record of the rehearsal with the sentences you would keep verbatim. The mark is for fluency under the adolescent's silence and the family's panic — the two conditions under which unpractised counsellors lose the sentence.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A risk conversation record and safety plan of 1,200 words from a supplied case: the direct question and its documentation; lethality assessment in order; the function-seeking inquiry; the safety plan in the adolescent's words with the rehearsed sentence; the family/accompaniment steps with the child's knowledge of each; and 300 words on the internalising gap's meaning for your service's intake questions.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Direct question asked plainly, documented | 25% |
| Lethality sequence complete and ordered | 20% |
| Function assessed by asking, not assumed | 15% |
| Safety plan in the adolescent's words, rehearsed | 20% |
| Accompaniment and family steps, child informed | 10% |
| Gap applied to service intake | 10% |

**Formative.** Write the one sentence you would use to open the risk question with a fifteen-year-old who has just said "it would be easier not to be here". Read it aloud. Revise it until it does not flinch.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Adolescence is internalising's onset window: the gap opens at puberty and tracks timing, rumination, body scoring and treatment of distress, while boys' internalising hides in other people's categories; regulation runs on instruments of unequal maturity with sleep as the first lever and rumination interruption by doing rather than arguing; self-harm has a grammar whose functions are assessed by asking; and the conversations that save lives are the direct risk question, the function-seeking inquiry, the safety plan built with the adolescent in their words, and the accompaniment that informs the child of every step — documented plainly, rehearsed until fluent, and reviewed until the weather passes.
:::

### Seminar questions

1. Why is the absence of the direct question a greater risk than its presence? What makes counsellors fear it?

3. Boys' internalising hides in anger and absence. Which intake question of your service would miss it?

5. A safety plan never revised was never used. How will you know yours was?
