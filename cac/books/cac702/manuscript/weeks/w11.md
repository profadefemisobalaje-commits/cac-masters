# Risk, Resilience and Positive Youth Development {.unit num="11" week="11" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why risk behaviour concentrates in adolescence without being universal or inevitable, integrating the dual-systems account (week 3) with peer context (weeks 4, 9).
2. **Describe** resilience as ordinary processes rather than rare traits — Masten's "ordinary magic" — and list the protective factors with the strongest evidence.
3. **Apply** cumulative-risk and turning-point thinking to a case: counting risks, locating the one factor that can move, and naming the turning point's mechanics.
4. **Describe** positive youth development and the five Cs, and state the programme features the evidence requires.
5. **Design** one PYD intervention for a real peer group, converting a risk group into a contribution group.
:::

### Where this week sits

Weeks 3, 4 and 9 supplied risk's mechanics; this week supplies the other half of the ledger — why most adolescents come through the interval intact, what protects them, and how services can build protection rather than only intercept failure. Resilience research reframes the counsellor's list from deficits to processes; positive youth development reframes the service from clinic to setting. The week ends with a design task, because PYD is not a theory to recite but a programme to run.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Ordinary_magic}
Masten's finding: resilience in development arises from ordinary adaptive systems — a capable caregiver, a close relationship, school engagement, self-regulation skills, faith or meaning community — rather than from rare individual toughness. In the room: the assessment question is not "is this child resilient?" but "which ordinary systems are still operating, and which have been lost?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Cumulative_risk}
The dose-response pattern: adverse outcomes track the **number** of risks more than any single risk, and protection works the same way in reverse — each added protective factor shifts the curve. In the room: count. A case with nine risks and one protection is a different plan from a case with three risks and four protections, whatever the presenting complaint.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Turning_point}
A discontinuity in a trajectory: a new relationship, a new setting, a new role (the team, the apprenticeship, the choir, the birth of a child, a migration) that changes the course rather than the moment. Turning points work by supplying a new identity narrative and a new peer set, not by insight. In the room: the mechanism behind every "and then everything changed" — and the thing a counsellor can sometimes help arrive.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Five_Cs}
Lerner's positive youth development model: competence, confidence, connection, character, caring — and, when the five are supplied by good settings, the sixth C, contribution, which turns outward toward community. The five are products of settings, not traits of children. In the room: the audit that converts "what is wrong with this adolescent?" into "what is missing from this adolescent's settings?"
:::

### Lecture I — Risk, counted honestly

Risk behaviour's adolescent peak is real — the elevated rates of accident, substance experimentation, unprotected sex and offending in the mid-teens — and it is explained without recourse to defect: the dual-systems gap (week 3) supplies the engine, the peer context (weeks 4, 9) supplies the accelerator, and inexperience supplies the rest. Three corrections keep the ledger honest. **Most adolescents do not:** the majority pass through the interval without serious risk behaviour, and the majority of those who experiment desist — adolescent-limited patterns are the norm, not the prelude. **Risk is concentrated:** a small share of adolescents carries a large share of the risk behaviour, and that share overlaps heavily with cumulative adversity rather than with adolescence itself. **Risk and opportunity share the engine:** the same reward-sensitivity and peer-orientation that carry the hazard carry the courage, the exploration and the learning of the interval — which is why the goal is steering rather than suppression, and why a service that only suppresses will also suppress the adolescent.

The counsellor's risk assessment therefore counts in both directions: the risk list (substance, weapons, sexual risk, offending, self-harm, exploitation, online conduct) and the protection list (one adult who knows, school belonging, a valued competence, a faith or meaning community, a future orientation with a date, a friendship of quality) — because the plan is built from the difference, and the protection list is usually the shorter document and the more movable one.

### Lecture II — Resilience as process

**Proposition 1. The short list of ordinary magic.** Across decades and samples, the protective factors recur: at least one stable, committed relationship with a capable adult; self-regulation skills (week 3's instrument, trained); school or work engagement with a belonging niche; a meaning or faith community; and a future orientation with concrete plans. None is exotic; all are intervenable; and the first — the committed adult — is the one the counselling relationship itself can sometimes supply for a season, which is both the hope and the limit of the role.

**Proposition 2. Cumulative risk changes the plan's arithmetic.** Single-risk interventions fail in high-count cases because the dose is the number: removing one of nine risks moves the curve negligibly. The work in high-count cases is therefore sequential and structural — add protections before subtracting risks (the relationship before the substance conversation, the meal before the monitoring), and recruit institutions (school, church, trade, health service) because a counsellor alone cannot move a count of nine.

**Proposition 3. Turning points are settings, not insights.** The mechanisms by which trajectories bend are documented: a new role that supplies identity and routine (the team, the apprenticeship, the choir, parenthood reframed), a new relationship that supplies the committed adult late, a new place that breaks the cue-context of old behaviour (the move, the migration, the new school). Counselling's contribution to a turning point is usually logistical and narrative: helping the role arrive, helping the story of the self change tense — from "I am the one who failed" to "I am the one who is training" — because the narrative is what lets the new setting hold.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Keep two lists per case from the first session: risks and protections, counted. Review the counts at every review date; the plan's success is visible as a change in the difference before it is visible in the presenting symptom.
:::

### Lecture III — Positive youth development as service design

PYD's claim is structural: adolescents develop well where settings supply the five Cs — **competence** in domains they value, **confidence** that survives scoring, **connection** to adults and peers who know them, **character** as rules that travel across situations, **caring** as sympathy practised in action — and where the settings then invite **contribution**, the sixth C, outward to family and community. The evidence on programmes is specific about features: sustained rather than episodic contact; an adult relationship rather than a curriculum alone; skill-building with real standards; opportunities for contribution with real consequences; and alignment across settings (home, school, club) rather than a single heroic hour per week.

For this programme's services the design consequences are concrete. The **peer group is recruited as the unit**: the set that carries risk also carries loyalty and skill (week 5's fidelity); a PYD conversion gives the set a contribution task with real consequences — the team's coaching of the juniors, the stall that funds the kit, the night-watch rota during the floods — because contribution supplied to a set converts its internal economy from daring to keeping. The **church and the market are settings,** not obstacles: the choir, the youth fellowship, the apprenticeship bench are the five Cs' native habitats in this context, and a service that cannot name its adolescents' settings cannot supply the Cs. And the **counsellor's own room** is audited by the same list: which C does a session supply — and which does it accidentally tax?

::: safeguard
PYD work with high-count cases does not replace protection work: where exploitation, labour, abuse or offending are present, the safeguarding and legal pathways of weeks 7, 8 and 10 govern first, and the PYD plan runs beside them. Contribution tasks must never place an adolescent in unsupervised charge of younger children where any safeguarding concern exists about the adolescent themselves — the rota is designed with the institution's child-protection officer, not around them.
:::

### Worked example 11.1 — Ada's setting audit on the five Cs

**Task.** Audit Ada's current settings against the five Cs at the week-11 review, and name the one Cs-supplying change the service can make this term.

**Solution.** *Competence:* choir (rising — the solo accepted, week 9) and school mid-band with two rising subjects (week 10): supplied, thinly but really. *Confidence:* fragile — the comparison machine audited and architected (week 6), the results chart re-read as slope (week 10): improving, still the weakest C. *Connection:* Blessing restored, the auntie's knowledge-supply reopened, the counsellor known: three names, which is the protective factor with the strongest evidence, present. *Character:* intact and unexamined — she keeps the choir's rules and the errand's promise; the C nobody has named to her, and naming it is free. *Caring:* present but inward — she cares for the auntie silently; no outward channel since the choir's withdrawal. *Contribution:* absent — and its absence is the finding: every other C has a holder, contribution has none. *The one change the service can make this term:* the choir's junior section needs a helper for the under-tens' rehearsal on Saturdays; Ada is asked by the choirmaster (not the counsellor — the setting must own the invitation) with the counsellor's prior rehearsal of the yes-and-no sentence so refusal stays safe. The change supplies contribution, re-weights competence with a witness, and places her in a room where the looking of week 2 cannot reach, because the under-tens do not look. Falsifier: attendance at four of six Saturday rehearsals by term's end; if she declines twice, the invitation is withdrawn without comment and re-routed through Blessing as a pair task, because contribution coerced is not contribution.

### Worked example 11.2 — Converting Kelvin's set

**Task.** Design a PYD conversion for Kelvin's peer set (five boys, 15–17, evening runs, one arrest) using the programme features the evidence requires.

**Solution.** *The unit is the set, not Kelvin:* the loyalty is the asset (weeks 5, 9); the plan recruits all five or none. *The contribution task with real consequences:* the ward's junior football league has no referees' assistant and no kit fund; the set is approached — through Kelvin and through the ward's sports officer, not through the counsellor — to run the Saturday-under-14s: two as linesmen (the uncle's trade supplies the flags and the repair of the posts, which puts the uncle's standards inside the task), two as the kit fund's stewards (money with a ledger is character with a consequence), one as the under-tens' coach, which is the role that changes a self-narrative fastest. *Sustained contact:* the league is a season, not an event; the sports officer is the committed adult the design supplies, with the counsellor in the background and the auntie's Sunday meal as the household anchor. *Skill-building with real standards:* the league's rules are the standards; the set's existing competence (football, nerve, organisation) is the curriculum, so nothing is remedial and nothing is a favour. *Alignment across settings:* the school knows (the football pride is already announced at the meal, weeks 6 and 8), the uncle's shop supplies Saturdays' apprenticeship hours around the fixtures (week 10's hinge), and the code-message (week 4) now reports the fixture rather than the run. *Risk count reviewed, not ignored:* the evening runs are not banned; they lose two nights to fixtures and the bag-rule conversation stands (week 4). Evaluation is dated and counted: fixtures staffed per month, one voluntary telling per boy per month to any adult in the design, and the attendance chart for Kelvin at week six (week 10's falsifier). If the league declines the set, the fallback with equal features is the ward's night-watch rota during the rains — contribution with consequences, supervised, and older than football in this community.

::: skills
**Practical 11 — the two lists and the design.** From a supplied high-count case: write the risk list and the protection list with counts; then design a PYD conversion for the adolescent's peer group against the five evidence features (sustained contact, adult relationship, skill with standards, contribution with consequences, cross-setting alignment). 450 words. The mark is for the design recruiting the group's existing loyalty and skill — a design that asks a set to become a choir has designed nothing.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A resilience and PYD analysis of 1,100 words: the two lists with counts and the plan's arithmetic stated; the ordinary-magic audit (which adaptive systems operate, which are lost); cumulative-risk sequencing (add protections before subtracting risks, with the first two additions named); one turning-point candidate with its mechanics (new role, relationship or place, and the narrative change it carries); and a PYD design against the five evidence features with dated, counted evaluation.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Two lists counted; arithmetic stated | 20% |
| Ordinary-magic audit concrete | 20% |
| Sequencing argued (protections first) | 15% |
| Turning point with mechanics and narrative | 20% |
| PYD design against five features, evaluation dated | 25% |

**Formative.** Name the committed adult of your own adolescence who was not a parent. Write the one thing they did repeatedly. That is the intervention.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Risk concentrates in adolescence without being universal, concentrated or permanent: the dual-systems engine, the peer accelerator and inexperience explain the peak, and most adolescents desist; resilience is ordinary magic — the committed adult, self-regulation, engagement, meaning, a dated future — assessed as operating systems rather than traits; cumulative risk makes the count the target, so protections are added before risks are subtracted and institutions are recruited because counsellors alone cannot move a count of nine; turning points are settings with new narratives, helped logistically rather than insightfully; and positive youth development supplies the five Cs through sustained settings with real standards and real contribution — the peer group recruited as the unit, the church and market named as settings, and the counsellor's own room audited by the same list.
:::
