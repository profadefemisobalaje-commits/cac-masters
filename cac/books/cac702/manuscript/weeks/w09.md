# Peers, Crowds, Friendship and Romance {.unit num="9" week="9" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the structure of the adolescent peer world — crowds, cliques, friendships, romantic dyads — and state what each layer supplies and what it costs.
2. **Distinguish** selection from socialisation in peer similarity, and explain why "they are a bad influence" is usually half of a true sentence.
3. **Explain** co-rumination and friendship quality as mechanisms: why the same friendship that protects can also transmit distress.
4. **Describe** romantic and sexual development as normative sequences with diverse timings, and counsel romance's endings and its dangers — coercion, control, image-based abuse — without moral panic.
5. **Work with** the peer group rather than against it: friendship repair, set-exit with face, and the digital village's etiquette.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 8 placed the adolescent in the household; this week places them in the second institution: the peer world, which supplies belonging, identity material, rehearsal for intimacy, and a substantial share of the semester's risk. The counsellor who treats the peer group as the enemy will lose the adolescent, because the peer group is where the adolescent lives; the counsellor who treats it as harmless will miss the mechanisms — selection, socialisation, co-rumination, coercion — by which friendships and romances carry both the cure and the wound.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Peer_layers}
The nested structure of the adolescent peer world: the crowd (a reputation and a label, loose and public), the clique (three to nine chosen companions, daily), the friendship (a dyad of disclosure, tested over time), and the romantic dyad (two, time, body, risk). Intimacy and risk both deepen toward the centre. In the room: locate the presenting wound's layer before choosing the intervention.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Selection_vs_socialisation}
The two engines of peer similarity: adolescents choose companions like themselves (selection), and companions then make each other more alike (socialisation). Both are real; their share varies by behaviour — strong for substance use and school engagement, weaker for personality. In the room: why removing a friend rarely removes the behaviour, and why the sentence "they are a bad influence" is half true.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Co-rumination}
Rose's construct: the excessive, repetitive discussion of problems within a friendship — high in disclosure, high in contagion; more common among girls' friendships and a mechanism by which close friendship simultaneously predicts support and internalising symptoms. In the room: the difference between a friend who listens and a friendship whose only language is the wound.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Romantic_sequence}
The normative ordering of romantic development — group-based mixed-sex activity, then dyadic dating, then romantic relationships with commitment and sexual intimacy — with wide individual and cultural variation and no required timetable; early and off-sequence involvement carries risk chiefly where supervision and support are absent, not because romance is itself pathology. In the room: the calendar question ("where are they in the sequence, and who knows?") rather than the moral one.
:::

### Lecture I — The peer world's structure and its two engines

**Proposition 1. Each layer supplies a different nutrient.** The crowd supplies a public identity and a place to be seen; the clique supplies daily belonging and a behavioural norm; the friendship supplies disclosure and the first experience of being known; the romantic dyad supplies intimacy, desire and the first negotiation of body and boundary. A deficit at any layer produces a different wound: crowd-exclusion reads as shame, clique-loss as loneliness, friendship-betrayal as grief, romantic loss as catastrophe — and the referral letter will say "low mood" for all four.

**Proposition 2. Similarity is manufactured twice.** Selection places like with like before any influence occurs; socialisation then amplifies. The practical consequence is exact: an intervention that separates friends attacks socialisation and leaves selection untouched — the adolescent will re-select within a week unless the underlying need (the set's warmth, the crowd's label) is met elsewhere. Hence week 4's bag rule and week 8's widening: add objects of loyalty rather than confiscating them.

**Proposition 3. Friendship quality, not friendship presence, is the variable.** A high-quality friendship — reciprocal, trusting, low in conflict — protects against victimisation and distress; a high-conflict or coercive "friendship" carries risk worse than solitude. Assessment therefore audits quality per named friend: who listens, who keeps secrets, who scores, who punishes. The audit is also the intervention's map, because one good friend is the most repairable asset in adolescent work.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** In every case, name the layer the wound lives in — crowd, clique, friendship or romance — and audit one named relationship per layer for quality. "Peer problems" is not a formulation; "clique-loss with one coercive friendship remaining" is.
:::

### Lecture II — Romance, sexuality and their dangers

Romantic development is normative and, on the evidence, developmentally significant: romantic experience in adolescence contributes to identity, to the capacity for intimacy, and to the regulation of emotion in relationships — which is why counselling that treats adolescent romance as a distraction from schoolwork has mispriced it. The sequence runs from mixed-group activity through dyadic dating to committed relationships, with sexual intimacy's timing varying widely by culture, faith and individual; the counsellor's calendar question is where this adolescent is in the sequence and which adults know, because risk concentrates where involvement outruns support rather than where it begins.

**The dangers have names and they are assessable.** *Coercion and control:* the partner who monitors the phone, forbids the friendship, dictates the clothing — control behaviours escalate in adolescent relationships as in adult ones, and the adolescent vocabulary for them is often love. *Image-based abuse:* the intimate image shared, or the threat of sharing, which is conduct with legal edges and a victim, whatever the image's origin and whatever the child's shame says. *Sexual health and pregnancy:* accurate local information, contraception and services, stated plainly, without the moral lecture that ends disclosure. *Consent:* the capacity to refuse and to be refused, taught as a skill with sentences, not as a slogan.

**Diverse orientations, same duties.** Romantic development runs on the same sequence for sexual- and gender-minority adolescents, with two additions: a community that may exist only online, and a disclosure geometry in which the wrong person knowing first can cost the child their home. The week 5 safeguard governs: safety first, the child's words and timetable, the family work that converts rejection into protection at the family's pace.

### Lecture III — Counselling the peer world

**Friendship repair** is the most cost-effective intervention in adolescent practice: one re-watered friendship moves esteem, disclosure and monitoring-knowledge at once. The method is behavioural and small — a standing errand, a shared task, a message script rehearsed in session — and it is evaluated by contact counts, not by sentiment.

**Co-rumination is treated by widening the friendship's language,** not by ending the friendship: the pair is given a shared doing (the walk, the drill, the project) so that the wound stops being the only topic; where co-rumination is the friendship's entire substance, the individual work of week 7 (rumination interruption) runs in parallel.

**Set-exit with face** is the route out of a peer group that must be left: every set contains an exit that does not read as betrayal — the injury, the apprenticeship, the auntie's shop, the team selection — and the counsellor's job is to find it with the adolescent and dress the leaving in it, so that fidelity (week 5) is honoured even in departure.

**The digital village's etiquette** is counselled as etiquette, not surveillance: what is forwarded, what is screenshotted, what is owed an answer tonight and what is not; the group chat's cruelty is named as conduct, and the bystander's sentence ("that is not how we talk about people here") is rehearsed, because bystander speech is the only regulation the village respects.

::: safeguard
Romantic disclosures carry three safeguarding edges: coercion and control by a partner (assess safety and means of contact; the child's phone may be the abuser's instrument); image-based abuse (the child is a victim; preserve evidence, report per jurisdiction, and never let shame organise the response); and age-asymmetry that constitutes an offence under the jurisdiction's law (where an adult or much older partner is involved, protection law governs and the accompaniment principle applies).
:::

### Worked example 9.1 — Ada and Blessing, at six weeks

**Task.** Evaluate the friendship-repair plan of week 6 (the standing market errand with Blessing) and decide what changes.

**Solution.** The record: five errands in six weeks (one missed for rain), two voluntary tellings to the auntie arising on the walk home (knowledge-supply reopened, week 8), one choir rehearsal attended with Blessing beside her and entry from the back (week 2's engineering), mood diary: two-a.m. nights down from six per week to two. The friendship audit per layer: crowd — Ada remains unlabelled and unbothered; clique — the old choir trio has not reformed, and Ada names no clique, which at fourteen is acceptable but thin; friendship — Blessing: listens, keeps secrets, does not score: one high-quality dyad, restored; romance — none, and no wish for any yet, stated without shame: normative. Co-rumination check: the errands' talk is prices, choir, the auntie — the wound is not the friendship's only language: healthy. Decision: the plan continues unchanged except one addition — the choir's next-term solo is accepted (week 6's deferred competence), because competence in a valued domain now has a witness, which is what converts achievement into esteem. Falsifier kept: if the two-a.m. nights return with the solo, the governing strand reorders to internalising (week 7) and the formulation is rewritten, not the plan.

### Worked example 9.2 — Kelvin's loyalty, and Ngozi

**Task.** Kelvin (16) has a girlfriend of four months, Ngozi, who is in SS2 at another school. She has asked him twice to stop the evening runs with the set; he has refused twice, saying "I cannot leave my people." The set has begun mocking her. Counsel the triangle.

**Solution.** The layers: the set is clique-plus-history (fidelity's oldest object, week 5); Ngozi is the first friendship-quality dyad with romance in it — the audit finds she listens, keeps secrets, and does not score: the highest-quality relationship in his life, and the only one asking him toward school. The mock-campaign is the set defending its boundary: exit-readiness is being punished, which is exactly the mechanism set-exit-with-face exists for. The intervention runs on three tracks. *With Kelvin:* name the collision aloud — "two loyalties, both real; the set wants all of you and she wants the best of you; that is not a choice between good and bad, it is a scheduling war" — and locate the exit with face: the goalkeeper's return (weeks 2 and 6) gives him evening training three nights a week that the set already accepts as football, not betrayal; the remaining two nights keep the code-message (week 4) and the auntie's meal. *With the triangle:* one session with Ngozi present in which the mock-campaign is named as conduct and Kelvin is given his bystander sentence to say to the set — rehearsed twice in session, because the sentence is his status cost and it must be fluent before it is spent. *With the household:* the auntie is told of the relationship with Kelvin present and in his words (week 8's geometry), and is asked for one thing only: that Ngozi is welcome at the Sunday meal — a loyalty object added inside the house, which is where widening works. Falsifier, dated: if within eight weeks the evening runs have not fallen to two nights, or the mock-campaign has escalated to Ngozi's route home, the exit-with-face has failed and the conversation turns to a clean break with the set — planned with Kelvin, dressed in the football, and honoured rather than hidden.

::: skills
**Practical 9 — the peer audit.** From a supplied case record: build the layer map (crowd, clique, friendship, romance) with one named relationship per layer; audit each named relationship for quality (listens / keeps secrets / scores / punishes); locate the presenting wound's layer; and write one friendship-repair or set-exit-with-face plan with a dated falsifier. 400 words. The mark is for names, not categories: an audit without named people is a theory, not an assessment.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A peer-world case analysis of 1,100 words: the layer map with named relationships and quality audits; selection versus socialisation argued for the presenting behaviour with the half-true sentence completed; co-rumination assessed where a close friendship exists; the romantic-sequence calendar with the support question answered; and one intervention plan (repair, widening, exit-with-face or bystander sentence) with its falsifier.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Layer map with named relationships and quality audits | 25% |
| Selection and socialisation distinguished and applied | 20% |
| Co-rumination and friendship quality assessed | 15% |
| Romantic calendar and support question | 15% |
| Intervention plan concrete with falsifier | 25% |

**Formative.** Name the friend who carried your own adolescence, and write the one sentence that tells you whether the friendship was nourishment, co-rumination, or both.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The peer world is layered — crowd, clique, friendship, romance — with intimacy and risk deepening inward; similarity is manufactured twice, by selection and socialisation, so separating friends treats half the mechanism; friendship quality rather than presence is the protective variable, and co-rumination is the tax close friendship can levy; romantic development is a normative sequence whose risks concentrate where involvement outruns support, and its dangers — coercion, image-based abuse, untaught consent — have names and assessments; and the counsellor works with the peer world by repairing one friendship, widening a friendship's language, finding the set-exit with face, and teaching the village's etiquette rather than policing it.
:::

1. "They are a bad influence." Complete the sentence with the half that is missing, and say what changes in the plan.

3. Co-rumination: when is ending the friendship the right call, and who decides?
