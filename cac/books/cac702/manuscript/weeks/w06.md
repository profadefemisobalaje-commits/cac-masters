# Self, Esteem and the Body Under the Comparison Machine {.unit num="6" week="6" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Describe** self-concept differentiation in adolescence and the self-esteem trajectory with its early-adolescent dip and its gender gap.
2. **Explain** the comparison machine: social comparison, curated feeds and audience metrics as a developmental instrument turned hostile, and name what it measures and what it cannot.
3. **Assess** body-image difficulty and its gradients, distinguishing normative dissatisfaction from clinical presentation, and locate the eating-disorder red flags.
4. **Counsel** with esteem's two levers — competence in valued domains and relationship that does not score — rather than with praise that inflates and then collapses.
5. **Design** one household or school change that reduces metric exposure without confiscation battles.
:::

### Where this week sits

Week 4 gave the adolescent the ideal; week 5 gave the ideal a name — identity. This week asks what happens when the ideal is aimed at the self: the self-concept splits into selves for each audience, esteem dips as the mirrors multiply, the body becomes the most public and most scored of all the selves, and a machine arrives — the feed — that industrialises comparison at a scale no previous adolescence met. The counsellor's work is to restore the two levers esteem actually runs on, and to disarm the machine without confiscating the phone, because the phone is also the child's village.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Self-concept_differentiation}
The adolescent's discovery that they are different selves in different relationships — with mother, with peers, with the teacher, online — and the early distress of the inconsistencies ("which one is me?"). Integration across audiences is the task; fragmentation under incompatible audiences is the risk. In the room: the sentence beneath "they don't know the real me" — sometimes true, sometimes the true me not yet assembled.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Esteem_dip}
Self-esteem falls for many children in the early-adolescent transition — new school, new mirrors, puberty's timing — and recovers across the teens; the dip is deeper on average for girls, and the gap tracks bodies, audiences and domains valued rather than any difference in worth. In the room: the pattern, not the level, is the finding — a low level that is stable is a different formulation from a falling one.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Comparison_machine}
The industrialisation of social comparison: curated selves, algorithmic feeds, and countable audiences (likes, views, streaks) that convert the imaginary audience of week 4 into a dashboard. The machine measures engagement, not worth; but the adolescent's developing system reads the dashboard as the audience's verdict, hour by hour. In the room: why the two-a-morning loop now arrives with numbers attached.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Esteem_levers}
The two processes that actually build esteem: competence in domains the adolescent values (not the domains adults value), and relationships that do not score — being known and held without metric. Praise that inflates ("you are the best") collapses at the first comparison; praise that describes effort and specific competence survives, and relationship outlasts both. In the room: the counsel to parents who have been feeding the machine with scores.
:::

### Lecture I — The differentiated self and its dip

**Proposition 1. Differentiation precedes integration and costs distress.** The child's unitary self-concept splits in early adolescence into audience-relative selves; the inconsistencies are felt as falseness until integration arrives later in the teens. Where audiences are compatible the integration is ordinary; where they are hostile to each other — the home self and the peer self that cannot meet, the faith self and the online self — the adolescent carries incompatible selves and the cost is shame or exhaustion, not confusion alone.

**Proposition 2. The dip is contextual and the gap is meaningful.** The early-adolescent esteem dip tracks the transition's new mirrors; its greater depth for girls tracks which bodies and behaviours the mirrors score, and the domains in which girls' competence is publicly valued. The pattern-level finding counsels against panic at a single low score and for attention to a falling trajectory across two terms.

**Proposition 3. Domains are weighted by the child, not by the adult.** Esteem is domain-specific and weighted by personal importance: a girl whose esteem rests on choir and friendship loses more in the choir's collapse than any report card can restore. Assessment therefore maps the child's own domain weights before any intervention, because an intervention in an unweighted domain is an intervention in a stranger's self.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Map the adolescent's esteem domains and weights in their own words — "what would hurt most to lose?" — before any esteem work. The list is the self's load-bearing wall schedule.
:::

### Lecture II — The comparison machine, precisely

The machine has three parts and each has a different intervention. **Curation:** the feed presents idealised selves — filtered bodies, staged moments, selected successes — as ordinary life; the adolescent compares their interior with others' exteriors, which is a comparison no human can win. Intervention is literacy, not confiscation: the feed deconstructed item by item in session ("who took this? how many were deleted? what was happening one minute later?"), because a deconstructed image loses its verdict-power. **Metrics:** counts convert the week-4 imaginary audience into a dashboard with numbers; the numbers measure engagement and algorithmic timing, not worth, but the developing system reads verdict. Intervention is architectural: notifications off during homework and after a set hour, the counts hidden where the platform allows, and one metric-free zone daily — the meal, the choir, the pitch — where no audience exists because no device does. **Algorithm:** the feed optimises for engagement and therefore for emotion, and negative comparison is engaging; the adolescent experiences the algorithm's business model as their own unworthiness. Intervention is the sentence that relocates the cause: "you are not the problem the feed found; you are the product it is selling attention around" — which, said once with evidence, is durable in adolescents because it recruits their formal-operational critique (week 4) on the machine's behalf rather than their own.

The machine is not only harm: it is also the village — the diaspora cousin, the choir group, the study thread, the identity community a small town cannot supply. Confiscation removes the village with the verdict; the work is architecture that keeps the village and starves the dashboard.

### Lecture III — The body: normative dissatisfaction and its red flags

Body dissatisfaction in adolescence is normative in the statistical sense and harmful in the developmental one: most adolescents, girls markedly more, report wishing something about their body different, and the dissatisfaction tracks the mirrors rather than the body. The clinical line is not dissatisfaction versus none but **grip**: dissatisfaction that organises the day — food restriction, avoided rooms, abandoned sport, the deleted photograph, the refused assembly — has grip, and grip is the referral criterion.

The red flags that end waiting: restriction with weight loss or growth faltering; purging or compensatory exercise; the adolescent who will not eat in company; exercise that continues through injury and fever; and, in boys, the muscularity-directed variant — the protein powders, the mirror hours, the rage at missed sessions — which is under-recognised because it wears the costume of discipline. Comorbidity is the rule: body grip with depression, with perfectionism, with the trauma history that makes the body a scene rather than a self.

The counsel to families runs on the two levers and three prohibitions: no commentary on anyone's body in the house, including the parents' own; no weighing as punishment or reward; no food policing at the table — the table is relationship, and relationship is the lever that outlasts the others. Where grip is present, the referral pathway of week 13 opens with medical review first, because eating difficulty is the one adolescent presentation where the body's state can outrun the counselling.

::: safeguard
Body and feed material carries two safeguarding edges: image-based abuse — the shared intimate image, the edited humiliation — is conduct by others with legal edges, and the child is a victim whatever the image's origin; and pro-harm communities (restriction, self-harm techniques) recruit through the same feeds, so a feed audit is part of assessment where grip or self-harm is present, conducted with the adolescent's collaboration rather than by seizure, because a seized phone is a lost village and a lost disclosure route.
:::

### Worked example 6.1 — Ada's domain weights and the choir

**Task.** Map Ada's esteem domains, locate the machine's contribution, and write the two-lever plan.

**Solution.** Weights, in her words: choir ("where I am my voice"), friendship ("Blessing only"), school ("I am average and it is fine"), home ("I am the quiet one"). The load-bearing walls are choir and one friendship — and both are currently damaged: the choir by the looking (week 2), the friendship by class separation and Ada's withdrawal. The machine's contribution, audited with her: an hour nightly on a feed of choir pages and school confession pages; the counts watched, not posted — she is a reader, not a poster, and the reader's injury is comparison without the village's reply. Plan, two levers: competence — the choir's return engineered on her terms (entry from the back, one named friend beside her, the solo declined this term and accepted next, her choosing); relationship — Blessing re-watered by one standing weekly errand together at the market, the auntie's household named aloud as a wall ("you are the quiet one **here**; here you are also the one Bisi calls first"). Machine architecture, without confiscation: the confession pages unfollowed together in session (she laughs at the third one — the deconstruction working), notifications off after nine, and the market errand as the daily metric-free zone. Falsifier, dated: if at six weeks the choir has returned and the mood has not moved, the governing strand is internalising (week 7) and the formulation reorders; if the feed audit finds pro-harm communities, week 13 opens the same week.

### Worked example 6.2 — The father's scores

**Task.** Kelvin's late father's brother visits monthly and ranks the boys' results aloud at the family meal. Kelvin's esteem in school domains is low and falling; he has stopped bringing books home. Counsel the household.

**Solution.** The ranking is the machine in its oldest form: a metric, delivered publicly, by a person the child loves and cannot answer. Three moves with the household, the uncle present. First, honour the intention and name the mechanism: "You rank them because you want them to rise. The ranking does the opposite — it teaches Kelvin that school is a place where he is read aloud and found small, so he has stopped bringing the evidence home." Second, replace the metric with the two levers: competence in a domain Kelvin weights — the goalkeeper's return of week 2 is now the family's public pride, announced at the meal in the ranking's old slot, so the ritual's force is recruited rather than fought; and relationship without score — one monthly walk with the uncle in which results are forbidden and football talk is mandatory, because the boy needs the man more than the man's table needs the numbers. Third, the prohibition stated once and kindly: no child's result read aloud in this house again; results are the child's to tell, in their own time, to whom they choose. The falsifier is behavioural and dated: books home, or not, within four weeks; and one sentence from Kelvin about the walk, which is the lever that will still be working at twenty-five.

::: skills
**Practical 6 — the feed audit, collaborative.** With a supplied case record or role-play: conduct a collaborative feed audit — what is followed, what is watched, what is posted, which hours, which communities; deconstruct two curated items item-by-item in session language; and write the architecture plan (notifications, counts, metric-free zone, village preserved). 300 words. The mark is for the village surviving the architecture.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** The adolescent self differentiates into audience-relative selves whose integration is the task; esteem dips in the early transition with a gender gap that tracks mirrors and valued domains; the comparison machine industrialises the imaginary audience into a dashboard that measures engagement and is read as verdict; body dissatisfaction becomes clinical at grip, with red flags that end waiting; and esteem is rebuilt on its two levers — competence in the child's valued domains and relationship that does not score — with architecture that starves the dashboard while keeping the village.
:::
