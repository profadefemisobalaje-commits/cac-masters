# Identity: The Crisis That Is Not a Crisis {.unit num="5" week="5" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Explain** Erikson's identity versus role confusion as a developmental task with a social precondition — the moratorium — and state who is granted one and who is not.
2. **Apply** Marcia's four statuses to one adolescent across three domains, and use status-language as description rather than rank.
3. **Describe** ethnic and religious identity development and their protective function under discrimination, and counsel across a difference you do not share.
4. **Hold** sexual orientation and gender identity development with accuracy: the diversity of trajectories, the minority-stress mechanism, and the evidence on affirmation.
5. **Interview** an adolescent about identity in their own registers — faith, work, friendship, digital self — without importing the textbook's domain list.
:::

### Where this week sits

Identity is adolescence's signature task: the assembly of the child's identifications, talents, loyalties and shames into a self that can hold under pressure and make promises. This week moves from Erikson's crisis to Marcia's statuses to the identities that carry the most weight and the most risk in real adolescent lives — ethnic, religious, sexual, gendered, digital — and installs the interviewing discipline that lets a counsellor hear which identity is actually in crisis, because it is rarely the one the referral names.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Identity_v_role_confusion}
Erikson's fifth crisis: the integration of childhood identifications and new capacities into a coherent self with commitments, against the confusion of a self that cannot cohere or commit. Its virtue is fidelity — the capacity to be loyal to people and to promises. In the room: the question beneath most adolescent distress — not "what is wrong?" but "who am I, and who will let me be it?"
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Moratorium}
The socially granted pause in which identity may be explored without penalty: the gap-year, the apprenticeship, the permitted doubt. Moratoriums are distributed unequally — by class, gender, culture and economy — and where none is granted, identity forecloses early or confuses long. In the room: the structural sentence behind "why has she not decided?" — because nothing around her can afford the question.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Identity_statuses}
Marcia's crossing of exploration and commitment: diffusion (neither), foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploration without commitment), achievement (both). Statuses are positions within domains, not traits of persons: the same adolescent may be achieved in friendship, foreclosed in vocation and in moratorium on faith. In the room: a map fine-grained enough to counsel with.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Minority_stress}
The chronic excess stress carried by members of a stigmatised identity — enacted, anticipated and internalised — over and above ordinary stress; the mechanism beneath the elevated distress of discriminated-against adolescents, and the reason their distress is not evidence of identity pathology but of social conduct. In the room: the sentence that relocates the treatment from the child to the environment.
:::

### Lecture I — Erikson's task and its social precondition

**Proposition 1. Identity is assembled, not discovered.** The materials are the child's identifications (the mother's voice, the father's trade, the teacher's standard), the adolescent's new capacities (week 4's instrument), and the society's available roles; the work is integration into a self that can keep a promise across situations. Role confusion is not rebellion but incoherence: a self whose parts cannot be in one room.

**Proposition 2. The moratorium is a privilege with a price list.** Societies grant pauses unevenly: the apprentice boy, the university-bound girl, the novice in the church; and deny them to the child who must earn at thirteen, marry at fifteen, or care for siblings at twelve. Where no moratorium exists, foreclosure is not a failure of exploration but an adaptation to necessity — and the counsellor who pathologises foreclosure in a child with no pause has blamed the child for the economy.

**Proposition 3. Fidelity is the virtue, and fidelity is testable.** Erikson's strength of this stage is the capacity for loyalty — to people, to causes, to a self-description held across pressure. The adolescent's fierce loyalties (to the group, the faith, the friend, the team) are therefore not symptoms to moderate but the stage's product in formation; the work is widening the object of fidelity, not cooling the capacity.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Map status in three domains per adolescent — vocation, faith or meaning, belonging — and write the domain beside each status. "Foreclosed" alone is an adjective; "foreclosed in vocation, moratorium in faith, achieved in belonging" is a formulation.
:::

### Lecture II — The identities that carry weight

**Ethnic and racial identity** develops through Phinney's sequence — unexamined, search, achieved — and under discrimination it carries a protective function: a achieved ethnic identity buffers the internalising cost of enacted and anticipated stigma, which is why the work with a discriminated-against adolescent includes the identity's pride strand and not only the wound strand. The counsellor across a difference they do not share does not claim the experience; they claim the obligation to know its shape: what the child is called, by whom, at what hour, and which adult in the building knows.

**Religious identity** in communities of faith is often the first available moratorium — the permitted doubt inside instruction — and the adolescent's religious questioning is frequently the safest form their identity work can take. Counsel that treats religious doubt as risk, or as compliance depending on the counsellor's own position, has misread the domain; the clinical question is whether the doubt is explored (moratorium, healthy) or foreclosed in either direction by fear.

**Sexual orientation and gender identity** develop on diverse trajectories with no single sequence and no required disclosure; what the evidence supports firmly is the cost of rejection and the benefit of affirmation — family and school acceptance associate with markedly lower depression, self-harm and suicide risk among sexual- and gender-minority adolescents, and minority stress explains the elevated distress without recourse to any identity pathology. The counsellor's duties are ordered: safety first (who knows, and what happens to the child if others know), the child's own words and timetable for naming, accuracy about what care and community exist locally, and the family work that converts rejection into protection at the family's own pace — because the family is usually the child's only home.

**Digital identity** is not a separate identity but a new theatre for the same task: the curated self, the audience metrics, the permanence of the posted moment. The clinical grain is the gap between the curated self and the felt self — where the gap widens into shame, the theatre stops being rehearsal and becomes a mirror with a score (week 6).

::: safeguard
Identity disclosures carry safety geometry: a sexual or gender disclosure in a session creates an immediate question — who else knows, and what happens at home if the wrong person learns it first? The counsellor's confidentiality sentence (week 14) is stated before disclosures are likely, not after; and where the child's safety requires a family conversation, the child is in the room for it, in their own words, at a time they help choose — except in the one limit where life governs and the rule is said plainly.
:::

### Lecture III — Interviewing identity

The textbook domain list is a prompt, not a questionnaire. The interviewing discipline has four moves. **Open in the child's registers:** "who are you, when you are most yourself?" — and follow the answer's vocabulary (football, choir, the market stall, the game clan) rather than translating it into domains. **Locate the commitments:** "what would you defend, even against your friends?" — fidelity's objects appear quickly and they are the self's load-bearing walls. **Locate the explorations:** "what are you still deciding about, that nobody is allowed to rush?" — the moratorium's contents, and the place the counselling can protect. **Locate the confiscations:** "what have you had to stop being, or hide, to stay safe or stay loved?" — the identity work that is not crisis but casualty, and usually the true referral beneath the referral.

The record carries the child's own words for each of the four, because identity formulations written in professional vocabulary are identity formulations about a stranger.

### Worked example 5.1 — Ada's three domains

**Task.** Map Ada (14) in Marcia's statuses across vocation, meaning and belonging, from the semester's accumulating record, and name the governing strand.

**Solution.** *Belonging:* achieved until the senior-school move — choir, two named friends, a pew — and now under active loss: the choir left, one friend moved classes, the pew unchanged but "the looking" makes it hard to walk in; status slipping from achieved toward diffusion under attack, which is why the loss reads as bereavement rather than transition. *Meaning:* foreclosed-in-the-good-sense until this year (faith received and held), now quietly in moratorium — two questions in session about whether God "sees the looking too", which is exploration, not collapse, and is to be protected rather than answered. *Vocation:* diffusion, age-appropriate at fourteen, except that the household's economics make foreclosure likely within two years (trade apprenticeship assumed); the file notes the assumption as a future foreclosure to keep in view, not a present problem. Governing strand: belonging under attack, because its loss is recent, concrete and reachable — the choir's return on her terms (week 2's formulation) and one friendship deliberately re-watered; the meaning moratorium is protected by not being rushed; the vocation question is diarised for SS3. The falsifier: if the belonging work succeeds and the low mood persists at six weeks, the governing strand was internalising all along and week 7's assessment opens.

### Worked example 5.2 — Kelvin's fidelity, widened

**Task.** Kelvin's loyalties are to his peer set — the same set whose evenings carry his risk. Counsel without demanding disloyalty.

**Solution.** The set is not a mistake to remove but the stage's product in formation: fidelity itself, attached to the only people who have reliably chosen him. An intervention that demands betrayal of them demands betrayal of the capacity his adulthood will run on. The work widens the object rather than cooling the capacity: (i) name the fidelity aloud and honour it — "you don't leave people; that is the best thing about you, and it is going to matter at thirty"; (ii) add objects rather than subtract them — the auntie's household as a loyalty he already carries but has never named as such, the repeating class as a debt to his own fourteen-year-old self who passed; (iii) give the fidelity a test it can pass without the set: the code-message leaving time reframed not as obedience but as keeping a promise to the auntie — "she waits up; your message lets her sleep; that is loyalty, not reporting"; (iv) protect the set-relationship from counselling's gossip: nothing about individual members is discussed with the family, so his trust in the room does not become another betrayal. Review at eight weeks against one observable: messages sent, and one sentence from him about who else he is loyal to. The falsifier: if the set's evenings escalate to carrying (week 4's bag rule broken twice), the widening has failed and the conversation turns to exit with face — a route out that lets him leave without disloyalty, which exists in every set and must be found with him, not for him.

::: skills
**Practical 5 — the identity interview.** In pairs, conduct the four-move interview on a supplied adolescent transcript or a peer in role: most-self register, commitments, explorations, confiscations. Record four verbatim sentences. Then write the three-domain status map with domains named, and one sentence naming the governing strand. 300 words plus the four quotations. The mark is for the child's words surviving into the map.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** An identity-status interview record: your four verbatim quotations, the three-domain map, the governing strand with its falsifier, and 400 words on which moratorium this adolescent is and is not granted, by whom, and what your service can do about the denial.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Child's words verbatim in all four moves | 30% |
| Statuses mapped per domain, not globally | 25% |
| Governing strand argued with falsifier | 25% |
| Moratorium audit structural, not sentimental | 20% |

**Formative.** Write your own adolescent identity in one sentence of each move — and note which move was hardest to answer. That is the move your clients will hide.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Identity is the assembly of a self that can keep promises; its crisis requires a moratorium that societies grant unevenly, so foreclosure is often economics rather than failure; Marcia's statuses map it finely enough to counsel with; ethnic, religious, sexual, gendered and digital identities carry the weight and the risk, with minority stress relocating distress from the child to the environment and affirmation carrying the evidence; and the interview that reaches any of it runs in the child's registers — most-self, commitments, explorations, confiscations — with their words kept verbatim into the file.
:::
