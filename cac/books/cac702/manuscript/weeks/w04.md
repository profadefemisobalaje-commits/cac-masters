# Thinking in Adolescence: Formal Operations, Egocentrism and Decisions Under Heat {.unit num="4" week="4" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Describe** formal-operational thought in adolescence — hypothesis, abstraction, the ideal against the real — and its uneven arrival across domains and schooling.
2. **Explain** adolescent egocentrism's two constructions: the imaginary audience and the personal fable, and use each to read a clinical sentence.
3. **Distinguish** hot from cold cognition in a decision analysis, and locate the divergence point for one case.
4. **Counsel** with the idealism of formal operations rather than against it: the adolescent's newly discovered ideals as therapeutic material.
5. **Design** one decision-support arrangement that changes the heat rather than the lecture.
:::

### Where this week sits

The adolescent mind acquires a new instrument — the ability to think about thought, to hold the hypothetical, to compare what is with what could be — and the instrument has side-effects: an audience that is always watching, a fable of personal exception, and an idealism that finds every adult arrangement wanting. This week is the cognitive adolescence of the series: what changes in thinking, what the changes cost, and how the counsellor recruits the new instrument to the work instead of arguing with its side-effects.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=Formal_operations}
Thought about the hypothetical and the ideal: reasoning from possibilities rather than only from objects, holding variables in mind and testing them, and — decisively for practice — comparing the real against the possible, which is the root of adolescent critique of parents, schools, religions and selves. In the room: the reason the fifteen-year-old can dismantle your argument and cannot yet weigh it against tomorrow.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Imaginary_audience}
The construction that others are as preoccupied with the self as the self is: the audience that notices the shirt, the stumble, the silence. Not vanity but a cognitive over-extension of the new ability to model other minds — modelled, now, relentlessly, upon the self. In the room: the mechanism under the shame spiral, the refused assembly, the deleted photograph.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Personal_fable}
The construction of personal uniqueness and exception: my experience is unlike anyone's, and the ordinary consequences do not apply to me — the fable that funds both the dare and the solitude ("no one has ever felt this"). In the room: the sentence beneath "it won't happen to me" and beneath "no one understands", which are the same sentence in two weathers.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Hot-cold_divergence}
The gap between decision performance in cold conditions (alone, unhurried, unaroused) and hot conditions (peers, emotion, immediacy); in adolescence the gap widens sharply, and the divergence point — the moment the decision moved from cold to hot — is usually identifiable in the narrative and is where the intervention sits. In the room: the analytic question "at which sentence did the room get hot?"
:::

### Lecture I — The new instrument and its uneven arrival

**Proposition 1. Formal operations arrive as a capacity and mature as a practice.** The adolescent who can hold a hypothesis in a chemistry argument may reason concretely about their own future in the same hour; the capacity is domain-sensitive and schooling-sensitive, and it is exercised where a culture exercises it — in argument, in examination, in faith instruction, in market negotiation. The counsellor therefore tests the instrument in the domain of the problem, not in the abstract.

**Proposition 2. The ideal becomes a tool, and tools are dangerous.** With the possible in hand, the adolescent measures the real: the parent against the ideal parent, the school against the ideal school, the self against the ideal self. The critique that follows is not disrespect but development doing its work — and the self-measurement is the strand that, unmoderated, becomes the perfectionism and body-comparison of week 6.

**Proposition 3. Metacognition makes the self an object of study.** The adolescent can now think about their own thinking, which is the precondition of every reflective therapy — and the precondition of rumination, which is reflection without an exit. The same instrument that powers the therapeutic conversation powers the two-a-morning loop; the difference is structure, and structure is teachable.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Locate the instrument before using it: one hypothesis question in the domain of the problem ("if your belief about the party is true, what would we see on the video?"). If the adolescent can hold it, the therapy can be collaborative; if not, the work is enactment and experience first, and reflection later.
:::

### Lecture II — Two mirrors: audience and fable

Elkind's constructions explain a family of clinical sentences. **The imaginary audience** reads: "everyone saw my hands shake"; "I cannot go back to that class"; "the whole church was looking". The audience is a cognitive over-extension — the new mind-modelling turned upon the self — and it is treatable as a hypothesis rather than as a fact: who, exactly, was looking? what did they see? what did they do in the next minute? The deconstruction is Socratic and brief, because the audience rarely survives its own headcount.

**The personal fable** reads two ways. As exception: "that pregnancy talk is about other girls"; "the bike without lights is fine, I know that road"; the fable here is a hypothesis about base rates that can be tested with the adolescent's own data. As solitude: "no one has ever felt this"; the fable here is a grief about uniqueness, and it is met not by headcount but by witness — the sentence "I hear that it feels unlike anything anyone has felt; I am here, in it, with you" precedes any cognitive work, because a fable of solitude dismantled before it is witnessed becomes proof of itself.

The two mirrors share a root and a remedy: the new ability to model minds, over-applied to the self, corrected by **evidence about real minds** (the headcount) and by **one real mind staying present** (the witness). Counselling supplies both, in that order, in the weather the sentence arrives in.

### Lecture III — Decisions under heat: the divergence point

Adolescent decision research converges on a pattern the counsellor can use forensically: in cold conditions adolescents' reasoning approaches adults'; in hot conditions it diverges, and the divergence is greatest where peer evaluation is present. The clinical method is to locate the **divergence point** in the narrative — the sentence at which the decision left the cold: "then Tunde looked at me", "then she said my name in that voice", "then the group went quiet". Before the point, the adolescent's own reasoning is usually sound and quotable; after it, the audience and the arousal govern.

Three interventions follow, in order. **Reconstruct the cold:** have the adolescent narrate their own pre-point reasoning aloud, in their words, and record it — the file now contains proof of competence that the post-point disaster obscured, and the adolescent hears their own cold mind, which is the strongest anti-fable evidence available. **Arrange the heat:** identify the two or three situational variables that moved the decision (audience, substance, hour, phone in hand) and engineer them for the next equivalent situation — a leaving time, a peer pair, a phone rule, a route; the plan is written with the adolescent, because an arrangement imposed is an arrangement evaded. **Rehearse the hinge:** the single sentence the adolescent will say at the divergence point next time ("I'm leaving at ten"), rehearsed aloud twice in session, because a sentence rehearsed in the cold is retrievable in the heat and a sentence invented in the heat is not.

::: safeguard
Hot-cognition analysis must never become consent analysis in reverse: where an adolescent's "decision" occurred under coercion, exploitation or an adult's grooming, the heat was manufactured by another person and the child is a victim, not a decision-maker. The divergence-point method applied to grooming locates the adult's engineering — and the record must say so in those words, because the file may one day be the child's evidence.
:::

### Worked example 4.1 — The assembly refusal, headcounted

**Task.** Ada (14) refuses to attend morning assembly because "everyone will see me walk in late and look at my skin". Work the imaginary audience as hypothesis, in order.

**Solution.** Witness first: "Being looked at has become a kind of pain for you. I am not going to argue you out of it today." Then the headcount, as three testable questions rather than a challenge: (i) *Who, exactly, is everyone?* — the assembly has four hundred pupils; Ada names three who might look. (ii) *What would they see?* — a girl walking in late; Ada's own prediction includes "they will see my face shining", and the question "shining how, at eight in the morning, in that light?" begins the evidence work about the skin itself (week 6's strand joins here). (iii) *What do they do in the next minute?* — Ada's answer, after thought: "they look at the person singing at the front." The audience survives the headcount as three names and one minute, which is bearable; the four-hundred abstraction was not. The arrangement: entry by the side door with the late-book for two weeks (the heat reduced while the evidence accumulates), and one rehearsed hinge sentence for the day she walks in the main door: "I'm late; I'm here." The falsifier, dated: if the refusal generalises beyond assembly to classrooms within three weeks, the audience is not the governing mirror and the formulation turns to week 7's internalising strand.

### Worked example 4.2 — Kelvin's divergence point

**Task.** Reconstruct the phone-snatch narrative to the divergence point and write the arrangement plan with Kelvin.

**Solution.** Cold segment, in Kelvin's words: "I knew the plan was stupid from the start; I said I would only carry the bag." The competence is on the record. Divergence point, located at one sentence: "then the owner shouted and the boys looked at me to move." Audience plus arousal plus a role ("the one who moves") — the hot trio. Post-point, the reasoning function is absent from the narrative, which is the signature, not a moral verdict. The arrangement plan, written **with** him and signed: a leaving time from any gathering where the set assembles after dark (ten, with a code message to the auntie that costs him no face); a peer pair — one named cousin who walks his route; the bag rule stated as his own sentence ("I don't carry for anybody; my hands stay empty") rehearsed twice aloud; and the hinge for the shout-moment: "I'm not the one; I'm going." Consequences for the offence proceed separately and fairly, and the file holds both: the accountability and the divergence map. Review at eight weeks against one observable — hands empty, messages sent — and against the falsifier: an incident inside an arranged situation reopens the peer-set question of week 9.

::: skills
**Practical 4 — the divergence interview.** In pairs, one narrates a real adolescent decision from a supplied transcript while the other interviews for the divergence point using three questions only: what were you thinking just before? who was there, and what could they see? what changed in your body at that sentence? Record the point, the two situational variables, and one rehearsed hinge sentence. Swap. 250 words each; the mark is for locating the point at a sentence, not at a mood.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A decision case analysis of 900 words: for a supplied hot-cognition incident, reconstruct cold reasoning verbatim, locate the divergence point at a sentence, name the heat variables, and write the three-part plan (reconstruct, arrange, rehearse) with a dated falsifier — plus one paragraph on whether the heat was manufactured by another person, and what changes if it was.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Cold reasoning quoted, competence visible | 25% |
| Divergence point located at a sentence with evidence | 25% |
| Heat variables named; plan in three parts, rehearsed hinge included | 30% |
| Coercion question asked and answered | 20% |

**Formative.** Write your own adolescent personal fable in one sentence, and the witness sentence you needed at fifteen.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Formal operations hand the adolescent the hypothetical and the ideal, and with them the critique of everything including the self; egocentrism's two mirrors — the imaginary audience and the personal fable — are the new mind-modelling over-applied, corrected by headcount and by witness; and decisions diverge from the adolescent's own cold reasoning at an identifiable hot point, so the work is to reconstruct the cold, arrange the heat and rehearse the hinge — unless the heat was manufactured by another person, in which case the child is a victim and the file says so.
:::
