# Puberty I: Bodies, Timing and Their Consequences {.unit num="2" week="2" hours="4"}

::: objectives
By the end of this week you will be able to:

1. **Outline** the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and its feedback, and state why puberty's first changes are invisible and nocturnal.
2. **Describe** the sequence of pubertal changes in girls and boys, and use stage language carefully in files and with families.
3. **Explain** the secular trend in pubertal timing and its principal drivers, without overclaiming any single cause.
4. **Analyse** the psychological consequences of early and late maturation in each sex, in context: the same timing, different meanings in different schools and peer groups.
5. **Counsel** a family through an off-timing puberty — early, late, or discordant with gender identity — with accuracy and without shame.
:::

### Where this week sits

Adolescence begins in the body, and the body's timetable is the first thing a counsellor must know because it sets the stage for everything else: the child whose body races ahead of the peer group meets a different social world from the child whose body lags, and neither world is the one the child's age would suggest. This week is endocrinology for counsellors: enough mechanism to be accurate, enough context to be humane, and the timing literature that turns "early" and "late" from adjectives into formulations.

### Key concepts

::: {.keypoint title=HPG_axis}
Hypothalamus, pituitary, gonads (with the adrenals contributing the first androgens): a feedback system whose pulse pattern changes in sleep years before anything is visible, so that puberty is under way long before anyone can see it — and long before the child can name what is happening. In the room: the reason a nine-year-old's mood can be pubertal before any adult would use the word.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Secular_trend}
The century-scale fall in pubertal onset, steepest in the early twentieth century with nutrition and infection control, continuing more slowly since; drivers debated across nutrition, body fat, stress and endocrine-disrupting exposures, with no single cause established. In the room: the reason "she is too young for this" is often false, and the reason the gap of week 1 keeps widening at its early end.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Off-timing}
Maturing markedly earlier or later than one's peer group; the consequences run through the social meaning of the difference — early-maturing girls face sexualised attention they did not seek, early-maturing boys gain status that recruits them upward in age among peers, late-maturing boys lose size in a size-valuing peer culture, late-maturing girls are largely spared the early girl's sexualisation and largely silent about the relief. In the room: timing effects are context effects, and the context is where the work is.
:::

::: {.keypoint title=Puberty_and_gender}
For a child whose gender identity does not match the body puberty is building, pubertal changes are not development but injury; the clinical conversation includes the existence of pause-and-pathways care where law and service permit, and always includes the child's own words first. In the room: the week's hardest counselling, and the week's clearest duty to accuracy without shame.
:::

### Lecture I — The axis that wakes

![The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis with its feedback loop: GnRH pulsing first at night, gonadotrophins, gonadal and adrenal hormones, and the hormones reporting back to the brain. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/books/cac702/figures/fig-02-01.png){width=94%}

**Proposition 1. Puberty is a change in a conversation, not the arrival of a substance.** The hormones were always present; what changes is the hypothalamus's pulse pattern, released from childhood inhibition, first at night — which is why growth hormone and the early gonadotrophin pulses arrive in sleep, and why the first pubertal evidence a family sees is often a change in sleep and growth rather than in shape.

**Proposition 2. The sequence is orderly; the timing is not.** Breast development or testicular enlargement first, then pubic hair, then the growth spurt, then menarche or voice-change late in the sequence — the order holds, the ages scatter across several years, and menarche arrives after the peak of the height spurt, which is why the tall girl in SS1 may still be years from cycles and the short one may not. Files that record "puberty complete" on shape alone are files that will misdate everything downstream.

**Proposition 3. The adrenals start their own clock.** Adrenarche — the adrenal androgen rise with its body odour, skin and hair changes — runs on a separate schedule from the gonadal axis, and it is usually the first thing the household notices; the child who "smells like a person now" at eight is not precocious on the gonadal clock, and the reassurance is worth a sentence in the consultation.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Date puberty in the file by stage and informant, not by impression: what has been observed, by whom, since when — and where the child is a transgender or gender-questioning child, record that the staging conversation follows the child's lead and the safeguarding and medical pathways of week 13.
:::

### Lecture II — Timing and its meanings

The secular trend is real and uneven: onset fell dramatically as nutrition and infection control improved, and continues to fall more slowly, with obesity, early-life stress and some chemical exposures each carrying partial evidence and none carrying the whole effect. The honest sentence to a family: "bodies start earlier than they used to, for reasons science has not finished arguing about; your daughter is not abnormal, and she is not behind — she is on today's curve."

The off-timing literature is the counsellor's part. **Early-maturing girls** face attention from older peers and adults that sexualises a child; their elevated rates of internalising difficulty and of early substance use track that attention and the peer company it recruits, not the hormones alone — which is why the intervention is the environment's conduct, never the girl's clothing. **Early-maturing boys** gain stature and status, are recruited upward in age by peers and by coaches, and carry risks that look like privilege: older company, older substances, older offences. **Late-maturing boys** lose the size economy of the peer group and show elevated teasing and, in some samples, later-recovering self-esteem. **Late-maturing girls** report the least distress of the four groups, and the finding is a window into what early maturation actually costs: the relief of the late girl is the measure of the early girl's burden.

All four patterns are context-dependent: a school that groups by age and prizes size amplifies; a school that groups by skill and prizes work dampens; a household with an adult who notices and names protects. Timing sets the stage; the cast decides the play.

::: safeguard
Two safeguarding edges attach to pubertal timing. First, the sexualised attention to an early-maturing child is adult and peer conduct, and where it crosses into harassment or abuse the child is a victim and the record must say so in those words — no formulation of "early maturity" may drift into implying invitation. Second, where a family seeks to "protect" an early-maturing daughter by withdrawing her from school or hastening marriage, the child's right to education and to childhood governs, and the counsellor's duty is to the child with the family in the room, not to the family's fear.
:::

### Lecture III — Counselling the off-timing body

**The early girl.** Accuracy first: what is happening, in what order, over what months, so the body stops being a rumour. Conduct second: the family's and school's obligations named plainly — no adult comments on her body, no older-peer contact unsupervised, the uniform and sport questions solved with her rather than for her. Meaning third: the sentence that protects the self — "your body is early; your childhood is not over; nothing about this makes you older than you are" — repeated by every adult in the house, because she will hear the opposite daily.

**The late boy.** The work is smaller and real: the peer economy of size named as an economy rather than a verdict; one competence domain where he is visibly good and publicly so; and the forecast, honestly given, that the size gap closes and usually reverses — with the dates of his own father's or uncle's late growth if the family knows them, because a family story outperforms a statistic.

**The child whose gender and body disagree.** The conversation begins with the child's words and ends nowhere in a single session. Three duties: accuracy about what puberty will do if it proceeds, because the child deserves the map; accuracy about what care exists — pause-and-pathways where law and service permit, and the referral route with its waiting times, because false hope is a harm; and the immediate work that needs no pathway at all — the social affirmation the family can give now, the school's conduct, and the child's own naming of themselves, which is protective in the evidence and costs nothing but courage.

::: practice
**Clinical implication.** Rehearse the puberty conversation in your counselling language until it is plain: axis, sequence, timing, meaning, conduct. Families do not forget the consultation in which a professional named the body without shame; they forget everything else about that month.
:::

### Worked example 2.1 — Ada at nine-and-a-half

**Task.** Ada's development began at nine-and-a-half: breast development and odour, then pubic hair; menarche at eleven-and-a-half, in a school where most girls began at twelve-and-a-half or later. She is now fourteen and says she "hates being looked at". Formulate the timing strand.

**Solution.** On-timing by population norms, off-timing by peer group: the distinction that organises everything. Between nine-and-a-half and twelve she was the earliest body in her class for two and a half years — the precise window in which the early-maturing girl's risks concentrate: sexualised comment (an uncle's joke, recalled verbatim in session three), older-boy attention at the gate, and the choir's withdrawal when her voice and shape changed before her friends'. The maintaining transactions now: she dresses to disappear, which reads as sullenness and recruits correction; she sleeps late, which the household reads as laziness and recruits conflict; both are protections she cannot name without naming the looking. The formulation's timing strand therefore reads: early-relative-to-peers maturation with sexualised attention history; current maintenance by concealment strategies misread as conduct problems; intervention aimed at the conduct of the environment (a school conversation about gate conduct, with Ada's consent and without her exposure), the household's reading of her strategies (week 8's reframing), and one domain where her body is an instrument rather than an object — she chooses it, and the choir, if it returns, must return on her terms. Falsifier, dated: if the "hated looking" sentence broadens into global self-worth collapse or eating restriction (week 6, week 13), the timing strand is not the governing strand and the formulation reorders.

### Worked example 2.2 — Kelvin's late cousin, and the family story

**Task.** Chuka (Kelvin's friend, fifteen) is the smallest boy in SS1, teased as "pikin", and has begun refusing sport. Counsel in the register of this week.

**Solution.** Three moves. The economy named: "In your school, size is money in the boys' market. That is a fact about the market, not about you." The competence domain: he is the best goalkeeper the junior team had two years ago and left when the size teasing began; the return is engineered not by persuasion but by a role the team needs now — penalty practice specialist, a public competence that does not require winning a tackle. The forecast with a family story: his uncle, present at the conversation, tells his own fifteen-year-small, twenty-year-tall history in ninety seconds, with the dates; the boy's face changes at the dates, not at the adjectives. The record notes the peer-economy sentence, the role, the story, and the review date at term's end with one observable: whether he stays after practice twice. The safeguarding note: teasing that becomes organised humiliation is bullying with a power asymmetry, and the school's duty is engaged the week it is named — this week.

::: skills
**Practical 2 — the timing audit.** For one adolescent case (supplied or your own, anonymised): date pubertal onset and current stage with informant; locate the child against their peer group's timing, not the population's; list the social meanings their timing has carried in their school and household; and write one environmental intervention and one meaning intervention. 400 words. The mark is for the peer-group comparison, which most audits omit.
:::

::: assessment
**Assessed task.** A timing analysis of 900 words on a supplied case: stage and informant, population versus peer-group timing, the social meanings carried, the maintaining transactions, and two interventions (environment, meaning) each with a dated falsifier.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Stage dated with informant; population v. peer group distinguished | 30% |
| Social meanings specified, not assumed | 25% |
| Transactions maintaining the current difficulty | 25% |
| Interventions concrete, dated, falsifiable | 20% |

**Formative.** Write the puberty conversation's first three sentences in your counselling language, plain enough for a frightened eleven-year-old.
:::

::: summary
**Synthesis.** Puberty is a change in a feedback conversation that begins in sleep; its sequence is orderly and its timing is not; the secular trend lengthens adolescence at its early end; and off-timing consequences run through social meaning — the attention, the status, the teasing — so the counsellor dates the body accurately, locates it against the peer group rather than the population, aims at the environment's conduct and the child's meaning, and holds the gender-discordant child's map and hope with equal accuracy.
:::
