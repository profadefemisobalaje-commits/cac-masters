CITE: 1 Corinthians 15; Revelation 21:1–5.
NARRATION:
At the core stand four confessions, held across nearly all traditions. Resurrection: death is not the final word, and the body matters. Judgment: history is answerable, and justice is real. Renewal: not escape from creation but its healing — a new heaven and a new earth. And hope: the virtue that carries the other three through suffering. The creeds compress all of it into two lines: he will come to judge the living and the dead; I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come.

ANIM: Symbols draw themselves in hairline gold one by one; labels fade per symbol; names set beneath.

CUE: Beasts, lamps, trumpets, counts — symbol and vision, not timetable | Daniel | Revelation
CITE: Apocalyptic is a recognized literary genre; cf. standard introductions to Revelation and Daniel.
NARRATION:
Much of the material comes from apocalyptic literature — Daniel, Revelation — a genre that thinks in vision, symbol, and number. Beasts are empires; lamps are witnesses; trumpets are judgements breaking into history. Read as literature, these texts are precise and powerful. Read as a timetable, they become something their authors never intended. Revelation's twelve hundred and sixty days, its beasts and its counts, function like the props of prophecy: recognizable to first readers, resistant to our arithmetic.

NARRATION:
Four classic postures organize the reading. The preterist sees much fulfilled in the first centuries, amid Rome's crisis. The futurist sees much still ahead. The historicist reads the visions unfolding across the whole of history. The idealist reads them as perennial symbols of the struggle between good and evil. Most careful readers borrow from more than one.

CITE: Revelation 20:1–6; the three millennial views are standard in Protestant eschatology; Catholic and Orthodox traditions read the passage variously.
NARRATION:
Then there is the millennium of Revelation twenty. Premillennialists expect Christ's return before a literal thousand-year reign. Postmillennialists expect the gospel to leaven history toward a golden age before the end. Amillennialists read the thousand years as symbol of the present age between the resurrections. Three readings, all within orthodox bounds, all held by faithful Christians today. Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic readers often decline the threefold scheme altogether, reading the millennium within the Church's present life.

NARRATION:
Which is why chronological prediction keeps failing, and keeps being rebuked. Announced dates pass; movements fracture; confidence is spent. And against every chart stands a saying of Jesus himself: concerning that day or hour, no one knows — not the angels, not the Son, but the Father alone. Eschatology that predicts what Christ declined to know has left the text behind.

NARRATION:
The surprising insight: Christian eschatology is chiefly a doctrine of hope and of ethics — of how to live now, in light of an ending that is certain in fact and unknown in date.

So ask the question the tradition actually presses: if the end is sure but the date is not, what would you stop postponing?
