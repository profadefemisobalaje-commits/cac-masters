NARRATION:
The New Testament itself speaks in several registers. Redemption borrows the marketplace: a price, a release. Reconciliation borrows the family and the treaty: enmity ended, relationship restored. Justification borrows the court: a verdict of righteous. Sanctification borrows the temple: set apart, made holy. Adoption and glorification open toward belonging and completion. No single image exhausts the claim. Paul can move between these images within a single paragraph, which is why harmonizing them into one system has always been partly a choice.

NARRATION:
And how does the cross accomplish salvation? The classic answers differ in accent. Irenaeus spoke of recapitulation: humanity summed up and remade in Christ. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa of ransom. Anselm, in 1098, of satisfaction owed to God's honour. Abelard of moral influence moving the heart. The Reformers of substitution bearing penalty. The East, of theosis: God became human so that humans might share the divine life. Gustaf Aulén's twentieth-century label Christus Victor recovered the classic ransom motif for modern theology, reminding the West that the oldest answer was victory, not payment alone.

CITE: Council of Trent, Decree on Justification (1547); cf. Ephesians 2:8–10.
NARRATION:
The sharpest historical argument concerns grace, faith, and works. Protestant confession holds justification by grace through faith, with works as fruit rather than ground. Rome, at Trent, taught a grace that truly transforms, faith working through love. The East speaks of synergy — divine grace and human freedom cooperating toward deification. Different grammar, one shared insistence: grace goes first. Recent joint Lutheran–Catholic declarations show how much of the old quarrel was grammar after all — a hopeful footnote to four centuries.

Nor does this stay abstract. A theology of salvation becomes a way of living: sacraments that enact grace; almsgiving that treats wealth as stewardship; ascetic discipline that trains desire; liturgies that rehearse the story until it shapes the person. Doctrine, in this sense, is not a conclusion but a curriculum — and communities differ in the syllabus.

Holding these differences fairly is a moral as well as an intellectual task. Each tradition prefers its own self-description; caricature is the cheapest error in theology. When we say what Roman Catholics, Protestants, or Orthodox believe about salvation, the standard is simple: would a thoughtful member of that tradition recognize themselves in our sentence?

The surprising insight: salvation is not one flat doctrine but a single confession carried in many grammars — legal, commercial, familial, sacrificial, cosmic. The traditions argue about weighting, not about the centre.

So ask yourself: which metaphor do you reach for first when you say saved — and what does that choice reveal about what you fear, and hope for?
