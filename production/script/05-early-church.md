We tend to picture the early church as a single tidy institution: one order, one liturgy, one accent. The evidence resists the picture. What we actually find is a family of communities spread across cities and continents, sharing a confession and yet differing in practice, emphasis, and temper.

NARRATION:
Consider the great centres. Jerusalem, where the movement began among Jewish followers of Jesus. Antioch, where the name Christian is first recorded. Alexandria, with its scholarly temper and its great catechetical school. Rome, the imperial capital whose church grew early and large. And beside them, Carthage, Edessa, Ephesus — each with its own memory and its own mind. Each centre developed a temper: Alexandria scholarly and allegorical, Antioch attentive to history and the literal sense, Rome pastoral and administrative, Carthage rigorous and moral.

NARRATION:
And the movement travelled further, sooner, than popular memory allows. Egypt and North Africa were Christian intellectual heartlands by the second century. The Syriac-speaking world around Edessa produced a distinct literary and liturgical tradition. By the fourth century, the Ethiopian court had embraced the faith. Christianity was never only a European story; it was Mediterranean, African, and Asian from the start. Syriac Christianity sang its theology: Ephrem's hymns carried doctrine in metre, a pedagogical method the Greek schools never matched.

NARRATION:
Practice varied too. Some communities met in homes around a shared meal; others, as they grew, assembled in larger halls. Baptismal seasons, fasting customs, and liturgical languages differed by region. And the orders of bishop, presbyter, and deacon developed unevenly, visible in one city's letters before another's. Even Easter was computed differently, and the second-century quartodeciman dispute shows how deeply a calendar could divide communities who shared one Lord. Uniformity came slowly, and never completely.

NARRATION:
Nor was disagreement a later corruption of an originally seamless unity. Paul publicly opposed Peter at Antioch over table fellowship, and wrote about it. The Jerusalem council debated whether Gentile converts must keep the whole law. Later generations argued with Marcion, with Montanists, with those who denied Christ's true flesh. Argument is not the church's failure; it is the church thinking. Irenaeus catalogued the disagreements of his day precisely so readers could weigh them; heresiology is evidence of argument, not of silence.

Why does this matter? Because it corrects two errors at once. It forbids the nostalgia that imagines a flawless, uniform beginning. And it forbids the cynicism that reads every difference as mere power-play. What held these communities together was not identical practice but a shared allegiance — one Lord, one baptism, one Scripture read in many keys.
