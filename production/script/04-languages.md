CITE: Aramaic sections: Daniel 2:4b–7:28; Ezra 4:8–6:18, 7:12–26.
NARRATION:
The Old Testament is written chiefly in Biblical Hebrew, with passages in Aramaic — in Daniel and in Ezra — the diplomatic language of the ancient Near East. The New Testament is written in Koine Greek, the common speech of the eastern Mediterranean after Alexander's conquests. Not the Greek of philosophers' academies alone, but the Greek of markets, ports, and ordinary letters. Koine was nobody's mother tongue and everybody's second language — which is why the New Testament's Greek is plain, direct, and portable.

NARRATION:
Behind those languages stand two great cultural worlds. The Ancient Near East gives Scripture its covenant forms, its law codes, its temple and sacrifice imagery. The Greco-Roman world gives it cities and courts, patrons and households, citizenship and trial. A psalm and an epistle emerge from different air, and each breathes that air into its sentences.

CITE: Renderings compared across standard English translations; no single English word carries the full field.
NARRATION:
Translation, then, is not a copying of words but a carrying of worlds. The Hebrew hesed holds covenant loyalty and tender mercy at once; English splits it into steadfast love, lovingkindness, mercy. The Greek dikaiosyne straddles righteousness and justice. Ekklesia means assembly before it means church. Every translation is an act of judgement, and a good one shows its seams honestly. Jerome, translating into Latin, argued for sense-for-sense over word-for-word and was accused for it; every translator since has lived inside that argument.

ANIM: Shelf resolves; volumes tilt forward in sequence on the spoken beats; a gloss line appears beneath the shelf.

NARRATION:
And genre decides how a text asks to be read. Hebrew poetry speaks in parallel lines and image; law speaks in cases; wisdom in provocation and proverb; prophecy in oracle and lawsuit; gospel in narrated witness; epistle in occasional argument; apocalyptic in vision and symbol. To read a poem as a statute, or a vision as a timetable, is to misread before one begins. The Psalms' parallel lines are not repetition but reinforcement; Revelation's numbers are not arithmetic but architecture.

NARRATION:
Which brings us to the practical rule of this section: context is not optional. A word lifted from its sentence, a sentence from its paragraph, a paragraph from its book, and a book from its world, will say almost anything we wish. Restore the context, and the text regains its own voice — sometimes stranger, often deeper, always more honest.

NARRATION:
Here is the surprising insight: the Bible is not one book speaking in one voice, but a library — many genres, three languages, two great cultural worlds, and centuries between its first and last pages.

So the next time a verse is quoted to you, ask gently: which world is it standing in, and what did it mean there?
