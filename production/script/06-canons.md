CITE: The Septuagint preserves books not in the Hebrew canon (e.g. Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees).
NARRATION:
Ancient Judaism itself carried more than one scriptural shape. The Hebrew canon — the Tanakh — settled into the collection most familiar today. But the Greek Septuagint, used widely by Greek-speaking Jews and then by the earliest churches, preserved additional books: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and the Maccabees among them. The church inherited that wider shelf. Which shelf a community inherited often depended on which language it prayed in: Greek-speaking churches kept the wider Septuagint, while later Latin and vernacular debates reopened the question.

NARRATION:
Hence the living traditions differ. Protestant Bibles, following the Hebrew canon, count thirty-nine Old Testament books. Roman Catholic Bibles, following the Septuagint's wider shelf as received at Trent, count forty-six. Eastern Orthodox Bibles include those and more — 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151 — and the Ethiopian Orthodox canon is broader still. No single number serves all. Numbering conventions add further variety: some traditions divide Samuel, Kings, and the Maccabees differently, so totals quoted in the wild may differ by a book or two without any real disagreement.

ANIM: First term and definition reveal; second follows; a thin gold divider draws between.

CUE: Deuterocanonical — "second canon", received as Scripture by Catholic and Orthodox traditions | Apocrypha — the Reformers' term: valuable for reading, not for establishing doctrine

NARRATION:
The names themselves carry the disagreement. Catholic and Orthodox traditions call these books deuterocanonical — a second canon, yet truly Scripture — and the Council of Trent reaffirmed them in 1546. The Reformers, appealing to the Hebrew canon, printed them apart as Apocrypha: profitable for manners and history, but not for establishing doctrine. Same books, different authority. Luther's German Bible placed the deuterocanonical books between the testaments with a famous preface; the English Apocrypha section descends from that decision.

NARRATION:
And canon is not a footnote; it shapes doctrine and practice. A community that reads 2 Maccabees finds textual ground for praying for the dead; a community that does not, lacks it. A wider shelf supplies resonances for wisdom christology and for the communion of saints. What a church reads quietly governs what a church can say. Conversely, a narrower shelf sharpens other emphases elsewhere; canon shapes not only what is said but what is easy to say.
