They read the Scriptures they had inherited: the Jewish Scriptures, in Hebrew and in the ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint. The earliest Christians did not begin with a new book. They began inside an existing scriptural world, and they read those older texts — law, psalm, and prophet — as pointing toward the events they believed they had witnessed.

Alongside those Scriptures ran a living oral stream: teaching, preaching, testimony, and song. Letters were written to particular communities in crisis or in conflict, then read aloud, copied, and passed from one church to another. For its first decades, Christianity was carried substantially by voice and memory — by people, not by a bound volume.

NARRATION:
The writings we now call the New Testament appeared gradually. Paul's letters come first, from roughly the fifties of the first century. The Gospels follow: Mark commonly dated around sixty-five to seventy, Matthew and Luke a decade or two later, John toward the close of the century. Each was written for a real community, facing real problems, in a real city. None of these writers imagined they were composing Scripture in a technical sense: Paul signs letters, not chapters, and the Gospels carry no authors' names on their title pages until later generations supply them.

CITE: Athanasius, 39th Festal Letter (367 CE), first extant list matching the 27-book NT; Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) were regional councils.
NARRATION:
Recognition came later still, and it was contested. A shortened canon proposed by Marcion in the second century provoked sharp response. The Muratorian Fragment preserves an early, incomplete list. Only in the year 367 does Athanasius of Alexandria set down the twenty-seven books exactly as we have them, and regional councils at Hippo and Carthage later affirmed a settled list for their churches. Even then the edges stayed soft for a while: some communities continued to read books beyond the list in worship, and a few within it carried local doubts — Hebrews in the Latin West, Revelation in parts of the Greek East. Agreement was a convergence, not a decree.

NARRATION:
Notice the distinction, because it is the heart of this section: writing a text and recognizing it as authoritative Scripture are two different acts, separated by centuries. The texts were composed in the first century; the church's formal, widespread agreement about the list matured in the fourth century and continued to be discussed long after.

Here, then, is the surprising insight: early Christians lived, worshipped, taught, and debated for generations before there was a universally agreed collection of twenty-seven books. The popular image of a single meeting voting a Bible into existence is a myth, and a stubborn one.

So consider this: if the earliest believers flourished before the closed canon, what held them together? Keep that question with you.
