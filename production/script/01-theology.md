CITE: Greek etymology; theologia appears in classical Greek (e.g. Plato, Republic 379a) before Christian adoption.
NARRATION:
The word itself is older and more demanding than that casual use. It is built from two Greek terms: theos, meaning God, and logos, meaning word, reason, or a reasoned account of something. Greek writers used it long before Christians did; Christian teachers later took it up, and gave it a new centre of gravity. To do theology, then, is not merely to feel something about the divine. It is to attempt a reasoned account of God.

Theology is therefore disciplined inquiry. It asks what may be said about God, about human beings, and about the relationship between them, and it asks those questions with methods, with sources, and with standards of evidence. That is why it has been taught in schools and universities for many centuries — from cathedral schools to the great medieval universities — and why it has never been the private property of clergy alone.

NARRATION:
The field divides into recognizable disciplines. Biblical theology studies the Scriptures in their own settings. Systematic theology arranges doctrine into a coherent whole. Historical theology traces how belief developed across time. Philosophical theology examines claims about God through reason. Moral theology asks how we ought to live, and practical theology asks how belief is lived out in worship, ministry, and community. Six doors, one house. The divisions are porous, of course: a historian reads Scripture, and a biblical theologian makes systematic judgments.

NARRATION:
How, then, is a theological claim judged? Not by the volume of its assertion, but by its appeal to shared sources: the careful interpretation of Scripture, the weight of historical evidence, the coherence of reason, and the long traditions of interpretation that communities have tested across generations. A claim that quietly ignores any of these is weaker for the omission, however confidently it is delivered.

NARRATION:
There is, then, a real difference between a personal conviction and an academically defensible argument. Both matter, and neither should be scorned; a community lives by conviction. But only the second can be examined, challenged, and refined in public — and it is precisely that openness to examination that turns belief into scholarship, and scholarship into a gift the whole world may inspect.

NARRATION:
Here is the surprising insight of this section: theology is not merely an activity of clergy. It is an intellectual discipline with its own history, its own research questions, and its own competing interpretations, as vigorous and as contested as any field in the humanities.

So ask yourself: when you next hear a theological claim, will you know which discipline it comes from, and which sources it rests upon?
