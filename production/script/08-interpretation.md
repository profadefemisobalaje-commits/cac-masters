Anyone can quote a verse. It takes a moment, and it feels like an argument. But a quotation is only a witness summoned to court; what it actually testifies depends on the questions asked, the context restored, and the honesty of the one who cites it. Interpretation is the discipline of asking well.

CUE: Exegesis — drawing the meaning out of the text | Eisegesis — reading our meaning into it
CITE: (none)
NARRATION:
Two Greek directions name the whole discipline. Exegesis leads the meaning out of the text: what did this say, to whom, and why? Eisegesis carries our meaning in: the text becomes a mirror, and we admire our own reflection. Every reader does both at times; the discipline is noticing which direction one is travelling.

NARRATION:
Context, then, is layered: a sentence within a paragraph, a paragraph within a book, a book within a testament, a testament within a canon, and a canon within an ancient world. Medieval readers added their own map — literal, allegorical, moral, and anagogical senses — while modern scholarship brought historical and textual criticism. Different maps, one conviction: meaning sits in relation. The historical-critical method, matured in the nineteenth century, asked new questions of authorship and editing; its excesses were corrected, its gains retained.

CITE: Standard manuscript counts in New Testament textual criticism; major 4th-c. codices: Sinaiticus, Vaticanus.
NARRATION:
Beneath interpretation lies the text itself, and the text has a history. We possess no autographs; we possess something arguably better for scholarship — thousands of copies, more than five thousand eight hundred Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, including great fourth-century codices like Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. Textual criticism compares them, and finds most differences trivial: spelling, order, abbreviation. Where variants matter — the longer ending of Mark, the woman caught in adultery — modern translations say so in the footnotes; honesty is printed, not hidden.

NARRATION:
Consider a beloved line: I can do all things through him who strengthens me. Quoted alone, it sounds like a promise of unlimited success. Read in its paragraph, Paul is describing something sturdier and stranger: he has learned to be content in plenty and in need, in hunger and in fullness, and it is for that endurance — not for every ambition — that strength is promised. A second example: Jeremiah's plans for welfare and a hope were spoken to exiles facing seventy years in Babylon, not to individuals choosing careers.

ANIM: Vignette one appears and is struck; two; three; a concluding line fades in beneath.

CITE: (none)
NARRATION:
Proof-texting is the habit of summoning verses as tokens in an argument they never joined: a line torn from its page, two texts set in false opposition, a caption borrowed from elsewhere and pinned to a verse that never claimed it. The remedy is not suspicion of Scripture but patience with it — and with the community of readers across time.

NARRATION:
The surprising insight: interpretation is not opinion with a citation attached. It is a discipline with methods, languages, manuscripts, and a long memory of readers.

So before a verse confirms what you already believe, ask the humbling question: what did it mean before it meant what I want?
