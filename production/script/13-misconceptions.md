### SCENE 95 | 52:32.0 – 53:02.0 | SLIDE 095
TITLE: Misconception 1 — "Christian theology began only in Europe."
VISUAL: Claim card: the claim set large in ivory and struck through with a fine gold line; beneath, the correction in gold with a small map glyph of Jerusalem, Alexandria, Carthage, Edessa.
ANIM: Claim sets; gold line strikes; correction reveals beneath with map glyph.
MUSIC: Bed crisp; a soft strike accent on the correction.
CUE: Claim: theology began only in Europe | Evidence: Jerusalem, Alexandria, Carthage, Edessa precede Europe's schools | Takeaway: ask where an idea was born
CITE: Cf. Sections 5 and 11 of this programme.
NARRATION:
First: Christian theology began only in Europe. False. It began in Jerusalem and the eastern Mediterranean, and its earliest great minds wrote in Greek and Latin from Africa and Asia — Origen, Athanasius, Augustine, Tertullian. Europe received a tradition; it did not originate it. Takeaway: ask where an idea was born.

NARRATION:
Second: the Bible was written in English. It was written in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. Complete English Bibles arrive only in the 1380s with the Wycliffe circle, then Tyndale in the 1520s, then the King James Version in 1611 — latecomers, however beloved. Takeaway: every English Bible in your hand is a translation, and a good one says so.

CITE: Cf. Sections 6 and 8 of this programme.
NARRATION:
Third: every denomination interprets Scripture identically. They do not — differing canons, differing hermeneutics, differing weights given to tradition and teaching office. The texts are shared; the readings are families of reading. Takeaway: when someone says the Bible plainly says, ask gently which tradition's plainness is speaking.

NARRATION:
Fourth: the Council of Nicaea selected the books of the Bible. It did not. Nicaea in 325 addressed the Arian controversy and produced a creed. The canonical lists come from elsewhere and later — Athanasius in 367, regional councils at Hippo and Carthage in the 390s. Takeaway: keep creedal councils and canonical history distinct.

NARRATION:
Fifth: the early church never experienced theological disagreement. The New Testament itself records Paul opposing Peter to his face at Antioch, and a council at Jerusalem debating the terms of Gentile inclusion. Disagreement is original, not accidental. Takeaway: a community arguing well is not a community failing; it is a community thinking.

NARRATION:
Sixth: theology is irrelevant to daily life. Yet doctrine quietly governs practice: what a community calls justice at work, how it sits with suffering, how it handles money, what it dares to hope at a graveside. Beliefs have behaviour attached. Consider how differently communities schedule rest, give wealth, and bury their dead — theology wearing ordinary clothes. Takeaway: judge a theology partly by what it does on an ordinary Tuesday.

ANIM: Claim strikes; columns rise; base line draws beneath.

CUE: Claim: one identical doctrine of salvation | Evidence: Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox grammars differ in weight | Takeaway: one centre, several grammars
CITE: Cf. Section 9 of this programme.
NARRATION:
Eighth: all Christians understand salvation in exactly the same way. They share a centre and differ in grammar — justification, transformation, theosis, each tradition's weighted accent. Takeaway: unity of centre does not require uniformity of sentence. And each grammar produces a different pastoral tone: forensic assurance, sacramental transformation, or deifying ascent.
