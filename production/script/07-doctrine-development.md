ANIM: Centre sets; orbiting labels enter one per beat on slow arcs; teacher names fade beneath each.

CITE: Contested positions associated with Apollinarius, Nestorius, and Eutyches; cf. standard patristic histories.
NARRATION:
After Nicaea, the question turned to Christ himself. How can one person be fully God and fully human? Apollinarius, protecting divinity, seemed to diminish the human mind. Nestorius, protecting distinction, seemed to divide the person. Eutyches, protecting unity, seemed to dissolve the natures. Each pressed one true instinct past its breaking point. Leo's Tome to Flavian, read at Chalcedon, supplied much of the council's vocabulary; doctrine often arrives as a letter before it becomes a definition.

NARRATION:
The councils answered in sequence. Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381, Ephesus in 431, and then Chalcedon in 451, whose definition still governs most Christian speech: one person in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation. Four negatives, holding a mystery open rather than closing it — and two further councils refining what Chalcedon left contested.

CITE: Augustine, Confessions; De Trinitate. Artistic reconstruction, not a contemporary portrait.
NARRATION:
In the Latin West, one figure shaped a millennium: Augustine of Hippo. His Confessions taught Christianity to narrate the self before God; his De Trinitate thought the triune God through the mirror of the mind; his polemics set the terms of the grace debates that would return again and again. To read Western theology is, largely, to read Augustine's descendants. His debate with Pelagius fixed the West's instinct that grace precedes merit — an instinct the Reformers would later claim as their own recovery.

ANIM: Vault line-drawing draws upward; lectern resolves; names fade in sequence.

CUE: Anselm — faith seeking understanding | Abelard — sic et non, the method of objections | Aquinas — Summa Theologiae
CITE: Anselm, Proslogion; Abelard, Sic et Non; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae (13th c.).
NARRATION:
Then came the schools. Anselm defined theology as faith seeking understanding. Abelard set contrary authorities side by side and taught the age to weigh them. And Thomas Aquinas raised the Summa Theologiae, a cathedral of argument in which objection answers objection until something clear remains. Medieval scholasticism was not the enemy of inquiry; it was inquiry institutionalized. The scholastic question-form — objection, sed contra, response, replies — trained Europe to hold disagreement inside a single page, a habit modern academia inherited.

ANIM: Field one resolves; dissolve to two; dissolve to three; labels set per field.

CITE: Council of Trent, 1545–1563.
NARRATION:
In 1517 the Reformation broke the West's unity: Scripture alone, faith alone, and a church reformed around the Word. Rome answered with the Council of Trent, defining and reforming at once. The East, neither Protestant nor Tridentine, continued its conciliar and liturgical path. One medieval inheritance, three enduring families of answer. And the Reformation was not one movement but several — Lutheran, Reformed, Radical — whose differences over sacrament and church order persist today.

NARRATION:
The surprising insight: doctrine's language grew, often in crisis, to guard what communities believed they had already received. That is development, not invention — a vocabulary sharpening around a confessed centre.

So ask yourself: which council do you imagine invented its doctrine, when in fact it was defending one?
