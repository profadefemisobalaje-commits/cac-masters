TITLE: Augustine and the African Councils
VISUAL: A council table in warm light with a bishop seated among elders; labels Hippo 393 and Carthage 397; Augustine's name beside.
ANIM: Table resolves; date labels set in sequence; name fades beneath.
MUSIC: Bed ceremonial but restrained.
CUE: Hippo 393 · Carthage 397 | Regional councils, listing the canon including the deuterocanonical books | Augustine's consent — and his insistence on degrees of authority
CITE: Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397); Augustine, On Christian Doctrine 2.8–12.
NARRATION:
In North Africa, councils at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397 list the canon — including the deuterocanonical books — for their churches. Augustine consents, and yet distinguishes: some books carry canonical authority; others, however venerable, do not bind the faith equally. Regional councils, note: not universal. Their lists travelled by prestige and by use, not by imperial command. Augustine's degrees of authority would echo through medieval exegesis for a thousand years, shaping how scholars quoted books they loved but did not call canonical.

ANIM: Left field resolves; seam draws; right field resolves.
MUSIC: Bed with two alternating colours.
CUE: East — Revelation's reception hesitant for centuries | West — Hebrews doubted until the fourth century | Doubt ran in both directions, on different books
CITE: Eastern hesitation on Revelation (cf. Eusebius's categories); Western hesitation on Hebrews before the 4th c.
NARRATION:
Doubt ran in both directions, on different books. In the Greek East, Revelation's visions made reception hesitant for centuries; some churches never read it in liturgy. In the Latin West, Hebrews — anonymous, and wielded against the reconciliation of the lapsed — waited until the fourth century for general acceptance. A canon with doubts recorded honestly is stronger, not weaker, than one pretending none existed. Both doubts were eventually resolved by usage rather than by any single ruling: what churches kept reading, they kept, and the keeping became the answer.

NARRATION:
Seven books settled last: Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, the two shorter Johannine letters, Jude, and Revelation — the antilegomena, the spoken-against. None was hidden; all were debated in the open, and all were received at length by consensus across the major churches. When a modern reader questions James or Revelation, they are not vandalizing the canon; they are re-opening a file the church itself kept open for centuries. Their late settlement is recorded, not concealed; a canon that documents its own arguments is sturdier than one that claims never to have had any.

### SCENE 24 | 14:10.0 – 14:48.0 | SLIDE 024
TITLE: Canon and Rule of Faith
VISUAL: A two-ring diagram: CANON (the list) and RULE OF FAITH (the baptismal confession) overlapping, with the intersection labelled interpretation governed by confession.
ANIM: Rings draw; intersection glows; label fades.
MUSIC: Bed contemplative.
CUE: The list and the confession grew together | Neither governs alone | Interpretation sits in the intersection
CITE: Rule of faith cf. Irenaeus and Tertullian; creedal summaries precede canonical lists.
NARRATION:
Here is a relationship popular accounts miss: canon and rule of faith grew together. The list says which books measure; the baptismal confession says what the church already believes when it reads them. Neither governs alone. Scripture corrects the confession; the confession guards the reading. Interpretation lives in their intersection — which is why communities with the same list can still read it differently. Where the rule hardened into rigid formula, reading froze; where the text was loosened entirely from the rule, reading drifted; the intersection is the discipline.

NARRATION:
The Old Testament question returned with force in the sixteenth century. Trent reaffirmed the deuterocanonical books as canonical; Reformation confessions appealed to the Hebrew canon; Orthodoxy kept the wider Greek shelf in use. Three reasoned positions, one shared inheritance. Understanding them is not ecumenical diplomacy; it is simply accurate description of how each shelf came to look as it does. The debate also produced prefaces, confessions, and catechisms — a whole literature of canon, which is often more instructive than the lists themselves.

ANIM: Aisle resolves in slow push-in; list fades in beside the lectern.
MUSIC: Bed with a chant-coloured pad.
CUE: What a church reads aloud, week by week, is what a church treats as Scripture | Lectionary use preceded and shaped formal lists
CITE: Liturgical reading as a canonical criterion; cf. Eusebius's "usage" and early lectionary evidence.
NARRATION:
Consider, too, the least glamorous criterion: the lectern. What a church reads aloud, week by week, in worship, is what a church treats as Scripture. Lectionary use preceded formal lists and shaped them; a book maintained in public reading acquired an authority no decree could manufacture and no decree could easily remove. The canon was settled in cathedrals and house churches as much as in councils. A book dropped from the lectern rarely survived as Scripture, whatever any list might say; public reading was the canon's daily, unsparing examination.

TITLE: What Canon Does Not Mean
VISUAL: A three-strike card: canon does not mean inspiration began at listing; does not mean an index of value; does not mean the end of interpretation. Each struck through with a fine gold line.
ANIM: Lines appear and are struck in sequence; a concluding line fades beneath.
MUSIC: Bed tightens then releases.
CUE: Not the start of inspiration | Not an index of value | Not the end of interpretation | Canon authorizes a library for a community's rule
CITE: (none)
NARRATION:
So clear away three confusions. Canon does not mean inspiration began at listing; communities recognized what they believed already breathed. It is not an index of value; excluded books were often respected, even beloved. And it is not the end of interpretation; the list authorizes a library for a community's rule, and the reading of it begins again every generation. Confusing these three confusions is how both fundamentalism and cynicism are manufactured: one worships the list as an event, the other dismisses it as a plot.

VISUAL: A question card with two scholarly notes beneath: Luther's prefaces questioning James and Jude within an accepted canon; modern canonical criticism studying how the list functions. A hold-open line at the foot.
ANIM: Question sets; notes fade in sequence; foot line draws.
MUSIC: Bed inquisitive; open cadence.
CUE: Luther praised James less, within an accepted canon | Canonical criticism asks what the list does, not only how it formed | Closed for rule; open for study
CITE: Luther's German prefaces; modern canonical-criticism literature.
NARRATION:
Is the canon closed? For rule, yes: no community adds books today. For study, emphatically open. Luther, within an accepted canon, still wrote prefaces that prized James less than Romans. Modern canonical criticism asks what the list does to reading, not only how it formed. A closed rule and an open inquiry are not contradictions; they are the discipline and its object. The list functions like a constitution: stable in its text, living in its interpretation, and strongest when both facts are held together without anxiety.

### SCENE 32 | 19:14.0 – 19:52.0 | SLIDE 032
TITLE: Misconception: Nicaea Chose the Books
VISUAL: Claim card struck through; beneath, the correction in two lines with dates; a small footnote on the myth's modern popularity.
ANIM: Claim sets and is struck; correction reveals; footnote fades small.
MUSIC: Bed crisp; strike accent.
CUE: Claim: the Council of Nicaea (325) selected the Bible's books | Correction: Nicaea addressed the Arian controversy; canonical lists run through Athanasius 367 and regional councils of the 390s | The myth is modern, not ancient
CITE: Nicaea 325; Athanasius 367; Hippo 393; Carthage 397.
NARRATION:
First misconception: Nicaea chose the books. It did not. The council of 325 confronted Arius and produced a creed; its canons concern church order, not a table of contents. The canonical lists run elsewhere and later: Athanasius in 367, Hippo and Carthage in the 390s. The myth is modern — a fiction's convenience — and it dies on contact with the documents. No ancient source anywhere attributes a biblical table of contents to Nicaea; the myth's ancestors are modern novels, not ancient minutes.

Second misconception, in two mirrored forms. That early Christians had no Scriptures at all — false: they had the Jewish Scriptures, in Hebrew and Greek, from the first. Three habits, practically. Read each book's own occasion first — the crisis, the city, the congregation. These habits cost nothing and return everything: occasions recovered, traditions understood, and a confession held in living conversation with its Scripture.
