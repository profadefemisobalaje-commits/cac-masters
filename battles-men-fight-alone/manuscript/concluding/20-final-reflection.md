# Final Reflection: You Are More Than the Battles You Fight {.front}

Every book has a sentence that it was written to say, and this book's sentence is in its title page and its last line: a man is not the sum of what he has survived.

The chapters have described the battles with a specificity that was necessary, because a man's difficulties are frequently conducted in generalities and the generalities are the mechanism by which they are not addressed. The emptiness behind the confident smile, the depression behind the composed face, the fear of failure that governs the decisions of a competent man, the marriage that is conducted as an arrangement of parallel responsibilities, the grief that returns as irritability in the eighth year, the position that cannot be stated to anybody, the call that is not made, the help that is not asked for. The book has named them because naming is the first instrument, and because a man who can name his position has already begun to conduct it.

The reflection that follows is not a summary. It is a set of seven statements about what the reader is, in relation to what he has carried, and each statement is a correction of a conclusion that men in difficulty frequently draw.

## The First Statement: Your Battles Are Not Your Identity

A man who has survived a great deal frequently organises his account of himself around the survival. He is the man who lost the business and rebuilt. He is the man whose father left. He is the man who carried the family through the years nobody remembers. He is the man whose marriage failed, whose son does not speak to him, whose health changed at forty-eight.

The account has a function, and the function is the reason it is maintained: it supplies a standing, an explanation, and a claim. Its cost is the one described in Chapter 44.4: the man whose difficulty has become his identity has an interest in the difficulty's continuance, and his present conduct is conducted as a footnote to his past.

The correction is not the erasure of the past. It is the subordination of the past to the present. The man is not the difficulty; he is the party who is currently conducting a position, and the position includes what he survived and is not limited to it. His competence, his relationships, his work, his obligations, his capacity to be useful, and his conduct in the present week are not derived from the difficulty. They are his, and they are larger than the account he has been keeping.

## The Second Statement: Your Silence Was a Strategy, and Strategies Can Be Changed

The book has been careful not to describe men's silence as a defect of character. It is a strategy, acquired in identifiable circumstances, for identifiable reasons, and it worked in the sense that it produced endurance, professional survival, and the protection of households that could not have conducted the material at the time.

A strategy can be changed, and the change is not a repudiation of the man who adopted it. The man who conducted his position alone at thirty-two, in a household with two young children and no reserve, was conducting the only arrangement available to him. The man of fifty-two, with a different position, different parties, and different instruments, is not required to conduct the same arrangement in memory of the first.

The book's finding is that men frequently hold the strategy out of loyalty to the man who needed it. The loyalty is understandable and it is misplaced: the earlier man's conduct is honoured by the use of what he learned, and not by the repetition of what he had no alternative but to do.

## The Third Statement: Your Strength Is Larger Than Your Endurance

The culture has supplied a definition in which a man's strength is computed by what he can bear. The definition is measurable in one dimension and it excludes everything that makes a man useful: his judgement, his competence, his presence, his capacity to repair, his generosity from a position of loss, his conduct of the parties who depend on him, and his capacity to state an accurate position and to be corrected in it.

This book has argued that endurance is one competence among several, and that the men who conduct the others are frequently stronger in the sense that matters to the people around them. The man who cannot state a difficulty and can carry an enormous weight is a man whose household is well provided and poorly known. The man who states the difficulty, uses the instrument, and carries the weight is a man whose household is provided and known, and the second arrangement is the one that survives the weight's eventual reduction.

## The Fourth Statement: Your Limits Are Not Your Verdict

The reconciliation of Chapter 45.3 is the hardest of the book's practices, because it requires the man to accept a boundary without accepting a conclusion.

A man who cannot reach a particular level in his profession has not been judged. A man whose body has failed in a specific respect has not been reduced. A man whose marriage has ended is not a man who cannot be loved. A man whose son will not speak to him has not been evaluated by the silence. Each of these is a limitation, and a limitation is an item, and the conversion of an item into a verdict is the operation that the culture performs on men and that men perform on themselves.

The reflection's statement is the one the book has supplied: the reconciliation relocates the standard, and it does not reduce it. The man who accepts what he cannot do retains every obligation of Chapter 41.6 and releases the requirement of a capacity that is not his.

## The Fifth Statement: Your Losses Are Real, and They Are Not the Whole Account

The book has insisted on the two ledgers, and the insistence is repeated here because it is the point at which both the culture's optimism and the man's despair are corrected at once.

What you lost is real: the years, the money, the relationship, the health, the standing, the person who died, the child who was not present for, the possibility that closed. Nothing that you learn afterwards repays those losses, and a man who is told that they were worth it has been told something false by a party who wishes to close a conversation.

What the losses supplied is also real: the information, the competence, the identification of your actual parties, the reduction of a fear, the capacity to conduct a position you did not know you could conduct. Those supplies are available, they are not automatic, and their extraction is the work of Chapter 44.

You are more than the battles you fight, and you are also more than the battles you have fought. You are the man who is conducting a position in the present, with the material the past supplied and the parties the present makes available.

## The Sixth Statement: Your Obligations Are Not Your Isolation

This is the book's central sentence in its reflective form.

The obligations are real and they are not to be reduced: the household that requires provision, the children who require protection and an accurate example, the work that requires competence, the parties who require your accountability, the community that requires your contribution, and the men who will be in your position in ten years and require the instrument.

The isolation is not a requirement of any of them. It was a requirement of a particular arrangement, in a particular economy, under a particular script, and the arrangement has been examined in this book and found to be separable from the obligations it claimed to serve.

A man may carry every obligation he holds and state his position to the parties who have a right to know it. A man may be composed in the settings that require composure and state his states to the parties who are entitled to them. A man may be accountable for his household and increase its capacity rather than reduce it. A man may endure, and he may not endure alone.

## The Seventh Statement: Your Next Chapter Is Conducted, Not Declared

The book has ended with a programme rather than a resolution, and the reflection ends in the same place for the same reason.

A declaration is conducted in a moment and produces a feeling. A conduct is conducted over a period and produces an arrangement. The reader who closes this book with a resolution to be different will conduct the resolution for a period that the case material puts at between three days and six weeks, and will then conduct the arrangement he had before, with the addition of a private sense of failure.

The reader who closes the book with four tables, one call, one date, and one party who will ask about the date will conduct an arrangement, and the arrangement will survive the failure of the feeling. That is the whole of the book's practical claim, and it is stated here in the plainest form available: **the change is not in what you have decided. It is in what has been scheduled, stated, and recorded.**

## A Closing Word

The book that ends here has described many men, and none of them was a hero and none of them was a case. They were men conducting positions that were frequently harder than their households knew, with instruments that were frequently fewer than the position required, under a definition of manhood that had supplied them with a standard and had removed the means of conducting it.

The reader is one of them, or is close to one, or will be.

The book's last counsel is not that the battles end. They do not, and a book that promised otherwise would be conducting the myth that Chapter 44.1 examined. The counsel is that the battles are conducted differently: with an accurate position instead of an account, with parties instead of solitude, with instruments instead of endurance alone, with a limit accepted rather than denied, with a loss conducted rather than buried, with a record written rather than a claim maintained, and with a standard that a man can hold without being destroyed by it.

That is what the title of this book has meant throughout, and it is what it means here at the end. The battle may be yours to fight. It was never yours to fight alone.
