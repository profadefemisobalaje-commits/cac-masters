# Epilogue: To Every Man Fighting a Battle Nobody Knows About {.front}

This book has been written in the third person for forty-five chapters, and it ends in the second. The change of address is deliberate. What follows is not an argument and it is not a summary. It is a letter to the men whose positions this book has described, and it is written to be read by a man who has recognised himself and has not yet decided what to do about it.

The letter has seven parts, and each part is addressed to a man in a particular position. A reader may find that one of the seven is his and that the other six are not. He should read the one that is his twice, and he should read the others once, because the men in the other six are the men he works with, sits beside, employs, fathers, or will become.

## To the Exhausted Man

You are tired in a way that sleep does not address, and you have been tired for longer than you will admit.

You have explained it to yourself in the available terms: the workload, the economy, the season, the age, the household's demands, the number of people who depend on you. Some of those explanations are true. None of them is complete, and you know it, because you have slept for nine hours and woken as tired as you went to bed, and because the tiredness is not in your body alone. It is in your capacity to be interested. It is in the length of time you can look at a problem before you have to look away. It is in the flatness with which you receive news that should have moved you.

The exhaustion you are carrying is frequently the exhaustion of a vigilance rather than of a labour. It is the cost of conducting a position whose accurate statement nobody holds: the arrangements you keep in your head, the parties who must not meet, the questions you have learned to answer in a particular way, the two accounts you are maintaining at once. That kind of tiredness does not resolve with rest, because rest does not reduce the vigilance. Only a change in the arrangement does.

The first thing to be said to you is that the exhaustion is information. It is not weakness, it is not ingratitude, and it is not a failure of character. It is your position reporting its own cost, in the only currency available to it.

The second thing is that you have been treating the information as an accusation. This is the mechanism this book has described more often than any other: the signal arrives, and the man's response is not to read it but to explain why he should not be receiving it. He works harder. He sleeps less. He reduces the occasions at which he might be asked how he is. He converts the exhaustion into a private failure, and the private failure into a further effort, and the further effort into more exhaustion.

The third thing is the instruction, and it is small. Before you address the exhaustion, address the one condition that produces it and that you can alter this week. Identify the party who does not hold your accurate position. Write the sentence. Name the day. The tiredness will not disappear on the day you speak, and the vigilance will reduce, and the reduction is the beginning.

## To the Struggling Husband

You are married to a woman who does not know what you are carrying, and you have a reason for that which you believe to be sound.

The reason is generally that you do not wish to add to her load, and the reason is frequently sincere. It is also frequently inaccurate, because she is already carrying something and what she is carrying is the uncertainty. She has noticed the change in your manner. She has noticed the telephone calls taken outside the room, the sleep that does not restore you, the silence at the evening meal, the questions you answer with a sentence that closes the subject. She has been conducting a position that is missing its most important fact, and she has been doing so without the information that would allow her to conduct it.

This book has described the accounting that men conduct in this position, and it has recorded its finding without softening it: the concealment is generally more expensive to the marriage than the fact would have been. The fact is a difficulty and difficulties can be conducted. The concealment is a difficulty with a second difficulty inside it, which is the discovery that the marriage's most consequential information was being held by one party.

What you are owed in this position is not blame. It is the accurate statement that this book has described in Chapter 42 and in Chapter 45: the position, at its true level, stated to the party who has a right to know it, followed by the household's participation in the arrangements that the position requires.

The letter's advice to you is specific and it is the same advice the book has given twice. Do not begin with the largest item. Begin with the fact that there is a position you have not stated. Then state the first item. Then ask her what she requires in order to conduct it with you. She may be angry, and the anger is frequently the response to the concealment rather than to the fact, and it is frequently survivable. She may be relieved, because a known difficulty is frequently easier to conduct than an unknown change. She may have information of her own that you do not have, which the case material of this book records more frequently than men expect.

## To the Father Carrying Invisible Burdens

You are carrying the household's position, and your children believe that the position is secure.

This is the most consequential of the seven positions, and it is the one men most frequently defend. The defence is stated in the language of protection: they are young, they should not be worried, they have their studies, they will have their own difficulties soon enough. The defence is sincere and it is frequently mistaken, for a reason this book has set out at length in Chapter 43.

Your children are not learning about difficulty from your words. They are learning from your conduct, and what your conduct is teaching them is the specific lesson that a difficulty is a thing a man conducts alone and does not state to the people he loves. You did not intend to teach that lesson. You are teaching it in the silence at the evening meal, in the telephone call taken outside the room, in the tiredness that is explained as work, in the manner in which your household conducts its difficulties and does not name them.

The alternative is not the disclosure of everything. It is the proportionate statement, which this book has described: one true sentence, accurate, brief, directed at what the child can receive, and followed by your own action. *The business is having a difficult year and I am conducting it. I have been worried about it. This is what we are doing, and this is what it means for the household, and you are not required to solve it.*

That sentence will do more for your son's adult capacity than a decade of protection, and it will do it in the same week, because what he is watching for is not reassurance. He is watching to see what a man does with what he carries. Give him an accurate answer and he will have one for the rest of his life.

## To the Man Experiencing Financial Hardship

You are in a difficulty that the culture treats as a verdict on your character, and you have been conducting it as though the verdict were correct.

The material of this book has been precise about the mechanism. The difficulty is a position with figures, obligations, arrangements, and a route, and it is capable of being conducted. The shame is a separate item, and it is frequently larger than the difficulty, and it is the item that prevents the difficulty from being conducted: the appointment not made, the arrangement not sought, the creditor not called, the party not told, the facility concealed until it becomes an emergency.

You are not your position. The book has said this in Chapter 41, in Chapter 44, and in Chapter 45, and it says it here in the plainest form available: a man's worth is not computed by the balance in his account, and the computation that says otherwise was installed in him by a culture that measures men by their results and by an economy that has no interest in his estimate of himself.

The practical instruction is the one the material supplies. State the position in figures, to yourself, this week. Then state it to one party. Then engage the professional route: the debt adviser, the accountant, the insolvency practitioner, the lawyer where guarantees or family property are involved. Then conduct the reductions in the order of Chapter 42.6, from the items nobody sees to the items everybody sees. The route exists, the case material's finding is that it is more available than the man in the difficulty believes, and the difficulty is conducted faster by a man who has stated it than by a man who is still protecting his account.

## To the Man Grieving in Silence

You have lost somebody, or something, and you have not been able to conduct the loss in front of anybody.

The loss may be a death, and it may be the end of a marriage, the loss of a child's regard, the end of a position that was the centre of your life, the failure of a body that was your instrument, or the closing of a possibility you had been carrying for thirty years. This book has treated all of these as losses, because the mechanism is the same: something that was part of the man's life has ended, and the ending requires a conduct that the man's arrangements do not supply.

The silence has a cost that this book has described and that you will recognise. The unprocessed loss does not disappear. It returns in forms that the man does not connect to the loss: the irritability in the eighth year, the flatness that he cannot explain, the drinking that increases by a bottle, the work that expands to fill the hours in which the loss would otherwise be felt, the numbness that he describes as being strong.

The instruction to you is the one the material supplies and it is not a demand for a display. The loss requires an audience of one. The audience may be a wife, a brother, a friend, a professional, a man who has conducted the same loss, or a group of men who have conducted theirs. The audience's task is not to resolve the loss and it is not to explain it. It is to be present while it is stated, which is the function that every culture has supplied through its gatherings and that many men have learned to conduct without.

You are permitted to grieve. The permission is not a concession to weakness and it is not a departure from your obligations. It is the conduct that allows the obligations to be carried without the cost that the case material of this book records in men who conducted the loss in silence for twenty years.

## To the Man Who Feels Forgotten

You have contributed, and nobody has recorded it, and the absence of the record has become the fact of your life.

You built the thing that others are now managing. You carried the household through the years that nobody remembers. You paid for the education, you attended the funeral, you kept the arrangement together, you did the work that made the achievement possible for a party whose name is on it. And the account of these years does not contain you, or contains you as a supporting figure, or contains an attribution that is not accurate.

This book has described the injury in Chapter 44.6 and it has stated that the injury is frequently real. What you have lost is not merely recognition. It is the record, and the record is the thing by which a man's life is conducted in the estimate of the parties around him.

The material's finding is not that the record will be corrected. It frequently is not, and the man who waits for the correction conducts his remaining years as a demand, which is the bitterness the book has described. The finding is that the man's conduct is not the record's hostage: the contribution was conducted, it produced what it produced, and its value is not computed by the account that others keep.

What remains available to you is the record you conduct yourself: the statement of what you did, in writing, for the parties who will need it; the transmission of what you learned to the men who are in the position you were in; and the construction of a contribution whose record you hold, in a setting where your office is small and your usefulness is visible. The case material records men who conducted this and reported a result that they had not expected: the work became valuable to them again, not because it had been recognised, but because it had been used.

## A Final Reflection on Dignity, Connection, and Hope

The seven positions are different and they share one structure, and the structure is the book's subject.

In every one of them the man is carrying something real. In every one of them the carrying has been conducted alone. In every one of them the isolation has been mistaken for a requirement of the carrying, and it has not been a requirement: it has been a habit, an inheritance, a script, a defence, and a response to a world that taught him, in thousands of small transactions, that a difficulty stated is a standing reduced.

The book has argued that the isolation is separable from the responsibility, and it has supplied the material by which the separation is conducted: the position stated, the parties named, the instruments identified, the limits reconciled, the loss conducted, the record written, the transmission made, and the date fixed.

It has argued, further, that the separation is worth conducting, and it has supplied the evidence for the argument in the case material rather than in reassurance: men who stated their positions and were not destroyed by it; marriages that became more difficult and more honest in the same year; households that conducted a reduction and were more capable afterwards; boys who learned from their fathers' statements rather than from their fathers' silences; men in their fifties who constructed parties they had not had in their thirties; men who were owed an acknowledgement that never came and who conducted their remaining years without it.

The book's final claim is about dignity, and it is stated here as the epilogue's closing sentence.

**Dignity is not the maintenance of an appearance under pressure. It is the accurate conduct of a real position by a man who knows what he is carrying, states it to the parties who have a right to know it, uses the instruments that the position requires, and remains accountable for what is his.**

That dignity is available to every man addressed in this letter. It does not require a different temperament, a larger income, a repaired marriage, a restored position, a corrected record, or a faith. It requires the acts this book has described, and the acts are small, and the first of them can be conducted this week.

You have carried a great deal, and you have carried it alone, and you have done it for reasons that were frequently good. The book's whole argument is contained in the sentence it has repeated since its first page, and it is repeated here for the last time, as an instruction rather than a description:

**You do not have to fight alone.**
