# References and Bibliography {.back}

## A Note on Sources and Their Use

This book is a work of synthesis and practical guidance written for the general reader, and it is not a monograph in the social sciences. Its use of the research literature is governed by four rules.

**The first rule is that no study, statistic, or quotation is invented.** Where the book refers to research findings, it refers to bodies of work that are established in the published literature. Where the book reports a figure, the figure is drawn from the case material of the book or from a widely reported and stable published source. The book does not report precise statistics that a reader could not verify, and it does not attribute specific sentences to named researchers unless the sentence is a matter of established record.

**The second rule is that findings are stated at the level of generality that the evidence supports.** The book therefore says *the research literature on emotion regulation distinguishes suppression from reappraisal* rather than citing a particular coefficient, and it says *the case material of this book records* when the observation is drawn from the composite cases rather than from published research.

**The third rule is that where the literature is contested, the book says so.** This applies in particular to post-traumatic growth, to the stage theories of grief, to the measurement of masculinity, and to the claims made for resilience training.

**The fourth rule is that the bibliography below is a guide to the literature and not a list of works cited by page.** The reader who requires a specific citation for a specific claim will find it in the works below, which are the principal sources for the material in the corresponding chapters.

## The Principal Bodies of Work Used in This Book

### On Masculinity, Gender Norms, and Men's Health

The book draws on the research tradition that treats masculinity as a set of socially constructed norms rather than as a fixed essence, and on the empirical literature that has examined the relationship between conformity to those norms and health outcomes.

The principal contributions to this tradition include the work of Joseph Pleck on the male sex role and on gender role strain; the work of James O'Neil and colleagues on masculine mystique and on gender role conflict; the work of Ronald Levant on masculine norms and on alexithymia in men; the work of R. W. Connell on hegemonic masculinity and on the social construction of masculinities; the work of Michael Kimmel on the history and sociology of American manhood; the work of Will Courtenay on men's health and on the social construction of masculinity as a barrier to health behaviour; and the work of James Mahalik and colleagues on the measurement of conformity to masculine norms.

The literature on men's help-seeking includes the work of Michael Addis and James Mahalik on men's reluctance to seek help; the work of David Vogel and colleagues on self-stigma and on the disclosure of distress; and the substantial body of research on the gender gap in mental health service use.

### On Emotional Development, Expression, and Regulation

The book draws on the developmental literature on emotional socialisation, on the adult literature on emotion regulation, and on the research on emotional suppression and its consequences.

Principal contributions include the work of James Gross on emotion regulation and on the distinction between antecedent-focused and response-focused strategies; the work of James Pennebaker on disclosure, expressive writing, and the consequences of concealment; the work of John Gottman and colleagues on emotion coaching in families and on the role of parental meta-emotion philosophy; the work of Nancy Eisenberg and colleagues on the socialisation of emotion and on children's regulation; and the work of Paul Ekman on the expression of emotion.

On the consequences of concealment, the book draws on the literature on disclosure and health, on the research on the costs of secrecy, and on the work of Sidney Jourard on self-disclosure, in its historical context.

### On Depression, Anxiety, and Burnout

The book's recognition material draws on the standard clinical descriptions of the depressive and anxiety disorders, particularly the diagnostic classifications maintained by the World Health Organization and by the American Psychiatric Association, and on the research on the presentation of depression in men.

Principal contributions include the work of Aaron Beck on the cognitive model of depression and anxiety; the work of Martin Seligman on learned helplessness and on explanatory style; the work of Susan Nolen-Hoeksema on rumination; the work of Christina Maslach and colleagues on burnout and its dimensions; the work of Wilmar Schaufeli and colleagues on the measurement of burnout; and the work of Ronald Kessler and colleagues on the epidemiology of mental disorders and on service use.

On the treatment literature, the book reflects the consistent finding that structured psychological therapies are effective for the depressive and anxiety conditions, that medication is effective for a substantial proportion of cases, and that the combination is frequently superior to either alone for moderate and severe presentations.

### On Grief and Loss

The book draws on the research that has revised the stage theories of grief, on the literature on continuing bonds, and on the work on the difference between ordinary grief and the grief that requires treatment.

Principal contributions include the work of Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, whose stages were formulated for the dying and have been widely and inaccurately applied to the bereaved; the work of Colin Murray Parkes on bereavement and its course; the work of Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut on the dual process model of coping with bereavement; the work of George Bonanno on the trajectories of grief and on resilience in bereavement; the work of Robert Neimeyer on meaning reconstruction; the work of Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman on continuing bonds; and the work of Mardi Horowitz and colleagues on complicated grief and its treatment.

### On Trauma and Recovery

The book draws on the mainstream literature on psychological trauma, on the recognition of post-traumatic stress, and on the routes to treatment.

Principal contributions include the work of Judith Herman on trauma and recovery; the work of Bessel van der Kolk on the body's response to trauma; the work of Edna Foa and colleagues on prolonged exposure and on the treatment of post-traumatic stress; and the work of Anke Ehlers and David Clark on the cognitive model of post-traumatic stress.

On post-traumatic growth, the book draws on the work of Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, and it reflects the critical literature that has examined the measurement and the limits of self-reported growth.

### On Marriage, Relationships, and Repair

The book draws on the observational research on couple interaction and on the literature on attachment in adult relationships.

Principal contributions include the work of John Gottman and colleagues on marital interaction, on the four behaviours associated with relationship deterioration, and on repair; the work of Sue Johnson on emotionally focused therapy and on attachment in couple relationships; the work of Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson on acceptance in couple therapy; and the work of Judith Wallerstein and colleagues on the long-term effects of divorce.

On betrayal and repair, the book reflects the clinical literature on infidelity and on the conditions under which repair is possible and the period it requires.

### On Fatherhood and Family Transmission

The book draws on the research on father involvement, on the transmission of patterns between generations, and on the development of boys.

Principal contributions include the work of Michael Lamb on the role of the father in child development; the work of Ross Parke on fatherhood; the work of Paul Amato on family structure and children's outcomes; and the literature on intergenerational transmission of parenting and of emotional patterns.

### On Financial Stress, Work, and Material Pressure

The book draws on the research on financial stress and health, on household debt, and on the relationship between economic pressure and family functioning.

Principal contributions include the work of Glen Elder on economic hardship and family process; the work of Rand Conger and colleagues on the family stress model; the research on debt and mental health; and the practice literature on debt advice and on personal insolvency.

### On Identity, Values, and Purpose

The book draws on the literature on identity development, on values clarification, and on meaning and purpose.

Principal contributions include the work of Erik Erikson on the stages of psychosocial development; the work of James Marcia on identity status; the work of Dan McAdams on narrative identity; the work of Shalom Schwartz on the structure of values; and the work of Michael Steger and colleagues on meaning in life and its measurement.

### On Wisdom, Adversity, and Bitterness

The book draws on the psychological literature on wisdom as a measurable construct, on the research on adversity and its outcomes, and on the literature on forgiveness and resentment.

Principal contributions include the work of Paul Baltes and colleagues on wisdom and on the Berlin paradigm; the work of Robert Sternberg on the balance theory of wisdom; the work of Monika Ardelt on wisdom as a personality characteristic; the work of George Vaillant on adaptation and on the longitudinal study of adult development; and the work of Everett Worthington on forgiveness and its distinction from reconciliation.

### On Behaviour Change, Habits, and Practice

The book's exercise material draws on the literature on habit formation, on implementation intentions, and on the conditions under which a practice is established.

Principal contributions include the work of Peter Gollwitzer on implementation intentions; the work of Wendy Wood on habit and context; the work of Charles Duhigg and of James Clear, whose popular treatments synthesise the research for the general reader; and the literature on self-monitoring, which is the mechanism underlying the book's insistence on written records.

### On Men's Groups, Peer Support, and Community Arrangements

The book draws on the practice literature on men's groups, on peer support, and on community-based mental health arrangements, including the evidence on the effectiveness of peer support and on the conditions under which community programmes are sustained.

## Official and Institutional Sources

The reader who requires authoritative descriptions of the clinical conditions, of the diagnostic criteria, or of the treatment guidance should consult the following, in their current editions.

- The World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases, in its current revision, and its guidance on mental health in primary care.
- The American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, in its current edition, with the caution that the manual is a classification and not a description of a person.
- The clinical guidance issued by the national health authority of the reader's own country, which frequently supplies free and locally appropriate guidance for the depressive and anxiety conditions.
- The World Health Organization's guidance on suicide prevention and on the organisation of mental health services, which includes material on the design of community arrangements.
- The publications of the International Labour Organization on occupational stress and on the management of psychosocial risks at work.

## The Case Material

The cases in this book are composite and illustrative. They are constructed from the author's professional experience and from the patterns recorded in the published literature, and they do not describe identifiable persons. Where a case includes figures, dates, or institutional details, those details have been altered and combined for the purposes of illustration. No case is presented as a clinical record, and none should be read as a prediction of the course of any particular person's position.

## A Note to the Reader Who Requires a Specific Source

A reader who requires a citation for a specific claim is invited to consult the works named above, which are the sources for the corresponding chapters. Where a claim cannot be traced to the published literature, it is a claim about the case material of this book, and it is stated as such in the text.

The book's preference throughout has been to describe a mechanism accurately and at the level of generality that the evidence supports, rather than to supply a citation for every sentence. This is a choice about the book's genre, and the reader who requires the higher density of citation will find it in the works listed above rather than in this book.
