# Glossary of Psychological and Personal Development Terms {.back}

This glossary defines the terms as they are used in this book. Where a term has a wider clinical meaning, the entry states the book's usage and notes the difference. The definitions are written for the general reader and are not a substitute for a professional's assessment.

## A

**Acceptance (of support).** In this book, an arrangement in which a party supplies an instrument that the man uses, while the man retains the decision, the responsibility, and the capacity. Distinguished from dependence, in which a party conducts a function the man cannot conduct.

**Accountability.** The condition of answering for a position and its consequences. Distinguished in Chapter 41 from authority over persons, which reduces the capacity of the parties concerned.

**Adaptation.** A conduct that has developed in response to a circumstance and that continues after the circumstance has changed. Much of what this book describes as a man's silence is an adaptation that was functional in its original setting.

**Addiction.** A pattern of use that is compulsive, continues despite significant harm, is accompanied by tolerance or withdrawal, and cannot be reduced by an act of will alone. See Appendix M.

**Affect.** The observable expression of an emotional state. Distinguished from mood, which is the prevailing state over a period.

**Alexithymia.** A difficulty in identifying and describing one's own emotional states. It is a dimensional characteristic rather than a diagnosis, and it is associated in the research literature with emotional suppression and with certain physical conditions.

**All-or-nothing thinking.** A cognitive pattern in which experience is interpreted in two categories with nothing between them. See Appendix H.

**Anger.** An emotional state arising from a perceived boundary violation, threat, or obstruction. In this book, treated as a signal requiring conduct rather than as a defect requiring suppression.

**Anxiety.** A state of apprehension and physiological arousal disproportionate to the actual threat, or persisting in its absence. Where it interferes with functioning over a period, it is a clinical condition. See Appendix B.

**Assertiveness.** The capacity to state one's position, needs, and limits in a form that is direct, specific, and respectful of the other party. See Appendix O.

**Attachment.** The enduring emotional bond between a person and a significant other, and the internal model of relationships that develops from early experience. The concept is used in this book in its mainstream sense and without technical elaboration.

**Attribution.** The assignment of cause to an event or a conduct. In Chapter 44, the structured division of an event between the man's own contribution, other parties, circumstance, and chance.

**Automatic thought.** The rapid, habitual interpretation that precedes deliberate reasoning. See Appendix H.

## B

**Belonging.** In this book, the condition in which parties hold a man's accurate position, their estimate of him is not conditional on a favourable appearance, and he has obligations to them that he conducts. See Chapter 45.4.

**Boundary.** A statement of what a man will conduct and will not conduct, made in terms of his own conduct. See Appendix O.

**Burnout.** A syndrome arising from chronic, unmanaged occupational stress, characterised by exhaustion, cynicism or detachment, and a reduced sense of efficacy. Distinguished from depression by its domain specificity. See Appendix B.

## C

**Catastrophising.** A cognitive pattern in which the interpretation proceeds to the worst available outcome and treats it as the likely one. See Appendix H.

**Cognitive restructuring.** The structured examination and replacement of inaccurate automatic thinking. See Appendix H.

**Composure.** In this book, the regulation of one's states and their appropriate conduct in the setting. Distinguished from the absence of an interior life, which the book describes as concealment.

**Concealment.** The withholding of information from a party who has a right to know it. Distinguished from privacy, which is a man's entitlement to conduct his own affairs without reporting them.

**Condition (as against limitation).** In Chapter 45.3, a circumstance that could be altered by effort, and is therefore addressed by the material of Parts III and XI, rather than a limitation, which is structural and not within the man's power to remove.

**Coping.** The conduct by which a person manages a demand that exceeds his current capacity. Distinguished in Chapter 43.5 from suppression, distraction, and achievement, each of which manages the state's visibility rather than the state.

**Crisis.** A period in which a person's ordinary arrangements are insufficient to conduct a demand and in which the risk of harm is elevated. See Appendix Q.

## D

**Defence mechanism.** An automatic mental operation that reduces the awareness of a state or a fact that would be difficult to hold. The term is used in its general sense and without commitment to any particular theoretical school.

**Dependence.** An arrangement in which a party conducts a function that the man cannot conduct, so that the party's withdrawal leaves him without it. Distinguished from acceptance. See Chapter 45.2.

**Depression.** A condition characterised by persistent low mood or loss of interest, accompanied by changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, and self-regard, present for at least two weeks and interfering with functioning. In men, frequently presenting as irritability, increased activity, or physical complaints. See Appendix B.

**Disclosure.** The statement of a position or a state to a party. In this book, distinguished from exposure: the disclosure is conducted by the man, at his timing, with his arrangements; the exposure is not.

**Distress tolerance.** The capacity to remain in the presence of an uncomfortable state without acting to remove it immediately.

**Dual awareness.** The capacity to hold two true but apparently contradictory propositions at once. Identified in Chapter 44.3 as a marker of emotional maturity.

## E

**Emotional competence.** In this book, the four capacities of Chapter 39.4: knowing what one feels, regulating it, stating it to a party who has a right to know, and conducting oneself appropriately in the setting.

**Emotional labour.** The work of managing one's own emotional expression in order to meet the requirements of a role or a setting. The concept is used in its mainstream sociological sense.

**Emotional literacy.** The vocabulary and the capacity to identify, name, and discuss emotional states.

**Emotional regulation.** The processes by which a person influences which states he has, when he has them, and how he experiences and expresses them.

**Empathy.** The capacity to understand another's state from their frame of reference. Distinguished from sympathy, which is a response to the state rather than an understanding of it.

**Endurance.** The capacity to continue under difficulty. In this book, a genuine competence, distinguished from endurance conducted in silence at the cost of health, marriage, and the capacity to ask.

**Exposure (as against disclosure).** The involuntary or uncontrolled revelation of a position, frequently with the loss of the man's control over its timing, its parties, and its framing. See Chapter 42.2.

## G

**Gender norms.** The socially shared expectations attached to being a man or a woman in a particular setting. Distinguished in Chapter 43.4 from roles, which are the expectations attached to a position and are necessary.

**Grief.** The response to a loss, conducted over time and frequently arriving in waves. Distinguished in Chapter 44.6 from bitterness, which is a settled account of an injury that has not been acknowledged.

**Grievance.** In Chapter 44, a wrong that another party has imposed and that another party could remove, and which is therefore addressed by a claim or an arrangement rather than by a reconciliation with limitation.

**Guilt.** A state arising from the evaluation of one's own conduct as wrong. Distinguished from shame, which concerns the self rather than the conduct.

## H

**Help-seeking.** The conduct of requesting an instrument from a party who can supply it. Treated in this book as a competence rather than as a confession of inadequacy.

**Hope.** In Chapter 45.6, the conduct of a man's position in the belief that the conduct is worth undertaking, independently of the outcome's probability. Distinguished from optimism, which is a prediction.

**Humiliation.** The state arising from the public reduction of one's standing. Distinguished from shame, which does not require an audience.

## I

**Identity.** The account a person holds of who he is. In this book, distinguished from role and position, which are the functions and standings a person occupies.

**Instrument (as against rescue).** A specific support that the man uses while retaining the decision and the responsibility. See Chapter 45.2.

**Isolation.** The condition of conducting a position without parties who hold it. Distinguished from solitude, which is a chosen and restoring condition.

## L

**Learned helplessness.** The reduction in effort and engagement that follows repeated experience of uncontrollability. The concept is used in its mainstream sense and with the qualification that the research literature has considerably refined it.

**Limitation.** In Chapter 45.3, a boundary on what a man can do, be, or become, which is not within his power to remove and is not a matter of effort.

**Loneliness.** The distress arising from a perceived gap between the relationships a person has and the relationships he requires. Distinguished from isolation, which is a condition of arrangements, and from solitude, which is chosen.

## M

**Masculine norms.** The culturally shared standards of manhood that the research literature has operationalised in several ways, commonly including self-reliance, emotional control, primacy of work, dominance, risk-taking, and the avoidance of anything perceived as feminine.

**Mind reading.** A cognitive pattern in which a person supplies other parties' opinions without evidence. See Appendix H.

**Modelling.** The acquisition of conduct through the observation of a significant party. See Chapter 43.1.

## N

**Narrative.** The account a person constructs of his history. In Chapter 44.4, distinguished from the instrument: the narrative describes the event, the instrument is what the man can now do.

**Negotiation (of an obligation).** The approach to a creditor, an institution, or a party before a payment is missed, in order to establish an arrangement. See Appendix P.

## P

**Panic attack.** A discrete period of intense fear or discomfort with prominent physical symptoms. It is a clinical presentation and is treated in Appendix B and Appendix Q.

**Perspective.** In Chapter 44.3, the capacity to locate an event in its actual size, exercised through the tests of time, audience, alternative, and the whole.

**Position.** In this book, the whole of what a man is carrying and conducting: his money, work, marriage, household, health, and standing. The term is deliberately wider than *problem* and narrower than *life*.

**Post-traumatic growth.** The reported positive change following adversity. The research literature is treated in this book with caution: self-reported growth is not always corroborated, and growth does not reduce the cost of the event.

**Pretence.** In Chapter 42, a state of affairs in which the man's position can continue only for as long as specified parties do not learn specified facts. Distinguished from privacy and from the ordinary management of presentation.

**Provision.** The conduct by which a household has what it requires. Distinguished in Chapter 41 from the exclusivity and anonymity that the culture frequently attaches to it.

## R

**Reflection (as against rumination).** Reflection is the examination of an experience in order to extract information and alter conduct. Rumination is the repetitive rehearsal of an experience without alteration. The distinction is developed in Chapter 44.2.

**Regulation.** See emotional regulation.

**Repair.** The conduct by which a man acknowledges an act, states its effect, and restores what can be restored. Identified in Chapters 39 and 43 as the most valuable practice in a household.

**Resilience.** The capacity to maintain or restore functioning under difficulty. In this book, treated as a set of arrangements and practices rather than as a trait, and distinguished from endurance, which does not necessarily include restoration.

**Rescue.** The conduct of a man's position by another party, which removes the man's exercise of the capacity and therefore reduces it. Distinguished from support. See Chapter 44.5.

**Rumination.** See reflection.

## S

**Self-care.** The ordinary arrangements by which a person maintains his health, sleep, activity, and relationships. In this book, treated as a component of the position rather than as an indulgence.

**Self-reliance.** The competence of conducting one's own position. In Chapters 45.1, a competence with a domain, which becomes a liability where the domain is expanded beyond what can be conducted alone.

**Shame.** A state concerning the self as deficient, exposed, or unacceptable. Distinguished from guilt, which concerns a conduct. Identified in this book as the principal engine of men's concealment.

**Silence (strategic).** The deliberate withholding of a position in order to protect a standing, a household, or a party. In this book, treated as an adaptation with a cost.

**Stigma.** The social devaluation attached to a characteristic, a condition, or a conduct. In men's mental health, the principal barrier identified in the research literature to help-seeking.

**Stress.** The response of a system to a demand exceeding its current capacity, resolving when the demand resolves or the capacity increases. Distinguished from anxiety, depression, and burnout in Appendix B.

**Suppression.** The deliberate or automatic inhibition of the expression of a state. Distinguished from regulation, which conducts the state rather than removing it from view.

## T

**Test of proportion.** In Chapter 44.3, the four questions by which an event's actual size is established: the test of time, of the audience, of the alternative, and of the whole.

**Transmission.** In Chapter 43, the process by which a household, a school, a workplace, an association, or a peer group supplies a boy with his conduct in the presence of emotion. The book's argument is that the transmission can be altered.

**Trigger.** A stimulus that initiates a state or a pattern. In this book, used in its ordinary sense and not as a judgement on the party or the setting concerned.

## V

**Values.** The principles a person holds to be worth conducting, independently of their immediate reward. See Appendix I.

**Vigilance.** In Chapter 42, the continuous attention required to maintain a pretence, and the specific cost that is paid before anything is discovered.

**Vulnerability.** The condition of being open to harm, and in the literature of relationships, the capacity to state one's position without guarantee of the response. In this book, distinguished from disclosure, which is a conduct rather than a state.

## W

**Wellbeing.** The condition of functioning adequately across the domains of health, relationships, work, and meaning. Used in this book in its ordinary sense.

**Withdrawal.** The reduction of contact, statement, or participation, frequently conducted in order to avoid a state or a question. See Chapter 39.3.

**Working definition (of manhood).** In Chapter 41.6, a definition stated in six clauses, each of which retains an obligation and removes an addition. See Chapter 45.5.

**Worry.** The cognitive activity of anticipating a negative outcome. Distinguished from anxiety, which is the state in which the worry is accompanied by physiological arousal and functional interference.
