# Personal Boundaries and Assertiveness Worksheets {.appendix letter="O"}

This appendix supplies the material of Chapters 20, 33, and 45 in practical form. It addresses the man who is competent, reliable, and unable to decline, and whose position is consequently conducted for other parties at his own expense.

## The Position

Three findings govern the appendix.

**The first is that a boundary is not a refusal of relationship.** A boundary is a statement of what a man will conduct and what he will not, and its purpose is the continuation of the relationship at a level he can sustain. The finding is that men frequently decline boundaries because they believe the statement will end the relationship, and that the relationships which end at a boundary were frequently being conducted at the man's expense.

**The second is that the man's inability to decline is frequently the material of Chapter 41.2's clause of provision and self-sufficiency.** The man's value is computed by his usefulness, and a refusal is experienced as a reduction in his value. The appendix therefore addresses the definition as well as the behaviour.

**The third is that assertiveness is a competence and is trained.** The exercises below are rehearsed in low stakes and conducted in high stakes, in the order supplied by Chapter 43.5's four-stage method.

### Exercise 3: The Belief

Record the belief that prevents the refusal, in the man's own words. The common forms are: *if I do not help, nobody will*; *it would be selfish*; *he has nobody else*; *I cannot be seen to refuse*; *I am the only one who can do it*; *my father would have done it*.

## Part Two: The Statements

### Exercise 4: The Four Forms

The four forms below are rehearsed, and each is written out in the man's own words before it is spoken.

**The direct refusal.** *I am not able to do that.* The refusal requires no justification, and the finding is that the justification is what invites the negotiation.

**The refusal with the alternative.** *I cannot do that. I can do [specific alternative] if that would help.*

**The boundary statement.** *I will not [specific]. I will [specific].* Its form is stated in terms of the man's own conduct, not in terms of the other party's character.

**The limit on the existing arrangement.** *I have been conducting [the arrangement] for [period]. I am reducing it to [specific] from [date].*

### Exercise 5: The Rehearsal

Each of the four forms is rehearsed three times with a party, in low stakes, before it is conducted. The rehearsal is conducted aloud, and the finding is that the capacity to state the form aloud is not transferable from reading it.

## Part Three: The Responses

The finding is that the man's difficulty is frequently not the refusal and the response to it. Four responses are recorded, with their conduct.

**The first response is the party's disappointment.** The conduct is the acknowledgement without the reversal: *I understand that this is inconvenient for you, and I am not able to do it.*

**The second response is the party's pressure.** The conduct is the repetition of the statement, unchanged, in the same words. The finding is that the repetition is more effective than the explanation, and that the explanation supplies the material for the negotiation.

**The third response is the party's accusation.** The conduct is the refusal to defend the man's character: *I am not going to argue about what kind of person I am. I am telling you what I can do.*

**The fourth response is the man's own guilt.** The conduct is the material of Appendix H, applied to the guilt's specific statement, together with the record of what the boundary produced over the following month.

## Part Four: The Boundaries That Require a Conversation

Some boundaries cannot be conducted in a single statement and require the conversation of Appendix E, Exercise 5. These are the boundaries in a marriage, in a household, in a partnership, and in a long professional relationship.

### Exercise 7: The Conversation

Prepare the six lines, then conduct the conversation.

| Line | The content |
|:----------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1. The subject | The arrangement that requires alteration, stated in one sentence. |
| 2. The fact | What has been conducted, with the dates and the amounts or the hours. |
| 3. The effect | What it has cost the man, stated as his own experience. |
| 4. The acknowledgement | What the man's own contribution has been: the requests accepted, the silences, the expectations he created. |
| 5. The request | What he requires, specifically, and from what date. |
| 6. The limit | What he will conduct if the request is not met, stated as his own conduct and not as a threat. |

### Exercise 8: The Record of the Arrangement

Where the boundary concerns money, time, or an obligation to the extended family, the conversation is followed by a written record, held by the household. The finding is that the written record converts a conversation into an arrangement, and that the arrangement is what the parties can conduct.

## Part Six: The Cautions

The first caution is that the boundary is not a punishment. The statement is conducted in the man's own terms and its purpose is the arrangement, and the finding is that boundaries conducted as punishments produce the conflict that the man feared and do not produce the arrangement.

The second is that the boundary requires the limit to be conducted. A boundary stated without the limit is a request, and a request that is not met and not followed by the limit teaches the parties that the boundary is not real.

The third is that the man's community may read the boundary as a change of character. The finding is that communities frequently interpret the man's new limits as a withdrawal from his obligations, and that the interpretation reduces over a period of months as the man continues to conduct the obligations he has retained.

The fourth is that the man may overcorrect. The finding is that men who have conducted an arrangement of unlimited compliance frequently conduct a period of excessive refusal, and that the period is usually necessary and should be conducted with a party who can observe it.
