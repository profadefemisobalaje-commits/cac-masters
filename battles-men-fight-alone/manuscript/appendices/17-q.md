# Men's Mental Health Support and Crisis Resources {.appendix letter="Q"}

This appendix supplies the help-seeking routes referred to throughout the book. It is written carefully, because a resource list that is inaccurate is worse than none, and because the reader may consult it in a condition in which precision matters.

## A Note on Currency and Locality

Services change: numbers are reassigned, hours are reduced, programmes close, and new ones open. The reader should therefore treat this appendix as a framework and not as a directory, and should confirm any number or address before relying on it. Where the reader is outside the country or region described, the framework still applies and the specific organizations must be located locally.

The appendix is arranged in three parts: the immediate crisis, the professional route, and the community and peer route.

## Part One: The Immediate Crisis

### When This Part Applies

This part applies where the reader, or a party in his care, is in immediate danger: thoughts of ending his life, a plan and the means to act on it, a recent attempt, thoughts of harming another person, or a situation in which a household member is not safe.

### The Principle

An immediate crisis requires an immediate human response and not a book. The reader should do three things, in this order.

**The first is to ensure that nobody is alone.** The party at risk should not be left alone, and the man conducting the support should stay with him or arrange for a party who can.

**The second is to remove the means.** Where the risk involves a specific method, the means should be removed from the environment by a party other than the person at risk: medication, a weapon, a vehicle, or any other item. This is the single most effective practical measure available to a household in the short term, and it should be conducted without negotiation.

**The third is to contact a professional or emergency service.** The routes below are the framework; the reader should establish the local equivalents before they are needed.

### The Routes

| The route | Where it is found | What it is for |
|:-----------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------|
| The emergency number | The national emergency number of the reader's country | Immediate danger to life, including a medical emergency |
| The police | The national police number | Immediate danger, including a threat to a party's safety |
| The hospital's emergency department | The nearest general hospital | A medical emergency, an overdose, an injury, a severe episode |
| A crisis line | National and regional crisis and suicide-prevention lines | Immediate emotional support, and direction to the local services |
| The general practitioner or family physician | The reader's usual clinic | A first professional contact, and a referral where required |
| The workplace's employee assistance programme | The employer's human resources function | Confidential counselling, frequently at no cost to the employee |
| The school or university's counselling service | The institution | Support for a student, and advice for a parent |
| A trusted professional known to the family | The reader's own network | A rapid route to assessment where the formal route is slow |

### What to Say

The reader who is supporting a man in crisis should use plain language and should ask directly. The belief that asking about suicide increases the risk is false. The questions are: *are you thinking of ending your life? Do you have a plan? Do you have the means? Have you made an attempt?*

The answers determine the response. Where the answer to the second and third questions is yes, the response is immediate and the means are removed.

### What Not to Do

Four responses should be avoided.

The first is the reassurance that closes the conversation: *you will be fine*, *think of your children*. The second is the comparison: *others have it worse*. The third is the secret: an agreement to keep the risk confidential between the man and one party, without a professional's involvement. The fourth is the man's removal from his ordinary supports: hospitalization is sometimes required, and it should be conducted with the household rather than as a punishment or a concealment.

## Part Two: The Professional Route

### The Practitioners

| The practitioner | What they hold | When to consult |
|:-------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| The general practitioner or physician | The physical review, the first assessment, the referral, the medication where indicated | Any change in sleep, appetite, energy, mood, or functioning that persists |
| The psychiatrist | The diagnostic assessment and the medication for the psychiatric conditions | Where medication is required, or where the diagnosis is unclear |
| The clinical psychologist | The assessment and the structured psychological treatments | The anxiety conditions, depression, trauma, grief, and the adjustment difficulties |
| The counselling psychologist or therapist | The talking treatments, frequently in a shorter or more focused form | The ordinary difficulties of marriage, work, grief, and transition |
| The addiction counsellor | The assessment and the treatment of the addictive conditions | Any of the patterns in Appendix M |
| The social worker | The practical arrangements: the household, the benefits, the protection, the care | Where the position includes a practical need that the clinical route does not address |
| The occupational health service | The fitness for work, the adjustment of the role, the return to work | Where the position includes the work and the health together |
| The financial adviser and the insolvency practitioner | The financial position and its arrangements | Where the position includes an unmanageable obligation (Appendix D) |
| The lawyer | The guarantees, the family property, the employment, the estate | Where the position includes a legal exposure |

### Preparing for the First Appointment

The reader should bring: the record of Appendix B, the four tables of Appendix A where they have been completed, the list of medications and substances, the dates of the significant events, and one page stating the single question he most requires answered.

The consultation is frequently short. The reader should state, in the first two minutes: what has changed, since when, what it has cost, and what he requires. The finding is that men who arrive with the record receive a different consultation from men who arrive with a general statement.

### What to Do If the First Appointment Fails

The first appointment fails for three common reasons, and none of them is a verdict.

The first is that the practitioner was not the right one for the condition. The second is that the man did not state the material. The third is that the condition requires a treatment that the first route does not supply.

The response in all three cases is the same: a second appointment, with a different practitioner where possible, and with the record in hand.

## Part Three: The Community and Peer Route

The community route is not a substitute for the professional route and it is frequently the condition on which the professional route is used.

| The route | The form | What it supplies |
|:-------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| The peer support group | Men or parties with a shared experience, meeting at intervals | The testimony, the instrument, and the removal of the isolation |
| The recovery group | The structured peer programmes for the addictive conditions | The structure, the accountability, and the accompaniment |
| The men's group or forum | A group of men meeting to examine their positions | The examination, the correction, and the belonging of Chapter 45.4 |
| The association or the trade body | The professional or community body to which the man belongs | The information, the office, and the belonging |
| The place of worship or the community body | The man's own community, where he belongs to one | The presence, the occasion, and the practical assistance |
| The family and the household | The parties of the man's own household | The presence, the arrangement, and the participation |
| The mentor | The single adult of Chapter 43.2 and the five functions of Chapter 44.5 | The presence, the information, the accompaniment, the limit, and the continuity |

## Part Four: For the Man Who Is Supporting Another

Five practices apply, and they are stated because the supporting party is frequently as isolated as the man.

1. **Ask directly about risk**, and act on the answer.
2. **Accompany rather than advise**: make the appointment, attend it, and wait.
3. **State your limit** and identify the professional route: *I do not know how to help with this part, and I know who does, and I will go with you.*
4. **Protect your own position**: a supervisor, a peer, a professional, or a group of parties conducting the same work.
5. **Do not conduct the rescue**: the material of Chapter 44.5's prohibitions applies in full.

## Part Five: The Note That Applies to Every Route

The finding of this book is that the routes exist and that men do not use them, and that the reason is not the absence of the route. It is the position described in Chapter 45.2: the belief that the difficulty is insufficient, the imposition too great, the understanding unavailable, the standing at risk, or the identity incompatible.

The appendix's final note is therefore addressed to that belief.

A man who contacts a professional is not declaring that he has failed. He is conducting the competence that the position requires, in the same manner as a man who consults an accountant about a facility, a lawyer about a guarantee, or an engineer about a foundation. The finding of the case material is that the men who use the routes are frequently the men whose households, work, and health are intact at the ten-year point, and that the men who do not are frequently the men whose positions are conducted alone until they are conducted by a crisis.
