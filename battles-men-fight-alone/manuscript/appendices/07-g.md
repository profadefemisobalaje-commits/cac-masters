# Fatherhood and Family Responsibility Assessment {.appendix letter="G"}

This appendix is the assessment instrument for Part V and Chapter 43. It addresses the reader in his capacity as a father, a father-figure, or a senior member of a household, and it is designed to establish what he is actually transmitting rather than what he intends to transmit.

## Part Four: The Provision and the Responsibility

The material of Chapters 17, 18, and 42.1's fourth domain.

1. Does the household know its position in figures? If not, what does it believe, and what is the distance between the belief and the position?
2. Are there obligations to the extended family that are conducted at the household's expense without the household's knowledge? Record them.
3. What does the household's provision actually consist of, and who conducts each component?
4. What arrangement has been made for the household's position in the event of the man's death, illness, or incapacity? Record whether it is written, whether the household knows it, and where the documents are.
5. What has the man promised the household that he has not conducted?

## Part Five: The Children's Adult Position

The section addresses the reader whose children are adult. Four questions apply.

1. What does each adult child believe about the household's history, and how accurate is that belief?
2. What has the man not stated to each adult child that they have a right to know: a diagnosis, a financial arrangement, a family history, a decision about the estate?
3. Which of the man's patterns has each adult child reproduced, and which has each rejected?
4. What repair remains available with each adult child, and what would conduct it?

## Part Eight: The Father Who Is Absent

Where the reader is not present in his children's household — because of separation, migration, work, estrangement, or the child's own withdrawal — four sections of the assessment apply in modified form.

The first is Part Two, which is conducted through whatever contact exists: the telephone call, the message, the visit, the occasional meeting. The finding is that the transmission operates through the contact that exists and not through the contact that the man would prefer.

The second is Part Five, which is frequently the most relevant section for the absent father of adult children.

The third is the material of Chapter 43.7's named person: where the man cannot supply the presence, he can ensure that a party supplies it, and the case material records men who identified a teacher, a coach, an uncle, or an older cousin for their child and asked that party directly.

The fourth is the repair of the man's own position, which is the material of Chapter 29 and Chapter 44.6: the absent father who conducts his absence as an account of the child's mother, the court, the employer, or the economy transmits the account to the child, and the account is the thing the child will carry.

## Part Nine: The Father of a Son Who Will Not Speak to Him

This position is addressed separately because the case material records it frequently and because the ordinary advice fails in it.

Four practices apply.

**The first is the acknowledgement.** The son's silence is frequently a response to something specific, and the acknowledgement of the specific matter — stated once, without a defence, without a request for forgiveness, and without a condition — is the act that the case material finds most frequently associated with a later resumption.

**The second is the standing availability.** The man states, in ordinary language and at intervals of months rather than days, that he is available and that he will not require anything of the son in order to be available. The statement's form is supplied in Chapter 43.6's script.

**The third is the absence of the campaign.** The intermediaries, the family meetings, the letters through relatives, and the occasions arranged to produce a reconciliation are experienced by the son as pressure, and the case material records that they prolong the silence.

**The fourth is the man's own conduct of the loss.** The silence of a son is a loss and it requires the material of Chapter 30. The man who conducts it as a grievance against the son, the son's mother, or the son's wife transmits the grievance to every party who will listen, and the transmission frequently reaches the son.

The assessment's finding for this position is stated with restraint: a proportion of these silences resume, frequently after years and frequently at an occasion that the man did not arrange; a proportion do not; and the man's task in both cases is the same, which is to remain available, to conduct the acknowledgement, and to conduct the loss.
