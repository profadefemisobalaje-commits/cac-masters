# Identifying and Challenging Negative Thinking Patterns {.appendix letter="H"}

This appendix supplies the cognitive material of Parts II and VI in practical form. Its subject is the automatic thinking that governs a man's conduct without his having examined it, and its method is the identification, examination, and replacement of that thinking.

## What Automatic Thinking Is

Automatic thinking is the rapid, habitual interpretation that the mind supplies before deliberate reasoning begins. It is not a defect; it is the mechanism by which a person conducts ordinary life without examining every stimulus. Its difficulty is that the interpretations are frequently inaccurate in a systematic direction, and that the man's conduct is governed by the interpretation rather than by the fact.

The material is stated in the book's own terms: a man's estimate of his position is frequently conducted by an interpretation, and the interpretation is frequently acquired in the circumstances described in Chapter 43. Its examination is therefore not a technical exercise. It is the correction of an instrument that the man has been using without inspecting.

## The Ten Patterns

The patterns below are the ones the case material of this book records most frequently in men. Each is stated with the form it takes in a man's ordinary thinking.

**1. All-or-nothing thinking.** The interpretation is conducted in two categories with nothing between them. *If I cannot provide at the level I provided before, I am failing my household. If the business is not growing, it is dying.* The pattern's consequence is that a partial success is experienced as a failure, and the man's effort is conducted against an impossible standard.

**2. Catastrophising.** The interpretation proceeds to the worst available outcome and treats it as the likely one. *If I lose this contract, I will lose the house, and my family will be finished.* The pattern's consequence is the anxiety described in Appendix B and the avoidance described in Chapter 7.

**3. The personal attribution.** The interpretation assigns to the man the responsibility for events that are not his. *The children are struggling because I worked too much. The project failed because I am not competent.* The pattern is the material of Chapter 20's unappreciated man and of the attribution error described in Chapter 44.2.

**4. The external attribution.** The interpretation assigns to other parties or to circumstance the responsibility for events that are the man's. *The bank withdrew the facility; that is why the business failed. My wife does not understand me; that is why the marriage is difficult.* The pattern prevents the alteration described in Chapter 44.2's fifth question.

**5. Mind reading.** The interpretation supplies other parties' opinions without evidence. *They think I am finished. My colleagues know that I am out of my depth. My father is ashamed of me.* The pattern is the material of Chapter 25.3's imagined audience.

**6. The should.** The interpretation is conducted by a rule that the man has not examined and that he applies without exception. *A man should never show weakness. A father should always be available. I should be able to handle this.* The pattern is the material of Chapter 41.2's five clauses and the eight tests of Chapter 41.3.

**7. The disqualification of the positive.** The interpretation removes the value of anything favourable. *That went well because the client was easy. The recognition is only because nobody else applied.* The pattern's consequence is that the man's record of his own competence does not accumulate.

**8. The comparison.** The interpretation is conducted against a party chosen for the comparison's outcome. *Look at what he has built. At my age he was already a director.* The pattern's consequence is a permanent deficit that no achievement reduces.

**9. The prediction.** The interpretation states the future as a fact. *There is no point in applying; I will not be considered. Nothing will change in this marriage.* The pattern is the material of Chapter 8's self-doubt and Chapter 29's regret, and its consequence is the non-conduct of available acts.

**10. The labelling.** The interpretation converts a conduct into an identity. *I am a failure. I am weak. I am the kind of man who cannot hold a family together.* The pattern is the material of Chapter 21's man in the mirror and Chapter 44.4's failure mode.

## The Method: Five Steps

The method has five steps and it is conducted in writing, on the form supplied at the end of this appendix.

**Step One: The situation.** Record the event that preceded the thinking, in one sentence, without interpretation.

**Step Two: The state.** Record the state that the situation produced, with its intensity from 0 to 10, and its location in the body.

**Step Three: The automatic thought.** Record the thought in the words in which it occurred, in the first person, without correcting it. If the thought is a sentence the man would not say aloud, record it anyway; the thoughts that govern conduct are frequently the ones the man will not say.

**Step Four: The examination.** Conduct the four questions.

1. **The evidence for.** What specifically supports this thought? Record facts, not impressions.
2. **The evidence against.** What specifically contradicts it? Record facts, and record the man's own record where the thought is about the man.
3. **The alternative.** What is another interpretation that the same facts would support?
4. **The consequence.** If I hold this thought, what will I do? If I held the alternative, what would I do differently?

**Step Five: The replacement.** Write a replacement statement that is accurate rather than positive, and state the act that follows it.

The replacement's form is important. *I am a failure* is not replaced by *I am a success*, which the man will not believe and which the evidence does not support. It is replaced by *I made a decision that cost me a great deal, and I have conducted three corrections since, and I am not a failure; I am a man with a record that includes this.* The replacement's function is accuracy, and its accuracy is what allows the man to act.

## The Specific Patterns of Men

The book has described four thinking patterns that are specific to the men in its case material, and the reader should examine his own record against them.

**The first is the strength test.** The man conducts a continuous private examination in which every difficulty is a test of whether he is strong enough, and every failure of endurance is a verdict on his manhood. Its form: *if I cannot carry this, then I am not the man I thought I was.* Its replacement: *the difficulty is a position that requires instruments, and my capacity is measured by whether I use them.*

**The second is the provider's ledger.** The man conducts a continuous accounting in which his value to his household is computed by what he provides and his inability to provide is computed as an absence of love. Its form: *if I cannot pay the fees this term, I have failed my children.* Its replacement: *my children require provision and they require a father; the second is not contingent on the first.*

**The third is the shame of the stated difficulty.** The man interprets the statement of a difficulty as evidence of inadequacy, and the interpretation is the reason he does not state it. Its form: *if I tell her, she will know that I cannot manage.* Its replacement: *the statement is the competence; the concealment is the thing that has been costing the marriage.*

**The fourth is the permanence of the past.** The man interprets his history as a fixed account that determines what is available to him. Its form: *I did not finish the degree, so this is what my life is.* Its replacement: *what closed is closed, and what is available is available, and the second is larger than the account.*

## The Cautions

Four cautions apply.

The first is that the method is not a prohibition on difficult thoughts. A man who has experienced a loss, a betrayal, or a failure will have thoughts that are accurate and painful, and the method's task is to establish whether they are accurate, not to remove them.

The second is that the method is not a substitute for treatment. Where the patterns are pervasive, where they are accompanied by the symptoms of Appendix B, or where they include thoughts of worthlessness or of death, the professional route applies, and the method is conducted alongside it.

The third is that the method is frequently insufficient alone. A pattern that is held by an arrangement — a position that is genuinely unmanageable, a marriage that is genuinely damaging, a financial position that is genuinely insolvent — will not be corrected by an examination of the thought, because the thought is accurate. The distinction between a distorted interpretation and an accurate description of a bad position is the most important judgement in the method.

The fourth is that the method is slow. Men frequently report that the first week produces nothing and that the fourth week produces a change in the interval between the thought and the act. The interval is the method's product, and it is sufficient.
