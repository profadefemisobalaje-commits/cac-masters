# Recognizing Addiction and Accessing Professional Treatment {.appendix letter="M"}

This appendix supplies the material of Chapter 23 in practical form. It is written for the man who suspects that his use of a substance or a behaviour has become an addiction, and for the man who is supporting another.

## A Note on the Word

The word addiction is used in this appendix in its ordinary clinical sense: a pattern of use that is compulsive, that continues despite significant harm, that is accompanied by tolerance or withdrawal, and that the person finds himself unable to reduce or stop by an act of will.

The word carries shame in many communities, and the shame is one of the principal obstacles to treatment. The material's position is stated plainly: an addiction is a health condition, it is treatable, the treatments are effective, and the man who seeks treatment is conducting a competence rather than confessing a defect.

## Part One: The Recognition

### The Six Questions

Answer each question honestly. Two or more affirmative answers, together with a harm in Part Two, justify the professional route.

1. Have you ever felt that you should cut down, and found that you could not?
2. Have other people annoyed you by criticising your use, and have you found their criticism unreasonable?
3. Have you ever felt guilty about your use, or about something you did while using?
4. Have you ever used in the morning, or to steady yourself before an ordinary obligation?
5. Have you ever used alone, concealed the extent of your use from your household, or lied about it?
6. Have you ever used to manage a state — anxiety, sleep, grief, anger, or shame — rather than for the ordinary social purpose?

### The Patterns of Men

The clinical literature records that addiction in men is frequently masked by a socially acceptable form of the substance or behaviour. The common presentations are stated below.

| The pattern | The mask | The sign that the mask has failed |
|:-----------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------|
| Alcohol | The professional's social drinking; the business's entertainment; the cultural occasion | Drinking alone; the early-morning drink; the drinking that the household does not see |
| Prescription medication | A medical need; the management of pain, sleep, or anxiety | Multiple prescribers; the early refill; the use beyond the prescription |
| Gambling | The business's risk; the investment; the sport | Concealed losses; borrowing; the preoccupation; the return to recover |
| Work | The provider's obligation; the professional's ambition | The work that no longer produces anything; the work that continues when the position requires rest; the collapse that follows cessation |
| Pornography and sexual behaviour | Privacy; the ordinary male appetite | The escalation; the concealed use; the effect on the marriage's intimacy; the time consumed |
| Gaming and screen use | Recreation; the ordinary entertainment of the age | The withdrawal from the household; the sleep loss; the use that the man describes as being unable to stop |

## Part Two: The Barriers to Treatment

Five barriers are recorded, with their answers.

**The first barrier is the shame.** The answer is the framing supplied above: the condition is a health condition, and the men who are in treatment are frequently the men whose households are intact ten years later.

**The second barrier is the concealment.** The addiction is conducted in secret and the secret is protected by the arrangement described in Chapter 42. The answer is the statement to one party, which is the first step of every route.

**The third barrier is the position.** The man believes that he cannot be absent from his work, his household, or his obligations for the period that treatment requires. The answer is the finding that the untreated condition produces a longer absence and a larger cost, and that the treatment's arrangements are frequently more flexible than the man assumes, including outpatient and day programmes.

**The fourth barrier is the belief that treatment requires a declaration of powerlessness that the man cannot make.** The answer is that the treatment routes are varied: psychological treatments, medication, structured programmes, peer support groups, and combinations of these. The man should choose the route with a professional, and the professional's task is to supply the routes.

**The fifth barrier is the previous attempt.** A man who has attempted and relapsed frequently concludes that treatment does not work for him. The answer is that relapse is a common feature of the condition and not a verdict on the man, and that a subsequent attempt conducted with a professional, a party, and an alteration to the arrangement is substantially more likely to succeed than the first.

## Part Three: The Professional Route

### The Sequence

The route is conducted in five steps, in this order.

**Step One: The statement to one party.** The man states the position, accurately, to one party: a spouse, a sibling, a friend, a professional, a peer in recovery. The statement's form is supplied in Chapter 43.6's script.

**Step Two: The assessment.** The man seeks an assessment from a qualified professional: a physician, a psychiatrist, a clinical psychologist, or an accredited addiction counsellor. The assessment establishes the condition, its severity, the physical risks of withdrawal, and the appropriate route.

**Step Three: The physical review.** Where the substance is alcohol, sedatives, or opioids, the withdrawal can be medically dangerous and requires supervision. The physical review precedes the cessation and it is not optional.

**Step Four: The treatment.** The treatment is selected with the professional and may include:
- a structured psychological treatment, including the approaches with the strongest evidence base;
- medication, where indicated, for the condition or for a co-occurring depression or anxiety;
- a structured programme, residential or outpatient;
- a peer support group, which the case material records as effective for many men, particularly when combined with a professional route;
- the treatment of any co-occurring condition, which is the item most frequently omitted and most frequently decisive.

**Step Five: The arrangement.** The man alters the arrangements that supported the use: the occasions, the parties, the routes home, the availability of the substance in the household, the working pattern. The finding is that the treatment without the alteration of the arrangement is frequently followed by a return to the arrangement and to the use.

### The Questions to Ask the Professional

1. What is the diagnosis, and what is its severity?
2. What are the treatment options, and what is the evidence for each?
3. What are the risks of withdrawal, and what supervision is required?
4. Is there a co-occurring condition — depression, anxiety, trauma, attention difficulty — and how will it be treated?
5. What is the role of my household in the treatment?
6. What should I do if I return to use?
7. What is the follow-up arrangement, and for how long?

## Part Four: For the Household

Where the reader is the household member of a man with an addiction, five practices apply.

The first is to state what you observe, specifically and without diagnosis: *I have noticed that you drink before the evening meal on most days, and that you have not slept well for three months.*

The second is to state the effect on you and on the household, specifically.

The third is to request the professional route, and to accompany rather than to negotiate.

The fourth is to protect the household's position: the money, the children, the safety. The finding is that the household which protects the man from the consequences of the use for years frequently delays the treatment.

The fifth is to obtain support for yourself. The material of Chapter 44.5's fourth condition applies: the household member who conducts this alone will collapse, and the collapse frequently produces a return to the arrangement that the addiction requires.

## Part Five: The Cautions

The first caution is that this appendix does not supply a diagnosis or a treatment plan. It supplies the recognition material and the route, and the route requires a professional.

The second is that the physical risks of withdrawal from alcohol, sedatives, and opioids are serious and can be life-threatening. Cessation is conducted under medical supervision.

The third is that the co-occurring condition is frequently the condition that the use was managing. A man treated for the addiction and not for the depression, the trauma, or the anxiety is a man whose treatment is incomplete, and the finding is that the incomplete treatment is the most frequent route to relapse.

The fourth is that the man's family and community frequently require time. The finding is that the household's trust is rebuilt over years and not over the period of the treatment, and that the man's task in the interval is the continued conduct rather than the demand for the trust.
