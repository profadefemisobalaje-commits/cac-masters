# Recognizing Stress, Anxiety, Depression, and Burnout {.appendix letter="B"}

This appendix supplies the recognition material that the book's clinical chapters refer to. It is written for the ordinary reader and it is not a diagnostic instrument. Its purpose is to allow a man to distinguish between four conditions that are frequently confused, to know when the professional route is required, and to describe what he is experiencing in language that a professional can use.

## Why the Distinction Matters

Stress, anxiety, depression, and burnout overlap in their symptoms and differ in their mechanisms and their treatments. A man who treats a depression as stress will attempt rest, which does not resolve it. A man who treats burnout as depression will accept a course of treatment for a condition he does not have, while the arrangement that is producing the exhaustion remains unaltered. A man who treats anxiety as a character defect will not seek treatment at all.

The distinction is a professional's task, and this appendix does not substitute for it. What the reader can do is recognise the pattern, record it, and present the record.

## Stress

**What it is.** Stress is the response of a system to a demand that exceeds its current capacity. It is a normal response, it is time-limited where the demand is time-limited, and it is not a disorder.

**What it feels like.** A sense of pressure, a narrowing of attention to the demand, irritability, disturbed sleep in the period of the demand, tension in the shoulders, neck, or jaw, a raised heart rate at the moment of the demand, and a return to ordinary functioning when the demand is removed.

**The distinguishing feature.** Stress resolves when the demand resolves or when the capacity increases. A man under stress who takes a genuine period away from the demand, and who returns to a position in which the demand has reduced, will recover within days or weeks.

**What helps.** The removal or reduction of the demand; the increase of capacity through rest, support, and delegation; the ordinary regulation practices of Chapter 3 and Appendix L; the conduct of the position statement of Appendix A, since a demand that is unstated is frequently a demand that cannot be reduced.

**When to seek help.** Where the stress has continued for more than a month without relief; where sleep has not recovered during periods away from the demand; where the man has begun to use alcohol, sedatives, or stimulants to function; where the stress is accompanied by chest pain, palpitations, or breathlessness that has not been investigated.

## Anxiety

**What it is.** Anxiety is a state of apprehension and physiological arousal that is disproportionate to the actual threat, that persists in the absence of the threat, or that generalises beyond it. Where it interferes with ordinary functioning over a period, it is a clinical condition and is treatable.

**What it feels like.** Persistent worry that the man cannot switch off; a sense of dread without an identified object; physical symptoms including a tight chest, a churning stomach, dizziness, sweating, trembling, and a raised heart rate; disturbed sleep with early waking; difficulty concentrating; irritability; avoidance of the situations in which the anxiety is felt.

**The distinguishing feature.** Anxiety persists in the absence of the demand. The man who is anxious about a specific meeting and whose anxiety resolves after the meeting is experiencing ordinary apprehension. The man whose anxiety persists across weeks, transfers from one object to another, and produces avoidance of ordinary occasions is experiencing the pattern that requires assessment.

**What helps.** The professional route is the first item: talking treatments, particularly the structured psychological therapies, are effective for the anxiety conditions, and medication is effective for some. Alongside treatment: the regulation practices; the reduction of stimulants; regular physical activity; the deliberate and graded return to the avoided situations, which is the specific behavioural treatment for avoidance and should be conducted with professional guidance where the avoidance is extensive.

**When to seek help.** Where the anxiety interferes with work, sleep, or ordinary occasions for more than two weeks; where the man is avoiding situations that he needs to conduct; where panic attacks occur; where the anxiety is accompanied by alcohol use; and immediately where the man has thoughts of harming himself.

## Depression

**What it is.** Depression is a condition characterised by persistent low mood, or by a loss of interest and pleasure in activities that were previously enjoyed, accompanied by changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, and self-regard, present for at least two weeks and interfering with functioning.

**What it feels like in men.** Depression in men frequently presents differently from the textbook description, and the difference is the reason it is missed. The common male presentations are: irritability and anger rather than sadness; increased work, drinking, gambling, or sexual activity rather than withdrawal; physical complaints including back pain, headaches, and digestive symptoms; a flattened affect that is described as being *fine*; a loss of interest in work that the man had valued; a sense of being a burden; and a specific form of fatigue that the man describes as *heavy* rather than *tired*.

**The distinguishing feature.** Depression does not resolve when the demand resolves, and it is accompanied by a change in the man's estimate of himself. The man who is exhausted by a difficult period and who retains his ordinary self-regard is not depressed. The man who has begun to conclude that he is worthless, that his household would be better without him, or that nothing will improve, is presenting the cognitive signature of depression and requires assessment.

**What helps.** The professional route is essential and it is effective: psychological treatments and, where indicated, medication. Alongside treatment: the maintenance of sleep and eating; the conduct of one small activity each day regardless of motivation; the reduction of alcohol; the statement of the position to at least one party; and the removal of the man's isolation, which is the condition that depression most efficiently maintains.

**When to seek help.** Immediately where the man has thoughts of death, of harming himself, or of the world being better without him. Urgently where the symptoms have persisted for two weeks or more, where the man has stopped working or has withdrawn from his household, where alcohol use has increased, or where the man has a plan and the means to act on it. Appendix Q supplies the crisis and professional routes.

## Burnout

**What it is.** Burnout is a syndrome arising from chronic occupational or role-related stress that has not been successfully managed. It is characterised by three dimensions: exhaustion, a sense of cynicism or detachment from the work, and a reduced sense of professional efficacy. It is not classified as a medical condition but as an occupational phenomenon, and this distinction matters for treatment: the arrangement is frequently part of the condition.

**What it feels like.** Exhaustion that sleep does not address; dread of the working day; a detachment that the man describes as *not caring any more*, frequently accompanied by guilt, since he did care previously; a sense that the work is pointless; irritability with colleagues and household; reduced concentration and increased error; and a specific pattern of the man working longer hours to compensate for a reduced capacity, which accelerates the exhaustion.

**The distinguishing feature.** Burnout is domain-specific. The man who is exhausted in his work and restored in his household, his friendships, and his rest is presenting burnout. The man whose exhaustion is present in every domain, including those he values, is presenting a picture that requires assessment for depression.

**What helps.** The reduction of the demand is the central intervention and it is frequently the one the man resists: the reallocation of the workload, the delegation, the removal of one commitment, the period of leave that is actually taken. Alongside it: the restoration of sleep; the conduct of one domain of the man's life that is not work; the professional route where the picture includes depression or anxiety; and the material of Chapter 37 on rest without guilt, which addresses the specific incapacity that prevents men from conducting the restoration.

**When to seek help.** Where the man's functioning has reduced for more than a month; where he has begun to make errors of consequence; where his drinking has increased; where the exhaustion is accompanied by the cognitive signature of depression; and where the man's position cannot be altered without a professional or institutional intervention, which is frequently the case.

## The Comparison Table

| Feature | Stress | Anxiety | Depression | Burnout |
|:------------------------------|:-----------------------------|:--------------------------------|:--------------------------------|:--------------------------------|
| Onset | With the demand | Frequently without an identified demand | Gradual, frequently unremarked | Gradual, over months |
| Duration after the demand ends | Resolves | Persists | Persists | Partly resolves with rest, returns on return to the arrangement |
| Self-regard | Retained | Retained, with apprehension | Reduced, frequently severely | Retained, with a sense of ineffectiveness |
| Sleep | Disturbed in the period | Early waking, difficulty falling asleep | Both increased and decreased | Insufficient, unrestorative |
| Appetite | Variable | Variable | Frequently changed | Variable |
| Alcohol | Occasionally increased | Frequently increased | Frequently increased | Frequently increased |
| The principal treatment | Removal of the demand | Structured psychological therapy | Psychological therapy and, where indicated, medication | Alteration of the arrangement |

## Recording the Position

The reader who recognises himself in any of the four columns should complete the following record before seeking professional advice. The record improves the consultation, and men frequently present to a professional with a general statement that produces a general assessment.

1. **The change.** What has changed, in what domain, and since when? State the date, or the period, as precisely as possible.
2. **The symptoms.** List the specific symptoms, with their frequency and their effect on your functioning. Include sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, interest, and mood.
3. **The function.** What can you no longer do that you could do six months ago?
4. **The substances.** What are you using to function, in what quantity, and since when? Include alcohol, sedatives, stimulants, and any medication not prescribed to you.
5. **The history.** Have you experienced this before? When, for how long, and what helped?
6. **The position.** What is happening in your work, your household, your marriage, your finances, and your health?
7. **The risk.** Have you had thoughts of harming yourself or anybody else? Have you made a plan? This question must be answered honestly and it must be stated to the professional.
8. **The parties.** Who knows? Who could accompany you to an appointment?

## Notes for the Man Who Is Supporting Another

Where the reader is supporting a man in one of these conditions, four practices apply.

The first is to ask directly about risk. The belief that asking about suicide increases the risk is false and it prevents the conversation that the man requires. The question should be plain: *have you had thoughts of ending your life?*

The second is to accompany rather than to advise. The man requires an appointment, and he frequently requires a party to make it and to attend it.

The third is to avoid the account that closes the conversation. The sentences *you will be fine*, *others have it worse*, and *be strong* are experienced by the man as a requirement to conceal. The sentences *I have noticed*, *I am not going anywhere*, and *what would help* are experienced as an opening.

The fourth is to protect your own position. Supporting a man in a depression or an addiction is a load, and the load requires a party of your own, which is the material of Chapter 44.5's fourth condition.
