# Grief, Loss, and Emotional Recovery Journal {.appendix letter="K"}

This appendix supplies the journal material of Part VIII and Chapter 30. It is designed for a man who has sustained a loss and who requires a structured means of conducting it, and it is conducted over twelve weeks.

## Before Beginning

Four notes apply.

The first is that grief is not a problem to be solved. It is a response to a loss, it has its own course, and the journal's purpose is to give it a place rather than to shorten it.

The second is that the journal is private. Its contents are for the man, and the finding of the case material is that men write more accurately when they know that the material will not be read. Where the man wishes to share a part, he should choose the part rather than the whole.

The third is that the journal is conducted alongside the material of Appendix B where the loss is accompanied by the symptoms described there. Grief and depression are different, they coexist frequently, and the second requires the professional route.

The fourth is that the journal is not a substitute for a party. A journal without a party is a sophisticated form of the isolation this book describes. The journal is conducted with at least one party who knows that it is being conducted.

## Part One: The Loss

### Week One: The Account

Write the account of the loss in the plainest available form, answering six questions.

1. What was lost?
2. When, and how?
3. What was the last ordinary day before the loss?
4. What was the last thing said, and by whom?
5. What was not said?
6. What did the loss cost, specifically: the years, the arrangements, the future that had been assumed?

The account is written without interpretation. Its purpose is the establishment of the fact, and the finding is that men frequently have not written the account and have been conducting a version of it for years.

### Week Two: The Relationship

Write the account of the relationship that was lost, in four parts.

1. What the party gave the man, specifically.
2. What the man gave the party.
3. What was unresolved.
4. What the man would say if the party could receive one paragraph.

### Week Three: The Unstated

Write the material that the man has not stated to anybody: the guilt, the anger, the relief, the resentment, the love that was not expressed, the matter that was concealed. The week's finding is that the unstated material is frequently the material that is governing the man's conduct, and that writing it is the first conduct of it.

## Part Two: The Waves

### Weeks Four to Eight: The Daily Record

For five weeks, record each day in three lines.

| Date | What arrived, and when | Its intensity (0–10) | What I did with it |
|:-----|:--------------------------------------|:---:|:-----------------------------------|
| | | | |

The record's purpose is the establishment of the pattern. The finding is that the waves reduce in frequency and not in intensity, that the man who records them stops interpreting their arrival as a failure, and that the interpretation is frequently the thing that produced the fear of them.

### The Weekly Review

At the end of each week, answer four questions.

1. Which wave was the strongest, and what preceded it? (The date, the place, the song, the occasion, the person.)
2. What did I do with it, and what did that produce?
3. What did I avoid this week, and what did the avoidance cost?
4. What did I conduct that I would not have conducted in the first month?

## Part Three: The Occasions

### Week Nine: The Calendar

Record the occasions that will arrive in the following year and that will be difficult: the anniversary, the birthday, the festival, the family gathering, the date of the death, the date of a marriage, the season.

| The occasion | The date | What I will do | Who will be with me | The alternative plan |
|:---------------------------|:-----------|:-------------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|
| | | | | |

The instrument's purpose is the removal of the occasion's ambush. The finding is that men who plan the occasions report substantially less difficulty than men who are surprised by them, and that the planning is not a denial of the loss but a conduct of it.

### Week Ten: The First Occasion Without the Party

Conduct the occasion, conduct the plan, and record what happened. Where the occasion was conducted successfully, record the elements that made it conductable; where it was not, record the element that failed and alter the plan.

## Part Four: The Continuation

### Week Eleven: The Relationship With the Lost

Write, in one page, the account of the man's continuing relationship with what was lost: what he carries, what he has learned, what he does in the party's memory or in the loss's consequence, and what he will transmit.

### Week Twelve: The Account for the Household

Write the account that the household requires: what the man has conducted, what he has learned, and what he will state to the parties who loved the same loss. The account is not the journal. It is shorter, it is proportionate, and it is written for the party who will read it.

### The Closing Page

Write five sentences and keep them.

1. What was lost.
2. What it cost.
3. What I have conducted that I could not have conducted in the first month.
4. What remains.
5. What I will do on the anniversary.

## The Losses That Are Not Deaths

The journal applies in modified form to the losses that the case material records most frequently in men and that the culture does not supply an occasion for.

**The end of a marriage.** The journal is conducted with the party in the first part only where the party is willing; otherwise it is conducted alone, and the material of Appendix F, Part Nine applies.

**The loss of a position.** The account is conducted on the work, the standing, the colleagues, and the future that had been assumed. The finding is that men frequently conduct the loss of a position as a financial problem and not as a loss, and that the unconducted loss returns in the forms described in Chapter 30.

**The loss of a capacity.** The journal is conducted on the body, the competence, or the independence that has been reduced, and it is the material of Chapter 45.3's fourth component.

**The loss of a child's regard.** The journal is conducted on the relationship, the plans, and the years, and it is conducted with the caution that the party is living and that the material may not be conducted as a grievance against them.

**The loss of a possibility.** The career that will not be conducted, the education that will not be completed, the household that will not be built. The finding is that this loss is the least acknowledged and the most durable, and that the reconciliation of Chapter 45.3 is its instrument.

## The Cautions

The first caution is that the journal does not shorten grief and does not remove it. Its purpose is the conduct of the loss, and the conduct is the material.

The second is that the journal may produce a state that requires support. A man who writes the account in Week One and finds that he cannot conduct the following week should not conclude that the journal has failed; he should conduct the material of Appendix B and the professional route.

The third is that the journal is not a verdict on the man's conduct of the loss. Men conduct losses differently, the differences are not defects, and the finding is that the man who compares his conduct of the loss with another party's is conducting an examination that produces no instrument.

The fourth is that the journal is repeated where a further loss occurs and, in some cases, annually. The finding is that the second administration of the same journal, conducted a year later, is the most useful, because it establishes what has changed.
