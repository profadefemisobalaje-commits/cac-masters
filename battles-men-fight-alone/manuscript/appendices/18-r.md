# Practical Case Studies and Guided Reflections {.appendix letter="R"}

This appendix supplies six cases for study and reflection. Each is composite and illustrative, each is constructed from the case material of this book, and each is presented with questions rather than with conclusions. The cases are designed for individual study and for the discussion groups of Appendix S.

## How to Use the Cases

Read the case twice. On the first reading, note what the man's position consists of. On the second, note what he did with it and what the arrangement produced.

Then answer the four questions that follow each case, in writing. The questions are not a test. They are designed to allow the reader to examine his own position through the case's material, which is frequently easier than examining it directly.

## Case One: The Man Who Was Never Asked

**The position.** Nnamdi was forty-one, a bank branch manager, married to Ada with three children. His father had died when he was nineteen, and he had supported his mother and two younger siblings from his second year at university. He had never been without work and he had never been in a difficulty that his household knew about.

In the year of the case, the bank restructured and his branch was merged with another. Nnamdi was offered a position at a lower grade, in a different town, with a reduction of thirty-eight per cent in his salary. He accepted within a day and told nobody. He conducted the move as a promotion in his household's account, and he conducted the salary reduction by drawing on a facility that he had held for years and had never mentioned.

**What he did.** For fourteen months he conducted the position alone. He rose at four to travel, returned at nine, and conducted the household's expenditure from the facility. He attended his mother's hospital appointments on Saturdays and his children's school occasions when he could. He did not tell Ada, and he did not tell his brother, who was by then a medical doctor in another city and who telephoned each month.

**The arrangement's outcome.** In the fifteenth month the facility reached its limit, and the bank required a repayment arrangement. Nnamdi's response was to seek a second facility at another institution, which was declined. He then began to borrow from a cooperative at a rate that he did not examine. In the seventeenth month, his brother telephoned Ada about their mother's care, and Ada mentioned that Nnamdi had seemed unwell. The position emerged in a conversation of two hours, in which Ada learned for the first time about the grade, the town, the salary, and the borrowing.

**The questions.**

1. At what point in the case was the position cheapest to state, and what would the statement have required?
2. What did Nnamdi's acceptance within a day indicate about his definition of his obligations, and which clause of Chapter 41.2 was governing it?
3. What did the fourteen months cost, in the four ledgers of Chapter 42.2?
4. Which party was available throughout, and what in Nnamdi's conduct maintained the party's absence?

## Case Two: The Man Who Could Not Grieve

**The position.** Femi was fifty-six, a civil engineer, whose wife of twenty-nine years had died eighteen months before the case. He had conducted the funeral, the family's arrangements, the estate, and the return to work within three weeks. His account to his colleagues was that he was managing. His account to his children was that their mother would have wanted them to be strong.

**What he did.** He worked. He increased his site visits and took on two additional projects. He stopped attending the gatherings at which his wife had been present, and he stated that the occasions were not for him. He did not visit the grave. He slept for four hours a night and conducted the remainder of the night with the television.

**The arrangement's outcome.** In the twentieth month, an incident at a site produced a response that his colleagues described as disproportionate: he shouted at a foreman for a minor error, and then sat in his vehicle for two hours. His daughter, who was twenty-four, stated to him that he had not cried since the funeral and that she had heard him at night. He stated that he was fine. In the twenty-second month he was admitted to hospital with chest pain, which was investigated and found not to be cardiac. The physician asked him three questions about his sleep, his appetite, and his wife, and referred him.

**The questions.**

1. What was the grief conducting itself as, in the absence of its conduct?
2. Which of the four channels of Chapter 43.1 did Femi transmit to his children, and what did they learn?
3. What was the audience he required, and what would have supplied it?
4. What does the case indicate about the relationship between grief and physical symptoms?

## Case Three: The Man With Everything

**The position.** Chuka was forty-eight and had built a logistics business over sixteen years. He had forty-two employees, two houses, three vehicles, and a position in his community that he had conducted carefully: the association's chairman, the community development committee, the school's parents' association.

**What he did.** He maintained the position. He attended every occasion, contributed at the level expected, lent to relatives, and conducted the business's accounts alone. He had not examined the business's position in figures for three years, and he did not know that two of his four largest customers represented sixty-eight per cent of the revenue.

**The arrangement's outcome.** In the year of the case, one customer's parent company entered administration and the second renegotiated its terms. The business's revenue fell by forty-one per cent in five months. Chuka's response was to conduct the position by borrowing and to maintain the standard of his contributions. He lent to a cousin in the eighth month. In the eleventh month, he could not meet the salaries, and he conducted the payroll from a facility that was secured on the second house.

The position emerged when his accountant, whom he had engaged in the twelfth month following a tax notice, asked for the position in figures and established that the business had been insolvent for four months.

**The questions.**

1. Which of the six domains of Chapter 42.1 contained the principal pretence, and what was the specific fact that had to remain unknown?
2. What was the function of the community contributions, and what did the case establish about the cost of the standing?
3. What is the specific design fault of Chapter 44.2's fourth question?
4. What would the statement of the position have required in the fifth month, and what would it have produced?

## Case Four: The Father and the Silence

**The position.** Tunde was sixty-three, retired from the customs service, with three adult children. His second son, Emeka, had not spoken to him for six years. The ostensible cause was a dispute about Emeka's marriage, which Tunde had opposed. The case material records that the dispute was the occasion and not the cause, and that the cause was Emeka's account of his childhood: a father who was present in the household and absent from the children, whose approval was conditional on performance, and whose anger was conducted without repair.

**What he did.** Tunde conducted the six years as a grievance. He stated the account at every family occasion, corresponded with Emeka through his daughter, and arranged two interventions, neither of which Emeka attended.

**The arrangement's outcome.** In the seventh year, Tunde's wife became unwell, and Emeka attended the hospital, where the two met in a corridor. Tunde's first sentence was that Emeka had taken six years from his mother. Emeka left. In the ninth year, Tunde attended a men's group at his association, at his daughter's insistence, in which the material of Chapter 44 was discussed. He conducted the review of Chapter 44.2 on the six years, and its fourth question produced an answer he had not considered: that his arrangements had made the silence possible, since his household had never conducted a repair, and since his approval had been the currency of the household's relationships.

**The questions.**

1. What was the injury in the case, and whose was it?
2. What did Tunde's conduct of the six years produce, and what would the acknowledgement of Appendix G, Part Nine have required?
3. What is the difference between the reconciliation Tunde sought and the conduct the case's material supports?
4. What does the case indicate about the transmission of the household's emotional arrangements across three generations?

## Case Five: The Man Who Asked

**The position.** Obinna was thirty-four, a secondary school teacher, unmarried, with a diagnosis of depression that he had conducted for four years without telling anybody. He had been treated twice and had stopped the treatment on both occasions because of the cost and the concealment.

**What he did.** In the year of the case, his head teacher asked him, in ordinary language, whether he was all right, and he said yes. Three weeks later, a colleague whose brother had conducted a similar condition asked him a specific question about his sleep. He stated the position.

**The arrangement's outcome.** The colleague accompanied him to the clinic, where he resumed treatment. The head teacher adjusted his timetable for one term. His household learned the position in the fourth month, and his mother's response was the one he had not predicted: she stated that her own brother had conducted the same condition and had never spoken of it.

In the second year, Obinna conducted the mentoring arrangement of Chapter 44.5 for two younger teachers. In the third year, he accepted the office of the school's staff welfare representative, in which he conducted the arrangement of Chapter 43.6's institutional design.

**The questions.**

1. What was the specific instrument that the colleague supplied, and why was it effective where the head teacher's general question was not?
2. What did the resumption of treatment require, in terms of Chapter 45.2's distinction between acceptance and dependence?
3. What did the transmission in the second and third years produce for Obinna himself?
4. What does the case indicate about the relationship between help-seeking and help-giving?

## Case Six: The Day

**The position.** Akin was fifty-two, an accountant in private practice, whose marriage had conducted fourteen years of parallel responsibilities and whose brother had died two years earlier in circumstances that Akin had not discussed with anybody.

**What he did.** On a Wednesday in the eleventh month of the case, he attended a routine appointment with his bank, answered a question about his household's position truthfully, and then drove to his brother's widow's house, where he stated that he had not conducted the grief.

**The arrangement's outcome.** The case material records the following year as one of substantial alteration: the marriage conducted four conversations of the form of Appendix E, Exercise 8; the practice took an associate, which reduced Akin's hours by a third; and the household conducted the Sunday ten minutes of Chapter 43.9. The case material records Akin's own account in the second year: *nothing that I was carrying was reduced. I stopped carrying it at the same time as everybody else was asleep.*

**The questions.**

1. What was the occasion, and why does the case material record it as ordinary rather than as a crisis?
2. What was the first statement, and what did it make possible?
3. Which of the four conditions of Chapter 45.6 were present at the beginning?
4. What does the case indicate about the relationship between the size of the change and the size of the act that produced it?

## The Guided Reflection

After conducting the six cases, answer the following questions in writing.

1. Which case is closest to your position, and what specifically is the resemblance?
2. Which case contains the act you have not conducted, and what would its conduct require this month?
3. Which case's outcome did you find least believable, and what does your disbelief indicate about your own position?
4. In which case did a party supply an instrument rather than a rescue, and what was the specific form?
5. What has the study of the six cases established about the relationship between the size of a man's position and the size of the act that alters it?
6. What will you conduct within thirty days as a result of this appendix?
