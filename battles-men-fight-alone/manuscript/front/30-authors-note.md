# Author's Note {.front}

A few clarifications will help the reader to get the most from this book and to understand what it can and cannot do.

**On the stories.** Every case story, vignette, letter, dialogue, and character in this book is an original composite created for teaching purposes. Names, occupations, cities, and details have been altered and combined so that no story corresponds to a single identifiable person. When a chapter says *Ade's story is composite*, or the caption of a case box describes material as illustrative, that is meant literally. These stories were written to represent patterns — the pattern of the man who cannot say he is struggling, the pattern of the marriage that goes quiet, the pattern of the father who provides and cannot connect — and the patterns are drawn from documented experience and professional observation, not from any individual's file.

**On evidence.** The book refers to research findings in general terms. Where a claim rests on a well-established body of work — for example, that suppressing emotion has physiological and psychological costs, that behavioural therapy is effective for many anxiety disorders, or that social support protects mental health — the text states the finding as established. It does not present invented statistics, and it does not attribute positions to named researchers by way of fabricated quotation. Readers who wish to pursue the underlying literature will find the direction of travel in the Recommended Reading section; practitioners and students should go to primary sources.

**On diagnosis.** This book describes experiences and patterns. It does not diagnose, and it cannot: diagnosis requires a qualified professional, a proper assessment, and the elimination of physical causes and other conditions. Statements of the kind *many men experience depression as irritability rather than sadness* describe common presentations; they do not mean that every irritable man is depressed, or that a reader who recognises himself in a description has a disorder. Where the text lists warning signs, it does so to prompt appropriate professional consultation, not to invite self-diagnosis.

**On terminology.** Several terms are used in specific ways throughout.

- *Distress* is used for the ordinary range of painful human experience — strain, worry, sorrow, anger, disappointment — that does not necessarily require clinical care.
- *Disorder*, *condition*, and *illness* are used for patterns that meet recognised diagnostic criteria and that respond to treatment.
- *Suppression* is used for the deliberate or habitual pushing away of emotional experience, and is distinguished from *self-control*, which is the capacity to choose how one acts on emotion without denying that it exists.
- *Manhood* and *masculinity* refer to social expectations attached to being a man, not to any claim about innate nature.
- *Partner* is used where the reader's circumstances may involve a wife, a cohabiting partner, or a long-term companion.
- The book uses *he* and *his* throughout because its subject is the experience of men. This is a limitation of the subject, not a judgement about whose suffering matters.

**On culture and context.** The book is written from a broadly international perspective, with sustained attention to African — and specifically Nigerian — realities. It takes seriously the extended-family system in which many men operate, the weight of communal expectation, the role of respect and shame in social life, and the practical obstacles to professional care in places where services are scarce, costly, or far away. It also refuses the false choice sometimes posed between culture and mental health: the recognition that men need support is not a foreign import, and asking for help is not a betrayal of family or community. Wherever possible, the book names alternatives that fit the reader's actual circumstances, including guidance that does not require money or a clinic.

**On faith.** The book is written as a secular, psychological, and practical work. It addresses the human realities of burden, duty, sorrow, hope, and change without appealing to religious doctrine, and readers who hold religious commitments will find nothing here that contradicts those commitments and nothing here that depends upon them.

**On safety.** Some chapters necessarily discuss severe distress, self-harm, addiction, and suicide. They are included because silence about these subjects is dangerous, and because a man who has reached that point deserves a book that speaks to it plainly rather than changing the subject. Readers who are in acute distress, or who are concerned about a man who may be, should turn to Chapter 34, Appendix B, and Appendix Q, and should not wait to finish the book first.

**On the exercises.** The practical exercises and appendices are tools, not requirements. Some readers will work through them methodically; others will use one worksheet and ignore the rest. Both approaches are legitimate. Readers who find that an exercise stirs up more distress than they can manage alone should stop, and should speak to a qualified professional before continuing. That is not a failure of the exercise or of the reader; it is information about what kind of help is needed.

**On privacy.** A man reading this book may do so privately, and may prefer that nobody know. That is understood. But the trajectory of the book, and the evidence behind it, point in one direction: the private reading is a beginning, and the change that lasts usually arrives in the company of at least one other person — a partner, a friend, a mentor, a counsellor, a physician, or a group of men who have agreed to be honest with one another. The reader is invited, at whatever pace he chooses, to move in that direction.

*Prof. Adeleke Daniel Durojaiye (PhD)*
