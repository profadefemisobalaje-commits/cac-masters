# Acknowledgments {.front}

A book about the things men rarely say must begin by thanking the people who said them.

Over the years in which this material was gathered, taught, discussed in seminars, argued over in counselling supervision, and tested in conversations with men from many walks of life, a large number of people gave something of themselves to it. Men who had carried a private weight for a decade agreed, often reluctantly, to describe it. Wives and partners explained what it looks like from the other side of a silence they had never been able to interpret. Counsellors, physicians, nurses, teachers, human-resource officers, clergy of many traditions, and community leaders shared what they see in their work and what they wish someone would write down. Colleagues in the behavioural and social sciences insisted, rightly and repeatedly, that compassion is not a licence for imprecision. The book is better for all of it, and its faults remain the author's own.

I am grateful to the men who allowed their experiences to be turned into the composite illustrations that appear throughout these pages. They were promised that no story would be traceable to a single person, and that promise has been kept: every narrative in this book is a composite, constructed to represent a pattern rather than an individual. What they gave was the truth of the pattern. That truth was often uncomfortable to hear and, I suspect, harder to tell.

Special thanks are due to the practitioners who read early drafts of the chapters on depression, addiction, trauma, grief, and suicide risk, and who corrected errors of emphasis, insisted on clearer safety guidance, and reminded me that a sentence which encourages a man to disclose his distress carries an obligation to tell him where help actually is. The clinicians who gave their time to that task did so anonymously and without reward. The reader who is helped by this book owes them a debt that will never be acknowledged in print.

I thank the academic colleagues and postgraduate students whose questions sharpened the argument in every part of the book — particularly those who pressed the hardest on the question that a book of this kind must never dodge: how do you write about men's suffering without denying the suffering of others, and how do you honour responsibility without turning it into a trap?

My thanks go to the publisher and editorial team for believing that this subject deserved a full book rather than an article, and for tolerating a manuscript that grew well beyond its original shape. The design, typesetting, and production of a volume of this size involve many hands, most of them unseen.

Finally, and most of all, I thank my family. Writing about the emotional labour that men perform in silence has a way of exposing how much of it one has been receiving without naming it. To those at home who carried on with patience while this book was being written: thank you. Whatever is wrong in these pages is mine; whatever is useful belongs, in large measure, to you.

*Prof. Adeleke Daniel Durojaiye (PhD)*\
*Ibadan, 2026*
