# Preface: Why This Book Must Be Written {.front}

In most countries for which reliable data are collected, men die by suicide at a substantially higher rate than women, even though women are more likely to be diagnosed with depression and to report depressive symptoms. Men are less likely to consult a doctor about mental-health difficulties and less likely to be in contact with mental-health services before a crisis. Men make up a large majority of deaths from alcohol-related causes, a large majority of the prison population, and a large majority of those who are homeless or sleeping rough in many cities. At the same time, in many societies, men remain the expected primary providers for extended families, the assumed decision-makers in moments of crisis, and the people to whom others turn for practical help without asking what the helping costs them.

Those two paragraphs sit side by side in public policy documents all over the world, and they are rarely read together. When they are read together, a plain pattern appears. A very large number of men are living with significant distress, and a very large proportion of that distress never enters a consulting room, a conversation, or a record. It is managed at home, in silence, at cost, and sometimes not managed at all.

This book exists because that pattern is not merely a statistic. It is the daily experience of men who are, in the ordinary sense of the word, functioning: men who go to work, pay school fees, attend meetings, fix what is broken, and are described by everyone who knows them as strong. It is also the experience of men who are not functioning, and who are ashamed of that fact in a way that makes it harder for them to recover. Both groups share a common feature: the difficulty of saying what is happening.

## The Three Reasons This Book Was Written

**First, because the vocabulary is missing.** Many men can describe a mechanical problem, a business difficulty, or a tactical challenge in precise terms, and cannot describe an emotional state in any terms at all. This is not a deficiency of intelligence; it is a deficiency of training. Emotional vocabulary, like any vocabulary, is learned. Where a man grows up in an environment that rewards the suppression of feeling and penalises its expression, he may reach adulthood with a rich professional language and a poverty of emotional language. Without words, experience remains unnamed; and what cannot be named cannot be examined, shared, or treated. A substantial part of this book is therefore an exercise in vocabulary-building: giving names to states that many men know intimately but have never heard described.

**Second, because responsibility and isolation are routinely confused.** There is nothing wrong with a man carrying a great deal. Responsibility is a legitimate and honourable part of adult life, and this book does not treat it as a pathology to be shed. What is wrong is the assumption that responsibility requires isolation — that needing others is a failure of manhood, that asking for assistance undermines authority, that a man who admits difficulty has disqualified himself from leading, providing, or protecting. That assumption is not a law of nature. It is a cultural arrangement, and cultural arrangements can be changed. The chapters that follow separate the burden, which is often unavoidable and frequently honourable, from the solitude, which is neither.

**Third, because the help exists but is not reached.** Effective treatments for depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, and alcohol dependence are well established. Counselling and psychotherapy are widely available in many places; telephone and online services have widened access considerably; and peer support, mentoring, and structured self-help programmes provide meaningful help to people who will never sit in a therapist's office. The gap is not primarily a gap in the availability of help. It is a gap in the route to it: men do not know what the first step is, do not believe it will work, fear being judged, or fear the consequences for their work, their marriage, or their standing. This book therefore treats help-seeking as a skill to be taught and rehearsed, not as a character trait to be mustered.

## What This Book Is Not

It is not a claim that men's difficulties are greater than anyone else's. Suffering is not a competition, and the fact that one person's pain is invisible does not make it worse. Women's struggles, and the structural disadvantages that many women face, are real and extensively documented. A book about men's silent battles is not an argument against that literature; it is a complement to it. Everyone benefits when men are better able to speak and better able to seek help, because the cost of male silence is paid not only by men. It is paid by partners who live with a wall they cannot name, by children who learn that emotion is unwelcome in the house, by colleagues, by employees, by communities, and by the next generation of boys who are watching.

It is not a book of blame. It does not blame women for expecting men to be strong, parents for raising them that way, employers, faith communities, or men themselves for having absorbed the message. Blame is an unproductive emotion in this territory. Understanding is more useful, and so is skill.

It is not a substitute for professional care, and it does not pretend to diagnose. Its role is to describe, to explain, to teach, and to point the reader towards competent help where that is what is required.

## Why Now

Patterns of work, family, and social life have changed faster than the expectations attached to manhood. Men are now expected to be emotionally engaged fathers and equal partners in domestic life while many are also carrying the older expectation of sole provision, in economies where sole provision is increasingly impossible. Migration, urbanisation, and the weakening of extended family support have removed structures that once absorbed distress. Digital communication has multiplied the opportunities for social comparison and public shame. And the ordinary arenas in which men once talked at length to one another — the workshop, the farm, the long journey, the evening walk home — have thinned out for many.

The result is a population of men who are more articulate, better educated, and more publicly accomplished than any generation before them, and who are, in many cases, lonelier than any generation before them as well. Long before the end of this book, the reader will find that word — loneliness — returning in almost every part, whatever the nominal subject of the chapter. It is the common thread of the silent battles, and it is the most treatable part of the problem.

The book is written for men and for the people who live and work with them. It is written for the man who is doing well and feels hollow; the man who is doing badly and feels ashamed; the man who is doing both at once and cannot explain how. It accepts that he is unlikely to ask for help in the way that a textbook recommends, and it works with that reality rather than against it — beginning, as he does, with the day-to-day burdens of ordinary life, and moving, as the chapters progress, towards the interior world those burdens conceal.

If, by the end, a man can name one thing he has never named, and say it to one person he trusts, this book will have done its work.

*Prof. Adeleke Daniel Durojaiye (PhD)*
