## 28.4 Grief for Something That Never Existed

This section sets out what is required in order to conduct a grief that has no object, and what the man should expect from it.

### The Naming

The first requirement is that the loss be named as a loss, and named in a form the man can accept. Four formulations are used in practice, and men report that at least one of them is workable.

**The formulation of loss.** *I lost the thing I intended to do, and I have never had anywhere to put that.* This is the most straightforward and it is the one men most frequently resist, because the word *lost* suggests that something was taken. Section 28.2's point — that the man's own decisions were correct — does not conflict with the naming: a thing can be lost by a correct decision as well as by a wrong one.

**The formulation of mourning.** *I am mourning a life I did not live.* This formulation is more accurate for men whose dream involved a specific alternative existence — the country, the profession, the version of the household — and it has the advantage of placing the object of the grief correctly: the man is mourning a life, which is a real loss, and not a possession.

**The formulation of the closed door.** *That route closed at a particular point in the past, and I have been behaving as though it were still open.* This formulation is the most practical and it is the one that most frequently leads directly into the work of Section 28.5.

**The formulation of the debt.** *I owe that intention a proper ending, and I have never given it one.* This formulation is useful for men whose difficulty is that the matter feels unfinished rather than painful. It converts the work into an obligation, which men manage well.

### The Grief Conducted Without an Audience

The loss has no public process, so the man must construct a private one. The following four elements constitute the form used in practice, and they are adapted directly from the material in Chapter 13 on burial and remembrance.

**1. The account, written.** The dream, in writing: what it was, when it was decided, what was attempted, and what stopped. The document should contain the four items from Section 28.1 and should be written in the past tense, which is the detail that begins the closing. Men report that the writing is frequently the first occasion on which the dream has been described accurately, and that the description is itself the greater part of the work.

**2. The accounting of the reasons.** Written separately: why the dream was set down, whether the decision was correct, whether it would be repeated, and what would have been required to do otherwise. The section should include the actual cost of the alternative — the costs that the imagined life did not include — for the reason given at the end of Section 28.3. This is the element that converts the man's envy of the imaginary man into an accurate assessment.

**3. The witness.** One person who is informed that the dream existed and that it is being closed. The requirement is not that the person should comfort the man; it is that somebody should know. Men in men's groups report consistently that the single act of telling one person is the step that alters the position, and the mechanism is the one described in Chapter 27.3: the account, delivered to another person, is changed in the man's own hearing.

**4. The marker.** A physical or dated act that marks the ending: a written page filed in a particular place, a small object retained or given away, an entry in a journal, a visit to the place associated with the dream, an hour set aside with a date and a decision. The marker's function is the transition that the ordinary arrangements would have supplied, and men report that it works despite its arbitrary nature. Its form matters less than its having a date.

### What the Man Should Expect

Six outcomes, described consistently by men who have conducted the work, and their ordinariness is the point.

The incidence of the material reduces. It does not disappear, and the reduction is generally gradual over several months.

The comparison with the men who went reduces, because the accounting of the alternative's costs has made the imagined life less attractive and because the man's own account of the dream's ending is now accurate.

The comparison with the imagined self reduces similarly, and this is the largest change men report.

The dream is available to be discussed. Men report that after the work, the dream can be mentioned in conversation without the accompanying private event, which is the practical measure of the closing.

The grief recurs on specific occasions — a milestone, a child's graduation, a funeral, a former colleague's obituary, a particular piece of music — with reduced intensity each time. The recurrence is not a failure of the work; it is the ordinary pattern described in Chapter 13.

The life that was built becomes available for the accounting described in Section 28.6, which the man has generally been unable to conduct while the dream remained open.

### Two Special Cases

**The shared dream.** Where the dream belonged to the man and another person and the other person has died, the work has an additional requirement: the man must grieve the person as well as the plan, and he must permit himself to close the plan without regarding the closure as a betrayal. The formulation used in bereavement practice is worth recording: the dream is not kept alive by the man's refusal to close it; the dream is kept alive by the man's conducting what it stood for. This matter is treated at greater length in Chapter 30's material.

**The dream that belongs to the next generation.** Where the man's dream is available to his children, a specific and delicate situation arises, and the chapter notes it because practice reports that it is frequently mishandled. A father who communicates to a son the value of the thing he himself did not do has transferred the material, and the receipt of it by the son produces exactly the pattern described in Chapter 19: a young man carrying an obligation that was not his own. The distinction to be maintained is between an example and an assignment. The example is *this is what I valued and what it looked like*; the assignment is *you will do what I did not*. The first supplies a model; the second supplies a debt.

## 28.5 When the Dream Dies Late

A substantial proportion of the dreams in this material die late: not in the years of the obligations, but at the point at which the obligations end, when the man finally has the time, the margin, and the freedom — and discovers that the dream is no longer available, or that he is no longer the man who held it.

### The Trigger

The trigger is generally one of five events, and each has the same structure: it removes the constraint that had justified the deferral.

**Retirement or redundancy.** The time has arrived and the money is different from the plan.

**The last child's completion.** The fees have ended, the household has reduced, and the man's account of why the dream was set down no longer applies.

**The death of the parent whose requirement had governed the decision.** The constraint is removed by a bereavement, which produces a compound event: the grief and the discovery that the reason for the decision has gone.

**The medical event.** An illness or an injury that establishes a horizon, and that converts a postponed dream into a closed one without a decision.

**The encounter.** A former colleague, a reunion, an obituary, a returned acquaintance: an event that presents the alternative life in a form the man cannot avoid.

### The Specific Errors of a Late-Dying Dream

**The man attempts to resume it in its original form.** He registers for a course he cannot possibly complete, initiates a business with no assessment of the market, migrates to a place he has not visited in thirty years, or commits to the discipline required at twenty at an age at which the body will not cooperate. The attempt frequently ends in a second failure, which is then added to the first as evidence.

**The man treats the discovery as a verdict.** The finding that the original form of the dream is closed is converted into an assessment of the whole life, which is the material of Chapter 24 and which is the reason the two chapters are adjacent.

**The man closes it in anger.** The dream is abandoned with a resentful act: the papers destroyed, the correspondence discarded, the subject forbidden in the household. Practice reports that this form of closing does not work, because the material is not closed but hidden, and it returns on the same triggers.

**The man transfers it to somebody else.** The pattern described at the end of Section 28.4: the dream is delivered to a son or a nephew as an assignment, and the man's material is then carried by a younger man who was not consulted.

### The Correct Handling of a Late-Dying Dream

**Establish what remains available.** The first practical step, and it is not a matter of attitude but of information: what of the dream's content is still possible in a modified form, and what is genuinely closed? The distinction between the two is frequently larger than the man assumes, and it is the work of the exercise at the end of the chapter. The man who intended to be a lawyer may not be able to practise; he may be able to study the subject, to teach it, to advise within a defined scope, to represent in a permitted forum, or to qualify in a related capacity. The man who intended to live elsewhere may be able to spend a defined period there annually. The man who intended to write may still write, and the fact that the book would have been better at thirty is not an argument against writing it at sixty.

**Distinguish the content from the form.** The most useful single distinction in this material. The dream's content is what the man was after: the practice of law, the life in another country, the craft, the land, the work with his hands, the position of responsibility, the recognition. Its form is the way he intended to obtain it at twenty-two. Frequently the content remains available through a different form, and the man's inability to see this is generally because the dream in his head is stored as the form: the examination, the visa, the building, the title.

**Set the deadline and the first step.** For a dream established as available, the man sets one first step and a date within thirty days, and the step must be small: the enquiry, the application, the visit, the enrolment, the conversation, the purchase of the material. Men report consistently that the first step taken is worth more to the position than any amount of further deliberation, for the reason given in Chapter 7.

**Close what is closed, properly.** The work of Section 28.4, conducted with the four elements: the account, the accounting, the witness, and the marker. The man who has decided that a dream is closed should conduct the closing rather than allowing the dream to stand open indefinitely. The most useful sentence available to men in this position is the one from the case in Section 28.3: the object of the envy was not the dream but an imaginary man who took the path and did not pay for it.
