## 45.2 Accepting Meaningful Relationships and Support

The previous section established what is released. This section addresses the act by which the release is conducted, and it proceeds from a finding that the reader may find surprising.

**The difficulty is not generally the absence of support. It is the refusal of support that is available.**

The case material of this book records this with a consistency that justifies the statement. A substantial proportion of the men who describe themselves as having nobody to call, on examination, have somebody: a wife who has offered, a brother who has asked, a colleague who has twice suggested a conversation, a friend from twenty years ago who telephoned and was not called back, a professional who is available and has not been approached. The absence is real in the man's experience and it is frequently an absence that his own conduct has produced and maintained.

The section therefore addresses the refusal before it addresses the construction. It proceeds in four parts: the six reasons a man refuses, the four fears that govern the refusal, the distinction between acceptance and dependence, and the practice of acceptance.

### The Six Reasons a Man Refuses Support

The reasons are stated in the form in which men give them, and each is followed by the material that answers it. The answers are not reassurances. They are observations from the case material, and the reader should note that the material does not attempt to establish that the reasons are irrational. Several of them are rational, and each of them is addressable.

**Reason One: "It would be an imposition."**

The reason is common, it is sincere, and it is frequently held by men who have spent their adult lives being the party who is imposed upon. The man's calculation is that the other party's time, attention, or resources are limited and that his difficulty does not have a sufficient claim on them.

The material's answer is a distinction that most men have never conducted: **the imposition is not the request; it is the unannounced arrival of the consequence.** The party who is asked in time can decline, can set the terms, and can contribute what he has. The party who is notified after the collapse is presented with a position that has already exceeded everybody's capacity. The case material's finding is that the parties in men's lives are frequently willing to be asked and are frequently unable to conduct the unannounced consequence, and the finding is stated in the words that one such party used in the material: *I would have preferred to be asked when it would have helped.*

**Reason Two: "Nobody would understand."**

The reason is frequently accurate in part. A man's position frequently contains material that the available parties have not experienced: the specific pressure of a business's cash flow, the humiliation of a professional failure, the particular structure of a marriage's difficulty, the fear of a diagnosis.

The material's answer is the distinction between understanding and usefulness. A party does not need to have experienced a man's position in order to be useful in it. The wife who has not run a business can hear it. The brother who has not been dismissed can accompany a man to an appointment. The friend who has not lost a child can telephone on the anniversary. The case material's finding is that the requirement of perfect understanding is one of the most efficient instruments by which men exclude every available party, and that it is rarely held consciously.

**Reason Three: "It would change how they see me."**

The reason is the material of Chapter 20.2's fear of the reduction of standing, and it is the most powerful of the six. The man's standing among the parties who surround him is frequently the asset he has built most carefully, and the disclosure of a difficulty appears to threaten it.

The material's answer has three parts. The first is that the party who has been told an accurate position and has not reduced his estimate of the man is the party whose estimate is worth holding. The second is that the reduction the man fears is frequently experienced by the other party as an increase: the case material records wives, brothers, and colleagues who reported that the disclosure had increased their respect rather than reduced it, because the disclosure was the first occasion on which the man had treated them as capable of it. The third is that the standing which cannot survive an accurate disclosure is the standing described in Chapter 42, and it is being maintained at a cost the man has already established.

**Reason Four: "I do not want to burden them."**

The reason is the most frequently stated and the most frequently misapplied. Its valid form is a genuine concern for a party who is themselves in difficulty, and the material does not propose that the concern be abandoned. Its invalid form is a general statement about the man's own value, which is to say that a difficulty of his is a burden and a difficulty of anybody else's is not.

The material's answer is a question that the case material finds effective: **if the positions were reversed, would you want to be told?** The answer is almost always yes, and the answer establishes that the man's calculation is not a general principle about burdens. It is a specific exemption that he has granted himself, and the exemption is the material of Chapter 20's unappreciated man and Chapter 41.2's clause of self-sufficiency.

**Reason Five: "Nothing can be done."**

The reason is a judgement about the position's solubility, and it is frequently wrong. The case material records men who had concluded that nothing could be done about a debt, a marriage, a diagnosis, a professional position, or a grief, and who discovered on disclosure that the party they spoke to knew of an instrument, an institution, a procedure, a variant of the arrangement, or a party who had been in the same position. The discovery is not guaranteed, and the frequency with which it occurs is sufficient to justify the statement of the reason as a prediction rather than a fact.

**Reason Six: "I have always handled my own affairs."**

The reason is the identity, and it is the most resistant. The material's answer has been stated in Section 45.1 and is repeated in its practical form: the man's identity is not in question. What is in question is whether the affairs he has always handled have been handled with information that he alone possessed, and whether that arrangement is producing the outcomes he believes it is producing. The case material's finding is that the question, put precisely, is frequently sufficient, because men who hold this reason are frequently rigorous about their own arrangements and have simply never examined this one.

### The Four Fears

Beneath the six reasons are four fears, and the section states them because the reasons are frequently the surface and the fears are the material.

**The first fear is the fear of dependence.** The man fears that acceptance will lead to a state in which he requires the party, cannot conduct his position without the party, and is therefore reduced. The fear is the material of Chapter 41's clause of self-sufficiency, and its correction is Section 45.2's third part.

**The second fear is the fear of exposure.** The man fears that the disclosure will supply the party with information that can be used against him, and the fear is frequently grounded in experience. Its correction is the selection of the party and the conduct of Section 45.2's fourth part, not the abandonment of the material.

**The third fear is the fear of the party's alarm.** The man fears that the disclosure will produce a response he will then be required to manage: the wife's distress, the brother's intervention, the friend's advice, the employer's concern. The fear is common and it is frequently the most accurate of the four. Its correction is the statement's form: the man states what he requires, which is generally to be heard, and states what he does not require, which is generally a solution.

**The fourth fear is the fear that the difficulty is not sufficient.** The man fears that if he discloses, the party will establish that the difficulty is ordinary, that the man is complaining without cause, and that the disclosure has made him look weak for nothing. The fear is the material of Chapter 33's man who must always have answers, and its correction is the recognition that the disclosure's purpose is the instrument and not the validation.

### Acceptance Is Not Dependence

The section now states the distinction on which the reader's conduct depends, because the fear of dependence is the strongest of the four and the distinction is the instrument that answers it.

**Dependence is an arrangement in which a party conducts a function that the man cannot conduct.** The dependent man does not hold the decision, the resource, the information, or the capacity, and its withdrawal leaves him without it.

**Acceptance is an arrangement in which a party supplies an instrument that the man uses.** The man retains the decision, the resource, the information, the capacity, and the responsibility, and the party's contribution improves the conduct of them.

The distinction is testable, and the test has three questions.

**The first question: who holds the decision?** In the arrangement the man is considering, the decision is his. The party may advise, disagree, examine, and warn, and the decision remains with the man and so does its consequence. A party who has taken the decision has moved from acceptance to dependence or to rescue.

**The second question: what remains if the party withdraws?** The man who has received an instrument retains it. The instrument may be a practice, a treatment, a route, a piece of information, an appointment, or a habit of statement, and the case material's finding is that these remain after the party's withdrawal. The man who has received a rescue does not retain it, and this is the test by which the difference is established.

**The third question: has the man's own capacity increased or decreased?** Acceptance asks the man to do something he could not do alone, and it therefore increases his capacity. Dependence conducts the function for him, and its effect is the reduction described in Chapter 20.4's substitution.

The distinction is the reason the book has insisted on the support's specificity throughout. **A support that is specific is an instrument and can be accepted. A support that is general is a rescue and must be declined.** The sentence *I will help you with anything* is generous and it is not an instrument. The sentences *I will go with you on Thursday*, *I will keep the children on Saturday while you attend*, *I will read the accounts and tell you what I see*, *I will call you on the first of each month*, and *I will not mention this to anybody* are instruments, and each of them can be accepted without any reduction of the man's position.

### The Practice of Acceptance

The section closes with the practice, because the reader who has accepted the argument requires an act.

The practice has four steps, and the case material finds them effective in the order stated.

**Step One: The inventory of the available.** The man lists the parties who are available to him: every person who has offered, suggested, asked, telephoned, or remained after a difficulty. The list is frequently longer than the man expects, and its conduct is the exercise of Section 45.7's first table. The list includes parties the man has not considered: the colleague who is not a friend, the sibling he has not spoken to in two years, the former classmate, the professional, the association's welfare officer, the member of his place of worship or his community, and the neighbour who has been kind.

**Step Two: The specific request.** The man identifies one instrument he requires and one party who could supply it, and he states the request in the form of Section 45.2's specificity. The request is short, it is specific, it names a time, and it does not require the party to conduct the man's position.

**Step Three: The reception of the response.** The party's response may be the instrument, a different instrument, a decline, or an alarm. The man's task is to receive the response without conducting the party's reaction, and to record what happened. The case material's finding is that the first request frequently produces an unexpected response and that the man's capacity to conduct the response is the determining factor in whether a second request is made.

**Step Four: The record.** The man records the request, the party, the date, and the result. The record's purpose is the correction of the man's estimate of the world, because the estimate is the thing that has prevented the request, and the record is the evidence that alters it. The case material's finding is that three recorded requests, conducted over a period of months, alter a man's estimate of the availability of support more reliably than any argument.

## 45.3 Making Peace with Personal Limitations

The section's subject is a reconciliation, and the word is chosen with care. It is not a resignation, it is not a denial, and it is not the acceptance of a diminished position that the man could alter.

The section begins with a definition. **A limitation, in this chapter's sense, is a boundary on what a man can do, be, or become, which is not within his power to remove, and which is not a matter of effort.** The definition has three components, and each one excludes a category that is frequently confused with limitation.

The first component excludes what the man could alter with effort: a competence he has not acquired, a qualification he has not obtained, a weight he has not addressed, a debt he has not restructured. These are not limitations in this section's sense; they are conditions, and they are addressed by the material of Parts III and XI.

The second component excludes what another party has imposed and what another party could remove: a dismissal that could be reversed, a contract that could be renegotiated, a judgement that could be appealed. These are grievances, and they are addressed by the material of Chapter 44.6.

The third component excludes what is temporary: a period of illness, a season of grief, a year of reduced income. These are states, and they are addressed by the material of Chapter 38.

What remains, after the three exclusions, is the section's subject: the limits that are structural, permanent, and not the man's to alter.

### The Four Categories of Limitation

The case material records four categories, and the reader should locate his own limitations in each.

**Category One: The limits of the body.** The man's height, his constitution, his chronic condition, his disability, his eyesight, his hearing, his stamina, his recovery from an injury, his age. The category is the one that men most frequently deny, and the denial is the material of Chapters 34 and 42's domain five. The case material records the specific form of the denial: the man who continues to work at the pace of his thirty-fifth year at fifty-five, the man who does not have the symptom investigated, the man who attributes his exhaustion to the workload rather than to the condition.

**Category Two: The limits of capacity.** The man's intelligence, his talent, his capacity for a particular kind of work, his aptitude for a particular profession, his ability to learn a discipline that requires a facility he does not possess. The category is painful because it frequently arrives late: the man discovers at forty-five that he will not reach the level he intended, and the discovery is conducted as a verdict on his life.

**Category Three: The limits of the past.** The years that were spent in a particular way, the education that was not obtained, the children who were not present for, the marriage that ended, the opportunity that was declined, the decision that was taken. The category is the material of Chapter 29's regrets, and its defining feature is that it is closed: no effort alters it.

**Category Four: The limits of what others will give.** The father who will not reconcile, the wife who will not continue, the child who will not speak, the colleague who will not acknowledge, the institution that will not correct its record. The category is the material of Chapter 44.6's ledger of the owed, and it is the one that men most frequently refuse to accept, because the refusal is experienced as a failure of hope.

### The Three False Settlements

The section now states the three ways in which men attempt to settle with a limitation and fail, because the reader will recognise at least one of them and the recognition is the condition of the fourth way.

**False Settlement One: Denial.** The man does not accept the limitation and conducts his position as though it did not exist. The form is familiar: the man of fifty-eight who works the hours of a man of thirty-five, the man whose back requires surgery and who continues to lift, the man who has not reached the level he intended and who attributes the fact to the market, the man whose marriage has ended and who speaks of it as a temporary separation in its fourth year.

The cost of denial is specific and it is paid later: the injury, the collapse, the years spent on an objective that was not available, the arrangement that was not made and that becomes an emergency. The case material's finding is that denial is frequently the man's first response and that it is frequently held for years.

**False Settlement Two: Resignation.** The man accepts the limitation and generalises it to his whole position. The form is also familiar: the man who is told that he will not reach the level and who concludes that he is finished; the man whose marriage has ended and who concludes that he is not a man who can be loved; the man whose body has failed in one respect and who concludes that his whole capacity is ended; the man whose son will not speak to him and who concludes that everything he built is worthless.

The cost of resignation is the loss of the position's remaining capacity. The limitation is real and it is one item, and the resignation converts it into a verdict on the whole. The case material's finding is that resignation frequently follows denial directly, and that the two are frequently conducted in the same year.

**False Settlement Three: Bargaining.** The man accepts the limitation in principle and conducts a series of arrangements whose purpose is to reverse it. The form: the man who accepts that he cannot continue at his former pace and who takes on a further commitment in order to prove otherwise; the man who accepts that the marriage has ended and who conducts a series of approaches whose purpose is the restoration; the man who accepts that his son will not speak to him and who conducts a campaign of letters, intermediaries, and occasions.

The cost of bargaining is the extension of the difficulty. The case material's finding is that bargaining is the most prolonged of the three, that it frequently consumes years, and that its conduct is frequently the material of Chapter 29.3's rumination in its behavioural form.

### The Reconciliation

The reconciliation is the fourth way, and it has four components. Each one is a specific act, and the case material finds that men who conduct the components in order make progress, and men who attempt the reconciliation as a single decision do not.

**Component One: The accurate statement.** The man states the limitation in writing, in the plainest available language, without the modifiers that soften it and without the generalisations that extend it.

The statement's form is: *I cannot [specific] any longer, or at all, and this is not within my power to alter.* The examples are deliberately ordinary: *I cannot work more than eight hours a day without a consequence that I now have to manage. I cannot reach the level of [specific position] in my profession, because the requirement includes [specific capacity] that I do not have. I cannot recover the years between 2011 and 2018. I cannot make my brother acknowledge what happened.*

The statement's value is not therapeutic. It is informational: a limitation that has not been stated accurately cannot be conducted, because the man's arrangements are being built on a position that includes a capacity he does not have.

**Component Two: The inventory of what remains.** The man states what the limitation does not affect. The inventory is not a consolation and it is not a denial of the loss. It is the establishment of the domains in which the man's capacity is intact, and the case material's finding is that the inventory is frequently longer than the man expects.

The inventory's form is a list: the competences that remain, the parties who remain, the years that are available, the work that can still be conducted, the relationships that can still be repaired, the contributions that the man can still make. The list is written, and each item is specific.

**Component Three: The reallocation.** The man alters his arrangements to fit the limitation. The reallocation is the section's operative act, and the case material's finding is that it is the component that distinguishes the reconciliation from an attitude.

The reallocation has three forms. The first is the **reduction**: the man ceases to conduct what the limitation makes impossible, and states the cessation to the parties who are affected. The second is the **substitution**: the man conducts the function by a different means, which is the material of Chapter 38.5's reconstruction and Chapter 39.4's competence. The third is the **delegation**: the man establishes another party to conduct the function, with the man's accountability retained, which is the material of Chapter 45.2's acceptance.

**Component Four: The grief.** The man conducts the grief for what the limitation has cost, and the grief is not optional. A limitation that removes a capacity removes a version of the man's life, and the version requires the conduct of Chapter 30 rather than a decision. The case material's finding is that men who conduct the first three components and omit the fourth frequently find that the reconciliation has not taken effect: the arrangements have been altered and the man continues to conduct the loss as a private grievance.

The grief's form is ordinary: the statement of what has been lost, the party who receives it, the period in which the loss is felt, and the occasion on which it is acknowledged. The case material records men who conducted the grief in a single evening with a single party, and men who conducted it over two years; both are within the material's range.

### The Reconciliation and the Standard

The section closes with a distinction that the reader should hold, because the reconciliation is frequently heard as a reduction of the standard.

**The reconciliation does not reduce the standard. It relocates it.**

The man's standard — the obligations of Chapter 41.6, the four competences of Chapter 39.4, the markers of Chapter 44.3 — is not a standard of unlimited capacity. It is a standard of conduct: provision, protection, competence, accountability, presence, repair, and the accurate statement of his own position. A man who cannot work twelve hours a day can conduct every one of them. A man who will not reach his profession's highest level can conduct every one of them. A man whose marriage has ended can conduct every one of them. A man whose body has failed in a specific respect can conduct every one of them, provided that he does not require of himself the capacity that the limitation has removed.

The finding is stated as the section's closing claim: **the men who reconcile with their limitations frequently conduct the standard more completely than the men who deny them**, because the denial consumes the capacity that the standard requires, and the reconciliation releases it. The man who accepts that he has eight hours a day has eight hours. The man who requires twelve has four and pays for them with the following week.
