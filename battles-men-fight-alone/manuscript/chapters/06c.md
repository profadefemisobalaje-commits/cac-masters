## 6.4 Why Some Men Resist Seeking Help

Men delay seeking treatment for depression for reasons that are specific, researchable, and largely rational within the terms of the calculation they are making. It is of no use to tell a man that he is being irrational. It is useful to answer each objection with information, and that is what this section does.

### The Eight Objections, and Their Answers

**1. "It is not that serious. I am still functioning."**

Functioning is not a measure of severity. Men with moderately severe depression frequently continue to work, provide, and manage, at a cost that is invisible and that is paid in sleep, health, relationships, and years. A better test is the one given in Section 6.2: how many of the features are present, and for how long?

**2. "It will pass on its own."**

Mild episodes frequently do resolve, and this is why the advice to wait is not always wrong. But episodes lasting more than a few weeks, or accompanied by several symptoms, or with a history of previous episodes, generally do not resolve without treatment. The practical rule: if it has lasted more than two to four weeks and is affecting sleep, work, or relationships, have it assessed.

**3. "I do not want to be a person who is on medication."**

Not every case requires medication. Psychological treatments are effective, and for mild and moderate cases they are frequently the first choice. Where medication is appropriate, it is generally prescribed for a defined period, its effects and side effects are monitored, and it is tapered under supervision. Many men who hold this objection accept either a talking treatment or a medication trial once the process is explained.

**4. "If it is on my record, it will affect my work."**

Confidentiality is a professional obligation in all health settings, and occupational health records in most jurisdictions are treated separately from personnel records. The practical risks of an untreated depression — reduced performance, errors, absence, resignation, or dismissal — are considerably larger than the risks of confidential treatment. Where a man has genuine reason for concern about records, he is entitled to ask about confidentiality explicitly before speaking.

**5. "Talking about it will make it worse."**

Disclosing to somebody who responds badly can be discouraging. Disclosing to a trained professional is a different experience, and the concern misunderstands what a consultation consists of. In practice, most men describe the first consultation as a relief, and the most common post-assessment feeling, in the author's experience, is not exposure but surprise at how ordinary the conversation was.

**6. "I have nothing to be depressed about."**

Depression is not a verdict on circumstances. It occurs in men with difficult lives and in men with comfortable ones, and the presence or absence of reasons does not determine the presence of the condition. This objection is often the same as the shame described in Chapter 5, expressed as an argument.

**7. "It is a spiritual matter, and I will deal with it in that way."**

Some men will resolve the matter through their religious practice, community, and personal discipline, and some of them will do so successfully. Without entering into questions of belief, the practical point is that a man may address both dimensions at once: religious practice, community support, and professional treatment are not mutually exclusive, and the most common tragedy in this area is the man who pursues one route exclusively for three years while the condition worsens. If prayers, counsel, and community support have not helped in two months, add professional assessment.

**8. "I do not know what to say or who to see."**

This is an information problem rather than a motivation problem, and it is easily solved. Section 5.5 of the previous chapter lists the routes; Chapter 35 describes how to make the approach; Appendix Q lists services current at the time of publication; and the exercise at the end of this chapter produces the written account that can be handed to a clinician.

### The Fourth Objection in Practice: A Note on Confidentiality

Because the fourth objection — the fear of consequences for work, family, or reputation — is the one that men mention most often in private conversation, a second comment is warranted.

Health professionals are bound by confidentiality rules in every jurisdiction surveyed for this book, subject to narrow exceptions that generally concern immediate danger to life. Beyond the legal rules, the practical experience of most men is that the confidentiality of a consultation is respected in practice, and that the people most likely to learn of a man's treatment are those he chooses to tell. The exception, and it is a genuine one, is small communities and families in which discretion cannot be relied upon. In such circumstances, men frequently prefer services outside the immediate area, telephone or online services, or a general physician with no social connection to the household. These options are consistently underused, and there is no reason not to use them.

## 6.5 Supportive Relationships and Evidence-Based Treatment

Two things help a man with depression, and the best outcomes come from both together: treatment, and support. This section sets out what each consists of.

### What Treatment Involves

The treatments with the strongest evidence base for depression are set out in Figure 6.4. In outline:

**Talking treatments.** Cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal therapy, behavioural activation, and related approaches have substantial evidence for depression, particularly mild and moderate cases. The typical course ranges from six to twenty sessions, and the treatment involves specific work rather than general conversation: identifying patterns of thought and behaviour that maintain low mood, planning activity, addressing difficulties in relationships, and practising skills between sessions.

**Medication.** Antidepressant medication is effective for moderate and severe depression and is generally recommended in combination with talking treatment. Men frequently have inaccurate beliefs about how it works, how quickly it takes effect (typically several weeks), and how long it is continued (usually months after improvement, to prevent relapse, and then tapered). These questions should be asked of the prescriber, and a man who receives a prescription without an explanation of the plan is entitled to ask for one.

**Combined treatment.** For moderate and severe depression, the combination of medication and psychological treatment is frequently the most effective option.

**Structured self-management.** Behavioural activation, exercise, sleep restoration, reduction of alcohol, and activity scheduling are components of treatment rather than alternatives to it. They are effective, they can be started immediately, and they are the basis of Appendix C of this book.

**Practical intervention.** Debt advice, employment support, housing assistance, family mediation, and the resolution of an acute practical crisis are, in many cases, part of the treatment, because they remove the continuing load that maintains the condition. Chapter 12 and Appendix D address the financial dimension directly.

**Crisis planning.** Where there is any risk of self-harm, a written plan specifying what the man will do, whom he will contact, and what the people around him should do is required. Chapter 34 and Appendix B provide the framework.

### What Support Involves

Support is not the same as treatment, and it is not a substitute for it. It consists of four elements, and they are the responsibility of the man's family, friends, and community.

**Company without pressure.** Regular contact that does not require the man to discuss anything. The most valuable form of support for most men in depression is presence: a friend who comes, sits, walks, or works alongside, without requiring an account of the condition.

**Practical relief.** Help with tasks: transport, a meal, a school run, an hour of cover at the shop, assistance with a form. Practical help reduces the load, which is a treatment-adjacent intervention, and it is more acceptable to men than emotional assistance.

**Encouragement to continue.** The most useful support in the middle weeks of treatment is the maintenance of the routine: a reminder of the appointment, an offer of transport to it, a check that the medication is being taken, and an unwillingness to accept the first refusal.

**Honesty about risk.** If a man has spoken of not being able to continue, being a burden, or having no reason to live, the appropriate response is to ask directly about suicide, to remain with him, to remove means, and to contact professional or emergency help. Appendix Q describes this in detail, and Chapter 34 sets out the framework. Silence on this point is the most dangerous form of consideration.


## 6.6 What Families, Friends, and Employers Can Do

This section is addressed to those around a man with depression, and it is included because the two most influential actors in most cases are a spouse and an employer, neither of whom has any clinical training.

### What a Spouse or Partner Can Do

Four actions make a measurable difference.

**Ask direct questions about behaviour, not feelings.** *You have been waking early for weeks. What is happening?* is more useful than *Are you depressed?* The first is observable and therefore difficult to dismiss; the second invites a denial that both parties know is inaccurate.

**Ask about suicide.** Where a man has said that he cannot continue, that he is a burden, or that others would be better off without him, the appropriate response is to ask directly whether he has thought of ending his life. This does not plant the idea; the evidence on this point is clear, and asking is the standard way in which risk is identified. Chapter 34 and Appendix Q set out what to do next.

**Attend the first appointment.** Men who are taken to a first consultation — literally accompanied — complete far more of the process than men who are merely advised to go. The company removes the friction, and it also removes the private negotiation in which shame usually wins.

**Do not accept the first refusal.** Most men refuse help once or twice before accepting it. A refusal followed by silence is read as agreement. A refusal followed by a repeated, kind, and specific offer — *I will come with you on Thursday morning; the appointment will take an hour* — generally ends in attendance.

### What an Employer Can Do

Depression costs employers substantially through absence, reduced performance, and turnover. The interventions that work are practical.

**Confidential support, available without justification.** An employee assistance programme, a named clinician, or a confidential telephone service that a man can use without explaining himself to a supervisor.

**Supervision that responds to change rather than to output.** The most useful signal available to a manager is a change in a man's temperament or participation — increased irritability, withdrawal from meetings, lateness, reduced tolerance of noise — rather than a change in his figures, which frequently fall last.

**Flexibility for treatment.** Appointments occur during working hours. A man who must choose between attending treatment and explaining his absence to a supervisor will frequently choose neither and abandon the treatment.

**Protection of confidentiality.** Where an employee believes that a request for help will be recorded against him, he will not make the request, and the organisation will bear the cost of an untreated illness in the form of errors, absence, and eventual resignation.

A final word to employers is warranted, because it is increasingly supported by occupational health research: the most effective single measure an organisation can take to reduce the toll of depression among male employees is to remove the assumption that a man who asks for help is not fit for responsibility. That assumption is not a policy, and it is enforced by ordinary remarks, by promotion decisions, and by how senior men speak about difficulty in public. It can be changed, and its removal is cheaper than the alternative.
