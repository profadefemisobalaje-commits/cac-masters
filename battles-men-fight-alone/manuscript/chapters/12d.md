## 12.6 Building a Sustainable Life

The final section sets out what a sustainable working life consists of in practice, and it is deliberately concrete, because the subject attracts generalities.

### The Seven Elements

**1. A ceiling on hours, established and measured.** The figure differs by person and situation; the requirement is that it exists, that it is written, and that actual hours are measured against it monthly. Men who do this report that the measurement itself produces about half of the reduction, because unrecorded hours are invisible even to the man working them.

**2. Sleep protected as a component of performance.** Seven hours as a working minimum for most adults, with the practical measures in Appendix N. This is the single highest-yield change available, and it is the one most frequently deferred.

**3. One physical practice, maintained.** The evidence for the effect of regular physical activity on mood, sleep, cardiovascular risk, and cognitive function is extensive and consistent. Its practical advantages for men are that it is measurable, that it is not a treatment, and that it provides a legitimate reason to be unavailable that no household questions.

**4. Medical care attended rather than deferred.** A routine check, treatment of chronic conditions, and a dental and eye examination where relevant. Men in this pattern typically have a list of deferred items, and the list should be worked through deliberately, item by item, with dates.

**5. One relationship in which the man is not the provider.** Described in Chapter 10, and repeated here because overwork eliminates friendships first. A sustainable life includes at least one relationship in which the man is known outside his productive function.

**6. A defined minimum for family presence.** Not the residual time left after work, which is zero, but a scheduled minimum: an hour a day with children, a fixed weekly engagement with a partner, a monthly activity with each child. Chapter 17 develops the practice.

**7. A structure for the transition out of full work.** The most neglected element of a sustainable life. A man who has organised his identity around output will find the end of working life disorienting unless the arrangement is planned in advance. This includes financial provision, but the more commonly neglected element is the non-financial one: what the man will do, and who he will be, when the output stops.

### The Practical Sequence

The elements above are not achievable simultaneously by a man working eighty hours a week. The sequence that men report as workable has four steps.

**Step One: sleep and one medical item.** Address sleep first, and complete one deferred medical matter. These two changes are attainable without altering any commitment to anybody else, and they produce a noticeable improvement within a month.

**Step Two: the hour count and the ceiling.** Record actual hours for two weeks, establish a ceiling, and choose one category to eliminate. This step requires the explanation referenced in Section 12.3.

**Step Three: family presence and one relationship.** Schedule the minimum allocations, and reinstate one friendship. This step requires the availability arrangement to be stated rather than merely practised.

**Step Four: structure and transition.** Establish the non-work element that will supply identity — an interest, a community role, a craft, a religious practice, a mentoring commitment — and begin a plan for financial provision and for the shape of later working life.

### The Annual Capacity Review

The arrangements described above decay. A ceiling accepted in January is quietly exceeded by June; a scheduled evening is cancelled four weeks in succession and then abandoned; a delegated function is taken back after a single failure. For this reason the sustainable life requires one maintenance mechanism, and it should be annual, brief, and written.

The review has six items.

1. **Hours.** The average of the last three months, against the ceiling. If the ceiling has been exceeded for three consecutive months, one category of work is removed in the following month, and the removal is a decision rather than an intention.
2. **Sleep.** The average of the last month, against the seven-hour working minimum, together with any change in the pattern — early waking, difficulty falling asleep, unrefreshing sleep.
3. **Health.** The status of every chronic condition, the completion of the appointments identified in the previous year's review, and any new symptom that has been present for more than a month.
4. **Presence.** The hours spent with each child and with a partner, measured rather than estimated, against the minimum allocations. Men are consistently surprised by this figure, in both directions.
5. **Relationships.** The number of non-work relationships maintained during the year, and when each was last contacted. Where the number has fallen below three, the review should identify a specific action rather than a general intention.
6. **Provision and transition.** The reserve position, the retirement provision, and the non-financial plan for the end of full working life. The last of these is the item that every man defers and that determines the quality of the decade after retirement.

The review takes an hour, and it should be scheduled at the same point each year, with the date confirmed in advance. Its value is not the diagnosis, which most men can anticipate, but the interruption of the drift: it converts a set of arrangements that are slowly being abandoned into a set of arrangements that are either being kept or deliberately changed.

### The Household Conversation

A sustainable life is not constructed alone, and in most cases it requires the agreement of a household. The conversation has four parts and should be held deliberately rather than in fragments.

1. **The position.** What the current pattern is doing: hours, sleep, health indicators, and the specific symptoms.
2. **The change.** What will be different, in specific terms — which hours, which days, which engagements, which contributions.
3. **The consequence.** What the household will lose, if anything: income, standard, convenience, or a specific arrangement.
4. **The purpose.** What the change is intended to preserve: the man's health, the marriage, the children's experience of him, and the household's income over the next twenty years rather than the next twelve months.

Appendix F provides the structure for this conversation, and Chapter 18 addresses its place within marriage generally.

## 12.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Booked the Appointment Three Times

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in occupational medicine, family practice, and employment counselling.

**Sunday is forty-nine, a transport company's operations manager in Lagos, responsible for a fleet of sixty vehicles and thirty-eight staff.** He has been in the role for eleven years. He supports a household of six, his mother, and a nephew's university fees. He earns well by any standard, and his obligations absorb the whole of it.

For four years, Sunday worked between sixty-five and eighty hours a week. The pattern developed during a period when the company lost two contracts and reduced staff, with the result that his department's establishment fell from fifty-one to thirty-eight while the work increased.

Three specific practices maintained the pattern. He personally supervised the dispatch of every vehicle, because a dispatch error costs the company money and his reputation. He answered the telephone at any hour. And he conducted the morning briefing at six o'clock himself, six days a week, because he believed that attendance at the briefing was a test of his commitment.

In the third year, his blood pressure was found to be elevated at a routine insurance examination. He was advised to return for assessment, and he did not. In the fourth year, he began to have episodes of chest tightness on exertion, which he attributed to fatigue. He booked an appointment at a private clinic three times, and rescheduled it three times: the first because a vehicle was involved in an accident, the second because the company's managing director was travelling and Sunday was covering, and the third because his son's school had an event.

His wife, Kemi, became aware that something was wrong through three observations: that he had begun sleeping with the television on, having previously required silence; that he complained of the noise of the children in a manner that was new; and that he had stopped attending the monthly meeting of his church's men's fellowship, where he had been a member for nine years. She raised it twice. He answered, on both occasions, that the year was difficult.

The event that forced the change was not dramatic. In November, during a morning briefing, Sunday lost the thread of what he was saying in the middle of a sentence, in front of fourteen people, and had to ask a colleague to continue. He was, in his own description, terrified. He went, the same day, to a hospital.

The assessment found: severe hypertension requiring immediate treatment; sleep apnoea, which explained a substantial part of the fatigue and which he had never considered because he did not know that snoring, witnessed by Kemi for years, was a clinical sign; and a moderate depressive episode, presenting as irritability and the loss of interest in his fellowship and his family. He was treated for all three: medication for the blood pressure, investigation and treatment for the apnoea, and a course of psychological treatment with an antidepressant.

The organisational changes came after the clinical ones, and their origin is worth recording. Sunday's first step, after his treatment began, was to tell his managing director the position — which he had avoided doing for a year — and the managing director's response was to appoint a deputy operations manager within three weeks, a step that could have been taken at any time in the previous four years and that had never been raised. The morning briefing moved to seven, and Sunday stopped attending it personally on two of the six days. His personal dispatch supervision was transferred, with training, over five months.

Eighteen months later, his blood pressure was controlled, his sleep had improved substantially, and he described the arrangement in the following terms: *I thought the company needed the hours. What it needed was the job. When I was moved out of the way on two mornings a week, nobody noticed, and the figures were the same. What I had been protecting for four years was not the company's performance. It was my own idea that I was the only one who could do it.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The physical condition was present and unexamined for a year.** Elevated blood pressure found at an insurance examination, followed by twelve months of deferral and three rescheduled appointments. This is one of the most common and most consequential patterns in men's health, and the delay is the mechanism by which treatable conditions become serious ones.

**A treatable sleep disorder was present and unrecognised.** Sleep apnoea is common, is frequently unrecognised in men, and produces fatigue, impaired concentration, and cardiovascular risk. It is worth naming in this chapter because it is a physical cause of the exhaustion that men attribute to workload, and it is diagnosed by investigation rather than by effort.

**The depression presented in the male register.** Irritability about noise, the loss of interest in a longstanding commitment, sleeping with the television on, and the loss of capability in front of colleagues — with no report of sadness at any point.

**The organisation's constraint was self-imposed.** The most instructive finding in the case is that a deputy was appointed within three weeks of the conversation and that the figures were unchanged after Sunday's personal supervision was reduced. For four years, the arrangement had been maintained by the man's belief that it could not be changed, rather than by the organisation's requirement.

**The change required a disclosure.** The appointment, the deputy, and the reduced hours all followed from one conversation with the managing director that Sunday had avoided for a year. The disclosure was not about his feelings; it was about his health, delivered with a proposed arrangement. This is the most effective form of disclosure available to a man in Sunday's position, and it is the method described in Chapter 35.

## 12.8 Practical Exercise: The Capacity Audit and the Sustainable Week

This exercise takes two hours at the outset and fifteen minutes weekly thereafter. It has five parts.

### Part One: The Capacity Audit

Answer the following in writing, with figures rather than impressions.

| Item | Your answer |
| --- | --- |
| Average hours worked per week over the last three months | |
| Average hours of sleep per working night | |
| Days of leave taken in the last twenty-four months, not counting public holidays | |
| Medical appointments deferred, and for how long | |
| Chronic conditions currently untreated or under-treated | |
| Alcohol consumed in an average week | |
| Days per week with any physical activity of thirty minutes or more | |
| Hours per week spent with children, excluding travel and television | |
| Hours per month spent with friends, excluding work contacts | |
| Days in the last three months in which I felt genuinely rested | |

The purpose of the table is to replace an impression with a record. Men who complete it are frequently startled by two or three of the figures, and the startlement is the beginning of the exercise.

### Part Two: The Exhaustion Test

From Section 12.1, mark the signs that apply: exhaustion not repaired by rest; disturbed sleep structure; flatness or irritability; unexplained physical symptoms; reduced cognitive performance; loss of interest in everything. Then apply the criteria from Chapters 5 and 6 and Appendix B, and note whether professional assessment is indicated. If two or more of the criteria for assessment are met, the exercise should pause here and the appointment should be made.

### Part Three: The Ceiling

Write your ceiling, in hours per week, and the date on which it takes effect. Then identify the one category of work that will be removed to meet it: weekend work, evening communication, the additional engagements, the travel, or the personal performance of a task that could be delegated.

### Part Four: The Minimum Allocations

Write the minimum allocation for each domain, and enter them in your calendar as recurring commitments.

- Sleep: ______ hours on working nights.
- Physical activity: ______ sessions per week.
- Partner: ______ per week, scheduled.
- Each child: ______ per week, scheduled individually.
- Friends: ______ per month.
- Medical: appointments to be made this month — list them, with dates.
- Rest: ______ per week, scheduled.

### Part Five: The Weekly Review

Fifteen minutes, the same day each week. Four questions: What were my actual hours? Which allocations were met and which were missed? What caused the misses? What changes next week?

The review is the mechanism that makes the whole exercise operational, and the evidence from behaviour-change practice is clear that the review, rather than the plan, is what produces the result.

## 12.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I have too much responsibility to reduce my hours. What do I do?"**

Reduce the categories that carry no responsibility, and protect the ones that do. ' Responsibilities' is a category that can be audited. Men who complete the audit in Part One frequently find that a substantial portion of their hours is consumed by activity that is either avoidable or delegable, and that the truly non-negotiable core is smaller than they assumed.

**"If I slow down, someone else will take my position."**

That is a real risk in some organisations and an imagined one in most. The test is the one Sunday's case demonstrates: ask for the arrangement, and observe the response. Where the response is genuinely punitive, the information is important, because it identifies an employer for whom the man's health is not a consideration — a finding that is better made at forty-nine than at fifty-nine.

**"I cannot sleep. What do I do first?"**

Establish the cause before attempting remedies. Snoring and witnessed pauses in breathing require investigation for sleep apnoea. Early waking with dread suggests anxiety or depression and warrants assessment. Poor sleep hygiene, alcohol, and caffeine are the most common correctable factors. Appendix N sets out the practical sequence, and Chapter 37 addresses sleep in detail.

**"How do I stop feeling guilty about resting?"**

By defining the rest as maintenance and by measuring the outcome. Men who schedule rest and then observe their own performance over two months almost always find that the effect on output is neutral or positive. The guilt is not reduced by argument; it is reduced by evidence.

**"What if my blood pressure or another condition is already a problem?"**

Then the priority order changes, and medical treatment comes before any organisational change, because the risk is immediate rather than cumulative. Appendix B and Chapter 34 provide the framework, and the appointment should be made this week rather than this quarter.

**"My father worked like this and lived to eighty."**

Some people do. The relevant question is not what is survivable but what is likely, and the evidence on prolonged overwork, untreated hypertension, and untreated sleep problems is consistent. In addition, a comparison with a previous generation should account for a different pattern of work, different diet, and — importantly — the fact that the men who did not survive are not available to be cited.
