## 10.5 Shared Financial Planning

The most reliable protection against provider overload is a household that plans its finances together. This section sets out the mechanisms, which are simple and which require almost no financial expertise.

### The Monthly Budget Meeting

The single most effective practice is a monthly meeting, of about forty-five minutes, at a fixed time, between the man and his partner, with four agenda items.

1. **The position.** Income received, obligations paid, and the balance. Figures only.
2. **The obligations list.** Every obligation, including those to relatives, with its amount, its duration, and its review date. This is where concealment re-enters if it is permitted to, which is why the list is written rather than remembered.
3. **The decisions.** Any new obligation, any reduction, and any change to the allocation. New obligations are agreed rather than announced.
4. **The next month.** The specific payments, the amounts required, and any event requiring expenditure.

Children of secondary-school age and above can be included in a simplified version of the first and fourth items, which has the secondary benefit of teaching financial literacy in the only context in which it is learned: a household's actual decisions.

Three rules make the meeting work. It is held whether or not there is anything new, because its regularity is what makes difficult disclosures possible. It concerns figures and plans rather than conduct, which keeps it from becoming an accounting of blame. And it concludes with the position written down and kept, so that the household has a record.

### The Priority List

A written order of priority for the household, agreed in advance, prevents most of the arguments that occur during a shortfall. A workable order, which households should adapt, runs as follows.

| Priority | Category | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Housing: rent or mortgage, and utilities | Loss of housing is the most disruptive single event |
| 2 | Food and basic household requirements | Non-negotiable |
| 3 | Medical care | Postponement produces larger costs later |
| 4 | Education | A commitment, and an investment with returns |
| 5 | Secured and legally enforced debts | Where default produces asset loss or proceedings |
| 6 | Transport required for income | The means of producing income belongs before discretionary items |
| 7 | Family support obligations | Real, and subordinate to the household's survival |
| 8 | Community obligations | Social capital, and reducible in a way that most others are not |
| 9 | Discretionary expenditure and display | The category that absorbs the shortfall in a managed household |

Table: A priority order for household expenditure. The essential feature is that the order is agreed in advance, in a calm month, so that it does not have to be negotiated during a crisis.

### The Written Schedule of Family Support

For every ongoing support obligation, record four items: the recipient, the amount, the duration, and the review date. The document serves three purposes: it establishes the total, which is otherwise invisible; it converts open-ended obligations into arrangements; and it makes the support visible to the man's partner, which is the single change most frequently described by men as having reduced conflict in their marriages.

### The Reserve

The purpose of a reserve is not to accumulate wealth; it is to convert a crisis into an inconvenience. Three to six months of essential household expenditure, accumulated in a stable account and not used for any other purpose, changes the consequences of a job loss, a medical episode, or a family emergency entirely. Men who have experienced a serious financial failure describe the building of a reserve as the single change they most wished they had made earlier. Where income is low and irregular, the reserve can begin as a very small monthly set-aside, and the practice matters more than the amount.

### The Structure of Accounts

A practical arrangement that reduces conflict and increases clarity, used by many households, is a simple three-way split: a household account for shared, essential expenditure, to which both partners contribute in agreed proportions; a personal account for each adult, which requires no justification; and a reserve account, which requires both parties' agreement for any withdrawal. This structure resolves the most common source of resentment — a partner spending from shared resources without discussion — without requiring either adult to justify personal expenditure.

### Retirement and the Man's Own Position

One item is systematically omitted from household planning, and it is the one that most affects the man's own later life: his own retirement provision and health cover. A man of forty-five supporting nine people frequently has no pension arrangement, no health insurance, and no assets held in his own name. The consequence arrives predictably: men in their sixties who are no longer able to work, who have no provision, and whose children are themselves under pressure. The remedy is simple and unpopular: a fixed monthly allocation to the man's own provision, treated as an obligation of equal standing to the others, from as early as possible. It is also the only item in this chapter that the reader cannot postpone without a consequence that he will personally experience.

## 10.6 Preventing Provider Burnout

Provider burnout is the condition produced by prolonged provision beyond capacity without recovery, and it is described in Chapter 33 in its general form. Its specific features in the provider role are these: exhaustion that does not resolve with rest; a loss of satisfaction in meeting obligations; a hardening of attitude towards the people being supported; a sense of being trapped; and a growing conviction that no amount of effort will change the situation.

### The Cost of the Arrangement to the Man Himself

The practices in this chapter are frequently resisted on the grounds that they serve other people. This final subsection states the position plainly: the arrangement of unlimited provision is as damaging to the man as to anybody else, and in four specific ways.

**The loss of the man's own future.** Where the household's requirements, the extended family's requirements, and the community's requirements are each met from the same income, the residual is zero, and the residual is what funds the man's own retirement, health provision, and later independence. A man of fifty who has provided for everybody and has no position of his own has not behaved generously; he has behaved imprudently, and the imprudence will be paid for in the third act of his life, when his own capacities are reduced and his obligations, in many cases, are not.

**The conversion of relationships into obligations.** Where every relationship is conducted through the mechanism of provision, the man's value to others is defined by what he supplies. The consequence is specific and painful: when the supply reduces, the man's relationships reduce, and the man discovers that he has been surrounded by clients rather than relatives. This discovery is frequently made in illness, in unemployment, or in retirement, which is the worst possible moment to make it.

**The erosion of the man's capacity to be cared for.** A man who has provided without limit for twenty years has had no practice in receiving. When his own requirement arrives — illness, incapacity, bereavement — he is unable to accept support, and the household has no practice in giving it, because the arrangement has never been reciprocal. The pattern described in Chapter 3, in which men cannot ask, is frequently the product of an arrangement in which they have never been asked.

**The loss of the man's own purposes.** The final cost is the least visible and the most consequential. A man defined entirely by the provision of support has no answer to the question of what he wants for his own life, and the question tends to arrive in the decade after fifty with considerable force. The practices in this chapter, and the personal definition in Chapter 11, are the two instruments by which the man's own purposes are preserved alongside his obligations.

### The Warning Sequence

The sequence by which provider burnout develops is worth setting out explicitly, because it allows early intervention. Chapter 2 gave the general sequence for responsibility without support; the provider-specific version is as follows.

1. **Full engagement.** The man meets all obligations and takes on new ones. He is effective and well regarded.
2. **Silent absorption of shortfall.** The obligations exceed the income, and the difference is met by the man's own rest, medical care, and provision. Nobody knows.
3. **Loss of discretionary capacity.** Holidays, leisure, friendship, and personal expenditure are eliminated. The man attributes this to the economic situation.
4. **Debt accumulation.** Borrowing to meet obligations, followed by borrowing to service borrowing.
5. **Diminished effectiveness at work.** Sleep disturbance, errors, reduced concentration, and the beginning of reputational consequences — the very thing the arrangement was designed to prevent.
6. **Withdrawal and hardening.** Reduced contact with the extended family; reduced patience with dependants; a growing private resentment.
7. **Break point.** An event — an illness, a lost job, an exposure, a collapse in a relationship — that exceeds remaining capacity.

### The Protective Practices

Five practices have a good record in preventing burnout for men in this position. They are listed in order of effect.

**Establish a total and a limit.** Covered above; it is the foundation of everything else. A man who does not know his total cannot know when it is exceeded.

**Establish one thing that is never sacrificed.** Sleep, medical care, or half a day a week. The item matters less than its status: an inviolable allocation, defended against every claim, which functions as the boundary marker for the whole arrangement. Men who maintain one such item report that it becomes the anchor for the rest.

**Convert open-ended obligations into arrangements with ends.** Duration and review dates, as described above. The psychological difference between an obligation with an end and an obligation without one is substantial.

**Maintain one channel outside the provider role.** A friendship, a sport, a group, or an activity in which the man is known for something other than his capacity to pay. Chapter 4 established the reasons this matters, and provider overload makes it more important, not less.

**Share the position with one person who is not a dependant.** The single strongest protective factor reported by men under prolonged provider strain is the existence of one relationship in which the man is not the provider: not a spouse, not a parent, not a child, and not a relative who receives support. A friend, a peer, a mentor, a professional, or a peer group serves the purpose. If the reader's own list of such relationships is empty, that fact is the most important finding of this chapter, and Appendix J is the place to begin.
