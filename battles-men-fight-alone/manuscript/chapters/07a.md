# The Fear of Failure {.chapter num="7" short="The Fear of Failure"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why the pressure to succeed operates so powerfully on men, and describe the specific forms it takes in family, work, and community life.
2. Analyse the mechanics of comparison — how a man's reference group shifts and why satisfaction declines even as achievement rises.
3. Describe the effect of financial failure on self-esteem, and distinguish between the loss of income, the loss of standing, and the loss of identity.
4. Recognise perfectionism and the fear of judgement as distinct but connected patterns, and identify their costs in work and family life.
5. Apply a structured method for recovering from a serious disappointment or failure, including the assessment of the actual position and the management of the account given to others.
6. Complete the separation of personal worth from performance, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.

## Introduction

A man loses a contract. The loss is serious: about forty per cent of his annual turnover, and with it the ability to service a loan he took eighteen months ago. He tells his wife that the client has postponed. He tells his brother nothing at all. He goes to the office every day and works on other prospects, and he performs, in every visible respect, exactly as he did before.

What has actually happened to him is not primarily financial. It is a verdict. Somewhere in the process of losing the contract he has accepted a proposition that he did not consciously formulate and would deny if asked: *I am the man who wins contracts. The man who loses this contract is a different man.* From that point he is not managing a business difficulty. He is concealing a change of identity, and every practical step that would address the business difficulty — telling his wife the truth, speaking to the bank, asking a competitor for a partnership arrangement, reducing his household expenditure — threatens to make the identity change public.

This chapter deals with the fear of failure, and it begins with the observation that the difficulty has less to do with failure itself than with the fusion between a man's performance and his sense of self. Failure is unavoidable. Every man who attempts anything will lose contracts, miss targets, be rejected, be passed over, make mistakes, and, at some point, disappoint people who depend upon him. The question is not how to avoid these events but what they are taken to mean, and whether a man can survive them with his judgment, his relationships, and his capacity to act intact.

## 7.1 The Pressure to Succeed at All Costs

The pressure to succeed is not a modern invention, but its form has changed in ways that matter. It is worth setting out what the pressure consists of before examining its effects.

### The Components of the Pressure

Four components operate together, and a man under pressure is usually responding to all four at once.

**The economic component.** For a large proportion of men, success is not a preference but a condition of household survival. The consequences of failure are concrete: school fees unpaid, medical treatment postponed, rent arrears, a family's standing in the community damaged. Where the man is the principal earner in an extended family, the economic calculation is more demanding still, as Chapter 10 examines. A book that treated the fear of failure as a purely psychological matter would be ignoring the fact that for many men the fear is proportionate to a real risk.

**The social component.** In most communities, a man's standing and his visible success are closely connected. Vehicles, houses, weddings, contributions at ceremonies, and the education of children are public evidence, and the evidence is read by neighbours, relatives, colleagues, and former schoolmates. The social dimension means that failure is not merely a private setback; it is an alteration of one's position in a system of regard that a man did not design and cannot opt out of.

**The relational component.** Men frequently experience their value to their families as conditional upon provision. This perception is not always accurate, and it is powerful. It produces the specific fear described in Chapter 10: that a man who cannot provide will lose his place in his own household — not through any decision by his wife or children, but because the household's structure presumes his usefulness.

**The internal component.** The fourth component is the one that this chapter is chiefly concerned with, and it is the one that men least often acknowledge: the man's own standard, internalised from childhood and carried without examination. The internal standard is frequently harsher than anything demanded by the family, the employer, or the community, and it is the component that keeps the pressure in place after the economic and social risks have been reduced.

### The Locked-In Effect

A distinctive feature of the pressure to succeed is that it reduces the man's available options precisely at the moment he most needs them. The mechanism deserves to be named, because it explains why men take risks that appear obviously inadvisable to outsiders. It has three steps.

**Success raises expectations.** A man who has earned a certain level of income is expected to maintain it. A man who has educated three children is expected to educate the fourth. A man who has been generous at a funeral is expected to be generous at the next.

**The cost of the expected standard rises.** Mortgage or rent, education, medical costs, transport, and obligations to relatives tend to rise faster than income, particularly in economies with high inflation.

**Risk becomes unavoidable.** To maintain the standard, the man must take on more debt, accept a larger contract with a larger penalty, invest in an uncertain opportunity, or work in circumstances he would previously have refused. Any reduction of ambition now requires a public admission that the standard is being lowered, which is precisely the admission he has organised his life to avoid.

The result is a locked-in state described by many men in mid-life: a position that cannot be sustained without risk, cannot be reduced without disclosure, and cannot be discussed with anyone. Figure 7.3 sets out the sequence by which this state produces a private crisis.

### What Is Actually Being Feared

Men who examine their fear of failure discover, with some surprise, that it is rarely a fear of poverty. It is a fear of one or more of four specific things, and the four require different responses.

| What is feared | What it looks like | What actually addresses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Loss of standing** | Being spoken of as a man who has declined; losing the position accompanying a role | Publicly redefining the standard; association with a different reference group |
| **Loss of usefulness** | Fear of becoming a burden; of no longer being needed by family or employer | Renewed contribution in a different form; mentoring; practical service |
| **Loss of the family's trust** | The fear that dependants will be disappointed, or will transfer their dependence elsewhere | Early, honest disclosure; involving the family in the plan |
| **Loss of self-respect** | The private conviction of having failed as a man | Separating worth from performance; correcting the standard; professional support |

Table: The four objects of the fear of failure. Identifying the specific object matters because the remedy for one is not the remedy for another.

::: keypoint
The fear of failure is rarely about money. It is usually about standing, usefulness, trust, and self-respect, and it is only the first of those that can be restored by earning more.
:::

## 7.2 Comparing Personal Progress with Others

Comparison with others is a universal human activity, and for men in mid-life it acquires a particular intensity. This section describes the mechanism, assesses the harm it does, and sets out what can realistically be done about it.

### Why the Comparison Happens

Four features of adult male life make comparison difficult to avoid.

**Age-mates provide a natural benchmark.** Men who grew up together, attended the same schools, and entered the workforce at the same time provide an obvious reference group, and the comparison is made in public: at reunions, at funerals, at weddings, and in the family house.

**Visibility is high.** In most communities, the markers of a man's position are visible: the state of his house, the school his children attend, the car, the contributions he makes, the employment of his relatives. A man who wishes not to be compared cannot easily avoid it, because the data are on display.

**Comparison is built into work.** Performance reviews, rankings, quotas, and promotions institutionalise comparison, and most men spend their working lives in structures that continuously report their relative position.

**Social media multiplies the reference group.** The digital environment presents a man with the achievements of a very large number of people, most of whom publish only their successes, producing a baseline against which his own ordinary life compares unfavourably. The effect is well documented in the research on social media and well-being, and men are not immune to it.

### The Two Mechanisms of Harm

Comparison causes harm in two distinct ways, and it is worth distinguishing them.

**The spiral.** The first mechanism, illustrated in Figure 7.2, is self-sustaining. A man reaches a goal; a more successful comparator comes into view; the standard is revised upward; the man works harder; the goal is reached again and produces no satisfaction because the comparator has also moved; and the process repeats with no point of arrival. The characteristic experience of the spiral is described with illuminating consistency: men say that they cannot remember the last time they felt satisfied for more than a week.

![The comparison spiral. Each attainment raises the reference standard rather than delivering satisfaction, so that effort increases while the return in well-being declines.](/figures/fig-07-02.png){width=84%}

**The misdirected judgement.** The second mechanism is more insidious. Comparison does not only produce dissatisfaction; it also produces a distorted assessment of one's own situation. A man whose business is modest but stable may, having compared himself with a former classmate who has expanded rapidly, conclude that his own position is a failure. The conclusion is not a feeling; it is an assessment, and it will guide his decisions. Men have destroyed sound businesses, sound marriages, and sound health by acting on assessments generated in this way.

### The Defects in the Comparison

The remedy for harmful comparison is not the instruction to stop comparing, which is unrealistic, but the systematic exposure of the comparison's defects. There are five, and writing them down is more effective than trying to feel differently.

**You are comparing incomparables.** The comparator has different starting conditions: a family that could raise capital, a locality with lower costs, health, timing, connections, and a set of luck. Comparing outcomes when the inputs differ is not an assessment; it is an error.

**You are comparing with a selective presentation.** What is visible is the accomplishment. What is not visible is the debt, the family strain, the health cost, and the hours. The comparison uses one man's highlight and another man's whole life, and no accurate judgement can be produced in this way.

**You are comparing a process with a moment.** The comparator is at a different point in a trajectory. A business that is expanding rapidly this year may collapse in three years; a career that appears slow may be more durable. Comparisons made at a single instant cannot distinguish these cases.

**You do not know what you would trade.** If the comparison is genuinely accurate — that is, if the other man has a better life in every respect you actually value — then the conclusion is not failure but information. In the great majority of cases, the man who examines the comparison finds that he would not accept the other man's trade: the hours, the family cost, the risk, the obligation. That discovery ends the comparison.

**Your reference group is not fixed.** This is the practical remedy, and it is the most effective single action available. A man chooses, to a greater extent than he believes, the people with whom he compares himself. Those who compare themselves with the most visible achievers in their society will live in permanent shortfall. Those who compare themselves with their own position five years ago, with their own values, and with the people to whom they are actually accountable will live in a state that can be judged fairly.

### A Practical Rule

One rule is worth carrying from this section: **before any comparison is allowed to influence a decision, write down the comparison and answer the four questions — what were the inputs, what is not visible, where in the trajectory is each person, and would I accept the trade?** The discipline takes ten minutes and prevents a class of expensive mistakes.
