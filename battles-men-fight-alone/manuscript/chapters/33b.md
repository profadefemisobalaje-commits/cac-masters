## 33.1 The Man Who Is Everyone's Solution

The role has identifiable forms, and most men who occupy it occupy several at once.

### The Nine Settings

**1. The family's financial provision.** The man who is called about fees, rent, hospital bills, transport, business capital, ceremonies, and debts. The specific structure of this form is that the demands are cumulative and are made by different relatives at different times, so that the man rarely sees the total. Men describe the discovery precisely: *I wrote them all down in one month and the number was four times what I had believed.*

**2. The family's mediator.** The man who is asked to settle disputes between relatives, to decide the allocation of family property, to speak to an offended branch, to hold the family's account of a matter. This form carries the material of the disputes, and its specific cost is that the man holds everybody's case about everybody, which is the accumulated confidences of Chapter 31 in a family register.

**3. The family's administrator.** The man who holds the documents, keeps the records, arranges the burial, manages the estate, stands as the next of kin, and is expected to know the procedure. The form is generally the position of the eldest son or of the most educated member, and it is frequently the position from which the conflicts of Chapter 26.3 develop.

**4. The community's man.** The association's officer, the cooperative's treasurer, the development union's chair, the person who receives the community's problems because he is the one with the standing to address them. The form is generally unpaid, frequently unending, and it is the one that a man's own household most often resents because its cost is entirely external.

**5. The workplace's support.** The manager to whom the staff bring their personal difficulties; the senior colleague who is consulted by junior staff about their careers; the person to whom the organisation's casualties are directed. The material of Chapter 31.3 applies, and the form is frequently not recognised by the man as a role at all, since it appears to be the ordinary conduct of a senior position.

**6. The friend's solution.** The man in the friendship group to whom the others come: the one with the vehicle, the contacts, the money, the practical capacity, the willingness, and the standing. This form is described in Chapter 26.1, and its cost is that the friendship is largely one-directional in the matter of reliance.

**7. The household's absorber.** The man who holds his own household's difficulties in addition to all of the above: the marriage, the children, the relatives in the house, the obligations of the extended family to the household, and the household's own crises. This form is universal and it is placed here because the household is frequently the party whose own requirements have been reduced in order to fund the others.

**8. The man who is consulted because he is competent.** The general form: the man whose competence attracts the demands. The lawyer is asked about the law by everybody including distant relatives; the doctor is asked about symptoms at every gathering; the accountant is asked to look at accounts; the builder is asked about a wall; the person who can write is asked to write the letter. The form's specific feature is that the man's competence is the mechanism of the demand, and the setting in which he might use the competence for himself is the setting he is least available for.

**9. The man who holds the family's material.** The recipient of the disclosures: the relative who tells him about the marriage, the brother who tells him about the debt, the cousin who tells him about the child, the parent who tells him about the other children. The form's material is confidential, which means it cannot be discussed with anybody, and the man holds it without a single arrangement in which it could be placed.

### The Structure of the Demand

The demands are not equal, and the chapter's practical material depends on identifying their actual structure.

**The demands that recur are the expensive ones.** A single large demand is bounded and is processed once. A recurrent demand — the monthly call, the quarterly request, the child's fees every term — is a standing arrangement and its total is far larger. Men describe the pattern precisely: *I could handle the big one. It was the small ones every month that took the whole of my margin.*

**The demands that arrive through several channels are the invisible ones.** The same family requests assistance through four different members, and the man never sees a consolidated position. The practice of writing all of them down in one month, described in the first form above, is the measurement that most frequently changes the man's understanding of his situation.

**The demands are generally not unreasonable individually.** This is the feature that makes the role resistant to correction. Each request is legitimate by the standards of the family, the setting, or the moment, and a man can answer yes to each of them while the accumulation is unsustainable.

**The demands are made of the person who has met them before.** The man's record is the mechanism. The relative who was assisted last year returns, and the relative who was not assisted does not, which means the arrangement selects for the concentration of demand in a small number of people and, within the family, generally in one man.

**The demands are frequently accompanied by a prohibition on refusal.** The specific transaction that men describe is the refusal's cost: the shame, the family's account of the man, the accusation of hoarding, the comparison with the relative who helps, the loss of standing described in Chapter 25. The refusal is available, it is expensive, and the man's calculation of the expense is frequently accurate.
