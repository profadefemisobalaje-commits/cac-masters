## 18.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"My wife says I do not talk to her, and she is right. But when I try, I have nothing to say."**

The absence of content is the finding, not the obstacle. A man in this position has usually spent twenty years not practising, and the material of Chapter 3 applies: begin with the physical and the factual — what the week has done to your sleep, what has worried you about money, what your body is doing — and the material develops from there. The recurring conversation in Part Five supplies the occasion, and the occasion is required before the content can appear.

**"If I tell her I do not feel seen, will it not sound like an accusation?"**

It will sound like an accusation if it is delivered as one, and it can be delivered otherwise. The form that works is the factual statement about the arrangement rather than the assessment of the wife: *we have not had a conversation about anything that is not the household in about two years, and I want to change that.* This form invites participation; the other form requires a defence.

**"I am the one who earns. Should she not be grateful?"**

Gratitude and being known are different requirements, and the first does not supply the second. Households do contain real gratitude, and men report that gratitude, where it exists, becomes easier to receive once the man has stopped requiring it as evidence of his own standing. The exchange described in Part Four of the exercise frequently produces more gratitude than any amount of provision, because it treats the wife as a person rather than as a recipient.

**"Is it too late at fifty-six?"**

No. The arrangement described in this chapter can be changed at any age, and the sequence is the same. Men who begin at sixty report two particular findings: that the transfer of functions takes longer than expected because the household has been organised around the man's management, and that the effect of being known is experienced more strongly by the man than by his wife, at least initially.

**"What if she does not want the conversation?"**

Then there is information to work with, and the framework in Section 14.6 applies. It is worth testing the premise first, however: a wife frequently declines an unspecified conversation about the marriage because she does not know what it will produce and because the previous conversations of that kind, if any, have produced conflict. A specific, scheduled, recurring arrangement with defined questions is accepted far more readily than a request to talk.

**"What if the problem is not the marriage but me?"**

Then the work in Section 18.5 is still the correct work, and the reading of Chapters 6, 21, and 37 is indicated. The distinction between a man's condition and his marriage is important and frequently missed: depression, exhaustion, an untreated condition, and long-standing grief all produce the experience of being unseen in a marriage that is not the source of the problem. The test is whether the man's sense of distance and emptiness persists in circumstances where the household is absent: if it does, the primary subject is the man.

::: summary
**Chapter 18 in brief.** A man marries and becomes a role: the person from whom permission is sought, to whom problems are brought, whose mood determines the household's atmosphere, and whose own needs are not brought to anybody. The role's expectations arrive from four sources — the family of origin, the wife's family, the community, and the wife herself — and produce three recurring conflicts: standing against partnership, loyalty against loyalty, and provision against presence. The role's decisive feature is that it is defined entirely by its function and contains no clause about the man's own requirements.

The need to be needed is a common pattern with genuine benefits and four hidden costs: being needed is not the same as being loved, and the arrangement reduces the man's information about which of the two he is receiving; the role exhausts the man who occupies it; the man's own requirements are suppressed because a person valuable for needing nothing must not need; and the household becomes structurally unable to function without him, which is his doing and, by mid-life, a genuine requirement.

Being unseen is distinct from being neglected. It occurs in marriages where the wife is attentive, competent, and managing the household well, and where she has never been given a picture of the man. Its signs include administrative answers, a wife's account of the man consisting of his conduct, disclosure that is transactional and late, the man's interior life being supplied by somebody outside the marriage, and the man's own inability to answer questions about what he wants.

Continuous responsibility imposes costs in four domains: in decisions, where the quality is reduced by isolation, persistence, fatigue, and the absence of capacity for substantial choices; in desire and intimacy, through depletion, the conversion of the wife into a function, the over-weighting of the intimate relationship, and the concealment of treatable sexual difficulties; in health, through the mechanisms documented in Chapter 12, whose first consequence is frequently a change in the household's experience of the man rather than in the man; and in development, where the capacity for learning, interest, and revision is consumed.

Recovering an identity beyond the role requires four elements — an activity that is not instrumental, at least one relationship in which the man is not the provider, a personal standard, and knowledge of his own history — and proceeds in five steps: recover one activity, recover one relationship, transfer one function, write the account, and make one disclosure.

A marriage in which emotion is permitted is built by arrangement rather than by affection: six arrangements (the recurring conversation, the designated hour, a rule about third parties, a rule about conflict, a sharing of the load, and the man's own support outside the marriage) and six prohibitions (no scoring, no disclose-and-withdraw, no requirement of a perfect response, no making the wife responsible for the man's state, no conducting the arrangements only while they are improving, and no treating the disclosure as a single event).
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete Part One of the exercise. How many functions do you hold, and which would be most difficult to release? What does the difficulty consist of?
2. What proportion of your conversation with your wife is administration, information, and personal? What does the figure tell you?
3. Which of the four costs of continuous responsibility in Section 18.4 is most evident in your life — decisions, intimacy, health, or development?
4. In Part Three, how many times did you write *I do not know*? Which question was the most difficult?
5. Who knows you — as distinct from relying on you? If the answer is one person or none, what is the first step in Chapter 4's sequence that you will take, and when?
6. Which function will you transfer, to whom, and by what date? What will you do when it is performed differently from your own preference?
7. What is the one disclosure you have been carrying longest, and what has it cost you to carry it? When will you make it, and where?

## Further Reading

- **On the provider role and men's wellbeing:** the research literature on the effects of the provider role on men's mental health, including the findings on role strain, the consequences of the loss of the provider role, and the association between provider strain and marital difficulty.
- **On men's experiences within marriage:** qualitative and survey research on husbands' experiences of marriage, including the findings on men's reliance on a spouse for emotional support, the reported difficulties of men in expressing need, and the evidence on men's satisfaction within marriages.
- **On demand-withdraw and silence:** the research on patterns in which one partner raises matters and the other withdraws, including the findings on the dissatisfaction associated with each role and the evidence on interruption.
- **On self-disclosure in marriage:** the literature on the connection between self-disclosure, intimacy, and marital satisfaction, including the findings on the reciprocal nature of disclosure and the effects of concealed material within marriages.
- **On men's mid-life transitions:** the literature on the mid-life transition in men, including the findings on the re-evaluation of identity, the search for meaning outside work, and the outcomes associated with the availability of interests and relationships beyond the household.
- **Practical resources:** couple counselling; men's groups focused on marriage and fatherhood; the material in Appendices H and M; and Chapters 12, 15, and 24 of this book, which address the working life, the marital arrangement, and the loss of self respectively.
