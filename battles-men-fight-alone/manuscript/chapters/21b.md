## 21.2 The Gap Between the Image and the Man

The gap is the difference between what a man shows and what he holds. It is worth defining precisely, because most men experience the gap as a general dissatisfaction and cannot identify its content, which prevents them from doing anything about it.

### The Contents of the Gap

A man's private account contains material that falls into six categories. The gap is the distance between what he shows and what is in these categories.

**1. Failures that are not disclosed.** The business that failed, the examination that was not passed, the job that was lost, the debt that was defaulted, the decision that was made badly, the child who has not prospered as expected.

**2. Conduct that the man regrets.** Not only the large items — an infidelity, a concealment, a moment of cruelty, a betrayal of somebody's confidence, an act in a period of desperation — but the ordinary and persistent items: the years of absence, the anger directed at the wrong people, the promise not kept, the relative neglected, the parent whose calls were not answered.

**3. States the man is in.** The fear, the exhaustion, the depression, the grief, the loneliness, the sense of having become someone he does not recognise. Chapter 3's material and Chapter 6's apply in full.

**4. Facts about the man's household.** The state of the marriage, the children's difficulties, the financial position, one member's conduct, the family's position in the community.

**5. Doubts about the man's own competence.** The private assessment that the position is held by luck, that the work is not understood, that exposure is a matter of time. This category is universal among men in positions of responsibility and it is described in detail in Chapter 8.

**6. The man's estimate of himself.** The conclusion at the bottom of the assessment: that he is not as good as people believe, that he has not done what he should have done, that he is a fraud, that the man others respect does not exist.

### Why the Gap Widens

The gap widens through five mechanisms, and each is worth naming because each admits a different intervention.

**Time.** Every year adds material to the private account and, in most cases, adds no material to the public presentation, which was fixed at a particular standard and is maintained rather than revised.

**The accumulation of unspoken items.** Each undisclosed item makes the next disclosure more expensive, because the disclosure of a new matter would expose the existence of the previous ones. The mechanism is the same as in Chapter 9's account of deferred disclosure and in Chapter 15's account of the unspoken ledger.

**The increase of responsibility.** A man's obligations grow as his household and his position develop, and the image's requirements grow with them, while his capacity to meet them is reduced by age, health, and the accumulated cost of maintenance.

**The absence of any corrective relationship.** Where nothing exists that can reflect the man's actual state — no confidant, no group, no professional relationship, no wife who knows — the private account is never corrected, and errors in it are never revised. Men whose self-assessment is most damaging are frequently those with the fewest relationships in which it might be tested.

**The comparison with an imagined standard.** The gap is measured not against the man's actual capacities and circumstances but against the version of himself that the image promised. Since the promise was always calibrated higher than the man's conditions allowed, the gap widens automatically.

### The Gap in Three Populations

The gap appears in a specific form in three groups of men, and identifying them is useful because each is frequently assumed to be free of the condition.

**The successful man.** The man whose position is publicly respected and whose private account records the failures, the compromises, and the concealments that produced it. His characteristic fear is exposure, and his characteristic statement is that people would think differently of him if they knew.

**The man who has done nothing conspicuous.** The man of ordinary means whose private account contains the sense of having not become what he should have become. His characteristic fear is assessment by the people who knew him young, and his characteristic statement is a comparison between himself and a contemporary.

**The man who has carried out of duty something that has damaged him.** The man whose private account contains the material of Chapters 9, 17, and 19: the household supported at the cost of the man's health, the ambition abandoned, the childhood spent working. His characteristic fear is that the account will be converted into an accusation, and his characteristic statement, made rarely, is that he does not know who he is apart from what he has carried.

### The Consequence of the Widening

Three consequences follow. The first is **the progressive reduction of what the man permits to be known**, described in Chapter 14, which eventually removes the possibility of being known at all. The second is **the escalation of behaviour that defends the image**, including the material in Chapter 9 and the patterns in Chapter 23. The third is **the arrival of an event that makes the gap unavoidable**: a collapse, a diagnosis, a separation, a loss of employment, a child's crisis — at which point the man's presentation and his position become visibly different, and the response is frequently severe, because the man has no practice in being seen.

::: keypoint
The gap between the presented image and the man's private account is universal. Its size is not. What determines the size is not the quantity of the man's failures but whether anything in his life permits him to be known as he actually is — and whether the gap is being measured against his circumstances or against a standard the image invented.
:::

## 21.3 Private Shame and the Fear of Exposure

Shame is the subject that organises the second half of this chapter, and it requires a definition because it is frequently confused with guilt.

### Guilt and Shame

**Guilt** concerns an act: what I did was wrong, and the remedy is repair, restitution, or amendment. Guilt is specific, and it is addressed by action.

**Shame** concerns the self: what I am is inadequate, and there is no remedy by action, because the object of the judgement is the person rather than the conduct. Shame is general, and its characteristic response is concealment.

The distinction is practically decisive. A man who feels guilt about an undisclosed failure can be moved to disclosure by the argument that the disclosure will end the guilt. A man who feels shame cannot be moved by that argument, because the disclosure will expose the person, and exposure is precisely what shame requires him to avoid. This is why exhortations to openness fail with men whose difficulty is shame: the advice addresses guilt and the condition is shame.

### The Features of Shame in Men

Six features are consistently reported.

**1. The conviction of being different.** The man believes that his difficulty is not shared: that other men manage, that his failures are of a kind that others do not have, that the disclosure would place him outside the group. The conviction is almost always factually false — the research on male secrecy consistently finds that the material men conceal most thoroughly is material that nearly all of them hold — and its falsity does not reduce its force, because the man's access to evidence has been closed by the concealment itself.

**2. The belief in exposure as catastrophe.** The man's assessment of the consequences of being known is total: that the household would reject him, that the community would withdraw, that his position would end. The assessment is rarely tested and is almost always overstated, as Chapters 12 and 16 both establish.

**3. The performance of the correct version.** Not the ordinary maintenance of an image but the active projection of a particular man: the generous one, the competent one, the settled one, the devout or the principled one. The performance consumes energy and produces a specific and characteristic distance: the man's relationships are pleasant and shallow, and he is frequently described by others as a fine person who is difficult to know.

**4. Anger at the prospect of exposure.** The most visible feature to others, and the one that most damages households. Where a matter approaches the material in the account, the man turns the subject, attacks the messenger, or withdraws. Chapters 2.3 and 14 describe the mechanism; the point here is that the anger is the response of shame rather than of guilt, and that it is often the man's most visible signal that shame is present.

**5. Contempt for those who are seen.** A specific and revealing feature: the man who is ashamed frequently expresses contempt for people who disclose, who fail publicly, or who require help. The contempt is a defence, and its recognition by the man himself is frequently the point at which the material becomes visible to him.

**6. The impossibility of receiving.** A man in shame cannot receive anything, because receiving requires an acknowledgement of need, and the acknowledgement confirms the material. The feature is described in Chapters 19 and 20 in other contexts, and it appears here as the principal obstacle to the recovery of the man's relationships.

### What Shame Produces

Five behaviours are produced, and they include the material of the earlier parts of this book.

**Concealment.** The defining behaviour, described throughout.

**Compensation.** Over-achievement, conspicuous generosity, the assumption of obligations, and the constant demonstration described in Chapter 2.3. Compensation is not hypocrisy; it is an attempt to be worth what the man does not believe he is.

**Avoidance.** Of the relationships, occasions, and subjects in which exposure is possible.

**Attack.** Anger at the person, frequently a wife, who has come closest to the material. The pattern produces the phenomenon frequently reported by counsellors: the wife's account of the marriage contains material that the husband denies, and her account is correct.

**Collapse into conduct.** Alcohol, sexual conduct, risk, spending, work — the material of Chapter 23. Where shame cannot be reduced by disclosure or by compensation, it is anaesthetised.

### The Route Out of Shame

Shame is reduced by one mechanism, and it is universal across clinical approaches: **exposure to a safe person, in graduated doses, with the outcome recorded.** The mechanism is not catharsis; it is evidence. Shame claims that the man's material cannot be known and that its disclosure would end his standing. The evidence that this claim is false is produced by disclosing a small item to a safe person and observing that nothing occurred.

The practical sequence, in the order given in the exercise at the end of this chapter:

1. **Select one item of low weight.** Not the central matter: a failure, an ordinary regret, a condition, a period of difficulty.
2. **Select one person and check the safety.** The criteria: has the person kept confidences in the past, and does the person have no stake in the material?
3. **Disclose in structured form.** The four sentences: what occurred, what it has cost, why you are saying it, and what you are asking for — which may be nothing.
4. **Record the outcome in writing.** What the person said, what happened to the relationship, and what happened to the man's assessment of the item after the disclosure.
5. **Graduate.** Where the outcome matches the report, select a heavier item. Where the outcome is worse than reported, analyse what occurred — it is frequently a matter of the person chosen rather than of the disclosure — and repeat with a different person before concluding that the material is unshareable.

The sequence is the treatment. Its value depends on its being graduated, written down, and repeated; and the men who complete it report a specific and characteristic outcome: not that the shame ends, but that its claim about the consequences of being known is demonstrated to be false, which reduces its force by a large margin.
