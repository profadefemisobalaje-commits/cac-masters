## 41.8 Warmth, Strength, and the Man His Household Needs

The chapter's material has one further subject, and it is supplied here because the reader who has conducted the analysis of his definition will arrive at it.

**The question.** If the definition is revised, what is left? The case material records the question in the reader's own form: *You have taken away the rules. What am I now?*

**The answer, in five statements.**

**First: the obligations are unchanged.** The reader's obligations to his children, his household, his marriage, his work, his debts, and the people who rely on him are the material of Parts III to V and they are not reduced by the chapter. The definition's revision removes the clauses that conceal the position, silence the load, isolate the man, convert accountability into authority, and identify him with his position. It does not remove a single obligation.

**Second: the competences are unchanged and they are increased.** The reliability, the judgement, the composure under pressure, the capacity to carry, the provision, the protection, the keeping of the man's word, and the conduct in the position. These are the man's competences and the chapter's revisions increase them, because the man's states become information, his instruments become available, and his household becomes a party rather than a protected object.

**Third: the man becomes available, and that is the largest single alteration.** The material of Chapter 39.3's first two costs, and the household's accounts in the cases of this book: what the household requires is not the man's softness in place of his strength and not his strength in place of his presence, but his presence as a person in the arrangements he is conducting.

**Fourth: the man's own life is added rather than substituted.** The material of Chapter 40: the man's own life is an item on his ledger. The definition's revision does not require him to choose himself instead of his household; it requires that he be on the list.

**Fifth: the transmission alters, and it alters without a lecture.** The material of Chapter 43: a man's sons, daughters, and the young men in his trade learn the definition from his conduct with his own material, his own fear, his own failures, and his own household. A man who conducts the chapter's revisions transmits a definition with the omissions filled, and the case material records the finding that the transmission is not conducted by instruction because it does not need to be.

**The chapter's closing statement to the reader.** The definition he inherited was written by people who were conducting their lives in conditions that were harder than his and with information that was smaller than his, and it produced the man he is and the household he has kept. Its revision is not ingratitude, and it is not the abandonment of the category: it is the completion of an unfinished document by the party who has the material that the authors did not have — which is the man himself, in his own household, with his own evidence, in his own years.

## 41.10 Common Questions Men Ask

**"If I let go of these rules, what will hold my household together?"**

The obligations of Parts III to V are not among the rules the chapter proposes to release, and the review in Section 41.4's classification supplies the boundary: the clauses classified as maintain are the ones the household's position depends on, and the clauses classified as revise, replace, or release are the ones that conceal the position, silence the load, isolate the man, convert accountability into authority, or identify him with his position. The finding from the case material is that the households of the men who conducted the revision describe the household as more secure and not less, and the mechanism is supplied by their own accounts: the household knows the position, the household participates in the arrangements, and the man is present in a form it can describe.

**"I was raised this way and I turned out well. Why should I change it?"**

The chapter's material does not require the reader to regard his raising as a failure. The case material's finding, and the one the chapter states plainly in Section 41.8, is that the definition's authors conducted their lives with less information than the reader now has, and that the definition's omissions are the material of this book's forty preceding chapters. The specific question the chapter puts to the reader is not whether he turned out well but the one in Section 41.4's eighth test: **what is the evidence for each clause, and where has it been tested?** The men in the case material who retained the majority of their definition retained it with the tests conducted and with the clauses stated in acts, and their own accounts record the difference between the definition they were given and the definition they now hold.

**"My wife is used to me as I am. Will this not disturb the household?"**

The alteration of a man's conduct alters the household's arrangements, and the case material records the disturbance honestly. The instructions that govern it are Section 41.6's three requirements and Chapter 40.6's Matter One: the substitutions are conducted rather than announced; the first step is conducted within the week; the statement is made to the ally and to the household; and the household's doubt is endured for the period it takes to calibrate, which the case material records as generally between six and eighteen months. The specific finding of Ibrahim's case — the wife's sentence about waiting twenty-two years for him to be bad at something — is recorded because it is reported in similar forms across the case material.

**"My father is still alive and he holds these rules. Do I owe him a conversation?"**

The reader owes his father the account and not the argument. The material of Chapters 29.6 and 30.6 applies: the man's own account of what his father gave him, stated once, with the addition stated as the man's own act and not as a correction of his father. The case material records two findings: the conversation is generally received better than the man's fear predicted, and the conversation's purpose is not the father's alteration, which is not available, but the man's own statement of the account, which is the material of Chapter 36's fourth condition and Chapter 44's material.

**"I do not want to be an example for my sons. That is pressure."**

The transmission is not conducted by the man's intention to be an example; it is conducted by his conduct, which the man's sons are already observing. The material of Chapter 43 establishes the mechanism and its direction: the sons learn the man's conduct with his own difficulty, his own fear, his own request for assistance, and his own repairs. The reader who does not wish to be an example has not thereby avoided being one; the specific choice available to him is what the example consists of.

::: summary
**Chapter 41 in brief.** A man's definition of manhood is assembled from eight sources — his father's conduct, his mother's and household's instruction, the institutions of his adolescence, his peer group, his household of origin's arrangements, his culture's presentation of men, his own experience, and his own conduct and its rewards — and the last of these maintains it in the present. The definitions are held as statements, and each is an obligation, a prohibition on a state, a prohibition on an act, a description of a status, or an inheritance without a function.

The five principal clauses are the provision, the composure, the self-sufficiency, the control, and the achievement. Each contains an authentic obligation and an addition: the provision's additions are exclusivity, silence, personalisation, and anonymity; the composure's addition is the extension of concealment into the settings where no requirement exists and into the man's own access to his states; the self-sufficiency's addition is that capability be conducted alone; the control's addition is the conversion of accountability for the household into authority over its members; and the achievement's addition is the identification of the man with his position. The revisions maintain the obligations and remove the additions.

The definition is enforced without an enforcer, by four mechanisms: the man's internal examination of his own conduct, the audience's monitoring, the real consequences of his own past disclosures, and — where it exists — his own substitute record. Eight tests examine each clause: what it requires in acts; whether it is possible; whom it serves; what it costs and who pays; what it omits; where it came from; what it would require if applied to others; and what the evidence is. The tests' most consequential finding is that the clauses held on the basis of a man's own experience are very frequently held on one or two occasions, generally in adolescence, and have never been retested.

The working definition has four parts: the obligations, stated accurately and in acts; the permissions, stated as acts; the prohibitions, stated as acts and not as states; and the measure, which is the man's whole conduct and contains at least three items the audience cannot see. The substitution replaces each revised clause with specific conduct in the reader's actual circumstances, with its first step conducted within the week and its statement read to the ally or the household. The analysis of a definition does not alter it; the substitution does.
:::
