## 4.5 Emotional Intimacy Beyond Romantic Relationships

One of the practical obstacles to rebuilding male connection is a category error that most men make without noticing: the assumption that emotional intimacy is a property of romantic relationships, and that the desire for closeness is therefore a desire for a wife, a partner, or a sexual relationship.

The assumption is false, and it causes real harm. It leaves a married man who is starved of closeness with only one available object for that need, which places an impossible weight on the marriage and frequently produces either resentment of a wife who cannot supply everything, or a sexual history that is unconnected to intimacy at all. It also leaves a single man with the belief that emotional closeness is unavailable to him until he finds a partner, which is both untrue and expensive in terms of years.

### Defining the Terms

**Emotional intimacy** is a state of being known, accurately and without performance, by another person, and of knowing that person in return. It has four components:

- **Disclosure** — the sharing of material that is not public: fears, doubts, regrets, hopes, and matters of shame.
- **Being received** — the experience that the disclosure did not damage the relationship; that the person is still there, and has not used the information against him.
- **Mutual knowledge over time** — a body of shared history such that each person can predict the other's reactions, and can describe the other's character accurately.
- **The absence of performance** — the ability to be in the person's company without managing an impression.

It should be clear that none of these components requires romantic or sexual involvement. They require time, honesty, and reciprocity, and they are achieved by men with brothers, with close friends, with mentors and mentees, with men in support groups, and — in a form that deserves separate emphasis — with professional counsellors and therapists.

### The Forms Available to Men

Four distinct forms of non-romantic emotional intimacy are available, and men frequently overlook at least two of them.

**Age-mate friendship.** The oldest and most natural form: a peer with whom a man shares history. Its advantage is the absence of hierarchy. Its difficulty is that both parties are under the same pressures, and therefore both find the arrangement easy to postpone.

**Mentorship in both directions.** A relationship with an older man who has passed through the same stages, and with a younger man for whom the reader is that older man. In the author's experience of working with men, mentorship is the most underrated form of male support. A younger man's respect gives an older man permission to be honest about difficulty, because the honesty becomes instruction rather than complaint; and the older man's experience gives the younger man language and hope. Chapter 44 develops this point as a strategy for the second half of life.

**Peer groups with a rule of honesty.** Structured groups — a men's fellowship with an agreed confidentiality rule, a supervised support group, a discussion group organised around a book such as this one, or a professional peer group with a leader — supply something that friendship cannot supply reliably: regularity, confidentiality, and an expectation that members speak. Appendix S provides material for such groups, and Appendix J provides a plan for starting one.

**Professional relationships.** A counsellor, psychotherapist, or physician with whom a man has a continuing relationship is a legitimate and, for many men, preferable site of intimate disclosure. Men who find it difficult to speak to friends often find it easier to speak to a professional, because the professional's role is defined, the confidentiality is explicit, reciprocity is not required, and there is no risk of the information circulating within the family or community. This is not a lesser form of intimacy. It is a different one, and for a proportion of men it is the form that works.

### The Particular Case of the Man Who Has Never Had This

A number of men arrive at mid-life having never had an intimate non-romantic relationship of any kind. These are frequently men who have been successful, reliable, and well-liked, and who have spent their lives in environments where male closeness was expressed exclusively through practical assistance: helping with a move, contributing to a funeral, offering advice, sharing a drink.

For such a man, the practical advice is to start with a low-risk, incremental approach, and Figure 4.4 sets out the sequence. Two instructions are worth emphasising. The first is that the initial disclosure should be a matter of *moderate* weight rather than maximum weight. Disclosing the heaviest material to a friend who has never received anything real from a man is likely to produce an unhelpful response and confirm the reader's worst expectations. The second is that reciprocity should be invited rather than expected. A question of the form *Has anything like that ever happened to you?* converts a confession into a conversation, and it is the single most useful sentence in the rebuilding process.

### The Professional Relationship: What It Is and What It Is Not

Because a proportion of men will find that the professional relationship is the only one currently available to them, it is worth describing it accurately, since misinformation about it is one of the main obstacles to its use.

**It is not friendship, and it does not need to be.** The professional relationship has a defined purpose, a defined time, a defined fee or service arrangement, and a defined confidentiality. These features, which men sometimes regard as making the relationship artificial, are in practice its advantages. Nothing said in the room will be repeated to a wife, a brother, a colleague, or a community. There is no obligation to reciprocate by listening to the other person's difficulties. There is no requirement to be interesting, pleasant, or competent. And there is a professional obligation to be useful rather than merely sympathetic — which many men find more respectful of their time than a long conversation with a friend who does not know what to do.

**It does not mean that something is severely wrong.** Men commonly delay seeking professional support because they believe that counselling is for people who are "mad" or "broken," and that going would signify a serious diagnosis. In practice, the majority of people who consult counsellors, psychologists, and physicians about emotional matters do not have a severe illness. They have a situation that is not resolving on its own: a bereavement that has not settled, a marriage in difficulty, a work problem producing sleeplessness, a loss of interest in things that used to matter, or a decision they cannot make alone.

**It is not a substitute for the work, and it is not a magic solution.** A professional can help a man understand what is happening to him, learn specific skills, address the effects of past events, and identify whether a treatable condition is present. He cannot remove the debt, restore the marriage, or reverse the loss. What he can do is ensure that the man is not carrying the situation in the dark, using methods shown to help.

**It can be private.** In circumstances where a man cannot afford to have it known that he is receiving psychological support — a real constraint in many families and workplaces — arrangements are available for confidentiality, including services by telephone and online, private consultations outside the local area, and services provided through employers or health schemes that are subject to confidentiality obligations. Chapter 35 and Appendix Q address the practicalities, including the question of cost and how to begin when the only available option is a general physician.

**It can be time-limited.** For many men, a small number of sessions — sometimes four to eight — is sufficient to clarify a situation, learn a technique, or complete a piece of long-deferred emotional work. The professional relationship is not a commitment for life, and it does not need to continue once its purpose has been served. Some men return years later for a further brief period of work; others never return, having obtained what they needed.

For the man who reads this chapter and concludes that he has nobody, the practical implication is therefore not that he must manufacture friendships before he can be helped. It is that a professional relationship is available, effective for many of the difficulties described in this book, and, in the author's experience, the route by which the largest number of men eventually arrive at the possibility of friendship — because learning to speak in one setting is what makes speaking possible in another.

## 4.6 Rebuilding Genuine Human Connection

The final section summarises the method. It is deliberately concrete, and it is designed to be workable for a man with almost no free time, because that is the situation of the men this book is written for.

### Step One: Take the Inventory

Write three lists.

**List A — Existing contacts.** Every man with whom you have spoken in the last year, in any context. This list is usually long, and its length is likely to be misleading.

**List B — Possible confidants.** Men, and women, who might plausibly become people to whom something real could be said: people who have shown discretion, who do not compete with you, who have kept in contact without a reason, or who have themselves disclosed something to you. This list is usually short — for many men, three to six names.

**List C — Reciprocity.** A single sentence for each name on List B, describing what that person currently receives from you and what you receive from them. The asymmetry in this list is the most useful information the exercise produces.

### Step Two: Make the Approach

Select one name from List B, and take one of the following actions within the next fourteen days.

- **The appointment.** *I want to talk to you about something. Can we set aside two hours on Saturday?* The appointment is the most reliable approach, because it converts a vague intention into a meeting, and because the phrasing signals that the subject is serious without disclosing its content.
- **The purposeless contact.** One message, with no request attached, to a man you have not spoken to properly in more than six months.
- **The reciprocity offer.** An offer of assistance to the person most likely to become a confidant: not a grand gesture, but specific and unsolicited — assistance with transport, an introduction, a practical task, a contribution that is needed.
- **The professional step.** If the inventory produces no names, or if the reader judges that the material he carries is too heavy for a friend, then the appropriate step is to make contact with a counselling service. Appendix Q explains the practicalities, including what to say when making a first appointment, and Chapter 35 deals with the objections men raise at this stage.

### Step Three: Establish the Structure

Connection that depends on intention does not survive contact with a mid-life schedule. Establish structure instead. Two arrangements are sufficient: a standing meeting with one person at a fixed interval, and a recurring group — an association, a fellowship, a men's group, a sports team, a professional peer group — with a consistent time and a defined membership. Chapters 26 and 27 develop these further, and Appendix J sets out a full twelve-month plan for building and maintaining a support network.

### Step Four: Practise Saying One True Thing

The hardest part of rebuilding connection is not the arrangement; it is the sentence. Men who have spent twenty years discussing logistics find that when the opportunity arrives they cannot think of anything to say, and they fall back on the familiar subjects. The remedy is preparation, and the preparation is a single written sentence, drafted in advance, of the form: *There is one thing I have not told anyone, and I have decided to tell you.*

Nothing further needs to be prepared. The sentence does the work, and what follows it, in the experience of most men who have said it, tends to arrive without much premeditation.
