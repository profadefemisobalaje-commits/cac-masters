## 16.5 Recovering from Rejection and Betrayal

This section sets out the recovery method. It is organised in phases, because the appropriate intervention differs by phase and because men frequently attempt the material of phase three during phase one, which is the reason that many recoveries fail.

### Phase One: The Acute Period (the first days and weeks)

The objectives of this phase are protection and stability, not resolution. Six measures apply.

**Stabilise the physical arrangements.** Sleep, food, activity, and the household's ordinary functioning. Where the injury involves a household matter, arrange the immediate practical position first: where the man will stay, how the children will be managed, whether money needs to be moved, whether a lawyer should be spoken to. Practical action in the acute phase is useful partly because it reduces the sense of helplessness identified in the ostracism research.

**Choose five people and tell no others.** The practical rule for the acute period: a small number of reliable people, in preference to a general announcement. Identification of the five matters, because the man's judgement is impaired and because the composition of the group determines the quality of the response: at least one person outside the family; at least one person with whom the matter can be discussed practically; and no person who is likely to become an avenue of circulation.

**Do not make irreversible decisions.** Resignation, sale of assets, relocation, a new relationship, an investment, a break with the family. The intensity of the acute phase is not a reliable guide to the man's eventual conclusion, and decisions made then are frequently regretted, sometimes years later, and sometimes permanently.

**Reduce exposure to the source.** Where the source is available on a device, the plan should be explicit: the removal of the application, the removal of the contact, the change of route, the change of the meeting. The measure is practical rather than symbolic, and its effect is measurable.

**Arrange for the moments of maximum intensity.** The two reliable periods are the night and the hours immediately after the discovery of a new piece of information. What helps: a person who can be called, an activity that can be performed at that hour, and, for the night specifically, the avoidance of alcohol, which is the standard method of prolonging the acute phase.

**Obtain professional and legal help immediately where indications are present.** Any indication of self-harm, any legal dimension, any threat to the man's safety or his children's, and any indication that the man's functioning has collapsed.

### Phase Two: The Reconstruction (weeks to months)

**Write the account.** The single most useful exercise. The account is written in three sections: what happened, in sequence and factually; what I did, including what I now regard as unwise; and what remains unresolved or unclear. Its purposes are to convert scattered rumination into a document, to identify what is genuinely unknown, and to permit the man to separate what he knows from what he has been constructing.

**Establish facts separately from narratives.** Two columns: what I know to be true, and what has been constructed. Men find, with consistency, that the quantity of established fact is much smaller than the volume of conclusion already drawn, and, relatedly, that a considerable part of the injury is being sustained by material that is inference rather than fact.

**Answer the question about the man's own judgement.** The material of the fourth injury in Section 16.3. Two things are required. First, an accurate assessment: what were the actual signals, and were they present and observable at the time, or have they become visible only with hindsight? The distinction is decisive, and men are systematically unfair to themselves on this point, because hindsight converts an ordinary and unremarkable relation of trust into a scene of foolishness. Second, a specific and proportionate change of practice: what precisely will be done differently with new relationships — the documentation, the verification, the discussion with a third person, the timetable of trust.

**Reduce the generalisation.** The conclusion *I cannot trust anybody* should be converted to a workable form: *I will trust in graduated steps, and I will verify what matters.* This is not cynicism; it is practice, and it has the effect of permitting relationships to be formed without ignoring what was learned.

**Convert the rumination.** An appointment with the material: thirty minutes a day, written, in which the subject may be considered. Outside that period, the material is not to be engaged with; where it intrudes, it is noted for the appointment. This is the same mechanism as the appointment used for the internal critic in Chapter 8, and it works for the same reason.

### Phase Three: Restoration (months to years)

**Recover what can be recovered.** The material injury should be pursued where it is capable of recovery: the legal route, the request, the negotiation, the retraction. Its value is frequently less in the sum recovered than in the removal of the position from the category of things done to the man and not answered.

**Restore standing deliberately.** Where standing has been damaged, the restoration requires presence, contribution, and time, in that order. The methods that do not work: the general denial, the campaign of correction, and the withdrawal that proves the allegation's consequence. The methods that do: the continuation of the man's ordinary participation; the statement of his position, once, factually, to those who matter; a visible record of the man's work over the period that follows, since a repair of the reputation is conducted by the ensuing conduct rather than the previous account; and, where the allegation was made formally, the formal answer — which should be given to the competent body rather than circulated.

**Address the humiliation.** The element that recovery methods most frequently omit. The man was not only injured but exposed, and the exposure is frequently the more painful and the more persistent element, because it is the part he cannot repair alone; it requires that others resume ordinary conduct toward him, which requires that he be present. The specific work: identify the small number of people whose opinion matters, ensure that they know the facts, and then require nothing further of them. That last step is the difficult one, because the aftermath of humiliation produces a strong demand for acknowledgment, and the demand is frequently the obstacle to the restoration.

**Decide about forgiveness.** Forgiveness, in the sense this book uses, is an internal decision about the man's own carrying of the injury: the decision to stop the account, to cease the internal prosecution, and to release the debt from the position it occupies. It does not require reconciliation, does not require the restoration of trust, and does not require the continuation of a relationship. It is not a moral obligation imposed by anybody, and it is not necessarily available in the period in which the injury is acute. Its practical value is that it ends the arrangement in which the man's life continues to be organised by the other person's conduct, which is the arrangement described in the exposure response. What it does require is that the man has first taken whatever practical steps are available to him, because forgiveness is not a substitute for legal advice, for the recovery of money, or for a boundary.

**Decide about the relationship.** Four options, each legitimate: continuation on revised terms; a boundary with continued contact, which is the appropriate form in most family and community situations; estrangement, which is appropriate where contact remains damaging and where the relationship's continuation is avoidable; and formal separation. The choice should be made in phase three rather than phase one, and it should be made on the basis of the assessment rather than the intensity of the feeling.

### The Rules of Recovery

Five rules summarise the method.

1. **Phase order matters.** Stability, then reconstruction, then restoration.
2. **Write it.** The material that is written becomes manageable; the material that is carried in thought does not.
3. **Convert every conclusion into a specific item.** A fact, a question, an action, or a decision.
4. **Take the practical steps before the emotional ones.** Legal advice, recovery, boundaries.
5. **Do not construct an identity from the injury.** The most reliable indicator of a recovery that has stalled is a man whose account of himself begins with what was done to him.
