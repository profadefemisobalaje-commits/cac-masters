## 31.7 Illustrative Case: The Principal Who Held Everything

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in leadership practice, occupational health, and family practice.

**Olusegun is fifty-four, the principal of a secondary school of about nine hundred students, in the position for eleven years, married to Funmi for twenty-nine years, with three children, the youngest of whom is twenty.**

The school's position at the beginning of the case was substantial and it was well known in the state: results rising over eight years, a building programme, a staff of sixty-four, and a reputation that made the school an attractive posting for teachers and a difficult one for a principal.

Olusegun's account of the load, given in an occupational health consultation and subsequently in a structured review, had four components.

**The decisions.** He described his average day as containing between thirty and fifty matters requiring a decision, of which about a quarter affected individuals directly: a teacher's request, a parent's complaint, a student's discipline, an allocation, a purchase, a staff matter. His account: *The one I could not forget was a boy I expelled in my fourth year. He did not finish. I do not know what happened to him. I expelled him for a reason that was correct and I have not stopped thinking about him in eleven years.*

**The example.** He described the requirement of being the school's standard, and identified the cost precisely: *I have not been seen in this town in a bad temper or in an untidy shirt in eleven years. Not once. I cannot remember what it is like to have a morning in which I am not being read.*

**The morale.** The staff's anxieties, the parents' expectations, the government's requirements, the students' material. His account of the emotional function of the position: *Everybody in the school brings their problem to my office. When the money is late, the staff come and I must explain it and I must be calm about it. Nobody brings me anything to be calm about. I am the office where the calm is deposited.*

**The material.** Olusegun's accumulated confidences were substantial and he listed them in the review: the teacher whose husband had left; the two teachers in a long-running dispute; the boy who was being abused at home and whose case had been managed over two years; the bursar's error that Olusegun had covered from the school's contingency; a member of the governing board who had asked him to do something improper and had made the request three times; the death of a student in his fifth year and the family's anger; and the continuing financial difficulty of two members of his staff. His account: *I keep them all in my head. I have never told anybody, and I could not tell my wife, because half of them involve people she knows.*

The position by the eleventh year, in the account of both Olusegun and his wife, contained six of the nine presentations from Section 31.4: sleep that did not restore him; the postponement of decisions, including the deferral of a staff appointment for nine months; the intolerance of other people's material, expressed in February of that year by his telling a teacher with a domestic difficulty that he should *take it up with the welfare committee*, which was not his manner; a private cynicism about the staff and the parents which he expressed in his own words as *sometimes I look at the assembly and I do not care about any of them*; the loss of the position's meaning, described as *I have built this place and I do not know why I am still doing it*; and the conduct at home, which Funmi described in the following terms: *He was not shouting. He was gone. He would come in, greet, eat, sit with the phone, sleep. If the children had anything to tell him, they told me. If I asked him anything about the school he said it was fine.*

Two matters made the position visible. The first was a medical episode: Olusegun was taken to a clinic with chest pain and a raised blood pressure during a week of the school's examinations, was investigated, and was told after three days that the cardiac findings were normal and that the blood pressure was likely to be responsive to his circumstances.

The second was Funmi's decision, taken without warning, to move temporarily to her sister's house with a written statement of her reasons. Her statement, which Olusegun produced in the review and which occupied two pages, had the following central passage: *You became the school eleven years ago. You are not the manager of the school; you are the school. There is no part of you that is separate from it and there is no part of you at home, and I am not able to live with an institution. You are carrying that place and you will not put any of it down, and I have watched you do it for eleven years and I am tired. I am not leaving you. I am leaving the school's husband.*

The work Olusegun conducted over the following eighteen months had six elements and he identified each separately.

**The professional arrangement.** From the clinic's recommendation, Olusegun engaged a counselling arrangement with a defined purpose. He described the first three months as unproductive and the fourth month as the point at which the arrangement changed: *She asked me, in the fourth month, what I had decided about the boy I expelled. I had not mentioned him for ten weeks. I began to cry, which I had not done since I was a boy, and I talked about him for the whole hour. In eleven years, I had never said his name to anybody.*

**The peer arrangement.** Olusegun joined a regional association of school principals and, on the recommendation of a colleague, a small group of five principals from different schools which met monthly. He described the group in the terms of Arrangement One: *We do not talk about the results. The first hour is arrangements and the second hour is what is actually happening. I have heard four men in that group describe what I have been hiding and none of them is a weak man. The first meeting I attended I said nothing for an hour and a half.*

**The retrieved information.** Olusegun established the practice from Arrangement Three, asking five specific people — two members of staff, the vice-principal in charge of administration, the bursar, and the chair of the staff welfare committee — the questions from Section 31.5 monthly. He described the outcome: *The second month, the welfare chairman told me that a teacher I had trusted was upsetting the younger staff. I did not know. I had not known for two years. The practice is the most useful thing I have introduced for myself in eleven years.*

**The boundary.** A written specification of the position's requirements, constructed with Funmi over two sittings, containing the hours, the days, a rule that the school's matters would not be conducted after 7 p.m. and a rule that Saturday would be the household's except in an emergency. He described the first six months of its operation as the hardest part of the whole process, because *the school objected in the way an institution objects: everything was urgent.*

**The place where he was not the principal.** Olusegun joined a football group of men of his age in the town, an arrangement that had nothing to do with the school, and where he was a participant and not a decision-maker. He refused a request to be its chairman in the second year. His account: *For two hours on a Sunday I am one of the men who cannot play well enough and nobody brings me a problem. It took me a year to enjoy it. I enjoy it now.*

**The household.** Over the fourth to seventh months, Olusegun conducted the household arrangement with Funmi and, separately, with the two children who were at home. The content was the fact rather than the material: the load, the accumulated confidences, the expelled boy, the chest episode, the arrangement he had entered, what the group was, what the boundary contained, and what he required of the household. Funmi's response is recorded because it identifies the arrangement's function: *He spent four hours telling me things I had not known in twenty-nine years and the thing I noticed was that he was answering a question I had asked for eleven years, which was whether there was anything in there. There was. There was a great deal. He had just not given it to anybody, and I had been guessing at what it was, and my guess was worse than what it was.*

Olusegun described the position in the fourth year after the episode in the following terms: *I am still the principal and the school is still hard. I said to the group in the second year that I had been carrying eleven years of other people's material and my own and I had never once said the words out loud, and four of the five men said the same thing. I have a group, a counsellor, a boundary on the paper on the wall of my office, a Sunday where I am not the principal, and a wife who now knows the reason I am quiet in the evening. I have not become different. I have become known, in three places.*

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The load was invisible in performance.** Eleven years of rising results, a building programme, and a staff of sixty-four, with the man's own state deteriorating behind them. This is the presentation described in Section 31.4 as the performance of performance, and the case is included because the men in this position are frequently the last in their organisations to be identified as being at risk, since the evidence available to everybody else is the results.

**The accumulated material was the specific burden, and it had no arrangement.** The expelled boy, the abuse case, the bursar's error, the board member's request, the staff's difficulties. The case is included because the phenomenon is, in the experience of practitioners, among the most under-recognised sources of load in leadership work: the man accumulates confidences because they are brought to him, and there is no arrangement in which his own position concerning them can be held.

**The spouse's statement identified the mechanism precisely.** *You are the school. There is no part of you that is separate from it and there is no part of you at home.* This is the identity collapse of Chapter 24 combined with the boundary failure of Section 31.3, and the case is included because the household's account is frequently the most accurate description of the man's position available anywhere, and it is frequently ignored until the household takes a measure.

**The arrangements were multiple, and no single one of them worked alone.** The professional arrangement supplied the disclosure; the peer group supplied the correction and the recognition that the condition was general; the retrieved information supplied the calibration; the boundary supplied the structure; the Sunday supplied the rest; and the household arrangement supplied the man's position to the people living with him. The case is included because men frequently attempt one of the six and conclude that the arrangements do not work, and because the six are designed as a set.

**The fifth element is the one men refuse.** The place where the man is not the leader, and the refusal of office within it. The case records the man's own difficulty with the arrangement — a year before he enjoyed it, and a refused chairmanship — and it is included for that reason: the element that appears the most trivial in the list is the one that supplies the man's own experience of being off duty, and it is the one leaders most frequently convert into another position.
