# The Empty Pocket Behind the Confident Smile {.chapter num="9" short="The Empty Pocket Behind the Confident Smile"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the forms that concealed financial difficulty takes among men, and explain why concealment is so common even in households built on trust.
2. Analyse the shame associated with financial hardship — its sources, its function, and its costs — and distinguish it from the entirely legitimate goal of preserving dignity.
3. Recognise the mechanisms by which a man lives beyond his means in order to maintain appearances, and identify the specific expenditures in his own life that exist for that purpose.
4. Describe the relationship between debt and emotional strain, including the symptoms it produces, and explain why financial difficulty is a genuine mental-health risk factor rather than merely a practical problem.
5. Apply a method for asking for assistance without surrendering dignity, including deciding whom to ask, what to ask for, and what to offer in return.
6. Begin a structured financial recovery using the exercise at the end of this chapter and the worksheets in Appendix D.

## Introduction

The measure of a man's public standing in most communities is partly financial, and the ways in which that standing is presented are visible to everyone: the school a child attends, the house, the vehicle, the contribution at a funeral, the willingness to pay for others at a table, the ability to respond to a request for assistance without hesitation. The performance is continuous and largely unremarked, because it is what everyone does.

Behind it, in an unknown but very large number of cases, is an entirely different reality. The house is mortgaged beyond its value. The fees were paid last term with borrowed money. The vehicle is on hire purchase, and two instalments are behind. The contribution at the funeral was made because to give less would have been noticed, and the money was taken from a payment that was required elsewhere. The man who bought lunch for six people has a bank account that will not cover his own transport home, and he has calculated this in advance and accepted the cost.

This chapter is about the empty pocket behind the confident smile. It deals with a subject that men discuss with less freedom than almost any other — less than marriage, less than health, and, in the author's experience, less than grief — and it does so in the belief that the concealment itself, rather than the shortage of money, is what does the lasting damage.

## 9.1 Financial Struggles Hidden from Family and Friends

Concealed financial difficulty takes a small number of recognisable forms. Men reading the list frequently recognise three or four of them in their own lives, including forms they had not previously regarded as concealment at all.

### The Forms of Concealment

**The edited account.** The man reports his position in a selectively accurate form. The new contract is mentioned; the loss of the previous one is not. The salary is stated; the deduction taken at source for a loan is not. Nothing said is false, and the listener therefore receives a picture that is materially wrong.

**The separate obligation.** The man carries a debt or obligation that his household does not know about: a loan taken for a relative, a guarantee given for a friend's business, a contribution to a family matter that was larger than reported, a shortfall in a business that he has covered from personal funds. The obligation is serviced quietly, from income that is nominally for the household.

**The deferred disclosure.** The man has decided to tell his wife, and will do so when the current difficulty is resolved, or when the quarter ends, or after the wedding, or once the school year is over. The deferral is renewed each time a new occasion appears, and it frequently lasts for years.

**The maintained standard.** The household continues at a level that the current income cannot sustain, and the gap is covered by borrowing, by the sale of assets, or by the accumulation of arrears. The standard is maintained not for the man's own satisfaction — he frequently has no interest in it — but because its reduction would be evidence of something.

**The private calculation.** The man carries the arithmetic in his head at all times: what is owed, what is due, which payment is being prioritised this month, which supplier is being delayed, which cheque will clear and which may not. This is not concealment in the sense of a secret, but it is the most isolating element of the whole situation, because it occupies a substantial part of the man's attention and cannot be discussed with anybody.

### Why Concealment Survives in Trusting Households

The most frequent objection to this material comes from men who insist that they conceal nothing, and whose households are, in fact, open in every other respect. It is worth examining why financial concealment survives even in genuinely good marriages, because the reasons are specific.

**Money is the last thing a man is permitted to be bad at.** Marriage and family norms have changed substantially in the direction of shared decision-making, but the expectation that a man will be economically competent has changed much less. A man may discuss his fear of dying, his childhood, his doubts about his faith, and the difficulty he has in connecting with his father; the one subject in which he expects to lose standing is the one concerning his ability to provide.

**The disclosure has immediate consequences for others, and they are not his own.** Telling a wife about marital unhappiness affects two people. Telling her about a debt affects the children's school, the household's food, the relative whose medical costs are being met, and the arrangements of the whole family. A man's assessment that disclosure will cause distress is usually accurate; what he fails to calculate is that the distress will arrive anyway, later, and larger.

**The information is frequently incomplete.** A man concealing a financial problem often does not know his own position precisely, because he has avoided the calculation that would produce the figure. He therefore cannot give a coherent account, and the attempt to explain becomes a confession of incompetence. This is the reason that the first step in recovery, as Chapter 7 explained, is to establish the position on paper.

**The concealment is protective of the man's own image in the eyes of people he loves.** This is the heart of the matter, and it should be stated without euphemism. Financial disclosure is frequently experienced not as the sharing of a problem but as the destruction of a self that the man has constructed for the benefit of his family. The disclosure feels, to him, like the end of being the person they think he is.

### The Costs of the Concealment

The costs are significant and they fall chiefly on the household that was being protected.

**The problem grows while it is hidden.** Concealed difficulty cannot be resolved by shared planning, cannot be renegotiated with the participation of a partner, and cannot be reduced by household expenditure adjustment, because the adjustment would require an explanation.

**The household's trust is damaged independently of the money.** Men frequently report, in the aftermath of disclosure, that the reaction they encountered was not primarily about the loss. It was about the exclusion — the discovery that a spouse had been making decisions, and carrying burdens, in relation to a situation she did not know existed. This damage is frequently the largest cost of the whole episode, and it is the one that takes the longest to repair.

**The man is isolated at the moment he most needs company.** Concealment removes the very support — practical, emotional, and advisory — that would reduce both the difficulty and its effect.

**The household is exposed to worse outcomes.** A concealed position continues to deteriorate in a manner invisible to the people who would be affected by it. Families that discover a debt at the stage of a court judgment have been deprived of the options that existed eighteen months earlier: restructuring, negotiated reduction, the sale of an asset at a reasonable price, the reduction of expenditure in a controlled manner.

::: keypoint
Concealed financial difficulty is not resolved by the man's own effort, because the effort required is not primarily financial. It is the decision to stop being the only person who knows — and until that decision is made, every month of concealment increases the damage done to the household that the concealment was intended to protect.
:::

## 9.2 The Shame Associated with Financial Hardship

Shame is the emotion at the centre of this chapter. Chapter 5 distinguished it from guilt, and the distinction matters especially here: **guilt** concerns what a man did or failed to do, and can be addressed by disclosure, amendment, and repair. **Shame** concerns what the man takes himself to be, and it produces concealment, because a self cannot be repaired by an apology.

### Where the Shame Comes From

Four sources account for most of the shame that men report in relation to money.

**The fusion of provision with manhood.** This is the deepest source and the one described throughout Parts One and Two of this book. Where a man's standing, usefulness, and identity are constructed out of provision, a financial shortfall is not a change in circumstances; it is a change in nature. This is the reason that men who lose income frequently become withdrawn, irritable, and evasive, and why the pattern is frequently mistaken for pride.

**The visibility of the standard.** Money shame is unusual in that it operates in front of an audience. The school knows whether the fees are paid. The landlord knows. The relative who asked for assistance knows that the assistance did not come. The community knows whether the contribution was made. A debt is private; a default is frequently public, and the anticipation of public knowledge is a substantial part of the shame.

**The comparison with the visible success of others.** Chapter 7 described the mechanism. In the financial domain, comparison is continuous and public, because a man's position is displayed in things that can be counted.

**The internalisation of the moral account.** Many men carry a set of inherited beliefs about money that function as moral claims rather than as practical theories: that a man who borrows is weak; that a man who cannot maintain his household has failed in a basic duty; that poverty indicates a defect of character; that provision is a matter of effort and therefore that failure to provide demonstrates insufficient effort. These beliefs are frequently held by men who satisfy none of them and who apply them to themselves without mercy.

### What the Shame Does

Shame produces five predictable behaviours in this domain, and each of them makes the situation worse.

**Concealment.** As described above.

**Refusal of assistance.** The man declines help that is available — from a relative, a cooperative, a bank facility with better terms, a professional adviser, an employer — because accepting it would make the position visible. This refusal is frequently rationalised on grounds of pride, which is a misdescription. Pride is compatible with accepting help; shame is not.

**Avoidance of professional advice.** The most costly behaviour of all. Men in financial difficulty frequently do not consult accountants, credit advisers, or lawyers, because the consultation requires disclosing the position. The consequence is that difficulties that could have been resolved by a professional in a week continue for years and end in proceedings.

**Continued display.** Because the position must remain invisible, expenditure on appearance is maintained or increased: the contributions, the ceremonies, the assistance to relatives, the standard of hospitality. The man is, in effect, paying a large annual premium for the concealment.

**Withdrawal.** Declining invitations, avoiding gatherings at which contributions are expected, and reducing contact with the people whose questions would be most searching. The withdrawal is then read by others as pride, which increases the shame, which increases the withdrawal.

### Distinguishing Dignity from Shame

A distinction is necessary, because this book is not recommending the abandonment of dignity.

**Dignity** is the maintenance of one's own self-respect and the honest treatment of others, irrespective of financial position. A man can be owed money, behind on payments, and dependent on assistance while retaining dignity completely: he is honest about his position, he acknowledges his obligations, he does what he can, and he treats everyone with courtesy.

**Shame** is the belief that one's worth has fallen. It produces concealment, evasion, and withdrawal, none of which preserve dignity, because each of them substitutes a false account for an honest one.

The practical test is simple. If a course of action requires a man to misrepresent his situation, it is a shame strategy and it will cost him more than it saves. If it allows him to state his position accurately — however uncomfortable the position — it is a dignity strategy, and it preserves his standing with anybody whose regard is worth having.
