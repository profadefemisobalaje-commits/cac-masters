## 20.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Thanked at the Funeral

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family practice, counselling, and the research literature on family functioning and marital contempt.

**Obinna is fifty-six, a pharmacist who owns a single retail outlet in a mid-sized city, married to Chidinma for thirty years, with four children between the ages of twenty and twenty-nine.**

The case was presented in counselling that Obinna had entered after a specific incident. His elder brother had died in the previous year, and Obinna had, over a period of about nine months, managed the brother's affairs: the hospital arrangements, the funeral, the settlement of the brother's debts, the transfer of a property to the brother's widow and children, and a dispute with a tenant that required the involvement of lawyers. He had paid approximately 2.8 million naira of the expenses himself, which represented most of the reserve that he and Chidinma had accumulated over four years.

At the funeral, three speakers thanked him by name. At the family meeting held a month afterwards to conclude the brother's affairs, the extended family's senior member thanked him again, publicly and at length, in the presence of his wife and two of his children. Obinna described the moment precisely: *I stood there being thanked for my brother by people I see twice a year, while my wife and my children sat in chairs four metres away and said nothing, which they have said for thirty years.*

The history that emerged over the following sessions had five components.

First, Obinna had been the household's sole earner from the third year of the marriage, after Chidinma's employment had ended with a business failure. He had funded: the education of four children to university level; the purchase and later the extension of the family house, which was in his name alone; the medical expenses of Chidinma's parents until both died; a substantial part of his own parents' care; the fees of two nephews; and, over eleven years, a series of unrecovered loans to three friends, one of which had funded the brother's business.

Second, none of the costs had ever been disclosed. Chidinma knew the household's income approximately and did not know: the size of the loans, the extent of the reserve's use, the two occasions on which Obinna had borrowed to meet the household's obligations, or the fact that he had declined an expansion of the pharmacy that he had wanted for a decade because the reserve had been consumed by obligations.

Third, the visible contributions of others were acknowledged and his were not. A sister who had provided food and organised the funeral's catering was thanked repeatedly for years in family conversations. Obinna's 2.8 million was, in the family's account, what was expected of him, and he had not mentioned the sum to anybody, including his wife, at the time.

Fourth, the internal sequence described in Section 20.4 had been completed. Obinna's account identified the stages precisely: the accumulation; the recognition of the pattern at his brother's funeral; the withdrawal of discretionary effort, which he described as his ceasing to offer assistance to his wife's family in a period when he had previously always offered; contempt, which he described in the following terms: *I had reached the point where I did not care what any of them thought of me, and I was correct and courteous in the house and I had nothing for them*; and the alternative audience, which took the form of the community's development association, in which he had become an office-holder and in which, in his own description, he had been valued for the first time in twenty years.

Fifth, the children had observed the sequence. His second daughter, in a conversation that formed part of the case, described her father as follows: *He paid for everything. He was not unkind. He was never pleased with anything, and I have never in my life seen him happy about anything we did. If I got a first-class result he complained about the fees. I learned not to tell him things.*

The intervention consisted of three elements, and Obinna's assessment of their relative value is worth recording because it differs from the sequence in which they were conducted.

The first element was the disclosure, conducted with Chidinma and, separately, with the four children, in the structure of Section 20.5: the record, the cost, the statement that nothing had been noticed, and the request. Its reception varied. Chidinma's response was the defensive counter-account — *do you know what I have carried in this house?* — which was accurate in substance: she had managed the household's administration, the children's education, and the care of three elderly relatives without assistance. Obinna's handling of the counter-account, which the counsellor had prepared him for, was to agree with it and to continue, and this was the single most important action of the whole case. His children's responses ranged from a daughter's tears to a son's question of whether the loans had been repaid.

The second element was conducted with the household's adults and took a different form: a written statement of the household's financial position, historical and current, including the decline of the reserve and the reason. Obinna described this as the measure that produced the most change in the household's conduct, because it gave Chidinma and the adult children a picture of what had been happening for eleven years and removed the assumption that the household's position was comfortable.

The third element was the household's practice: the weekly naming of a specific contribution and, at the review, the inclusion of cost alongside outcome. Obinna described the practice as artificial for the first month and as the reason that the arrangement did not revert, and he identified one specific effect: his daughter's statement — *I learned not to tell him things* — was raised in a household conversation in the fourth month, and the conversation that followed was, in his account, the first of his adult life with his children.

His summary, two years later, was: *I was thanked by everybody except the people who had received the most. I did not want thanks. I wanted them to know what it had cost, and I had spent thirty years being careful that they should not know.*

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The contribution was acknowledged outside and not inside the household.** The extended family's public thanks, offered at a funeral and at a meeting, contrasted with thirty years of silence at home. This asymmetry is common and it explains why men in this pattern are frequently more valued by their communities than by their households: the community sees the occasional contribution, which is an event; the household sees the sustained contribution, which is the baseline.

**The wife's counter-account was entirely valid.** Chidinma had carried the household's administration and the care of three elderly relatives, and she had done so without acknowledgement of a different kind. The case's central action was Obinna's agreement with her counter-account, followed by the continuation of his own. A man who treats the counter-account as an evasion will convert the exchange into a competition, and the outcome of a competition of that kind is that both parties withdraw.

**The undisclosed financial position was the operative concealment.** Not the man's feelings and not the household's conduct, but eleven years of a declining reserve, presented to the household as a stable position. Its disclosure changed the household's behaviour in a manner that no amount of conversation about recognition could have produced, because a household that does not know its position makes requests in good faith that the household's position cannot support.

**Contempt had been reached, and its cost was visible in the children.** Obinna's account of his own state at Stage Four, and his daughter's description of a father who was never pleased, are the same fact described from two sides. The research on marital contempt and its effects is directly relevant, and the case's most important consequence was not the change in Obinna's marriage but the conversation with his children in the fourth month.

**The alternative audience was already established.** The community association had been supplying the recognition the household did not, and the case's work therefore included the reduction of that dependence. Men in this pattern frequently hold positions in organisations where they are valued, and the positions are frequently treated as hobbies; in this case, the pattern was identified as a substitute for a relationship, and its role was reduced deliberately.
