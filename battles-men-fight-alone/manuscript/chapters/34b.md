## 34.1 The Point at Which Silence Stops Protecting

The distinction between functioning and dangerous silence is not a matter of the material's severity or of the man's feelings. It is determined by three criteria, and a silence that fails any one of them should be treated as requiring action.

### Criterion One: The Silence Has Stopped Doing Its Job

Silence performs four functions, and each is legitimate:

- it protects a position, a relationship, or a household from a consequence;
- it buys time in which the man can decide what to do;
- it prevents the distribution of material to people who cannot carry it;
- and it holds an arrangement steady through a period in which the arrangement must hold.

The criterion concerns whether these functions are still being performed. The silence has stopped working when:

- the protection has failed, because the material is affecting the man's conduct and the people around him have noticed the effect even though they do not know the cause;
- the time has passed without a decision being made, and the silence has become the arrangement rather than a stage in it;
- the people the man was protecting have been affected by consequences they cannot explain, which is a general finding in the material on men's concealment;
- and the arrangement is no longer being held steady but is being maintained against the information the people in it require.

A man can test this criterion directly: **the silence was supposed to protect something, and the reader should be able to state what, and to state whether the protection is still in force.** Where the answer is that the silence was intended to protect his household from worry, and the household is now worried without knowledge, the silence has stopped performing its function and is producing the outcome it was intended to prevent.

### Criterion Two: The Material Is Operating Anywhere

The second criterion is the most decisive and the easiest to apply. A silence is a decision not to speak; it is not a decision for the material to cease operating. The material operates somewhere, and the places are identifiable:

- in the man's sleep, appetite, energy, and body;
- in his temper, his patience, and his conduct toward the people closest to him;
- in the drinking, the risk, the spending, the work, the screens, the sexual conduct;
- in his judgement, through the four forms described in Chapter 27.3;
- in his attention, through the occupation of the same material at the same hours;
- and in the avoided settings, persons, and subjects described in Chapter 29.3.

The criterion is applied as follows: **the man identifies where the material is currently operating, and if it is operating anywhere other than in a decided arrangement, the silence is producing a consequence.** The finding is not a judgement. A man may decide to carry a material and accept the consequences; what he cannot do is carry it and be unaffected, and the belief that the material is contained because it has not been spoken is the specific error this criterion corrects.

### Criterion Three: The Silence Requires the Man's Continued Unavailability

The third criterion concerns what the silence costs the man's capacity, and its form is the arrangement described in Chapter 21.2: the maintenance of an account for other people, the management of the disclosure, the avoidance of the settings in question, and the attention required to hold all of it together.

The criterion is met when the maintenance of the silence has become a substantial activity in the man's life: when the day contains a set of hours, settings, or persons that the man manages in order to keep the material in place. The measure is the one used in Chapter 29.3: a man who is managing a subject is not carrying the material; the material is carrying him.

### What the Criteria Do Not Say

**They do not say that the man must disclose.** The criteria establish that a silence is producing effects; they do not prescribe a single response. The available responses include the disclosure, the professional arrangement, the stated limit, the private arrangement of Chapter 30.6, and a decision to continue carrying the material knowingly, which is legitimate provided it is a decision. What the criteria exclude is the position in which the man believes that the silence is cost-free, because in that position the arrangement is generally the one described in Section 34.3.

**They do not apply to every private matter.** Many of the things a man has not told his household are not the concern of this chapter: the ordinary difficulties, the private history, the matter that belongs to somebody else, the thing he has decided to carry. The criteria are applied to the material that is operating somewhere, and the reader should be able to identify that material specifically.

**They do not require the man to be well.** The chapter's material concerns the risk in a silence, and a man may hold a difficult matter and be functioning. The criteria concern the operation of the material and not the man's mood, and their value is that they can be applied in a low period without requiring an evaluation of the man's state.

::: keypoint
A silence becomes dangerous when it stops performing its function, when the material is operating anywhere other than in a decided arrangement, or when the maintenance of the silence has become a substantial activity in the man's life. The criteria are specific, they can be applied to the reader's own position, and they establish the requirement for action without prescribing the form of the response.
:::

## 34.2 What the Unspoken Does to the Body and the Mind

The material on concealment is substantial, and its findings are the strongest available argument against the belief that a silence about a difficulty is cost-free. Three mechanisms are documented, and each is described here in the terms used in the research.

### Mechanism One: The Physiology of Sustained Suppression

The research on emotional suppression describes a consistent pattern in people who habitually withhold the expression of what they feel: elevated physiological load, measurable in cardiovascular and endocrine terms; a higher frequency of physical symptoms; and the persistence of the physiological response rather than its resolution. The finding that matters for this chapter is that suppression does not reduce the response; it removes its expression, so the man's body continues to carry the material without the discharge that expression or the disclosure of the material would supply.

The manifestations reported consistently by men with sustained concealment of a substantial matter are: sleep that does not restore them; a persistent physical tension, frequently in the chest, the head, the neck, and the stomach; the blood pressure; the alcohol; the appetite; and the exhaustion that the case material in Chapters 30 and 31 describes. Chapter 27.3 supplied the mortality findings; this section supplies the mechanism.

### Mechanism Two: The Cognitive Load

The second mechanism concerns the man's attention, and it is the most practically consequential.

Maintaining a concealment consumes attention continuously: the material must be remembered, the account must be kept consistent, the settings in which it would be raised must be managed, and the responses to the questions that arise must be prepared. The research on the effects of concealment and of unresolved material on cognitive resources describes what follows: reduced working memory capacity, impaired performance on tasks requiring attention, and a specific inefficiency in the domains in which the material is most active.

The consequence in men's lives is not the loss of their competence. It is a reduction: the man's work, his decisions, his patience, and his presence are all operating at a lower capacity than his ability would ordinarily supply, and the reduction is frequently attributed to age, tiredness, workload, or the position. Men who have conducted a disclosure, or established an arrangement in which the material is held, describe the change with notable consistency: *I could think again.*

### Mechanism Three: The Modification of the Man's Conduct

The third mechanism concerns the material's effects on the man's behaviour, and it is the one that most frequently brings the man into contact with a service: in the end, through a consequence.

The patterns are documented in the material on men's concealment and are described in this book throughout:

**The avoidance.** The settings, persons, and subjects that the man manages in order to keep the material out of view, together with the arrangements he declines and the places he no longer goes.

**The compensating conduct.** The drinking, the work, the risk, the sexual conduct, the spending, the screens: each supplying a function of relief, escape, or numbing in place of the arrangement that the material requires.

**The reduced conduct.** The matter of Chapter 25.2: the positions not applied for, the decisions deferred, the relationships not maintained, the ambition adjusted downward, since the material occupies the space and the man's available capacity is reduced.

**The severity at home.** The material of Chapters 17 and 18: the man whose household receives the consequences of a state they cannot explain and whose behaviour is read as being about them.

**The judgement.** The four forms of Chapter 27.3: the irreversible commitment made without a second opinion, the concealment continued beyond the point at which disclosure was affordable, the adjustment made in private and never revisited, and the conclusion formed without challenge.

### The Specific Finding on Timing

The research supports a finding that men in the case material report repeatedly in their own words, and it is the practical case for addressing the material early rather than late.

The cost of a disclosure rises with delay, and it rises in three specific ways.

**The material grows.** An undeclared debt accumulates interest, penalties, and the additional arrangements the man constructs to manage it; an untreated condition progresses; an unrepaired wrong accumulates consequences; a household's misconstruction of the man's conduct becomes established as its account of him.

**The account becomes difficult to deliver.** A matter concealed for a period carries a second component: the reason for the concealment. The man must explain not only the material but the years, which is the arrangement described in Chapter 21.3 and which is considerably more difficult to conduct than the original disclosure.

**The audience's response becomes harder to manage.** The household that has been managed for years has its own account of the period, and the disclosure must be received against it. Men describe this precisely: *I could have told her in the first year. In the fifth year I had to tell her that everything she had believed for four years was not what was happening.*

The finding is the practical conclusion of the section: the earlier the making of an arrangement — the disclosure, the professional relationship, the stated limit — the lower the cost of every part of it, and the delay is the variable that the man controls.
