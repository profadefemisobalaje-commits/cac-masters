## 18.4 The Cost of Continuous Responsibility

The husband's role requires continuous responsibility: for the household's finances, its decisions, its standing, its difficulties, and its crises. This section sets out what continuous responsibility costs, in four domains.

### The Cost in Decisions

**The quality of decisions made alone.** A man who is the household's sole decision-maker decides without review, at speed, under load, and with no independent assessment. The consequences are visible in the material of Part III: the loan taken on the basis of a projection that no second person examined, the business entered without an assessment of the household's exposure, the property purchased without a title search.

**The persistence of decisions that should be revised.** A decision made by a man alone is a decision he defends, because revising it is an admission. Where the decision is made jointly, revision is a joint conclusion. The difference is substantial and it is one of the strongest arguments for shared decision-making within a household, which is otherwise debated on grounds of authority rather than of accuracy.

**Decision fatigue.** The research on self-regulation indicates that the capacity for considered judgement is finite and that it is depleted by sustained requirement. The practical consequence for a husband is specific: decisions of consequence that arrive at the end of a working day, made by a man who has already exercised judgement continuously for ten hours, are systematically worse than the same decisions made in the morning by a rested man. Men in this pattern frequently recognise this and do not correct it, because the structure of the household's day leaves them no alternative.

**The avoidance of the large decision.** A related and less obvious effect: a man under continuous load defers the substantial decisions — the business, the move, the career change, the medical course — because the continuous load leaves no capacity for them. The deferral is not indecision; it is the absence of the capacity to think at the required scale.

### The Cost in Desire and Intimacy

The effect of continuous responsibility on a marriage's physical relationship is frequently discussed in moral terms and it is, more accurately, a matter of physiology and arrangement.

**Depletion.** Sexual desire is affected by stress, sleep, alcohol, and load. A man at the point of exhaustion frequently has reduced or absent desire, and the reduction is misattributed by both parties: by the man to age, by his wife to rejection.

**The conversion of the wife into a function.** Where the whole of the household's arrangements are the man's responsibility, the wife's role in his experience becomes one of the arrangements. The transition from partner to administrator is incremental and it is mutually produced, and its sexual consequence is well known.

**The use of the relationship as a relief rather than a meeting.** Where a man's intimate life is the only place in which he is not performing a function, it acquires a weight that the relationship cannot carry. Men in this position frequently report high desire and low satisfaction, which is a description of a relationship being used to meet a requirement rather than to conduct a relationship.

**The concealment of sexual difficulties.** The most specific and most treatable element. Erectile difficulty, loss of desire, and premature ejaculation are all considerably more common in men under sustained stress, sleep deprivation, and untreated hypertension or diabetes, and they are concealed rather than treated in a large proportion of cases. Chapter 21 addresses this subject in full, and the practical point here is that a sexual symptom in a man under continuous responsibility is frequently a signal of a physical or psychological condition rather than evidence about the marriage.

### The Cost in Health

The health effects of the role are the same as those described in Chapter 12, and they are worth restating in the marital context because their first consequence is frequently a change in the marriage rather than in the man.

The man's irritability, absence, and withdrawal are registered by the household as a change in his regard for them. His reduced capacity for patience is registered as a change in character. His deferral of medical care is registered as indifference to the household's future. And where the health event eventually arrives — the diagnosis, the surgery, the incapacity — the household's arrangements, which depend entirely on the man's functioning, are required to change at short notice and without preparation.

### The Cost in the Man's Own Development

The final cost is the least visible and the most difficult to recover. Continuous responsibility consumes the capacity for development: for learning, for change, for the pursuit of interests that are not instrumental, and for the revision of the man's own direction.

The consequence appears, characteristically, in mid-life, in the form described in Section 18.5: a man of fifty who can describe his obligations in full detail and cannot describe a single thing he does for its own sake. It is worth stating plainly that this is a deficit with consequences for the household as well as for the man, since a father or husband who has no interior life cannot supply one to a child or a partner, and the relationship is restricted to the functions he performs.

## 18.5 Rediscovering Identity Beyond the Role

This section is the practical centre of the chapter. It addresses the specific project that a man in mid-life must conduct if he is to avoid the outcomes described in Chapters 24 and 25: the recovery of a self that is not reducible to function.

### The Problem, Stated Precisely

A man of fifty who has occupied the husband's role for twenty years has, in most cases, three components to his identity: his work, his household, and his standing in the community. All three are functions. None of them answers the question *who is this man when he is not performing?* — and the question is asked with increasing force in the decade following fifty, when the work reduces, the children leave, and the household's administration becomes lighter.

### The Four Elements of a Recovered Identity

**1. An activity that is not instrumental.** Something performed because it is interesting or satisfying, and not because it produces income, standing, or benefit to others. The specific nature is not important: football, music, woodwork, fishing, reading, a language, cycling, photography, the study of a subject with no professional application. What matters is that it is the man's own and that it produces nothing for the household. Men frequently resist this element on the grounds that it is self-indulgent, and the resistance is the script described in Chapter 2. The practical answer: an activity that produces nothing for anybody else is what confirms that the man exists as something other than a provider.

**2. At least one relationship in which he is not the provider.** Described in Chapter 10 and repeated here. It may be a friend, a sibling, a peer group, or a mentor, and it must be a relationship in which the man is known and not needed.

**3. A personal standard.** Not a role's requirements but the man's own account of what he intends: work, family, conduct, contribution, and the matters he will not do. Chapter 11's exercise provides the method.

**4. Knowledge of his own history.** A man who cannot describe what has happened to him — the jobs, the decisions, the losses, the reasons — has an identity composed entirely of the present arrangement. The record described in Section 17.6 serves this purpose, and men report that the writing is itself the work.

### The Practical Sequence

Men who recover these elements do so in a sequence, and the sequence starts smaller than most expect.

**Step One: recover one activity.** Choose one, of no benefit to anybody else, and schedule it weekly for eight weeks without evaluation. The first three or four occasions will feel like a waste; the evaluation should be deferred until the end of the eight weeks.

**Step Two: recover one relationship.** Contact two people from the period before the household's requirements took all of the man's time. Propose a fixed arrangement, which is described in Chapter 4: a monthly call, a fortnightly walk, a standing meal.

**Step Three: transfer one function.** The most difficult step and the most consequential. Choose one household function the man performs — the family's medical arrangement, one relative relationship, the school matters, the household's record-keeping, the mediation of family disputes — and transfer it, with training, to the wife, an adult child, or a sibling. The transfer will be resisted in some cases and executed imperfectly, and it should be sustained for six months before evaluation. Chapter 10 provides the method, and the case of Godwin in that chapter illustrates the effect at scale.

**Step Four: write the account.** The personal standard of Chapter 11 and the record of Section 17.6, completed at whatever pace is available. The output is a document that answers the question of who the man is apart from his functions.

**Step Five: disclose one thing.** The step that makes the other four real and the one that requires the material of Chapters 14 and 35: one disclosure to the wife, of something that has not been said, in the form described in Section 14.4.

### What to Expect

Three expectations should be managed. Recovery is not a transformation of the household; it is the incremental addition of a person to a role, spread over months. The household will resist some of the changes, because a functioning arrangement is valuable to everybody in it, and the resistance is not hostility. And the effect on the man appears before the effect on the marriage: men consistently report that the first changes — the activity, the relationship — produce an improvement in mood and energy within weeks, while the marital effect requires the disclosure in Step Five and takes considerably longer.
