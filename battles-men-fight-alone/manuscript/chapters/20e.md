## 20.8 Practical Exercise: The Contribution Inventory and the Statement of Position

This exercise has two parts and takes about three hours in total, spread over two sittings.

### Part One: The Inventory

Write out, in specific and dated terms, what you have contributed over the last ten years. Use the following table and add rows as required.

| The contribution | To whom | The years | The financial value, if any | The cost to me, specifically |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | |

Include: income provided and the obligations it met; education funded; medical expenses met; loans made and their recovery; properties purchased or maintained; guarantees given; assistance to extended family; disputes mediated; care provided; work declined and opportunities not taken; health and rest deferred.

The fourth and fifth columns are the ones that matter. Men completing this exercise frequently discover that the financial value of the contribution is larger than they had estimated and that the cost has never been totalled at all.

### Part Two: The Silence Audit

For each contribution, mark the level of the household's knowledge.

| Contribution | Nobody knows | Somebody knows the fact but not the cost | The household knows the fact and the cost |

| | | | |

Then answer three questions.

- What proportion of the contribution falls in the first column?
- Who, specifically, does not know — and why have they not been told?
- Which single item, if known, would most change the household's conduct?

### Part Four: The Statement of Position

Prepare the five parts of the conversation in writing.

1. **Purpose:** one sentence, stating that the material is information and not a complaint.
2. **The record:** the items from the inventory, in sequence, with the figures.
3. **The cost:** what the contribution has required of you, including the health items, the work declined, and the years of secret management.
4. **The absence:** one factual sentence stating that it has not been noticed.
5. **The request:** what you want to be different in the household's conduct, and what you will be changing in your own.

Then specify: the person or persons, the date, the place, and the duration. Rehearse it once with somebody who is not a member of the household.

### Part Six: The Reciprocity Check

Answer the following, honestly, before conducting the statement of position.

- What does each member of my household contribute that I have never acknowledged?
- When did I last name a specific contribution made by my wife, by each child, or by a relative?
- Which of my household's contributions have I registered as their baseline rather than as contributions?

The answers are not a reason to withhold the statement; they are the material of the reciprocal practice that accompanies it. A household in which recognition runs in one direction is a household in which it does not run at all.

## 20.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not weak to need to be told that I am appreciated?"**

The framing is the obstacle. The requirement being described in this chapter is not for reassurance; it is for the household's knowledge of its own position. A household that does not know what its arrangements cost cannot make informed decisions about them, and the man's concealment of the cost — which is the common alternative — has produced the situation described in Chapters 9 and 10. Stated that way, the statement of position is a management action rather than a request for comfort, and men find it easier to perform in those terms.

**"I told her and it turned into a quarrel about what she has done. What now?"**

Return to the conversation with the handling in Section 20.5: acknowledge her account in full and without defence, then continue with your own. The two accounts are not in competition, and the fact that hers is valid does not make yours invalid. Where the quarrel repeats across several attempts, the pattern is information about the marriage rather than about the subject, and Section 14.6 applies.

**"My children are adults and have their own lives. Is there any point?"**

Yes, and the form changes. With adult children the statement of position is generally unnecessary; what is required is the disclosure of the man's position, conducted once, and the establishment of a practice of contact that is not confined to assistance. Adult children who discover, at forty, what their father was carrying at fifty frequently revise their account of him, and the revision is worth the conversation.

**"Does this not amount to asking for gratitude, which is precisely what I was taught not to do?"**

The distinction is between requiring gratitude and supplying information. Information is supplied; gratitude is not required. Households that have been given the information frequently respond with gratitude, and that response is welcome and is not the purpose. Where no response occurs, the man has lost nothing: he has corrected the household's account of its own position, which is a gain on its own terms and which improves the household's decisions.

**"What if the household genuinely does not care?"**

Then the finding is important, and it bears on the decisions in Chapters 14 and 15. Men in this position describe a useful test: the household's response to the disclosure, in particular its response to the second and third attempts after any initial defensiveness has passed. A household that does not care can be distinguished from a household that is uninformed, and the distinction determines what the man does next.

**"I am not good at speaking. Can I write it?"**

Yes, and written form has specific advantages: the record can be read without distortion, the figures can be checked, and the exchange does not depend on the man's composure. The practice recommended in Section 20.5 is that the record is written and read aloud, and the read-aloud element matters, because a document handed over is a delivery and a document read is a statement.

::: summary
**Chapter 20 in brief.** Being taken for granted is distinct from the absence of praise. Recognition is the household's knowledge that a contribution exists; appreciation is its valuation; gratitude is its expression. What men most commonly lack is the first, and the experience has seven features: regular and therefore invisible work; no occasion attached to the contribution; discussion of deficits but not of surpluses; no discussion of the man's own affairs; acknowledgement of occasional contributions by others while the sustained contribution goes unmentioned; requests made without reference to what has already been provided; and the impossibility of raising any of it without appearing to request praise.

Three of the mechanisms are operated by the man, and they should be stated before the household is assessed: he conceals the cost, he declines the acknowledgements that are offered, and he does not describe what he wants. Sacrifice is uniquely invisible because it has no output, is indistinguishable from what the man would have done anyway, is rarely announced — since announcing it would create an obligation — and is eventually reconstructed by the man himself as an ordinary decision.

Unrecognised contribution accumulates into a ledger whose effects are selective attention to deficits, disproportionate reactions, the withdrawal of goodwill, and a grievance that cannot be raised. Bitterness develops in six stages: accumulation, recognition of the pattern, withdrawal of discretionary effort, contempt, the alternative audience, and the arrival of the material when the obligations reduce. Contempt is the pivot and the most damaging stage; it is one of the strongest predictors of marital dissolution and it is difficult to reverse alone. The condition is repairable at Stage One or Two with one conversation and at Stage Six only with years of work.

Stating one's own value requires four conditions: the purpose must be information rather than thanks; the material must be specific and dated; the delivery must be without accusation; and the statement must conclude with a specific request. The conversation has five parts — purpose, record, cost, the absence of acknowledgement stated once, and the request — and the expected responses are receipt, defence, conversion into a score, and hostility, each of which has a specific handling. Three practices make the outcome durable: stop concealing the cost, stop reducing acknowledgements, and state requirements as they arise.

A durable correction is a household practice rather than a conversation. Six practices establish a culture of recognition: naming a specific contribution weekly; registering cost alongside outcome; marking the sustained contribution annually; distributing the acknowledging role to children; treating the man's own maintenance as a contribution rather than a withdrawal; and telling the household's story including its difficulty. Three practices should be avoided: formal rituals imposed on an unwilling household, using the practice as a forum for accounts, and requiring acknowledgement to arrive in a preferred form.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete Part Two. What proportion of your contribution does nobody know about? Which single item, if known, would most change your household's conduct?
2. When did you last reduce or deflect an acknowledgement that was offered to you? What did you say, and what did it cost the record?
3. Which contribution of your own have you registered as somebody else's baseline — and which contribution of somebody else's have you done the same to?
4. From the six stages in Section 20.4, where are you? If you are at Stage Four, what has contempt cost you in the last two years?
5. What is your alternative audience, and how much of your requirement does it satisfy? What would it look like if that audience were the only one that knew you?
6. Write the five parts of the statement of position in Part Four, and answer: what single sentence of it is most difficult to say aloud? Say that sentence to one person this week.
7. Which two household practices will you establish, with whom, and beginning on what date? What will you do if the household receives them with indifference?

## Further Reading

- **On social exchange within families:** the research on equity and reciprocity in intimate relationships, including the findings on the association between perceived inequity, satisfaction, and stability, and the evidence on the effects of unrecognised contribution on relationship outcomes.
- **On housework, invisible labour, and its distribution:** the literature on the distribution of unpaid work within households, including the findings on the visibility of different forms of labour and the perceived fairness of the distribution.
- **On contempt and marital outcomes:** the observation research identifying contempt as a predictor of relationship dissolution, together with the experimental literature on the effects of contemptuous communication on the person receiving it.
- **On hostility and health:** the research associating chronic hostility and anger with cardiovascular risk and other health outcomes, including the findings on resentment as a distinct and measurable construct.
- **On appreciation and prosocial behaviour:** the literature on gratitude and the expression of appreciation as influences on behaviour within families, including experimental findings on the effects of expressions of appreciation on relationship maintenance.
- **Practical resources:** structured communication and marriage enrichment programmes, which generally include an explicit practice of expressing appreciation; the material in Appendices H and I; and Chapters 15 and 17, which address the marital arrangement and the paternal role in their respective forms.
