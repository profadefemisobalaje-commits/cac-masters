## 19.8 Practical Exercise: The Early Responsibility Audit

This exercise takes about two hours and should be completed in two sittings, with the second sitting at least a day after the first. It is written for men who took up adult responsibility before the age of eighteen, and also for men whose responsibility arrived later but was not chosen.

### Part One: The Record

Answer the following in writing, with dates and specifics.

1. **The occasion.** What ended your childhood? When did it occur? How old were you, and what was the month?
2. **The transfer.** Who, if anybody, told you what your position was? What were the words used, if you remember them?
3. **What you took up.** List the roles you acquired: provision, care of siblings, protection, emotional support of a parent, the household's administration, representation, mediation, work.
4. **What you gave up.** List what you stopped doing: school, sport, friendships, play, particular ambitions, the period of your life in which nothing was required.
5. **Who knew.** Who in your household, your family, or your community was aware of what you were carrying, and who was aware of what it cost?

### Part Two: The Instruction Set

Write the sentences you say to yourself about responsibility and need. They are usually in the present tense and they usually sound like facts. Examples from men's accounts: *If I do not do it, nobody will.* *I cannot rely on anybody.* *I have to be the strong one.* *I must not add to the load.* *It is my duty because I am the eldest.* *I cannot rest until it is done.*

For each sentence, complete the following.

| The sentence | Where and when it was formed | Is it true in my present circumstances? | What would be an accurate revision? |

| | | | |

### Part Three: The Unpresented Requirement

Identify one requirement you had in that period and never presented. It should be specific: the schooling, the apprenticeship, the time, the assistance, the acknowledgement, the permission to be a child.

Then write the statement described in Section 19.6, Step Three, in four sentences.

- What occurred, factually.
- What it cost, specifically.
- Why you are stating it now.
- What you are and are not asking for.

Then decide to whom it will be stated, and when. It may be stated to a person, or it may be written in a letter that is not sent, or it may be stated to a counsellor. The requirement may be presented in any of those forms; the condition is that it be stated explicitly somewhere outside the man's own mind.

### Part Four: The Current Load

Using the transfers in Part One, list every function you currently perform for your family of origin.

| Function | For whom | Since when | Who would do it if I stopped? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | |

Then select one function to transfer, with the specification required in Part Four of the exercise in Chapter 18: the function, the recipient, the training period, the date, and what the man will do when it is managed differently from his preference.

### Part Five: The Recovery List

Write the specific items that were not obtained in the period in question, and for each, mark it as **recoverable now**, **partially recoverable**, or **permanently lost**.

| The item | Status | The specific act, if recoverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

Then take one recoverable item and specify it as an arrangement with a date: the qualification, the lessons, the sport, the interest, the competence, the received care, the safe failure.

### Part Six: The Grief

Write, in no more than 300 words, a direct statement of what was lost. Not the circumstances, and not an account of the people involved; the loss itself. Read it once aloud, then put it away for a week and read it again.

The purpose of this part is stated plainly because men in this pattern frequently attempt to skip it: the grief is not optional, and the recovered items in Part Five will not hold their value while the loss remains unacknowledged. The form is not required to be eloquent. It is required to be accurate.

## 19.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"What I did kept my family alive. Is this chapter telling me it was wrong?"**

No. The chapter states explicitly that the arrangement produced real achievements, frequently including the family's survival, and that men in this pattern are usually the most capable people in their households. What the chapter argues is that the arrangement had costs that were never counted, and that a man who wants to know what he is still paying can find out. The recovery work is not a repudiation of what the man did; it is the completion of it, in the sense that the household's survival was the objective and the man's own life was the collateral.

**"My mother is still alive and needs me. How do I reduce anything?"**

The transfer, not the withdrawal. The most workable arrangement in these cases is the redistribution of the functions among siblings and relatives, conducted with the parent's knowledge and, where possible, their agreement, and accompanied by a clear statement of what continues. Chapter 10 provides the method and the language. Where the man is the sole surviving support, the reduction should be confined to the functions that are not the parent's direct care, and the sequence in Section 18.5 applies.

**"How do I present a requirement from thirty years ago without sounding bitter?"**

By delivering it as information rather than as a debt. The statement in Part Three of the exercise has four sentences, and the third of them — why you are stating it now — is the one that prevents the material from being received as an accusation. The accurate reason is generally the same in every case: *because nobody has ever been told, and because it has been affecting how I behave.* A listener who receives that sentence will generally not defend himself, because no defence has been requested.

**"I do not remember my childhood. Is that significant?"**

It is common in this pattern and it is explained by the mechanism of Section 19.2: a child conducting a continuous calculation does not form the ordinary autobiographical record of the same period, because he was not attending to his own experience. The gap is not evidence of a worse history; it is evidence of an occupied one. Men frequently find that material returns when they begin to write, and the exercise in Part One is designed to permit that.

**"My siblings do not see it the way I do and it has caused a quarrel."**

Discontinue the account with them. The work of this chapter is not the correction of the family's record and it does not require any agreement. Where a sibling responds with comparison, dispute, or a suggestion that the man is complaining, the correct action is to drop the subject and continue with the transfers and with the recovery list, both of which are entirely within the man's control.

**"Is it not self-indulgent to grieve at fifty for a childhood that was lost at fifteen?"**

The judgement that this work is self-indulgent is itself an instance of the instruction set in Part Two, and the same analysis applies to it as to any of the other sentences: is the claim true? The practical evidence is that the work produces effects that the man's household notices: a reduction in the sense of emptiness, an improvement in the capacity to receive, and a greater availability in the household's relationships. On those grounds it is not leisure; it is maintenance.

::: summary
**Chapter 19 in brief.** A childhood ends early when responsibility is transferred to a child before the child is an adult. The occasions are specific — the death or departure of a parent, a parent's illness, poverty requiring income, family conflict, displacement, or a parent's own incapacity — and the roles acquired fall into five categories: provision, care, protection, the household's emotional load, and, in some cases, the surrogate spouse position. The distinguishing feature of premature adulthood is not the volume of work but the bearing of the consequence: a childhood ends when a child discovers that nobody is standing behind him.

The weight the child carried consists of four elements: the continuous calculation of risk, coupled with low control, which becomes a lifelong vigilance; the suppression of the child's own requirements, which produces the adult who cannot ask or receive; the premature assumption of authority, which produces unusual competence and a specific difficulty with authority over the man; and the absence of any person to whom the child could be a child, which leaves a capacity undeveloped rather than damaged.

The requirement to be strong for the family is imposed by the parent's visible state, by direct instruction, by the sharing of the household's confidence, by the absence of any response to the child's own difficulty, by the child's role in the family's reputation, and by the extended family's appointment of the child as the household's representative. The arrangement is maintained in adult life because it is required and rewarded, and its costs appear in the exclusion of the man's own difficulty from every relationship, the selection of relationships that reproduce the arrangement, the particular difficulty of illness and incapacity, the household's inability to know him, and the delayed arrival of the material when the obligation ends.

The documented long-term effects of parentification include the default position of responsibility, difficulty in identifying and expressing needs, hyper-responsibility combined with anxiety, difficulty in receiving, overwork as a stabiliser, discomfort with play and rest, elevated rates of depression and anxiety in mid-life, difficulty in the relationship with the parent for whom the man was responsible, and a specific and unnameable grief for the childhood itself.

Recovery has two parts. What can be recovered: the experience of being looked after, the practice of unstructured time, abandoned interests, interrupted education, ordinary competences never taught, and the practice of failure without consequence. What must be grieved: the time spent working instead of being a child, the absence of a person to whom the man could be a child, the loss of the parental relationship in its protective form, and the version of the self that did not develop.

The work of addressing the boy inside the man consists of naming the operating instructions formed in that period, revising them into accurate statements about the present, stating one requirement that was never presented, and giving the boy something he did not have — the same list, taken as a specific and dated plan. Where the material involves abuse, violence, or addiction, or where a depressive or anxiety condition is present, professional treatment comes first, because a man who is depressed will conduct this work as a further account of his own inadequacy.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. At what age did your childhood end, and what was the occasion? Who told you, and what were the words, if any?
2. From the five roles in Section 19.1, which did you hold? Which do you still hold?
3. Complete Part Two. Which of your instructions was formed longest ago, and is it still true today?
4. What requirement did you have in that period and never present? What is the statement's third sentence — the reason you would state it now?
5. From the documented effects in Section 19.4, which three are present in your life: the default position of responsibility, difficulty expressing needs, hyper-responsibility with anxiety, difficulty receiving, overwork, discomfort with rest, mid-life depression, difficulty with the surviving parent, or grief for the childhood?
6. Which function do you still perform for your family of origin that somebody else could hold? What is the date on which the transfer begins?
7. From Part Five, which recoverable item will you take up, and by what date? What will you do when somebody tells you it is frivolous?

## Further Reading

- **On parentification:** the research literature on parentification and role reversal in families, including the distinction between emotional and instrumental parentification, the conditions under which outcomes are positive, and the findings on the effects of early caregiving on adult wellbeing.
- **On young carers:** the international research on children who provide care, including the findings on the effects of caring on education, health, and social development, and the interventions that reduce the harm.
- **On the eldest child:** research on birth order and its relationship to responsibility, achievement, and the assumption of family roles, together with the specifically cultural literature on the eldest son's position in families where the role is formally defined.
- **On "the man of the house" instruction:** the qualitative literature on the internalisation of adult roles by boys, including studies of boys whose fathers are absent and of the ways in which responsibility is communicated without being discussed.
- **On the effects of childhood adversity:** the literature on adverse childhood experiences and their long-term effects on health and functioning, including the findings on the cumulative effect of adversity and on the factors that improve outcomes.
- **Practical resources:** adult education and second-chance qualification programmes; counselling for men who became responsible early, which is a distinct presenting issue with a specific treatment approach; men's groups with members of comparable history; and the material in Appendix C and Chapter 37 on treatment.
