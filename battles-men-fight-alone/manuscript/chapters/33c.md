## 33.2 How a Man Becomes Everyone's Solution

No man chose the position, and the sequence by which it is acquired is consistent across the case material.

### The Six Steps

**Step one: the man becomes competent or solvent.** The relative with a position, a trade, a salary, an education, a vehicle, a house, a network, or a reputation for getting things done. The competence is the precondition, and it is generally acquired through the man's own effort and frequently at cost, as Chapters 19 and 25 describe.

**Step two: the first request is met.** It is small, it is reasonable, and the man can meet it. He does, and it is the correct conduct in the circumstances and the beginning of the arrangement.

**Step three: the record is established.** The family, the community, and the workplace have learned something specific: this man can be asked. The learning is the mechanism, and it is not malicious; the family is allocating its difficulties to the person who can address them, which is what any group does.

**Step four: the requests multiply.** Additional relatives, additional categories, additional settings. The multiplication is not a decision by anybody; it is the operation of the record. The man's capacity, meanwhile, is not measured by anybody, including himself.

**Step five: the refusal becomes unacceptable.** At the point at which the man attempts to decline a demand, the arrangement's terms become visible. The refusal is met with the family's account of him, the comparison, the reminder of what was done for him, or the withdrawal of a relationship, and the man's calculation generally favours compliance. Men describe the moment precisely: *That was the year I understood that I was not their brother. I was their facility.*

**Step six: the man's own material accumulates without an arrangement.** The man is holding forty people's difficulties and none of his own is held anywhere, which is the position described in the introduction. The step arrives without an event and it is generally invisible until the presentations of Section 33.3 appear.

### The Four Rewards

The role is not only a burden, and the chapter should record its returns accurately, because the man's ambivalence about the position is otherwise inexplicable.

**Standing.** The man is important, consulted, and known. In settings where formal recognition is scarce, this standing is a substantial return, and the material on it is in Chapter 25.

**Identity.** The role supplies the man's answer to the question of who he is: the one who helps, the family's pillar, the man who can be relied upon. The material of Chapter 24 applies, and the role's contribution to the man's account of himself is generally far larger than he acknowledges.

**Company and connection.** The role supplies a continuous supply of contact, information, and involvement. The man knows what is happening in the family and the community because he is at the centre of its demands, and a man who withdraws from the role discovers how much of his social position was carried by it.

**The avoidance of the man's own material.** A specific and important return, and men in the role describe it when they are finally able to: the constant occupation with other people's difficulties supplies a legitimate reason not to address his own. The position is documented in the literature on caregiving and in the counselling of men in the role, and it is the reason that the man's own material is frequently discovered at the point at which the demands reduce, which is the structure of Chapter 24.

### The Two Explanations the Man Gives

Men explain the role in one of two ways, and both should be examined.

**The obligation.** *They are my family. Who else will help them? I am the only one who can.* The explanation is frequently accurate in its first part and it should be tested against the four tests of Section 33.4, since the family in question generally contains other adults whose capacity is unexamined.

**The character.** *That is who I am. I cannot refuse a person in need.* The explanation is frequently a description of the arrangement rather than of the man, and it is the form that most frequently conceals the rewards and the avoidance described above. Where the man cannot refuse, the question of what he would be if he declined is open, and the material of Chapter 24 applies directly.

## 33.3 What the Role Costs

The five costs are documented, and they are described here in the order in which they appear in the case material rather than in the order of severity.

### Cost One: The Accumulation

The man is carrying the material of everybody who has brought him something, and the material is a load in its own right. The relevant findings concern vicarious load — the second-hand stress experienced by people who attend to the difficulties of others — and they are consistent across settings: the material is real, its effects on the body and on the man's judgement are measurable, and the man's awareness of it is frequently low because his attention is directed outward.

The specific feature in this role is that the accumulation is not acknowledged. A professional who carries other people's material has a title, a caseload, supervision, and a stated limit; the man in this role has none of the four, and the absence of an arrangement in which his own position concerning the material can be held is the reason the material does not leave him.

### Cost Two: The Erosion of His Own Resources

The man's money, time, attention, and emotional capacity are consumed by the role, and the consumption is progressive.

**The money.** The demands, the guarantees, the loans that are not repaid, the fees, the capital, the maintenance. The man's own plans — the retirement, the investment, the property, the children's schooling, the medical care he has postponed — are funded from what remains, and Men describe the arithmetic with a particular flatness: *I put four children through school and I have not put one of my own through university.*

**The time.** The role's hours are not marked in any calendar and its total is considerable: the visits, the arrangements, the telephone calls, the funerals, the disputes, the journeys, the offices.

**The capacity.** The man's attention and patience, which are finite, are directed outward, and the household that lives with him receives the remainder. This is the transaction described in Chapter 18 in another setting, and it is the one that produces the household's account described in the case material: *He is the best man in the world to everybody in this town and he is not present in his own house.*

### Cost Three: The Resentment That Cannot Be Stated

The most damaging of the five and the one men describe most reluctantly. The man resents the demands, and the resentment cannot be stated, because:

- the demands are legitimate;
- the family's standards do not admit the resentment;
- its expression would be met with the account described in Step Five of Section 33.2;
- and the man's own identity includes being the one who helps.

The resentment therefore operates in forms other than speech, and the forms are identifiable: the flatness at occasions, the withdrawal from the family's conversations, the unexplained irritability, the severity about small matters, the increased distance from the specific relative who has made the demands, the alcohol, and the private calculation of the score. This is the material of Chapters 15 and 20 in a different register: a ledger that cannot be raised and therefore accumulates, and it is the pattern that most frequently precedes the man's own collapse or his exit from the family's arrangements.

### Cost Four: The Transfer to the Household

The household pays for the role, and the payment is frequently invisible to the man, who has accounted the money that was given away and not the household's own arrangements that were reduced to fund it.

**The reductions.** The household's expenditure, schooling, medical care, housing, and occasions are reduced or postponed.
**The absences.** The man's time and attention are elsewhere, and the household's arrangements are conducted by the spouse without the man.
**The future.** The household's plans — the move, the business, the property, the children's education — are deferred until a later year that the arithmetic does not support, and the household's expectation that the deferral is temporary is generally not borne out.
**The particular difficulty of the household's position.** The spouse who raises the matter is placed in the position of opposing the family's welfare, and the household's objections to the arrangement are therefore difficult to state. Men in the role describe the household's position with a recognition that arrives late: *She was not being selfish. She was the only person in the whole thing who was counting.*

### Cost Five: The Collapse of the Man's Own Arrangements

The final cost and the one described in the previous chapters of this book. The man in this role has generally not built the arrangements of Chapter 27: no group, frequently no confidant, no place where he is not the provider, no peer arrangement, no professional arrangement, and no recipient for his own material. The role has supplied the company and the identity, which is the reason the man has required nothing else.

The consequence appears at the point at which his own load exceeds his capacity: the illness, the business loss, the redundancy, the loss of a spouse, the child's crisis, the serious mistake at work. The collapse of the man's own position then discovers that the arrangement was one-directional and that the man's own arrangements do not exist.

The finding from this material, and the one most frequently reported by men who have reached this point, is not that the family failed to help, though it may have. It is that the family had never been informed that there was anything to help with, because the man's material had no channel, and that the family's own capacity, which frequently exists, had never been developed because the man had been the solution.
