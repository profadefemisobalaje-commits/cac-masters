## 23.8 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Never Drank at Home

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in addiction treatment, family practice, and the occupational literature on concealed dependence.

**Femi is forty-nine, a branch manager in a commercial bank, married to Nkechi for twenty-one years, with three children aged between fourteen and twenty.**

The case emerged through an occupational referral. Femi had been observed by two colleagues over a period of about eight months: arriving late three or four times each month; a change in his handwriting and in the accuracy of his figures; a period of four days in March during which he was reported to be unwell; and, in June, a customer complaint concerning a call in which Femi's speech had been slurred. He was referred to the bank's medical service, which referred him for assessment.

Femi's account, given at assessment, was that he did not have a drinking problem because he never drank at home. The pattern he described, once he had begun, was the following.

He drank on the way home from work, in the vehicle, from a bottle kept in the boot, between the office and the house, on approximately four days each week for about six years. He drank at the bar attached to a hotel near his office, in the early evening, on two or three days a week, with the intention of a single drink and, in his description, "one or two more than that." He drank at the weekends, at a club, from Friday until Sunday, at levels he described as social. He never drank in the morning, he never drank in the house, and he had never been intoxicated in front of his wife or children, which he regarded, at the time of the assessment, as the evidence that no problem existed.

Four features of the history emerged subsequently.

First, the pattern had developed from a specific and datable event: a role change six years earlier, in which Femi had been moved from an operations position in which he had been highly competent to a branch management position requiring sales performance, at which he had no experience and which he had disliked from the first month.

Second, the consumption had increased gradually, and the increase was in quantity rather than in frequency for the first four years and in both thereafter. He had attempted to stop entirely three times, for periods of between eleven days and six weeks, and each attempt had ended in a period of heavier use than before.

Third, his sleep had deteriorated seriously over about two years: he was waking at approximately 3 a.m. and lying awake, and he had begun to require a drink at that hour on some nights. This symptom, which he had not mentioned to his physician at an insurance examination eight months earlier, was the clearest clinical indicator of dependence.

Fourth, the concealment had produced a set of secondary arrangements that had become substantial in their own right: a second fuel budget to account for the extra journeys; the scheduling of his arrival at home after the children had gone to bed; the removal of a telephone call log that he had worried about; and, most significantly, a persistent refusal of two family holidays on the grounds of work, because he could not maintain the pattern away from home.

The assessment identified alcohol dependence at a moderate level with an associated depressive episode, which had been present for about two years and which he had described to himself as being tired of the job.

The treatment was as follows.

The initial period required a medically supervised withdrawal, because of the quantity and duration of use, and this was conducted over five days with medication. Femi described this period as the most significant element of the whole process: *I had not known that I was physically dependent. I thought I had a habit. When I understood that I could not stop safely without a doctor, I understood what the last six years had actually been.*

The withdrawal was followed by a course of medication and weekly psychological treatment, both continuing over about eighteen months, and by attendance at a structured group programme, which Femi joined reluctantly in the fourth month and continued for two years.

The disclosure to Nkechi was conducted in the eighth week, with the practitioner present, and it produced, in Femi's description, the response he had least expected: she had known something was wrong for at least four years, had concluded that he was having an affair or had decided that he did not want the family, and had spent the period attempting to manage an unnamed situation. The disclosure required, in the following months, a second conversation that Femi had not anticipated: the account of what the concealment had done to her.

The arrangement changes were substantial and they were not limited to the drinking. Femi took a reduction in grade and a transfer back to an operations position in the second year, with a salary reduction of about twelve per cent, and described the decision as the one that consolidated the recovery: *The job was the reason for the drinking and everybody knew it except me. I have never been good at selling and I spent six years being bad at it and drinking about it.*

Three years after the assessment, Femi was abstinent, his depressive episode had resolved, and he described the position as follows: *I never drank at home. That was my evidence and it was the whole of my lie. What I learned in the first month was that the important question was never where I drank. It was why, and where I was going at six o'clock in the evening.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The concealment was structured around a plausible indicator.** *I never drink at home* was the man's evidence for the absence of a problem, and its plausibility was the reason that neither his household nor he had identified the condition for six years. The case is included because this pattern — dependence maintained entirely away from the household, or in a form the household cannot interpret — is common among men in employment.

**The pattern had a datable origin.** A role change that the man disliked and could not refuse, followed by a gradual increase in use. This is the ordinary history of dependence in men: not a pathology that arrives, but a method of management that develops from a specific situation and becomes independent of it.

**The physical dependence was the pivot.** The withdrawal requirement was the first fact that contradicted the man's own account, and his statement — that the understanding of physical dependence changed his understanding of the previous six years — is the reason that Section 23.5 places assessment first in every case.

**The wife had constructed a different explanation.** As in Section 23.4, the household's conclusions in the presence of unexplained behaviour are generally about the man's regard for the family. The second conversation, conducted months later, concerned the effect of the concealment rather than the drinking, and the case is included because this second conversation is frequently omitted from the recovery work and is frequently the substance of the marital repair.

**The recovery required an arrangement change.** The transfer and the reduction in grade were not incidental to the treatment; in Femi's account they were its mechanism. This is the pattern in Section 23.6 — that recovery which leaves the arrangements untouched has a lower prospect of durability — illustrated at the level of a man's working life.

## 23.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"How do I know whether I have a problem or I simply enjoy drinking?"**

Four questions are decisive. Have I attempted to reduce or stop and failed? Has my use increased over the last two years? Has anything important been affected — health, marriage, work, money, judgement? And do I experience any symptom when I stop — sleeplessness, tremor, anxiety, sweating? Three affirmative answers indicate a situation that requires assessment. The questions are answered accurately only after a period of honest recording, which is the reason Section 23.5 places the record first.

**"Will my employer find out?"**

This depends on the jurisdiction and the profession, and the accurate answer is obtained from a practitioner or a confidential advisory service rather than assumed. Two general points are relevant: treatment obtained through a health service is generally confidential, and the situation is materially better addressed before an incident than after one. The man who is considering treatment should ask the specific question of the specific practitioner: what is disclosed, to whom, and under what conditions.

**"Can I manage this myself?"**

Where the pattern is harmful use rather than dependence, the measures in Route Three are appropriate and frequently sufficient. Where dependence is present, the evidence indicates that self-management is rarely sufficient and that the attempts consume years that the condition uses to progress. The assessment in Route One determines which situation applies.

**"My wife says she will leave if it continues. Is that fair?"**

It is a legitimate position for a household to hold, and it is frequently stated at a point that the household has reached after years rather than in a first response. The relevant questions for the man are whether the statement is specific, whether it is implemented, and whether he has obtained treatment. The research on family pressure indicates that a stated and implemented consequence, accompanied by the availability of treatment, is among the more effective interventions available.

**"What if I return to use after a year?"**

Then the plan resumes and the return is treated as information, as described in Section 23.6. The single most useful action is to inform the practitioner or the group within days, because the isolation that follows a return is the mechanism by which a single episode becomes a return to dependence.

**"My son is using. What do I do?"**

The material in Section 23.7 applies in order: assessment, the specific and repeated offer of help, the removal of the rescues, the stated and implemented consequence, the household's own support, and the safety arrangements where required. The specific addition for a father is the preservation of the relationship, which is the resource that is most frequently consumed by the attempt to manage the condition; and the material in Chapter 17 on being a person the child can approach applies in full.

::: summary
**Chapter 23 in brief.** Addiction is distinguished from harmful use and from habits by the presence of compulsion, impaired control, withdrawal, tolerance, the neglect of obligations, and continued use despite harm. It is a condition with physiological, psychological, and social components; it responds to treatment; and it is sustained by concealment, which is therefore the principal target of intervention.

Men's principal forms are alcohol, gambling and betting, other substances, sexual conduct, gaming and screen use, work, and other behaviours including expenditure and risk-taking. Every form shares two features: the concealment escalates faster than the behaviour, and the behaviour always serves a purpose — the management of a state — which is why the behaviour persists and why identifying its function is the first step of treatment.

Concealment is maintained by six forces: standing, the anticipated household reaction, the belief in self-management, the fear of formal entry into treatment, shame, and the absence of any person to confide in. Its costs frequently exceed the costs of the condition itself, principally through delay, the household's wrong conclusions, the compulsion to compensate, and the loss of the years in which the man could have been known.

The routes to recovery are assessment; professional treatment including outpatient work, medication, residential treatment, and group programmes; the measures a man can take himself, comprising honest recording, environmental change, and substitution; and, finally and not first, the conversation with the household, conducted with professional support and after treatment has begun. Relapse is a common feature of recovery rather than a verdict on the man, and the variables that determine the outcome are the speed of return to treatment and the maintenance of a continuing arrangement.

Where the person with the condition is somebody else, the actions that help are a professional assessment, a stated and implemented consequence, a boundary defined in advance, a specific and repeated offer of help, and support for the household itself. The actions that do not help are the repeated request, the rescue that removes the consequences, the moral lecture, the threat that is not carried out, the secrecy maintained on the man's behalf, and the claim that nothing can be done until the person wants help. Where violence or the endangerment of children is present, the safety material in Appendix G takes precedence over everything else.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Applying the four questions in Section 23.9, do you have a situation that requires assessment? What have you not recorded honestly, and when will you?
2. From the forms in Section 23.2, which applies to you — and what is the function the behaviour serves? What state is it managing?
3. Which of the six forces maintaining concealment in Section 23.3 operates most strongly in your case, and who is the one person to whom the disclosure could be made?
4. What has the concealment cost you in the last three years, in money, health, sleep, and relationships? Write the figures.
5. If you are the person with the condition, what is your danger-point plan: the three situations in which the risk is highest, and what will be done in each?
6. If somebody in your household or family is affected, which of the unhelpful actions in Section 23.7 are you currently performing — the repeated request, the rescue, the lecture, the unimplemented threat, or the maintained secrecy?
7. What is the arrangement in your life that the behaviour is serving — the job, the marriage, the hours, the environment — and what change would be required for the recovery to hold?

## Further Reading

- **On the nature of dependence:** the standard clinical literature on substance use disorders, including the diagnostic criteria for harmful use and dependence and the evidence on the physiological mechanisms of reward, tolerance, and withdrawal.
- **On alcohol and health:** the substantial research literature on the health effects of alcohol, including the current evidence on the relationship between consumption and mortality, and the findings on the medical management of withdrawal.
- **On gambling:** the research on gambling disorder, including its mechanisms, its recent amplification through mobile and online betting, and the evidence on treatment.
- **On treatment:** the literature on the effectiveness of the different treatment modalities, including outpatient, residential, medication-assisted, and peer-support approaches, and the findings on what determines their outcomes.
- **On relapse:** the research on relapse and its prevention, including the identification of high-risk situations and the evidence that the speed of return to treatment predicts the long-term outcome.
- **On families:** the literature on the effects of addiction on households, including the guidance for family members on the actions that help and those that do not, and the material on the support that family members require themselves.
- **Practical resources:** national and regional confidential telephone advisory services; addiction treatment services, including those attached to general hospitals and those provided privately; peer-support groups for the man and separate groups for family members; and the safety material in Appendix G.
