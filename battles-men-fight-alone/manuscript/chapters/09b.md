## 9.3 Living Beyond One's Means to Maintain Appearances

The phrase *living beyond one's means* is used as a moral criticism, and the moral framing conceals the machinery. This section sets out the mechanism, because a man who understands how the pattern operates can identify it in his own life with precision and interrupt it at a specific point.

### The Four Pressures

Free spending above one's level is not, in most cases, driven by appetite. Four pressures produce it, and men recognise them immediately when they are named.

**The cost of belonging.** In many communities, participation in social life requires expenditure that is not optional: the contribution at a funeral, the gift at a wedding, the levy for a development association, the amount expected at a naming ceremony. To give less than the expected amount is not merely to spend less; it is to make a statement, and the statement is noticed. Men frequently describe this expenditure as the largest single avoidable cost in their budget, and describe simultaneously that it cannot be avoided without social consequences.

**The cost of the position.** A man whose work or standing places him in a particular tier is expected to present accordingly: the vehicle appropriate to a bank manager calling on clients, the premises suitable for the business, the clothing appropriate for the professional, the hospitality expected of an office holder. Some of this expenditure is genuinely instrumental — it produces revenue — and some is not, and the two are frequently confused by the man paying for them.

**The cost of the promise.** Unfulfilled commitments to the household: the standard that was established in a better year, the school that was chosen when income was higher, the house that was built at a particular standard, the assistance to a relative that was promised and is now permanent. Each of these is a claim established in the past and maintained in the present, and none of them can be reduced without an explanation.

**The cost of concealment itself.** As Section 9.1 established, this is the element most frequently overlooked. A man who is concealing a financial difficulty spends money to make the concealment credible: attending the events, maintaining the standard, keeping up the contribution, avoiding the conversation that a visible reduction would provoke.

### The Mechanics of the Spiral

The four pressures are sustained by three financial mechanisms, and it is worth naming them because each has a specific intervention.

**Debt used to fund consumption rather than to produce income.** Borrowing for an income-producing asset is a business decision with a calculable return. Borrowing to maintain a standard of living produces an obligation without a return, and the obligation compounds. Where a man's borrowing is predominantly of the second kind, the position is deteriorating by definition, and the rate of deterioration is calculable.

**Sequential obligations.** A household managing a shortfall frequently services creditors in order of visibility rather than in order of cost: the ones who will embarrass most are paid first, and the ones with the highest interest are paid last. This maximises the total cost of the debt while protecting the appearance, which is the definition of the pattern.

**Sale of productive assets.** When borrowing capacity is exhausted, the household sells assets that produce income — a vehicle used for business, land, equipment, a business stake — in order to meet obligations. The sale provides temporary relief and permanently reduces capacity, and it is very frequently concealed until the asset is gone.

### The Interruption Point

Each of the three mechanisms has a corresponding intervention, and the practical value of this section lies in the fact that a man can act on all three without disclosing anything to anybody, which makes them available as first steps.

1. **Separate borrowing into productive and consumptive.** List every obligation and mark each as producing income, maintaining an asset, or funding consumption. The third category is the one to stop. No new borrowing in that category, from today.
2. **Reorder creditors by cost rather than by visibility.** Pay the highest cost first, and — this is the part men find hardest — communicate with the visible creditors honestly and early rather than avoiding them. The conversation that a man is avoiding is usually with precisely the person who would negotiate.
3. **Protect productive assets.** Identify the assets that generate income, and treat them as protected: they are not available to meet consumption obligations, whatever the social cost of the alternative.

### The Reduction Conversation

At some point, the pattern requires a reduction in the visible standard, and the reduction requires an explanation to the household, the extended family, and, in some cases, the community. Chapter 10 sets out the method in full; three principles apply here.

**Reduce in categories rather than across the board.** A household handles a specific reduction better than a general anxiety. *The second car goes; we will use transport for a year* is manageable. *We must cut everything* is not.

**Give the honest reason, in one sentence.** *The business lost two contracts and I have been covering the shortfall with a loan; I am not willing to continue that, so we will live at the level we can afford for a year while I rebuild.* The sentence is short, factual, blames no one, and concedes nothing to shame.

**Do the reduction in one decisive movement, not gradually.** A staged reduction produces a prolonged period of uncertainty and repeated disappointment. A single, clearly stated change, held to, produces a settled arrangement.

## 9.4 Debt, Anxiety, and Emotional Strain

Debt is not only a financial condition. It is a psychological condition with a well-documented symptom profile, and men who carry it frequently present the symptoms long before they disclose the cause.

### The Symptom Profile

The following symptoms are reliably reported by men carrying significant debt, across countries and income levels.

| Domain | Typical experiences |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep | Waking early with dread; difficulty falling asleep; dreaming of owing or being unable to pay |
| Attention | Intrusive arithmetic during work; inability to concentrate; forgetting tasks and appointments |
| Mood | Irritability, especially at home; flatness; abrupt loss of interest in previously enjoyable activities |
| Body | Tension in neck and shoulders; headaches; stomach symptoms; palpitations |
| Behaviour | Avoiding telephone calls; avoiding the post; avoiding particular places and people; increased alcohol use; withdrawal from social contact |
| Thinking | Catastrophic projections; a sense of being trapped; repeated rehearsal of impossible solutions |
| Relationships | Secrecy from a partner; irritability towards children; declining invitations; reduced sexual interest |

Table: The symptom profile of concealed debt. The pattern closely resembles that of the anxiety and depressive presentations described in Chapters 5 and 6, which is one of the reasons the underlying cause is frequently missed.

The practical significance of the table is twofold. First, a man reading this list and recognising himself should consider whether an undisclosed financial obligation is contributing to symptoms he has attributed to overwork, age, or family life. Second, clinicians and counsellors meeting a man with this profile should ask directly about debt and obligations, since the subject is rarely volunteered.

### Why Debt Is a Distinctive Kind of Stress

Debt has four features that distinguish it from other difficulties, and each of these features explains why men find it so difficult to carry.

**It is continuous.** A bereavement recedes; a debt does not. The obligation is present every month, at every meeting with the creditor's name, in every family discussion about money, and every time a plan is proposed that requires funds.

**It has a party.** Unlike a business failure or an illness, debt involves an identifiable person or institution that expects payment and that has a legitimate claim. This transforms a difficulty into a relationship, and frequently into one involving shame, avoidance, and the anticipation of judgement.

**It is quantifiable, and therefore demonstrable.** A man may not know his own position precisely (most men in this situation do not), but he knows that there is a figure, and that the figure represents his failure in a countable form.

**It is loaded with moral content.** Debt carries a moral vocabulary — owing, failing to pay, dishonouring an obligation — that other difficulties do not. Men frequently describe the shame of owing money as more severe than the loss of an actual amount.

### The Consequences That Make it Worse

Three consequences of debt-related strain make the financial position worse, and it is worth stating them plainly because the pattern is so common.

**Impaired financial decision-making under stress.** Decisional capacity is degraded by chronic stress and poor sleep — a finding established in Chapters 1 and 5 and confirmed in the substantial research literature on scarcity and financial decision-making. Men under financial strain make worse financial decisions: they accept high-interest loans, they make hurried sales, they decline the slower option that would cost less.

**Self-medication.** Alcohol use rises, which costs money and reduces the capacity to work effectively.

**Withdrawal from the people who could help.** As described above. The withdrawal removes both the practical assistance and the advice that would resolve the difficulty.

### The Alternative: Debt as a Project

The practical alternative to carrying debt as a private shame is to treat it as a project: a defined problem with a defined position, a plan, an adviser, and a review date. Chapter 7 set out the sequence; this chapter adds three points specific to debt.

**Obtain the figure.** Total obligations, and for each: the amount, the rate, the security, and the default consequences. Men frequently discover, on completing this, that their position is materially better than they feared, because fear supplies an estimate rather than a number. They also frequently discover a smaller obligation with a punitive rate that can be eliminated quickly, producing both relief and a visible success.

**Speak to the largest creditor first.** Creditors prefer payment to litigation, and a man who approaches a creditor before default with a specific proposal is treated very differently from a man who avoids contact. Appendix D provides a template for that communication.

**Take advice from somebody who has no stake in the outcome.** Free or low-cost financial counselling services, cooperative societies, credit unions, professional accountancy bodies with pro bono schemes, and reputable advisory services are all available in most settings. Where the amounts are significant, or where there is a risk of legal proceedings, professional advice is not a luxury; it is the difference between a manageable restructuring and an unnecessary judgment.
