## 33.7 Practical Exercise: The Inventory and the Distribution

This exercise takes about three hours, over four sittings. It is the most demanding exercise in this part of the book, and it should not be conducted in a single sitting.

### Part Three: The Distribution (Sitting Four)

**Section A: The stated arrangement.** Write the terms for the three largest recurring items: the amount, the date, the condition, and the duration.

**Section B: The refusals.** For one item that fails the four tests, write the refusal in the short form of Section 33.4, in no more than two sentences, with the alternative where one exists. Record the date on which you will deliver it, and the person you will tell afterwards.

**Section C: The referral map.** Compile the written list: for each category of demand you currently meet — the legal, the medical, the financial, the educational, the procedural, the employment, the property, the dispute — the institution, practitioner, or agency to which it properly belongs, with the contact and the route of access.

**Section D: The family's arrangement.** Specify the meeting or the arrangement in which the family's obligations will be allocated: who will be present, what will be presented, what contribution you will state, and what you will ask of each other party.

**Section E: The development of capacity.** Name the two people whose capacity you will develop, the specific matter you will assign to each, and the date. Record the rule: once assigned, you will not perform it.

**Section F: Your own arrangements.** Specify the confidant, the group, or the professional arrangement, the date of the first step, and the person you will tell that you have established it.

## 33.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"If I stop, who will help them?"**

The question is the role's own logic, and the tests of Section 33.4 are the answer. In the case material, the family's other capacity is generally present and unexamined: the arrangement has taught the family that the man will manage, and the family has organized itself accordingly. The specific finding is that the man's reduction is generally accompanied by the appearance of other contributions, frequently from people the man had not considered — and the case material also includes the instances in which it is not, in which the demand is genuinely the man's and is met in a stated and bounded form rather than indefinitely.

**"My family will say that I have changed and that I think I am better than them."**

The response is common and the chapter records it accurately rather than dismissing it. The material on standing in Chapter 25 applies, and the practical measures are those of Section 33.4: the refusal given briefly, without argument about the demand's legitimacy, consistently, and with an alternative where one exists; and the man's participation in the family's arrangements continued at the level available to him. Men who have conducted the change report that the family's account of the man adjusts over a period measured in years, and that the arrangement itself adjusts considerably faster.

**"I do not want the money back. Why should I state terms?"**

Because the terms are not for the debt's recovery. The stated arrangement supplies three things: the person's knowledge of what has been given, which prevents the position in which the man's assistance is invisible to the people who receive it; the boundary at which the assistance ends; and the man's own record, which is the inventory's material. Men describe the specific difference: an assistance given with terms is a matter they can name in conversation, and an assistance given without them is a matter they carry.

**"My relative is genuinely incapable. Should I refuse help to a person who cannot manage?"**

No, and Section 33.5 states the exception explicitly. Where the incapacity is genuine — a child, a person with a disability, a person in an acute crisis, a person whose age or condition removes the capacity — the assistance is appropriate. What changes is the arrangement: the assistance should be given in the form the incapacity requires, with the stated terms and the duration appropriate to it, and with the man's own arrangements in place, since a man who carries a genuinely dependent person indefinitely requires exactly the arrangements of Chapter 27. The distinction the chapter draws is between the genuine dependency and the arrangement in which a competent adult's difficulty is met on his behalf.

**"I would feel like a fraud. My whole standing is that I am the one who helps."**

The material of Section 32.3 and Chapter 25.5 applies directly: the man's standing should be moved from the role to the conduct. The reader should test the proposition by asking what remains if the role is removed: in the case material, the man's competence, his record, his obligations discharged, and his reliability are all present and are not dependent on the role's unbounded form. A man who has helped a family for twenty-five years does not lose his position by distributing the load; he loses the arrangement in which he was the family's only structure, which is a change in the arrangement and not in the man.

**"How long does the change take?"**

The inventory takes a month, and the man should not proceed without it. The household arrangement and the refusals can be conducted within two to three months. The family's arrangement takes a meeting and, in the case material, from six to eighteen months to settle into its new form, with a period of dispute in the middle. The man's own arrangements — the confidant, the group, the professional relationship — are the last and they should not be deferred indefinitely, because the arrangement in which the man is the load-bearing point of forty people is the arrangement that most requires a place in which his own position is held.

::: summary
**Chapter 33 in brief.** The man who is everyone's solution occupies nine settings at once: the family's financial provision, its mediator, its administrator, the community's man, the workplace's support, the friend's solution, the household's absorber, the man consulted because he is competent, and the holder of the family's confidences. The demands that recur are the expensive ones; the demands arriving through several channels are the invisible ones; each demand is generally reasonable individually; the demands are made of the person who has met them before; and refusing them carries a cost that is frequently accurate.

The role is acquired in six steps — the man becomes competent or solvent, the first request is met, the record is established, the requests multiply, the refusal becomes unacceptable, and the man's own material accumulates without an arrangement — and it is sustained by four returns: standing, identity, company, and the avoidance of the man's own material. Its five costs are the accumulation of other people's material, the erosion of the man's money, time, and capacity, the resentment that cannot be stated, the transfer of the cost to his household, and the absence of any arrangement for the man's own position.

The correction begins with four tests: whose responsibility is this in principle; whose capacity is this, and who else has been asked; is this mine to do or mine to make possible; and what will this cost my household, has that cost been counted, and has the household been consulted? The available answers are full assistance, partial assistance, the arrangement instead of the payment, and the refusal — which should be short, should not argue about the demand's legitimacy, and should be consistent.

The distinction between assistance and rescue is the chapter's ethical core: assistance supplies what the person requires in order to address his own matter, and rescue removes the matter from him. The markers of rescue are that the matter recurs and that the man's involvement is required for it to be addressed at all, and the corrected forms are assistance made conditional on the change, assistance supplied through an arrangement rather than to the person, assistance with a limit and a duration, and the construction of the person's own capacity. The load is distributed through six arrangements: the written inventory, the family's own arrangement, the referral map, the stated terms, the man's own arrangements under Chapter 27, and the household's participation. The objective is not withdrawal but distribution: the man becomes one of several load-bearing points rather than the only one, which is a benefit to the people he supports as well as to him.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the inventory. What was the total, in money and in hours, and how does it compare with your own estimate before you began?
2. What did your household postpone, reduce, or cancel in the same period, and did you count it at the time?
3. Apply the four tests to your three largest recurring items. How many of them are genuinely yours?
4. Which of the seven forms of resentment in Section 33.3 are present in your case, and from which person has your distance increased without explanation?
5. Apply the two markers of rescue. Which of your assistance is functioning as a rescue, and what arrangement is it maintaining?
6. Write the three refusal statements and the three stated arrangements. What is the sentence in each that you most fear saying aloud?
7. Name the two people whose capacity you will develop, the matter you will assign, and the date — and write the sentence you will use if you are tempted to perform it yourself.

## Further Reading

- **On caregiving and vicarious load:** the literature on carers and on vicarious or secondary stress, including the findings on the physical and psychological effects of attending to the difficulties of others, the factors that protect carers, and the evidence on the arrangements that reduce the load.
- **On boundaries and assertiveness:** the practice literature on personal boundaries, including the material on refusal, on the form of an effective refusal, and on the evidence concerning the sustainability of arrangements constructed without limits.
- **On enabling and codependency:** the literature on enabling behaviour in families affected by addiction and by chronic difficulty, including the findings on how assistance can maintain the arrangements it is intended to address, and the evidence on the forms of support that are associated with better outcomes.
- **On family obligation and the allocation of support:** the research on intergenerational and extended-family support, including the findings on who is expected to provide, the concentration of support in particular family members, and the effects on those members and their own households.
- **On delegation and the development of others:** the practice literature on delegation, on the development of other people's capability, and on the evidence concerning the conditions under which delegation succeeds, including the rule that the delegating person must not complete the task once it is assigned.
- **Practical resources:** family mediation and community mediation services; legal advice on estates, guarantees, and family property; financial counselling; carers' support services; and the material in Appendices D, G, J, and O.
