## 4.3 The Disappearance of Meaningful Friendships

The thinning of men's friendships in adulthood is one of the most reliably observed patterns in the social research on adult life, and it happens in stages. Naming the stages matters, because a man who recognises the third or fourth stage in his own life can often intervene before the fifth.

### Stage One: Reduced Frequency Without Loss of Warmth

The friendship remains entirely intact in substance, but the meetings become less frequent. There are reasons, and the reasons are good: new employment, a baby, a move, a change of shift. Contact continues by telephone and, in the modern setting, by message. Both parties are certain that the friendship is unchanged.

This stage is not a problem. It is a stage that must be navigated deliberately if the later ones are to be avoided, and the navigation consists of establishing a rhythm that does not depend on circumstance. The friends who survive middle age are, in most cases, those who moved from meeting by accident to meeting by arrangement.

### Stage Two: Conversation Shifts to Logistics

The content of the conversation gradually narrows. Two men who once discussed everything now discuss what is necessary: the school fees, the family meeting, the coworker's promotion, the price of cement. Neither has decided to narrow the subject matter. The narrowing happens because logistics are what both men have in common at that stage of life, and because the deeper subjects require conditions that neither now has: time, privacy, and the absence of the other's spouse, children, or colleagues.

At this stage, contact is preserved while intimacy declines. A man may still speak to his friend weekly and not have told him anything real for two years.

### Stage Three: Contact Requires a Reason

Once the relationship has become transactional, the contact becomes conditional on a reason. A man does not call his friend to ask how he is; he calls because he requires something, or because there is news to report, or because a mutual acquaintance has died. The calls continue, and their number is not necessarily reduced. What has vanished is the call that has no purpose, and with it the possibility of the conversations that only purposeless calls produce.

### Stage Four: Withdrawal Under Strain

The fourth stage is the dangerous one, and it is a defining feature of male loneliness. When a man comes under serious strain — financial difficulty, marital conflict, depression, bereavement, an illness — the last thing he does is to call his friends. He withdraws. The reason is a combination of shame and consideration: he does not wish to be seen in his present condition, and he does not want to add his difficulty to theirs. He does not answer messages as promptly. He is busy when invitations arrive.

From outside, the withdrawal is read as a change in the man's regard — *he has become proud*, or *he no longer has time for us* — and the invitations gradually stop. From inside, the withdrawal is a strategy of concealment that has the effect of removing the very relationships that might have helped.

### Stage Five: The Empty Network at a Time of Need

The fifth stage is the one men describe in surveys and in counselling rooms, sometimes decades later: the moment at which a serious crisis — a divorce, a diagnosis, a bereavement, a business failure — occurs and the man realises that he has nobody to call. Contacts, yes. Advisers, sometimes. Colleagues, certainly. But nobody to whom the sentence *I am not managing* can be said.

The experience is frequently described as astonishing. The man in question has been a reliable friend to others for years; he has attended their weddings and funerals, contributed to their welfare, and given assistance to their relatives. He has done everything that friendship requires in its public dimension, and he has arrived, at the age of fifty-two, with no one in the private dimension. The pattern is presented in Figure 4.3.

### Reversing the Pattern: The Practical Rule

The step that prevents the whole sequence is the conversion of friendship from an accident into an arrangement. Specifically, three rules do most of the work, and they are deceptively modest.

**Fix a recurring time.** Two hours, every second week or every month, with one man or two, at a fixed time. The content of the meeting does not matter; the recurrence does. What is under construction is not a conversation but a structure that will produce conversations over the following ten years.

**Make contact without a purpose at least once a week.** One message to one person, containing nothing that requires a response: *I was thinking about our conversation last month. How is your mother?* The purposeless message is what distinguishes friendship from a professional relationship.

**Disclose something first.** For reasons developed in Chapter 26, men rarely initiate disclosure, and every male friendship in which disclosure has occurred was started by somebody. This is the only reliable method of finding out whether a friendship can carry anything real, and it consists of testing the water with a matter of moderate weight — a worry about money, a disagreement with a wife, a concern about a father's health — and observing the response.

### What Men Report When They Examine the Pattern Honestly

When men are asked, in confidence, to describe what happened to their friendships, a small number of descriptions recur, and they are worth recording because they are more persuasive than any general claim.

**The one-year gap.** Men frequently identify a specific year in which contact with a group of friends collapsed: the year of a new job, the year a child was born, the year a parent became ill, the year of a relocation. In most cases, the collapse was not a decision but an overload: for twelve months there was genuinely no capacity, and at the end of the twelve months nobody resumed the arrangement, because resuming it required an initiative that no one had ever needed to take before.

**The numbers that do not add up.** Men who are asked to count their actual close contacts are frequently surprised. A man of fifty who describes himself as having many friends may, on examination, identify two men he has spoken to in the last six months, neither of whom knows anything important about his life. The discrepancy between the felt sense (*I have plenty of friends*) and the audit (*I have two, and they do not know me*) is one of the most useful discoveries a man can make about himself, and it is available to anyone with a notebook and twenty minutes.

**The funeral test.** A widely reported experience among men is the discovery, at a funeral, of the quantity of affectionate regard that exists for them and the absence of any relationship through which it can be expressed. Men describe standing at a graveside and being embraced by a dozen men who tell them sincerely that they are brothers, while knowing that none of those relationships has ever involved a private conversation, and that the embrace will not be followed by a telephone call.

**The silence about loss.** Men who have suffered a serious loss — the death of a child, a divorce, a business collapse, a diagnosis — report a characteristic and painful pattern: a period of intense support immediately after the event, followed by an almost complete cessation of contact within a few months. The surrounding network responds to the crisis and then withdraws, because the ongoing condition is harder to address than the event. The man is left to carry the long part of the loss alone, and he usually does so without complaint, interpreting the withdrawal as evidence that he should stop mentioning it.

**The unchanging conversation.** Two men who have been friends for thirty years may find themselves, at a reunion, repeating the same series of stories from their twenties for the fourth time. The stories are affectionate and they are true, and they are also a substitute for anything current, because neither man has any practice in speaking about the present. Men recognise this pattern with a particular kind of sadness: the friendship is deep, old, and entirely frozen.

## 4.4 When Everyone Needs You but Nobody Checks on You

There is a variant of this loneliness that deserves separate treatment because it has an identifiable structure and a predictable timeline. It is the condition of the man who has become, within his family and community, an institution.

### The Structure of the Condition

The man in question has, over years, been constructed as the household's infrastructure. He is expected to solve financial problems, mediate disputes, provide representation, host visitors, fund ceremonies, transport people, and be available at all hours. The construction was not his decision. It is the outcome of a series of small assignments, each of which he accepted because there was nobody else.

In such a position, the man receives two things: demands and respect. He does not receive three things that people need just as much: enquiry about his own state, permission to be temporarily unavailable, and reciprocation — the experience of being helped rather than only helping.

### The Effects of Chronic One-Directional Support

Sustained one-directional support produces a predictable set of consequences, and they are important enough to list.

**Unacknowledged resentment.** The man does not feel angry, at least not consciously. He feels tired and, occasionally, oddly hollow. The resentment is present but is suppressed along with everything else, and it tends to emerge in unhelpful forms: harshness about small matters, a withdrawal of warmth from a particular family member, or a refusal to assist in one specific case that becomes disproportionate to the occasion.

**A hardening of the boundary-free pattern.** Because assistance has always been provided, the family's expectations increase. Where a boundary has never existed, its first introduction — the first *no* — is experienced by everybody as a rupture rather than an adjustment, which is why Chapter 10 devotes substantial space to introducing boundaries without destroying the relationship.

**A collapse in reciprocity within the marriage.** Wives of such men sometimes report that they have themselves stopped offering support, because the household system has assigned them a different role: managing the children, the household, and their own employment, while the husband is the household's external interface. Both partners then feel unsupported, and each believes the other is responsible.

**Loss of the man's own initiative.** A man who has been the answering machine for a family for fifteen years can struggle to identify what he wants when asked. There is a specific and disconcerting experience reported by men in this position: asked what they would like to do on a free Saturday, they cannot answer. The capacity to have preferences about one's own life is not destroyed, but it has not been exercised for a long time, and it requires deliberate recovery. Chapter 40 is addressed to this work.

### The Reciprocal Question

There is one practical test that clarifies the situation and often produces the necessary change. It is a question the man asks himself, and it may also be put to him by a counsellor or a friend: **If I were the one in need, whom would I call, and would they come?**

The answer is frequently not *nobody* — most men identify one or two names — but neither is it a list. What matters is what happens next. The man who identifies one or two names has identified the beginning of a plan, and Appendix J, Section 2, turns that identification into an approach. The man who identifies nobody has identified the precise task in front of him, and it is the most important task in this chapter.
