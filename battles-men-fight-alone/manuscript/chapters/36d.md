## 36.4 The Sequence and the Work

The work has a sequence, and the sequence is supplied by the four conditions of the previous section. This section describes the stages, the durations, and the relapses.

### The Five Stages

**Stage One: The stabilisation.** Duration: weeks to months. What is conducted: the material of Chapter 34's assessment and safety plan where it applies; the immediate practical emergencies, including the financial, the medical, and the addiction-related; the sleep; the arrangements of Chapter 27.5, at least to the level of one recipient and one professional or group; the household's arrangements where the man's conduct is affecting it. The stage's marker: the man can state where his material goes and can name the person or the arrangement.

**Stage Two: The account.** Duration: months. What is conducted: the account in its written and spoken forms; the witness; the inventory of Chapter 36.1; the identification of the categories and the sequence; and, where the material requires it, the professional arrangement's structured work. The stage's marker: the man has described the material in full to one recipient and has a written version that he has produced himself.

**Stage Three: The mourning and the repair.** Duration: months to years. What is conducted: the mourning arrangements of Chapter 30.6 for each loss identified; the repairs of Chapter 29.5 for each wrong the man has identified as his own; the work of Chapters 26 and 33 where the material concerns relationships and obligations; and the correction of the record, which is frequently the most consequential element — the correction of the household's account of the man, the correction of the man's own account of himself, and the supply of the information that the arranged consequences require.

**Stage Four: The reconstruction.** Duration: years. What is conducted: the identity work of Chapter 24 where a role has been lost; the measure of Chapter 25.5 where the man's account of his worth rests on results; the measures of Chapter 28.6 for the dreams that were set down; the arrangements of Chapters 31, 33, and 35 for the load and the asking; and the construction of what the man will do with the years remaining.

**Stage Five: The reconnection and the maintenance.** Duration: continuous. What is conducted: the arrangements maintained rather than completed — the professional relationship where it continues, the group, the peer arrangement, the household's meeting, the dates of Chapter 30.6, the measures of Chapter 25.5, and the review described in Chapter 29.6's element five. The stage's marker: the arrangements exist without the man's having to construct them again after each difficulty.

### The Three Findings About the Sequence

**The stages are conducted in parallel as well as in sequence.** A man stabilises while he writes; he mourns while he reconstructs; the repairs continue after the reconnection. The sequence is a principle of priority rather than a timetable.

**The sequence is frequently reversed by circumstance, and this is not a failure.** The bereavement, the redundancy, the illness, the collapse of a business, or the loss of a relationship removes the man's stability and returns him to Stage One, and the return is normal. The finding from practice is that the return is shorter each time, because the arrangements have been constructed once and can be reoccupied.

**The work is not conducted alone.** Every stage requires at least one person beyond the man, and the arrangements are the mechanism. The chapter repeats the point because it is the finding that the case material supports most strongly: the men who conduct the work with the arrangements recover; the men who attempt it alone surface the material and return to the concealment.

### The Relapses

The word is used deliberately, and its meaning should be distinguished from the sense in which it is used in Chapter 23.

**The material's return.** The account has been constructed, the arrangements exist, and the material returns — at an anniversary, at a milestone, in response to a trigger, in a low period. The return is the ordinary course of the material and not evidence of failure. The handling: the arrangements are reoccupied, the material is described to the recipient, the date of Chapter 30.6 is observed, and the man continues with the conduct of the following day.

**The withdrawal from the arrangements.** The man stops attending, stops writing, stops calling, and the material resumes its operation. The handling: the arrangements are treated as requirements rather than as responses to the man's state, which is the reason the practice material prescribes the fixed time. The finding is that the fixed time is the element that saves the arrangement during the periods in which the man does not wish to attend.

**The resumption of the compensating conduct.** The drinking, the work, the risk, the spending, the screens. Chapter 22's material applies; the finding specific to this chapter is that the resumption should be treated as information about the man's state rather than as a moral failure, and that it frequently indicates that the sequence has moved too fast or that a stage has been attempted without the support that it requires.

### The Duration

The chapter supplies the answer plainly because men's expectations on the subject are the cause of a substantial proportion of the abandoned work.

The first change — the reduction in the material's unbidden arrival, the availability of a recipient, the improvement in the sleep — is generally reported within three to six months of the arrangements' establishment. The substantial change — the alteration of the man's conduct toward his household, the reduction of the material's operation in the observed places, the change in the man's account of himself — is generally reported at one to three years. The man's own account of the whole, offered in retrospect, is generally in decades: *I was twenty years carrying it and it took about four years to put it down.*

The men who abandon the work do so overwhelmingly in the first six months, before the first change has appeared, and their reason is generally the absence of the change they expected. The chapter's instruction on this point is therefore the most useful sentence it contains: **the first measure of progress is the existence of the arrangements and not the state of the man.** A man who has established the recipient, the professional, the group, the written account, and the household's arrangements has conducted the work's first two stages regardless of how he feels on the day.

## 36.5 When Professional Treatment Is Required

The chapter states the conditions and the treatments in plain terms, and it takes the position recorded in the introduction: where a condition requires treatment, the man's refusal of treatment is an arrangement with a known cost, borne principally by the people who depend on him.

### The Six Conditions

**1. Traumatic exposure with continuing symptoms.** The intrusion of an event — the images, the nightmares, the physical responses, the flashbacks; the avoidance of the settings associated with it; the hypervigilance, the startle response, the disturbed sleep, the irritability. Where these have persisted beyond about a month and are affecting the man's functioning, the assessment is required, and the treatment for the specific condition is established and effective.

**2. Depression that has not lifted.** The persistent lowering of mood, the loss of interest, the alteration of sleep and appetite, the fatigue, the difficulty in concentration, the sense of worthlessness, and the material of Chapter 34.3's first category. The assessment is required where the pattern has persisted for weeks, where it is impairing the man's functioning, or where the man's own conclusion is that nothing can be altered.

**3. Anxiety, panic, and the physical presentations.** The panic attacks, the persistent worry, the physical symptoms that have been investigated without a physical cause, the avoidance that has reduced the man's life. The conditions are treatable and the treatments are structured and brief in many cases.

**4. Substance and behavioural dependence.** The material of Chapters 22 and 23, and particularly where the man's own attempts have produced the pattern of Chapter 23.6: the reduction followed by the return. The treatment is established, and the finding from practice is that the man's own attempts are frequently the reason the material's severity is underestimated — a dependence that has required several attempts at control is generally a dependence that requires treatment.

**5. The consequences of severe or prolonged adversity in childhood.** The material of category one in Section 36.1: the persistent difficulty in relationships, the man's account of himself, the difficulty in regulating states, the pattern of the man's own conduct that he does not understand. The treatment is available in the forms developed for this material, and the finding is that the conditions are treatable and are frequently untreated because men do not present.

**6. The material of Chapter 34.3's first category, at any point.** The risk to the man's own life requires immediate assessment, and the chapter repeats the instruction of Chapter 34.5 without qualification.

### What the Treatments Consist Of

The following descriptions are supplied in plain terms, and the reader should note that the specific treatment is a matter for the practitioner's assessment.

**The talking treatments with the strongest evidence base.** Cognitive behavioural therapy, which examines the relation between the man's interpretations of events and his state, and which is structured, time-limited, and oriented to specific difficulties. Trauma-focused treatments, which address the traumatic material directly by established procedures that allow the material to be described and situated without its being relived at full intensity, and which are the treatments of choice where traumatic exposure is the principal matter. Interpersonal treatments, which address the man's difficulties in his relationships and in his conduct of the roles described in Parts III to V of this book. The treatments for substance dependence, which combine the behavioural work of Chapter 22 with assessment of the related conditions and with the supports of Chapter 23.

**Medication.** For specific conditions — the moderate and severe depressions, the anxiety conditions, and others — the practitioner may recommend medication alongside or before the talking treatment. The relevant findings for the reader: the medication is a treatment of the condition and not a substitute for the arrangements; the effect is generally apparent in weeks rather than days; the man should not discontinue it on his own; and the practitioner's monitoring is a component of the treatment.

**The setting.** Treatment is delivered through the general practitioner, a hospital or clinic's mental health service, a private practitioner, an employee assistance programme, a specialist service for a specific matter — the addiction, the trauma, the bereavement — or a service for a specific group. Appendix Q of this book supplies the routes by category, and the general practitioner is the entry point where the man is uncertain.

### The Two Notes for Men

**The conditions are ordinary and the treatments are effective.** The material that requires treatment is neither a consequence of the man's character nor a permanent state. The research on outcomes is consistent: the conditions are common, they respond to treatment, and the earlier the treatment the better the outcome.

**The barriers men report are the barriers the arrangements are designed to address.** The concern about the record, the position, the standing, and the disclosure is addressed by the confidentiality obligations of professional practice, by the routes that can be taken outside the man's setting, and by the practical material of Chapters 31 to 35. A man is not required to choose between his position and his treatment, and the finding from practice is that the choice appears artificial once the arrangements have been examined.
