## 32.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"If I say I do not know, will they not lose confidence in me?"**

The finding from the practice is unambiguous and it should be recorded accurately: in the settings that matter — the man's household, his organisation, his profession — a bounded answer increases confidence, because the people around him are making decisions on his information and require it to be accurate. The settings in which the admission costs standing are the settings in which nothing is established by the answer: the informal exchange, the competition among men, the environment in which the answer's function is the display. A man should distinguish the two, and should apply the protocol in the first and decline the exchange in the second.

**"I have been telling my organisation something that is wrong for years. What do I do?"**

Correct it, once, in writing, with the four elements: what I said; what I now know; the effect of the error; and what I am doing about it. The men who have done this report a specific and uniform outcome: the correction was received as information rather than as an admission, and the incident was not raised afterwards. The alternative — the error left in place and discovered elsewhere — produces the outcome described in the first cost of Section 32.2, and it is generally the more expensive of the two.

**"What if the setting requires certainty? My staff need to believe I know what I am doing."**

The distinction is between the man's certainty about the direction and his information about the details. The staff's requirement is the first: that the man knows where the organisation is going, that he will decide, and that he will hold the position through difficulty. Their requirement is not the man's total information, and the men with the strongest record of this distinction describe the arrangement precisely: the man says *I do not know the rate* and *I know the direction*, and both are believed because both are accurate.

**"My questions are not the technical ones. My questions are about my own life, and there is nobody to ask."**

Then the material of Chapter 27 applies and the arrangement is the required step: a professional, a peer group, one person. The question *what do I do about my life* is not a question that a man can answer from information he already holds, which is the reason that the isolated man's decisions are recognisable by the four forms described in Chapter 27.3. The protocol does not help here, because there is no answer to be found; the arrangement helps, because the question changes when it is spoken to somebody.

**"I am the senior man in my field in this state. There is nobody to refer to."**

Then the arrangement must be constructed beyond the state: the professional association's technical section, the practitioner in the capital or the neighbouring state, the correspondence, the periodic consultation, the visiting expert. Where the man's position genuinely has no referent in his environment, the arrangement itself becomes a component of his responsibility — the item that the man must construct because the organisation's accuracy depends upon it — and the material of Chapter 31.5 applies to its construction.

**"My father never said he did not know, and I am not going to change now."**

The reader should consider what the statement is protecting, in the terms of Section 32.2's costs. The form is inherited, the requirement is genuine, and the case material is consistent on the consequence: the arrangement is transmitted to the next generation and its costs are paid by the man's household, his organisation, and, in the proportion of cases in which the risk travels through the man's work, by people who had no part in it at all. The material of Chapter 43 on the next generation applies, and its finding is that the transmission is broken by conduct and not by the man's account of the matter.

::: summary
**Chapter 32 in brief.** A man in a position of responsibility is subject to a requirement to have answers: whatever the matter, he is expected to know, and his uncertainty is not among the responses the role permits. The requirement has six sources — the position's functional need for decisions, the cultural instruction that a man should know, the household's requirement as the man understands it, the form inherited from a father who never admitted ignorance, the man's own account of himself, and the rewards that attend a prompt answer irrespective of its accuracy.

The requirement has four costs. The bluff, in which the man supplies an answer he does not have, in forms that include the confidently wrong answer, the answer carried by its delivery, and the answer that ends the enquiry and thereby prevents the question from reaching somebody who knows. The arrested learning, since a man who cannot ask in public does not address the matters he does not understand, and acquires a persistent gap that would have closed within months. The distorted information, since a man who must always be correct is not brought bad news, and the organisation's information is thereby shaped by his own intolerance. And the withdrawal of the people around him, in which the household manages alone, the children select their disclosures, and the man discovers years later what was being carried beside him.

The knowledge trap is the position in which the man's standing rests on his knowing. Its five forms are the self-taught expert without a credential to fall back on, the professional whose authority is broader than his expertise, the man whose marginal knowledge is being tested in the exchange among men, the man who holds the family's or the institution's history, and the man who has succeeded to a position previously occupied by somebody who knew. Several of the questions a man receives are, in any case, not questions that his authority equips him to answer, and the classification is fourfold: within his authority and his information, within his authority and outside his information, outside his authority, and not a request for information at all.

The correction is a protocol in three parts: what I know, stated accurately; what I do not know, stated plainly and in the same register; and what happens next, with the name, the method, and the time. It is applied in meetings, in households, to decisions that require information the man lacks, and in the correction of answers he has been giving incorrectly. Its second half is the use of questions as a competence: what am I missing; who disagrees, and what is their case; what would have to be true for this to fail; what is the part of this that nobody has raised; and what have I done in the last month that I should know about. The man who asks has not ceased to decide, and the man who says he does not know has not become uncertain. He has become the man whose answers can be relied upon, which is the only standing that the people who depend on him actually require.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Of the six sources in Section 32.1, which is the strongest in your case, and what evidence in your conduct supports that assessment?
2. Record your material for the arrested learning: what have you never asked, in which setting, and what has the gap cost you?
3. Who has stopped bringing you matters, and what did you do in the period in which they stopped? What is your household managing without your knowledge?
4. From Section 32.3, is your standing composed substantially of your knowledge? What would your position be if you could no longer supply it?
5. Classify the last ten questions of consequence you were asked. How many were within your authority and your information?
6. Write the three-part reply for a matter currently pending, in your own wording, and state the date on which you will deliver it.
7. Write the five questions and the names of the people you will ask. Which of the five do you least want to ask, and why?

## Further Reading

- **On men's help-seeking:** the research literature on men's reluctance to seek help, including the findings on self-reliance, on the fear of loss of standing, and on the interventions that have been shown to increase help-seeking among men.
- **On overconfidence and expert judgement:** the research on calibration and overconfidence, including the findings on the relationship between status and the credence given to a speaker's assertions, and the evidence on the domains in which confident experts most reliably overstate their knowledge.
- **On psychological safety and the reporting of error:** the literature on psychological safety in teams, including the findings on the conditions under which bad news and errors travel upward, the association between those conditions and performance, and the practices that leaders use to obtain candid information.
- **On questioning as a leadership practice:** the practice literature on the use of questions in leadership, including the material on inquiry as distinct from advocacy, on the value of assigning the case against a decision, and on the evidence concerning pre-mortem and failure-analysis techniques.
- **On learning in adult life and in mid-career:** the literature on adult learning and on skills renewal, including the findings on the acquisition of new competence in middle and later life and the evidence on the value of recognised assessment routes for men without formal credentials.
- **Practical resources:** recognised prior learning and competency assessment services; adult education and technical training; professional associations; occupational health and employee assistance services; and the material in Appendices I, N, and P.
