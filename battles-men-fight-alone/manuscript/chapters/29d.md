## 29.5 Repairing What Can Be Repaired

The classification in Section 29.1 determines the work, and Class Two — the wrong — is the class in which an obligation exists and a repair may be possible. This section sets out the repair in the form required by the material.

### The Four Repairable Forms

**1. The obligation to a person who is available.** The debt, the damage, the unfair arrangement, the wound that was inflicted on somebody the man still sees. This is the most common repairable form and the most frequently deferred. The repair has three parts, set out below.

**2. The obligation to a household.** The man's conduct has damaged a household arrangement: the money was taken from a purpose it was committed to, the affair created an arrangement of concealment, the violence produced a household that manages the man rather than living with him. The repair in these cases is generally longer, involves conduct rather than a statement, and is frequently conducted in the presence of an arrangement outside the household — a counsellor, a mediator, a professional arrangement — because the household's own resources have been spent. Chapters 18 and 22 to 23 provide the material.

**3. The obligation to a person who is dead.** The repair is to the available relation: to the surviving spouse, the children, the estate, the record. Men report the specific finding that the repair of a matter with a dead person through the living relative is not a substitute and does relieve the position, for the reason given in Section 29.6: the material is converted into conduct that reaches somebody.

**4. The obligation with no identifiable creditor.** The category of the private wrong: the man was involved in something that harmed people he cannot name, at a time and in a system he cannot now repair. In this case the repair takes the redirection form of Section 29.6, and the requirement is that the redirection correspond in some stated way to the original harm rather than being a general charitable gesture.

### The Three Parts of a Repair

**Part One: The acknowledgement.** The man states what he did, to the person concerned, without qualification, without the explanation that converts it into a defence, and without requesting anything.

The rules for the acknowledgement are specific, and their violation is the most common reason a repair fails:

**Do not explain.** Explanation, even when accurate, converts the acknowledgement into a case. Where the circumstances genuinely mitigate the matter, they should be offered after the acknowledgement and only if the other person asks.

**Do not defend.** The phrases that begin *but you also* or *if you had* terminate the repair and restart the original dispute.

**Do not request anything.** Not forgiveness, not confirmation that the matter is closed, not an acknowledgement that the man has acted well. The request converts the acknowledgement into a transaction in which the injured person is required to supply something, which is the reverse of the repair.

**Do not use the past tense to distribute the responsibility.** *Mistakes were made* and *things happened* are forms of non-acknowledgement. The man's own act is the subject of the sentence.

**Accept that the response may be adverse.** The acknowledgement may produce anger, refusal, silence, or an account of the matter that the man does not recognise. The man's position should be that he has supplied the acknowledgement and that the response is not his to manage or to correct.

**Part Two: The restitution.** The material element, where one exists: the repayment, the compensation, the property returned, the correction of the record, the arrangement restored, the obligation performed. Restitution is the part of the repair that men omit and it is the part that the injured party most frequently requires, since the acknowledgement without restitution is a statement and the restitution without the acknowledgement is a payment.

Where the material sum is large relative to the man's capacity, the restitution is conducted in the form used in debt settlement and it is worth setting out: the full accounting of what is owed, presented to the person and stated without concealment; the offer of what can be paid; the schedule with dates; and the performance of the schedule without requiring further contact. The findings from practice are consistent: a partial restitution performed with accuracy is worth substantially more to both parties than an indefinite intention to repay in full.

**Part Three: The statement of what will be different.** The specific commitment about the man's future conduct, stated to the person concerned where that is appropriate and written in the record where it is not. The statement must be specific and checkable, following the standard used elsewhere in this book: not *I will be a better father*, but *I will attend every school event, and I will not be absent from the house on Sundays*. The third part's function is to convert the repair from a transaction about the past into an arrangement about the future, which is what the injured party most frequently requires.

### What the Repair Does Not Require

**The injured party's forgiveness.** The repair is conducted for the man's obligation, and the response is a separate matter. The research and the practice both indicate that the absence of forgiveness does not leave the repair incomplete: a wrong acknowledged and made good is a repaired wrong whether or not the relationship is restored, and the man's position afterward is that of a man who has discharged his obligation rather than a man who has been pardoned.

**The restoration of the relationship.** The relationship may continue, in a changed form, or not at all. The man should consider that the restoration is a separate request and that it should not be attached to the acknowledgement.

**The repetition of the disclosure.** Having stated the matter once, the man does not return to it, and does not require the injured party to discuss it again. Where the man requires a recipient for the material beyond the injured party, the recipient is the witness described in Section 29.6.

### The Common Failure

The most common failure of a repair is a repair that is too small and too late and that is conducted for the man's relief rather than for the obligation. Its signs are recognisable: the acknowledgement is delivered as a means of obtaining reassurance; the man's performance of the restitution is incomplete; the statement of what will be different is general; and the man's subsequent account makes clear that he has settled the matter in his own mind while continuing to behave in the conduct that caused it. Men should apply a simple test before beginning: if I could not tell anybody that I had done this, would I still do it? A repair that requires an audience is not a repair.

## 29.6 What Cannot Be Repaired

This section addresses the class of material for which no repair is available: the person is dead, the harm is permanent, the act is past and the person may never know, or the harm was distributed across people and years in a way that no acknowledgement can address.

### The Available Positions

Four positions exist, and the man is generally operating one of them whether or not he has chosen it.

**1. The indefinite sentence.** The material is carried as a standing penalty. Its costs: it alters no conduct, it consumes the man's attention, it renders his ordinary goods illegitimate, and it does nothing for the person harmed, who, being dead or absent, is not the beneficiary of the suffering. This is the default position, and it is where most men with an irreparable matter are.

**2. The forgetting that does not occur.** The attempt to put the material away: the place avoided, the subject closed, the paperwork destroyed, the years passed in the practice described in Chapter 9. Its characteristic failure is that the material is not put away but relocated, and it returns in the forms described in Section 29.3 with the addition of the man's anxiety about the material itself.

**3. The cheap absolution.** The matter is dismissed in the man's own account by a general proposition: *everybody makes mistakes; it was another time; I was young; nobody is perfect; I have done more good than harm.* The position is cheap in a precise sense: it is obtained without cost, and it is unstable, because the man does not believe it and must maintain it against his own knowledge. Its practical form in the material is well documented: the man who asserts his absolution most firmly is frequently the man who is most occupied by the matter.

**4. The carrying with accuracy.** The position the chapter recommends, and it should be described precisely, because it is not comfortable and it is not a resolution. It consists of five elements.

### The Five Elements of Carrying With Accuracy

**Element One: The account, stated once and completely.** In writing, in the man's own hand, with the event, the man's own conduct, the harm caused, and the man's assessment of what he did. There is no requirement to be fair to himself in this document and there is a requirement to be accurate: the account should distinguish what the man did from what he could not control, which is the work of Class Three in Section 29.1.

**Element Two: The witness.** One person who receives the account, in the form described in the repair: the statement of the act, without explanation, without defence, and without a request. The witness is generally not a member of the family and not somebody who would be harmed by the knowledge. Where the material is severe, the witness should be a professional.

The function of the witness requires some explanation, because it is the element men most frequently refuse. The man has been the sole holder of the material, and he has held it as an account that exists only in his own mind. Delivered to a person who receives it without either judgement or absolution, the account becomes a fact in the world, held by two people, and this changes the man's relation to it in the specific manner described in Chapter 27.3: the account, delivered aloud, alters in the man's own hearing. Men who have conducted this element consistently report that the material is thereafter present in a different form: present, acknowledged, and not requiring management.

**Element Three: The stated position on self-punishment.** Written by the man, and it addresses the fourth reason from Section 29.2 directly. Its content is the man's own answer to the question of what his suffering has achieved. The following points are the ones used in practice, and the man should adopt only those he can state honestly in his own words.

The material's cost is not discharged by the man's suffering. If the suffering were a payment, the account would need a recipient, and there is none: the person harmed is not assisted by the man's early hours or by the occasions he declines.

The suffering is not the same thing as the matter. The man's punishment is a private arrangement that he maintains; the matter is the harm he caused. The two are related and they are distinct, and one does not reduce the other.

The further cost that self-punishment imposes is paid by people who did not cause the harm. The household that lives with a man who is unavailable at particular moments, the children who receive a reduced version of their father, the work that is not attempted — the ledger of the man's self-punishment is charged to people who had no part in the original event.

The energy is the remaining resource. A man's attention and capacity are finite, and the same capacity that is consumed by the punitive mode is the capacity that would be required for the repair of what can still be repaired, or for the conduct described in Element Four.

**Element Four: The redirection into conduct.** The material is converted into conduct that corresponds to the harm. This element is the one that men report as the most effective and it is the one the chapter asks the reader to construct with care.

The correspondence should be stated, so that it is not a general benevolence: a man whose regret concerns a young man he failed becomes a man who intervenes with young men; a man whose regret concerns a parent he did not attend to becomes a man who visits the old; a man whose regret concerns a family he left in difficulty becomes a man whose obligations are discharged with accuracy and without publicising; a man whose regret concerns the silence he kept becomes a man who speaks at the moment when speaking is possible.

The rule of the correspondence is that the conduct must be of the same kind as the harm and must be performed without the requirement that it be observed. Conduct that is performed in the man's visible account — the public charitable act in respect of a private matter — is generally a substitute for the work rather than the work, and men who have conducted this element well report that it is almost entirely unobserved.

**Element Five: The dated reckoning.** The material is conducted once more on a stated date, annually or at the interval the man chooses, and the reckoning is a review rather than a rehearsal: what has the conduct of the past year been; has the statement of what will be different held; is there any part of the matter that has become available for repair? The element's function is to establish that the material has been conducted, and its practical benefit in men's accounts is that the remaining fifty-one weeks are relieved of the material: it has a place, it holds a date, and it is not required to arrive unannounced.

### What This Work Does Not Promise

The chapter records the limits plainly. The material does not disappear. Men who have conducted the work describe the regret as present but not occupying: it is available, it is an accurate part of the man's knowledge of himself, and it no longer arrives as a sentence. Men describe a specific change in its occasion: it is engaged when they do the corresponding conduct, and not otherwise. Two men may conduct the same work and one may find that the material arises several times a year and another that it arises twice; both positions are within the range the chapter describes, and neither is a failure.

The chapter's final position on the irreparable is stated in the form used in the practice literature and it is worth recording in its own right: a man is not required to be at peace with a matter that is not at peace. He is required to carry it accurately, to make good what can still be made good, to convert the remainder into conduct, and to refuse the arrangement in which his own suffering is treated as payment for a harm that does not benefit from it.
