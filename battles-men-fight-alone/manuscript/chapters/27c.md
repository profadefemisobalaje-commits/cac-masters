## 27.4 What Stops a Man from Reaching Out

The barriers must be treated accurately, because a man who is told that he should reach out has generally already considered the question and has reasons. This section sets out the barriers in the order of their frequency in men's accounts, with the practical answer to each.

### The Eight Barriers

**1. *There is nothing to say.*** Frequently accurate and frequently the expression of a specific inability: the man's difficulty does not take the form of a narrative he has practised. A difficulty that occupies the man continuously may have no sentences attached to it, which is why the man interprets his state as nothing to say rather than as difficulty in finding the words.

**The answer:** the opening does not have to concern the man's state. It may concern a shared activity, a practical matter, or a request for help with something else. The state is frequently delivered later, after the arrangement exists, and it arrives more easily in the middle of an activity than at the beginning of a conversation.

**2. *It will be used against me.*** The barrier with the most basis in the man's own history, and the one Chapter 26's material addresses. It is frequently correct in its judgement of specific people and incorrect as a general rule.

**The answer:** the graded reliance of Section 26.5. The material is delivered in proportion to what the recipient has demonstrated, and the first delivery should be small. If the man requires a recipient with an obligation that is governed by rules rather than by goodwill, the professional route exists and is described in Section 27.5.

**3. *It will cost me at work or in my position.*** A real consideration in specific professional environments: the regulated profession, the senior position, the small community, the employer with a record on health matters.

**The answer:** the confidential professional route, and the separation of the professional identity from the personal difficulty. A general practitioner, an employee assistance programme where it exists, a private practitioner paid out of pocket, a practitioner in a neighbouring city, and, for the specific professions and conditions where it is required, the licensed route with its confidentiality obligations. The practical finding is that the men who bear the greatest risk from disclosure are the ones who most benefit from an arrangement whose confidentiality is a professional obligation rather than a favour.

**4. *I will be a burden.*** The barrier that men report most frequently in the framing of other people's interests, and the one that is most consistently unfounded in practice. The evidence from the people who are asked is clear: they do not experience the request in the terms the man anticipates.

**The answer:** the recognition that the other person's experience of being asked is generally positive, which the men who have reached out consistently report as their own finding. In addition: the request should be specific and bounded, and it should, where possible, be reciprocal. Men manage their concern about burdening others very effectively when the arrangement includes something that the man supplies in return.

**5. *He has his own problems.*** The barrier that reflects the man's accurate assessment of his friends' circumstances. Because men's networks are small, the man's two available contacts are frequently carrying their own substantial loads.

**The answer:** the reciprocal arrangement, and the finding that the two-way channel sustains the contact considerably better than the one-way. Men in groups with an agreed rule of honesty report this consistently: the arrangement holds because each man has something to bring as well as something to obtain.

**6. *It will change how my household sees me.*** The barrier with the most substantial basis, and the one that concerns the man's authority in his own home. The concern is that a man who discloses difficulty in the household's range will be repositioned within it.

**The answer:** this barrier governs the material delivered to the household and does not govern the material delivered to a person outside it. The requirement of Chapter 35's material applies: a man may hold the household as one thing and one or two arrangements outside it as another. Where the material is an addiction, an addiction to a substance, or a behaviour with consequences for the household, the disclosure to the household becomes a requirement of the treatment, for the reason Chapter 23 sets out.

**7. *I do not know who to call.*** A practical barrier rather than a psychological one, and a very common one, particularly in Forms Three, Four, Five, and Six of Section 27.1.

**The answer:** Section 27.5. Where no name exists, the professional route is the entry point, and the entry point for a man who will not talk is the general practitioner's consultation, which requires only a physical complaint.

**8. *It will not help.* ** The last barrier, and the one that frequently conceals the others. Its form is a prediction: *nothing changes; talking about it changes nothing; I know what I would be told; I have tried before.* The prediction is generally constructed from one or two experiences and it is applied generally.

**The answer:** the standard is not resolution but alteration, and the alteration is documented. The specific finding from men who have used the arrangements: the situation frequently does not change, and the man's capacity to carry it does. The finding is modest and it is entirely reliable, and it is the reason the arrangements in this chapter are described as functional rather than therapeutic.

### The Barrier Behind the Barriers

Four barriers in the list — the second, the third, the fourth, and the sixth — are variations on one proposition: that the material, once delivered to another person, becomes a fact in the world and will have consequences. This is true, and it is the reason the graded approach in Section 27.5 matters: the arrangements are built by degrees, with each delivery small enough to be survived, and the man's confidence in the arrangement is built from the outcomes of the earlier deliveries rather than from an argument.

## 27.5 Building a Network from Nothing

This section sets out a staged method for a man who has no arrangement at present and who requires one. The stages are arranged so that each is available on the basis of the previous stage, and the first stage requires nothing of the man that he cannot supply on the day he reads it.

### Stage One: The Identification of One Person

The identification is conducted in writing, and it uses four criteria. The person must be **available** — that is, reachable and still present in the man's life; **not dependent** on the man, not subordinate to him, and not in his household; **not part of the material's audience** — not the relative who would circulate it, not the colleague in the same office; and **in possession of a record** of some reliability in his conduct.

The candidate list is generated by finding the answer to a single question: *who, over my adult life, has ever asked me a question that was not about my position or my usefulness?* The list is generally short. Where it is empty, Stage One's alternative is the professional route described at Stage Four.

### Stage Two: The Contact Without the Material

The second stage requires the man to contact the person without delivering any material. The purpose is the restoration of the contact and the establishment of the arrangement; the material is not required and should be withheld.

The forms, in ascending order of difficulty. First, a message; not a letter and not a long message, but an ordinary contact with a reason: a greeting and a real question about the other person. Second, a telephone call of three to five minutes with a stated reason for ending it, which removes the requirement of an extended conversation. Third, a meeting for a practical purpose — to collect, to deliver, to attend something, to assist — which is the form men find easiest because the purpose supplies the structure.

The men who attempt Stage Two report, consistently, that the contact is received more positively than expected. This is the finding the chapter asks readers to accept provisionally until it is confirmed by the man's own experience.

### Stage Three: The Reciprocal Arrangement

The third stage converts the contact into an arrangement, and the form is the offer of something rather than the request for it. The purpose is to establish a channel in which the man is a supplier as well as a recipient, which is the form that men's arrangements actually take when they hold.

The forms: an offer of specific practical assistance; a share of something real — the market information, a contact, a demand, a supplier, an opportunity; an arrangement for a recurring activity. The last of these is the most durable: the Saturday arrangement, the monthly meeting, the standing task. Men's friendships are carried by recurring arrangements far more than by conversation, and the recurring arrangement is the structure in which conversation eventually occurs without being sought.

### Stage Four: The Delivery of Material

The fourth stage is the delivery of a small item of the man's actual material, and its purpose is not resolution. Its purpose is to establish that the channel will hold.

The rules for the first delivery. Keep it **small**: an ordinary difficulty, a concrete difficulty, or a difficulty that is already past. Keep it **specific**: not the man's general state, but one event with dates and facts. Keep it **non-catastrophic**: the item should not require the recipient to take action, reconsider the man's character, or bear a burden. And keep the **principle**: the delivery establishes the channel and permits the full material to be delivered later, in the manner Chapter 4.6 describes — in the middle of an activity, without announcement, as an ordinary fact.

Where the first delivery goes badly, the man should recall that the outcome is information about that particular person, which is the finding of Section 26.5, and not a verdict on the arrangement generally.

### Stage Five: The Professional Route

The professional route is an arrangement in the technical sense: a defined time, a defined fee or service, a specified confidentiality, and a purpose that the man does not have to construct. It has three advantages over the personal arrangement and one disadvantage.

The **advantages.** It is available today; it requires no existing relationship; its confidentiality is governed by an obligation rather than by a favour; it imposes no reciprocity; the man is not required to manage another person's concern; and the material can be delivered in full at the first meeting without any of the four barriers of Section 27.4 applying.

The **disadvantage.** It is not the arrangement that will be available at two in the morning, and the research on men's support consistently finds that professional relationships do not supply the functions that personal arrangements supply. The professional route is therefore the **entry point** and not the destination: it makes the delivery of the material possible today, and it supplies the platform from which the personal arrangements in Stages Two to Four are built. This sequence — professional first, personal arrangements built over the following months — is the route that men who have nobody to call most frequently take, and it should be stated plainly in the chapter.

**Where to begin, for a man who will not talk:** the general practitioner's appointment, which requires a physical complaint and supplies an opening. The practitioner's three questions — sleep, appetite, and how long this has been going on — are part of routine assessment, and the man who answers them honestly has already delivered material.

### Stage Six: The Group

The final stage, and the one that supplies the arrangements that survive the loss of any individual person. A group of men with an agreed rule of honesty: a men's group, a professional association, a group with a defined purpose, a discussion group, a group of fathers. The group supplies the three functions that a single relationship cannot supply: membership and standing, the availability of more than one man, and the correction of the man's own account by people whose positions differ from his.

The group's practical form matters, and the guidance from men's group practice is consistent: a defined purpose, a small number (five to nine), a fixed time, a rule of confidentiality, and a rule against advice-giving for the first twenty minutes of any man's material.
