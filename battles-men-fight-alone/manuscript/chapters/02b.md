## 2.2 The Pressure to Provide, Protect, and Perform

The three central duties interact, and the interaction is the source of most of the pressure that men describe. Each duty is manageable in isolation. Together they produce an arrangement in which the man is required to be simultaneously the household's income, its security service, and its public representative, with no defined hours, no relief, and no acknowledgement that the combination is a job rather than an identity.

### Provide

The provision duty is the most heavily enforced and the most concretely measurable. Its features are worth stating precisely.

**It is open-ended.** The obligation is not to a figure but to a standard: the household's needs as they arise, the family's expectations as they are expressed, and the community's assessment of what a man in his position should be able to do. Since the standard is set by comparison rather than by arithmetic, the obligation has no ceiling.

**It is continuous.** Rent, food, transport, fees, medical costs, and instalments recur monthly. A man who meets them is not, at any point, finished; he is merely current. The absence of a completion point is one of the reasons men describe financial responsibility as the most fatiguing of all their obligations, even where the amounts are manageable.

**It is personalised.** The obligation is attached to the man rather than to the household, which means that a shortfall is experienced as a personal failure rather than as a household difficulty. This is the mechanism by which an ordinary economic event — a delay in payment, a bad month, a client's default — becomes an injury to identity.

**It is publicly verifiable.** Unlike the other duties, provision leaves visible evidence: the house, the vehicle, the school, the contributions. Since the evidence is public, the assessment is public, and the man's standing is under continuous review.

**It is frequently carried in secret.** Where a man's provision has become impossible, the concealment described in Chapter 9 begins: the borrowing, the second obligation, the maintained standard, the private calculation.

### Protect

The protection duty is less discussed and it operates continuously in the background of a man's day.

Its forms include physical protection, in which a man is expected to intervene in a confrontation and to accompany relatives through dangerous or hostile situations; financial protection, in which he is the person who handles an emergency; social protection, in which he represents the household in disputes with institutions, landlords, employers, and officials; and emotional protection, in which he is the person who absorbs the household's shocks and communicates them without panic.

Three features of the protection duty deserve attention.

**It is expected in conditions where it is impossible.** A man may be expected to defend a position against an employer, a police officer, a landlord, or a group of relatives, with no legal knowledge, no resources, and no allies. The expectation is applied regardless, and the man's inability to meet it is registered as a failure of courage rather than as an absence of means.

**It is dangerous.** The expectation that a man will intervene is a documented contributor to men's elevated rates of injury and death from violence and road traffic incidents. Research on male mortality consistently identifies risk-taking and the refusal to withdraw from confrontation as contributing factors, and the mechanism is the protection duty operating in its public form.

**It is not negotiable by the man alone.** A man who declines the protective role in a family dispute, in a community matter, or in a confrontation is not merely declining a task; he is departing from the script, and the departure will be interpreted as cowardice by people whose opinion he needs.

### Perform

The performance duty covers the widest range and is the least visible, because it operates inside the other two.

Its domains include professional achievement, sexual capacity, fatherhood, the management of the household's affairs, and simple competence in the ordinary matters of life: a man who cannot repair, negotiate, calculate, or drive is frequently assessed on his inability rather than on his other qualities.

Three features of the performance duty produce specific damage.

**Performance is measured by comparison.** As Chapter 11 establishes, the standard is a position rather than a level, and the man is therefore in permanent competition with a reference group that is visible to him and that includes his brothers, his colleagues, and his contemporaries.

**Failure is public.** A man's professional failure, business collapse, or divorce is known, discussed, and remembered. Chapter 16 treats the consequences, and it is worth noting here that the public character of failure is what makes men so heavily invested in the maintenance of appearance, and therefore in the concealment described in Part Three.

**The duty is not suspended by illness.** A man who is unwell is still assessed on his performance, and men report with consistency that the expectation is not reduced for a period of ill health; it is frequently intensified, since a man's incapacity places the household's arrangements in question.

### The Combined Pressure

The three duties produce a specific psychological arrangement, which men describe in nearly identical terms: a permanent readiness.

The features of permanent readiness are recognisable. The man is unable to rest without justification. He experiences ordinary leisure as a debt. He is attentive to threats — financial, professional, familial — at a level that is invisible to others and continuous. He does not consider himself to be under stress, because stress would imply that the arrangement is optional; he considers himself to be in the ordinary condition of a man. And when the arrangement is described to him, he agrees with the description and does not change it, because he can identify no alternative that would meet the obligations he has accepted.

::: keypoint
Permanent readiness is a physiological state, not merely an attitude. The research on chronic stress shows that sustained alertness produces elevated cortisol, disrupted sleep, raised blood pressure, impaired immune function, and reduced cognitive flexibility. Twelve years of permanent readiness produces the health outcomes described in Chapters 32 to 34 — and the men who present with them are frequently surprised, because they did not consider themselves to have been under stress at all.

## 2.3 The Fear of Being Considered Weak

This section addresses a fear that governs more male behaviour than any other, and it begins with a distinction that is rarely made explicit.

**The fear of being weak** is a fear about capacity: that the man may not be able to meet what is required. It is a rational fear, it is sometimes well founded, and its appropriate management is training, planning, assistance, and the revision of commitments.

**The fear of being considered weak** is a fear about standing: that the man will be assessed as inadequate by others, with consequences for respect, authority, and position. It is a social fear, and its management is concealment, overcorrection, and the avoidance of everything that would create the appearance.

The two are frequently experienced together and they are not the same. A man may be entirely capable and terrified of appearing incapable; a man may be genuinely at the limit of his capacity and indifferent to others' assessments. The distinction matters because the second fear cannot be resolved by becoming stronger. It requires a change in what the man believes the assessment depends on.

### What Weakness Means to Men

Men's accounts make it possible to set out what the accusation of weakness consists of. It is not fatigue, and it is not illness. The five elements are:

1. **Dependence on others.** Any arrangement in which the man needs something from somebody else: money, help, presence, or consideration.
2. **Visible emotional distress.** Tears, trembling, panic, or an inability to speak about a matter without breaking down.
3. **Failure to meet an obligation.** A debt unpaid, a fee unmet, a duty declined, a decision having to be reversed.
4. **The disclosure of a private matter.** Telling anyone about the household's difficulties, the marriage, the man's own condition, or a failure.
5. **A decision taken by somebody else.** Being overruled, being managed, discovering that others have decided a matter of consequence to the man.

The common element in all five is exposure: vulnerability to another person's assessment. This is why the fear is so difficult to satisfy — it is not a fear of a state but of being seen in a state, and concealment is therefore the only tactic the man has. The tactics that conceal well produce the consequences described in Chapters 3, 5, and 9.

### The Behaviour It Produces

Five recognisable patterns follow from the fear, and men who find four or more of them familiar should treat the audit exercise at the end of this chapter as a priority.

**Concealment.** The ordinary response, and the one on which this book's earlier chapters are built.

**Overcorrection.** Where concealment is impossible, the man demonstrates the opposite in an exaggerated form: the man in a difficult financial position who buys a vehicle, the man with a failing marriage who becomes publicly affectionate, the man with an untreated illness who takes on additional work. Overcorrection is expensive and it is reliable: it converts a manageable problem into an unmanageable one.

**Avoidance of assistance.** Men in this pattern decline help that is offered, and they do so in a manner that is difficult for the person offering to challenge. They also fail to seek assistance that requires an admission: medical assessment, financial advice, counselling, legal advice, a request for an extension from a creditor.

**Aggression toward the messenger.** Where somebody raises the matter — a wife, a friend, a colleague, a physician — the man's response is frequently disproportionate, and the disproportionate response is registered by the messenger as a reason not to raise it again. The pattern is a principal reason that men in difficulty are surrounded by people who know something is wrong and say nothing.

**The constant demonstration.** The most socially expensive pattern: the man whose working life, relationships, and spending are organised around the continuous demonstration of strength. The pattern is exhausting and it is transparent to the men closest to him, but it can be sustained for thirty years, and it is frequently the arrangement that produces the material of Chapters 32 to 34.
