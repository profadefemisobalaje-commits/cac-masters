# The Battle Against Personal Weaknesses {.chapter num="22" short="The Battle Against Personal Weaknesses"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Name the personal weaknesses that men most commonly struggle against, and describe the features that make each of them difficult to change.
2. Describe the hidden cost of private shame, including its effects on honesty, relationships, and decision-making.
3. Explain why willpower alone fails to produce durable change, and identify the mechanisms by which behaviour actually changes.
4. Identify his own patterns and triggers, distinguishing between the situations that produce the behaviour and the reasons he gives for it.
5. Apply a practical programme of change, including environment, substitution, measurement, and relapse handling.
6. Establish accountability and honest self-examination without exposing himself to destructive disclosure, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

There is a category of struggle that men carry that is not described by any of the subjects in the earlier parts of this book. It is not the burden of the provider role, or depression, or marital silence, or grief. It is the specific and private matter of character: the things a man does that he does not want to do, the things he fails to do that he wants to do, and the habits that have survived his repeated attempts to break them.

The form of this struggle is distinctive, and it has four features that make it more difficult than it appears from outside.

It is **private**. The man's failure is known to him, and, in most cases, imperfectly acknowledged. Where a man's weakness is known to others, the knowledge is frequently partial: the household is aware of the drinking but not of the gambling; the wife is aware of the temper but not of the reason for it.

It is **repetitive**. A weakness is not a single event; it is a pattern that has recurred through many attempts at change, and its recurrence is the reason the man has concluded that it cannot be altered.

It is **attached to self-respect**. The specific character of shame is the subject of Section 22.2. It is sufficient to note here that a man's weakness is the single thing he is least able to discuss and most likely to be assessed on by himself.

And it is **frequently concealed from precisely the people who could help**, because disclosure of the material risks the standing that the man believes is the only resource he has.

This chapter proceeds on a premise that many men find uncomfortable at first and useful afterwards: a weakness is not a verdict on a man's worth, it is a behaviour with a function, and it can be changed by arrangement rather than by willpower. The arrangement consists of the situations in which the behaviour occurs, the substitute that performs the same function, the measurement that makes the pattern visible, and the accountability that removes the isolation. None of these is a matter of resolve, and all of them are within the man's control.

## 22.1 Naming the Weakness: What Men Struggle With

Men's struggles are more uniform than they appear, because the material is rarely discussed. The following eight categories account for the substantial majority of what men describe when the subject is raised in a setting where disclosure is safe.

**1. Anger and the temper.** The most commonly reported weakness, and the most damaging to households. Its features: a threshold that is reached too quickly; a disproportionate response calibrated to accumulated strain rather than to the event; a tendency to discharge the response at the people with the least power in the man's life, which is generally his household; and, after the event, a specific form of shame in which the man's regret concerns the household's experience of him rather than the matter that began the incident.

**2. Alcohol.** Considered in Chapter 23, and mentioned here because the man's own account of it typically begins as a matter of weakness rather than of dependence: *I drink more than I should and I have tried to stop three times.*

**3. Sexual conduct.** The category that is least discussed and most frequently held against the man. Its forms: the compulsive pursuit of sexual material or encounters; conduct conducted outside a committed relationship; and, in a specific and painful variant, the man's involvement in a practice that he regards as inconsistent with his own values. The material in this category is concealed with the greatest care, and the concealment is the principal obstacle to its treatment.

**4. Gambling and betting.** Its prevalence in men is substantial, its amplification by mobile technology is significant, and its concealment is almost total. The specific feature of gambling as a weakness is its association with shame and with the belief in a single large recovery, which is the mechanism by which the losses accumulate.

**5. Lying and concealment.** A weakness that is frequently reported in men who consider themselves honest, and which concerns the ordinary untruths by which they manage their households: the concealment of expenditure, of the financial position, of an obligation, of a contact, of an absence. Chapter 9 addresses the financial form; the general form is listed here because men frequently experience it as their principal failing.

**6. Procrastination and sloth.** The most commonly named item. Its features: the deferral of matters that are within the man's capacity, the use of activity as a substitute for the matter deferred, an increase in the deferral's cost with time, and the man's own explanation of laziness, which is generally inaccurate: the deferral is frequently produced by the fear described in Chapter 7, by the absence of a structure in which the task can be done, or by the condition described in Category 8 below.

**7. Pride and contempt.** The disposition that is least commonly named and most commonly described by others. Its features: the requirement to be right; the difficulty of conceding; the use of status in dealings with those who have less; the readiness to assess others. Men frequently identify it only when somebody who knows them well states it directly, and the identification is frequently followed by a period of significant progress.

**8. The behaviours that are symptoms of an untreated condition.** The man who is unable to do anything, who has withdrawn from his interests, who drinks heavily in the evening, or whose temper has changed over a defined period may be presenting the symptoms of a depressive or anxiety condition, a sleep disorder, a chronic pain condition, or a hormonal condition. The specific test: has the behaviour changed during the last two years, and was it preceded by a change in mood, sleep, or energy? Where the answer is yes, the condition is the primary subject, and Chapter 6 and Appendix B apply.

### Three Principles for Naming

**Name one, not a catalogue.** A man who sets out to correct everything at once will correct nothing. The evidence on behaviour change is consistent on this point, and the practical rule is one weakness at a time, for a defined period, with the others recorded for later.

**Name it in the form of a behaviour.** Not *I am angry* but the specific conduct; not *I am dishonest* but the specific untruths and the situations in which they occur. The general form cannot be changed because it has no behaviour attached to it.

**Name the smallest version that is the problem.** The temper expressed as shouting at the children at the end of a working week is a changeable target; the temper as a general statement of character is not. Where the man's own preference is the general statement, the correct response is to treat the general statement as a description of the target area and the specific behaviour as the objective.

## 22.2 The Hidden Cost of Private Shame

The second section examines what the private carrying of a weakness costs, and it is deliberately placed before the material on change, because the shame is the mechanism that prevents change and because its costs extend well beyond the weakness itself.

### The Six Costs

**1. The distortion of the man's account of himself.** A single concealed matter acquires a weight in the self-assessment that is disproportionate to its actual content. Chapter 21's account of the private account applies in its most concentrated form: a man who has concealed one pattern for twenty years will frequently report that it has become the principal fact about him, and the assessment is made in isolation, without correction, and without comparison.

**2. The loss of the relationship's truth.** Where a wife does not know a matter of substance about her husband, the marriage is conducted in a partial reality. The specific consequences are described in Chapter 9: the wife's inferences, formed from the available information, will be wrong and will be unfavourable, and the man's behaviour — the withdrawal, the irritability, the absences — will be explained in the only terms available to her. This is the cost that men most consistently underestimate.

**3. The energy of concealment.** Every undisclosed matter requires maintenance: the explanations, the timing, the tracking of what was said to whom, the avoidance of situations in which the matter could emerge. The cost is continuous and it is drawn from the same account as the man's capacity for work, judgement, and relationship.

**4. The escalation of the behaviour.** The relationship between shame and repetition is well established in the clinical literature, and its mechanism is direct: the behaviour produces shame, shame produces distress, and the behaviour is the means by which the distress is managed. A man who drinks to manage his distress about his drinking is the plainest illustration, and the pattern is identical in gambling, sexual conduct, and anger.

**5. The prevention of help.** Shame prevents disclosure, and disclosure is the route to treatment. Men whose conditions are the most treatable — alcohol dependence, gambling disorder, compulsive sexual behaviour, mood disorders — are frequently the men who arrive latest, and the delay is frequently measured in years or decades, with the result that conditions which respond to early intervention have become entrenched.

**6. The transmission to the household.** A household in which a matter is concealed produces a set of secondary effects: the children learn that some matters are not discussed; the wife develops a vigilance that is frequently read by the man as nagging; and the family's arrangements are adapted around a matter that nobody has named. The case in Section 22.7 illustrates the effect precisely: the children's most common statement, in adulthood, is that they knew something was wrong and never learned what.
