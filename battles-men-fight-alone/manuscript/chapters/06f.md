## 6.10 Common Questions Men Ask

**"How do I know whether this is depression or just a difficult period?"**

The practical test is the one set out in Table 6.1 and in Section 6.3: how many features are present, for how long, and what are they costing? A difficult period is proportionate to an event, fluctuates, and resolves. Depression is persistent, present on most days, disproportionate to circumstances or plainly beyond what the circumstances account for, and it interferes with sleep, work, or relationships. If the symptoms have lasted more than two to four weeks and are affecting functioning, have it assessed; the assessment itself is inexpensive in time and will settle the question.

**"If I am diagnosed, will I have to take medication for the rest of my life?"**

No. Many cases are treated with psychological therapy alone. Where medication is used, it is generally continued for a defined period after improvement — commonly several months — and then reduced gradually under supervision. A proportion of people with recurrent or severe depression continue treatment long-term, and this is a clinical decision made with a specialist rather than a default pathway.

**"Can I recover without anybody at work or at home knowing?"**

Confidential treatment is possible, and many men use it. The more important question is whether secrecy about the fact of treatment is in your interest. In practice, most men find that at least one person at home should know, for three reasons: that person can support the treatment, can observe changes you may not notice, and can act if matters worsen. Confidentiality of clinical information is a professional obligation; the discretion of the household is a decision you can make deliberately.

**"What if treatment does not work?"**

A minority of people do not respond to the first treatment, and this is expected rather than exceptional. The standard approach is to review after an agreed interval, adjust the treatment or the type of therapy, or combine approaches, and to check that other conditions — anxiety, alcohol use, thyroid function, chronic pain, sleep disorder — are not confounding the picture. The important instruction is not to conclude that you cannot be helped on the basis of one treatment that did not work.

**"I cannot afford private care. What are my options?"**

Public hospital services, primary health centres with trained staff, subsidised counselling services attached to universities and non-governmental organisations, employer schemes, telephone helplines, and online services. Appendix Q sets these out in detail for Nigeria and for other African countries, together with guidance on cost and on what to ask when making contact.

**"Does seeking treatment risk my position at work?"**

Clinical information is confidential, and occupational health records are generally separate from personnel records. The practical risks of untreated depression — declining performance, errors, absence, resignation, and dismissal — greatly exceed the risks of confidential treatment. If you have a specific concern about your workplace, raise it with the clinician before you begin, and ask what will be recorded and who may see it.

::: summary
**Chapter 6 in brief.** Depression is a real condition, not sadness and not a weakness, with a defined set of symptoms, a duration, and an effect on functioning. It varies in severity, and severity determines treatment. It is frequently accompanied by anxiety, substance use, and physical illness, and a competent assessment looks for more than one condition.

Depression in men is systematically under-recognised for four reasons. Symptoms are attributed to character; men present physical complaints rather than emotional ones; men answer the standard screening questions negatively because the questions concern sadness and crying rather than anger and flatness; and men conceal the condition deliberately, which removes the support that would protect them.

The characteristic male presentations are three. Irritability, in which the threshold for frustration falls sharply and the household bears the brunt. Withdrawal, frequently from everything except work, so that extra hours are read as dedication while relationships are emptied. And emotional numbness, in which the man feels nothing rather than sadness, loses pleasure along with pain, and observes his own absence from the events that should move him. Additional features include early waking, physical complaints, increased alcohol use, risk-taking, reduced self-care, and flat statements of hopelessness.

Untreated depression is dangerous in six ways: death by suicide; worsening and recurring episodes; poorer physical health outcomes; substantial effects on partners and children; loss of work capacity and income; and the development of alcohol or drug dependence through self-medication. Waiting is a decision with a cost, and the cost is highest for men.

Men resist help for reasons that are largely rational within their own terms, including the belief that functioning means wellness, the expectation that the condition will pass, the dislike of medication, the fear of records and reputation, the fear that talking will worsen matters, the absence of reasons to be depressed, the preference for spiritual resolution, and sheer lack of information. Each objection has a practical answer, and the most common outcome of a first consultation is relief rather than exposure.

Treatment with a strong evidence base includes talking treatments, medication for moderate and severe cases, combined treatment, structured self-management, practical intervention in the circumstances that maintain the condition, and crisis planning where risk is present. Support consists of company without pressure, practical relief, encouragement to continue, and honesty about risk. Treatment and support work best together, and neither is a substitute for the other.

Recovery has a shape: behaviour improves before mood, activity is restored before the desire for it returns, and consolidation requires attention to the conditions that made the episode likely. Relapse prevention rests on recognising your personal early signature, keeping one person informed, maintaining sleep, exercise, and reduced drinking, and obtaining an explicit follow-up plan. Recovery does not require a man to become fluent in emotional language; precision about sleep, behaviour, symptoms, and consequences is sufficient for treatment to work.

The exercise produced a two-week symptom log, a written account in professionally usable form, and a decision point with a date, a route, a person to inform, and a prepared first sentence. For the man who cannot yet act, the remedy is to lower the first step until it is trivial, to ask somebody to accompany him, and to treat resistance as a symptom of the condition rather than as a reason to postpone.

## Further Reading

- **On depression generally:** the World Health Organization's depression resources, including its fact sheets and its guidance for non-specialist health workers, which are available at no cost. National health services publish comparable materials, usually with practical advice on when to seek help.
- **On depression in men:** the research literature on sex differences in symptom presentation, including studies showing that irritability, anger, and substance use are more prominent in male presentations and that standard screening instruments under-detect depression in men.
- **On treatment:** the clinical guidelines issued by national and international bodies on the treatment of depression, including guidance on psychological therapies, medication, and combined treatment, together with evidence reviews on behavioural activation and exercise as components of care.
- **On suicide prevention:** the substantial body of work on male suicide risk, including what families and friends can do, and the guidance produced by suicide prevention organisations. Appendix Q lists services that are available to men in Nigeria, in other African countries, and internationally.
- **On recovery:** first-person accounts by men who have recovered from depression, particularly those written for a male readership; and practical guidance for families on supporting a man through the treatment process.
