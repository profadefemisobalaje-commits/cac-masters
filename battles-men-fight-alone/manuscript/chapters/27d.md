## 27.6 Illustrative Case: The Widower Who Had Nobody to Call

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in bereavement services, primary care, and men's group practice.

**Musa is sixty-one, a retired vehicle mechanic, widowed for four years, with three adult children in three different cities.**

Musa and his wife, Hauwa, married in 1988 and lived in the same town for thirty-four years. He operated a workshop with two apprentices; she was a teacher and, in the description her family gave, the person who knew everybody.

The pattern of the marriage was, in Musa's own account, this: *She had the house, the family, and the visits. I had the workshop and the boys. When there was a wedding or a naming, she told me where to go and what to wear. When somebody died, she told me what the family was doing. I did not have her friends and she did not have the workshop.*

Hauwa died in 2022 after a short illness. Musa's account of the following two years, which he gave in a primary care consultation and later in a men's group, had five stages.

**The first was activity.** For approximately seven months, Musa managed the funeral, the family's arrangements, and the estate, and he described this period as *full*: *Everybody was calling, the house was full, and I was the one who knew what to do.*

**The second was the emptying.** From about eight months, the calls reduced, the house became quiet, and the two apprentices left, one for a salaried position and one to start a business in another state. Musa's business had already been declining, and he retired formally in the following year. His account: *I sat in the parlour and I listened to the whole compound. By that time there was nothing to repair in the house and no reason to go out.*

**The third was the discovery.** Musa was unwell in the second year — a period of fever and severe headaches which he attributed to malaria and which did not resolve. His account of the discovery: *I was going to the hospital for three days and I had nobody to take me. My daughter in Abuja offered to come. I told her not to come. My son in Lagos sent money. I sat in the taxi on the third day and I asked myself who I would call if they told me it was serious. I went through everybody. I could not find anybody. I was very surprised. I had thought I was a man with a large family.*

**The fourth was the concealment of the discovery.** Musa told nobody about it, and described the following six months as the worst period of his life: *I did not want my children to think their father was a man who had nobody. They would have worried, and then they would have arranged things, and I would have been the old man they were arranging.* His sleep deteriorated, his appetite reduced, and he described a period in which he stopped leaving the house except for the market.

**The fifth was the intervention, which occurred through his doctor.** In the second consultation regarding his sleep, the general practitioner asked the four questions that constitute routine assessment in this area: whether he was eating, whether he was sleeping, how long the sleep had been affected, and whether he had anybody he spoke to. Musa's account of the fourth question: *He asked me if I had somebody I talked to. I told him I was fine. He wrote on the paper and he said, well, the question is on my form, and you have answered it, and he did not look at me while he said it. I sat there for a while and then I told him, and I am not sure why. I said, there is nobody to call. That was the sentence.*

The arrangements that followed consisted of four elements, and they were constructed in a deliberate order.

**The first element** was a defined professional arrangement: monthly consultations for a period of six months with a practitioner seen through the hospital, with the sleep addressed as a presenting problem. Musa's account: *The first month I did not say anything about the marriage. I talked about the sleeping. It was easier to go for the sleeping.*

**The second element** was the reinstatement of one friendship from before the marriage consumed his time: a man with whom he had worked in the same workshop in the 1990s and to whom he had not spoken in eleven years. The reinstatement was conducted by the man himself, on the practitioner's instruction, and Musa described it in these terms: *I called him about a spare part. I did not have a spare part to ask about; I looked up the name of a part and I called him. He talked for forty minutes and asked when I was coming. I said I would come in two weeks. I went. We sat in his shop.*

**The third element** was a men's group of seven men of similar age which met fortnightly, which Musa joined through a community association in the third month. The group operated the arrangement used by men's groups generally: a rule that nothing said leaves the room, no advice for the first twenty minutes of any man's material, and no requirement for any man to speak about himself.

**The fourth element** was the disclosure to his children, conducted individually, over about four months. Musa's account, which is the part of the case most worth recording: *I told each of them the same thing. I told them that their mother had done all the family's visiting, that I had discovered in the hospital that there was nobody for me to call, and that I had been ashamed about it, and that I had not wanted them to see it. My daughter cried for about an hour. My son in Lagos was quiet and then he said that he had the same problem, and he was forty-two years old. I did not know that.*

Musa's summary, two years after the consultation, was: *I did not need to be helped; I needed to be reached, and I did not know how to be reached, and the doctor asked me a question off a form. I would like men to know the thing that I did not know for two years: the sentence I was afraid to say — there is nobody to call — was the answer to the question that took me out of the house. It did not shame me to say it. It was the only sentence I have said in my life that changed the thing it described.*
:::

Six features of this case deserve isolation.

**The isolation was produced by the household process.** Musa's arrangements with the family, the community, and the occasions were carried by his wife, and he described this precisely: *She had the house and the family. I had the workshop.* This is Process Five from Section 27.2, and the case is included because the finding is common and rarely anticipated: the arrangements that men lose when a spouse dies or a marriage ends are frequently not their own, and their absence is discovered at the point of need.

**The discovery arrived through a practical emergency.** The hospital, the taxi, and the sentence: *who would I call?* This is the pattern identified in Section 27.1's second form, and it is included because men frequently describe the moment precisely and describe it as the first occasion at which they counted their arrangements.

**The case moved through Forms Three and Four of Section 27.1 in sequence.** Musa first could not ask — his position was Form Three, the man with arrangements who cannot use them, since his children were available and he declined their help. And his arrangements had been structured by his responsibilities, which is Form Four. The sequence, in which one form conceals the next, is common.

**The entry point was a form rather than a confidence.** The general practitioner's four questions on a printed form, asked without eye contact, supplied the opening that the man could not construct. The case's practical teaching is the value of an arrangement in which the delivery of the material is routine rather than an act of disclosure: the man is asked, and answering a question is a different act from volunteering a statement. This is why Stage Five of Section 27.5 is placed as the entry point, and why it is described as an entry point rather than a destination.

**The arrangements were built in an ascending order of difficulty.** The sleeping first, the marriage later; the friend contacted about a spare part; the group joined in the third month; the children told in the fourth month. The man's own account of the sequence — *the first month I did not say anything about the marriage; it was easier to go for the sleeping* — is the graded method of Section 27.5 in practice, and the case is included because men are frequently instructed to begin with the largest item, which produces the outcome the man fears.

**The disclosure to the children produced a finding the man had not anticipated.** His son, aged forty-two, had the same position. This outcome is reported with striking consistency by men who disclose to their adult children, and it is included because it indicates the reach of the material in this chapter: the condition is transmitted, it is transmitted in silence, and a single disclosure frequently reveals that it is held by both parties and by neither man's arrangement.
