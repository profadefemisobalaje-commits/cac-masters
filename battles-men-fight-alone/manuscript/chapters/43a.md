# Teaching the Next Generation of Men to Speak {.chapter num="43" short="Teaching the Next Generation of Men to Speak"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the four channels through which boys acquire their conduct in the presence of emotion, and identify each channel in his own history and in his household.
2. Explain what is actually transmitted when a boy learns silence, distinguishing the expression that is withheld from the lesson that is taught by the withholding.
3. Analyse the specific contribution of parents, teachers, coaches, artisans, and mentors, and the reason the influence of a single adult is frequently decisive.
4. Identify the harmful propositions contained in common statements about manhood, and challenge them without lecturing, shaming, or abandoning the standard the boy requires.
5. Teach the six competences of emotional coping to a boy of a stated age, using the four-stage method of demonstration, rehearsal, feedback, and application.
6. Design the conditions under which a boy will ask for help: the permission, the named person, the routine, the confidentiality, and the follow-through.
7. Conduct an emotional curriculum within a household, a school, a workshop, an association, or a community, and state its measures.
:::

## Introduction

Every chapter of this book has described a man conducting a position largely alone. This chapter turns to the point at which that position was formed. It is placed at the end of the book's argument for a reason that is not sentimental. The patterns described in Parts I to XI are not produced afresh in each generation. They are transmitted, and the transmission has a mechanism, a curriculum, a set of instructors, and an examination that is conducted continuously and without notice.

The chapter's subject is therefore not children in the ordinary sense. It is the reproduction of a man's silence, and the possibility of interrupting that reproduction at the point where it is cheapest to interrupt: in the years before the pattern has become a man's character, his marriage, his health, and his household's arrangements.

The chapter begins from a finding that the case material of this book records with unusual consistency. Men who describe themselves as unable to speak about what they carry do not generally report having decided, at some point, to become silent. They report having learned it. The learning was not conducted in a single conversation. It was conducted in thousands of small transactions, most of which nobody in the household remembers: the boy's report of an injury and the adult's instruction to stop crying; the boy's account of a humiliation at school and the adult's advice to hit back; the boy's question about his father's absence and the adult's answer that the father is working; the boy's tears at a funeral and the instruction to be a man; the boy's fear before an examination and the assurance that fear is for women; the boy's disclosure of a failure and the silence that followed it.

None of these transactions was intended to produce a man who cannot speak. Most of them were intended to produce a man who can endure. The distinction between the intention and the effect is the chapter's central concern, because it explains why the pattern survives the good will of the people who transmit it. **The men and women who teach a boy to be silent are, in the great majority of cases, trying to protect him.** They are preparing him for a world they believe to be hard, and they are using the preparation they received. The preparation works in the sense that it produces endurance. It fails in the sense that it also produces the specific incapacity this book has described: the inability to state a position, to name a state, to ask for an instrument, or to accept a party's participation before the position has collapsed.

The chapter's approach has five commitments, and the reader should hold them because each one corrects a common error in the material.

**The first is that the chapter is not an indictment of parents.** The material of the book's earlier chapters establishes the position of the fathers and mothers who transmitted the pattern: they were conducting households under economic pressure, often with their own unaddressed losses, frequently without a model of any other method, and generally with the sincere belief that the preparation they were giving was the kindest thing available. The chapter's interest is in the mechanism, not in the allocation of blame. A reader who finishes this chapter resentful of his father has misunderstood it, and has also lost the opportunity the chapter supplies, because the pattern is interrupted by the man who can describe what happened to him without either excusing it or being destroyed by it.

**The second is that the chapter is not a programme for making boys emotional in the sense that word is used dismissively.** Nothing in the chapter proposes that a boy should be encouraged to express every state at every moment, to abandon standards, to treat his own discomfort as the central fact of every room, or to conduct his interior life as a public performance. The material of Chapter 39.4's four competences is the standard: the boy is taught to know what he feels, to regulate it, to state it to a party who has a right to know, and to conduct himself appropriately in the setting. This is a discipline, and it is a harder discipline than silence, which is why boys frequently prefer silence when it is available.

**The third is that the teaching is conducted by conduct rather than by instruction.** The research literature on the acquisition of emotional conduct is consistent on this point, and the case material of this book confirms it: a boy learns what is done with a feeling by observing what the adults around him do with theirs, and he learns it more reliably than he learns anything they say. A father who instructs his son to speak about his difficulties while conducting his own position in complete concealment is teaching the son the second lesson and not the first. The chapter therefore treats the adult's own practice as the curriculum's first component, and it treats the adult's repair after failure as the second.

**The fourth is that the teaching has an age structure, and the structure is not optional.** A method that is appropriate for a boy of seven is inappropriate for a young man of seventeen, and the reverse. The chapter supplies the age structure in Section 43.4, and it supplies it as a working guide rather than as a set of rules, because the reader's boy is a particular boy and the material of the book's earlier chapters establishes how much variation exists in the timing of a young man's development.

**The fifth is that the chapter's unit is not the individual boy but the arrangement around him.** A household can teach a boy to speak, and a school can unteach it within a term. A workshop can supply the conditions in which a young man's competence is built, and a peer group can punish the disclosure that the workshop made possible. The chapter therefore addresses five institutions in Section 43.6, and it supplies a design for each, because the reader who is not a father is still frequently a teacher, an employer, a coach, an uncle, a neighbour, an association's officer, or a man whose conduct is being observed by a boy who has not told him that he is watching.

The chapter's sequence follows the outline. Section 43.1 describes how boys learn emotional expression, and supplies the four channels of transmission. Section 43.2 examines the influence of parents, teachers, and mentors, and states the specific mechanism by which a single adult's influence becomes decisive. Section 43.3 addresses the harmful propositions about manhood and the practice of challenging them. Section 43.4 supplies the skill curriculum and its age structure. Section 43.5 addresses help-seeking, and the conditions under which a boy will use an instrument that is available to him. Section 43.6 turns to the community, and supplies the design principles for the five institutions. Section 43.7 presents an illustrative case. Section 43.8 supplies the practical programme, and the chapter's exercise is conducted within it.

A note on the chapter's limits is required before it begins. This is not a manual of child development, and it does not substitute for the professional advice of a teacher, a paediatrician, a psychologist, or a clinician where a boy's difficulties are significant. The material that follows is the book's ordinary standard: an account of mechanisms that are well established, a set of practices that the case material supports, and a collection of exercises that a reader can conduct and evaluate. Where a boy's position includes a disorder, a disability, a trauma, a bereavement, or a safeguarding concern, the chapter's material is conducted alongside professional advice and not in place of it.

## 43.1 How Boys Learn Emotional Expression

The section's task is to describe the mechanism of transmission. It proceeds in four channels, because the research literature and the case material agree that the learning is not conducted through one route, and because a reader who addresses only one channel will find that the others restore the pattern he has attempted to change.

The channels are modelling, instruction, reinforcement and punishment, and the observation of consequence. They operate simultaneously, they frequently contradict one another, and the contradiction is itself instructive. A boy who is told that men do not cry, who observes his father crying alone in a vehicle, and who observes that the crying is never mentioned afterwards, has received three lessons at once, and the third is generally the one that governs his conduct in later life.

### Channel One: Modelling

Modelling is the acquisition of conduct through the observation of a party who is significant to the observer. The research literature on imitation and observational learning is extensive, and the findings relevant to this chapter can be stated without exaggeration in four propositions.

**The first is that the model's conduct is learned more reliably than the model's instruction.** Where the two conflict, the conduct generally prevails, and the prevalence increases with the model's significance to the boy. The father who says *you can talk to me* while conducting his own difficulties in silence is modelling silence, and the sentence he speaks is received as the household's official position rather than as its actual practice.

**The second is that the boy learns the model's conduct in the presence of emotion specifically, and not merely the model's conduct in general.** A father may be talkative, warm, and available in ordinary circumstances and become entirely inaccessible when he is afraid, ashamed, or grieving. The boy learns the second conduct with greater precision, because it is the conduct that appears at the moment when he requires a model.

**The third is that modelling includes the model's management of the aftermath.** The boy observes not only what the adult does with a feeling but what the adult does afterwards: whether the feeling is mentioned, whether the adult repairs what he damaged while conducting it, whether the household is permitted to refer to it, and whether the adult's conduct in the following days acknowledges it. A single incident of an adult's anger teaches a boy less than the household's subsequent treatment of the incident. Where the incident is never mentioned, the boy learns that certain events are not to be discussed; where the adult apologises the following morning and names what happened, the boy learns that events of that kind can be discussed, and he learns it from the repair rather than from the anger.

**The fourth is that the boy models the same-sex adult's conduct with a specificity that is frequently understated in accounts of parenting.** The finding in the case material is consistent: boys attend closely to what the men around them do with fear, shame, grief, and tenderness, and they treat those observations as the operative definition of what a man is. This is the material of Chapter 41's five clauses, and this chapter's contribution is the observation that the definition is not transmitted by the clauses being stated. It is transmitted by the clauses being conducted.

The practical consequence for the reader is direct and uncomfortable. **The reader's own practice is the chapter's first curriculum, and it cannot be replaced by any material in this chapter.** A man who conducts Section 43.8's programme while maintaining the position that Chapters 42 and 24 describe will produce a boy who can recite the programme and cannot use it. The reverse is also true and is frequently more powerful: a man who conducts a single honest sentence about his own difficulty, in the boy's presence, at a moment when the boy can observe the aftermath and observe that the household survived it, has taught more than a year of instruction.

### Channel Two: Instruction

Instruction is the explicit material the boy is given: the sentences, the rules, the proverbs, the corrections, and the explanations. Its influence is smaller than modelling, and it is not negligible, because instruction supplies the vocabulary and the permissions with which the boy interprets what he observes.

Four features of instruction matter for this chapter.

**The first is that instruction supplies names.** A boy who is given the word for what he is experiencing can report it; a boy who is not given the word reports the nearest available substitute, which is frequently a physical complaint, an accusation, or a silence. The finding in the case material is that men who can name their states with precision generally had one adult who supplied the names, and that the supply was frequently incidental: a teacher who asked a question in a particular way, an aunt who said *you are not angry, you are frightened*, a coach who said *that is disappointment, and it will pass*.

**The second is that instruction supplies permissions.** The sentence *you may tell me when something is wrong* is an instruction, and its effect depends on the conditions supplied in Section 43.5. Where the permission is stated and the response to the first disclosure is punitive, inattentive, or dismissive, the permission is withdrawn and the withdrawal is remembered for longer than the sentence was spoken.

**The third is that instruction supplies the standard against which the boy evaluates himself.** The sentences *boys do not cry*, *a man provides*, *do not disgrace the family*, and *you are the man of the house* are not merely prohibitions. They are criteria, and the boy will conduct his adult life by evaluating himself against them, frequently without knowing that he is doing so. The material of Chapter 41.3's eight tests is the adult form of this mechanism.

**The fourth is that instruction's contradictions are resolved in favour of conduct.** The reader should not conclude from this that instruction is useless. He should conclude that instruction is a supplement, that it is most effective when it names what the boy has already observed, and that it is least effective when it contradicts what the household does. The sentence that works is not *men can speak about their difficulties*. It is *I found it hard to speak about mine, and this is what happened when I did*.

### Channel Three: Reinforcement and Punishment

The third channel is the household's and the community's response to the boy's expression. It operates through attention, approval, acceptance, status, ridicule, exclusion, physical punishment, and the withdrawal of affection, and it is the channel that produces the most rapid learning.

The research literature on operant learning supplies the general form, and this chapter's concern is the specific pattern that the case material records. Four responses are common, and each teaches something different.

**The response of approval for non-expression.** The boy reports nothing, conducts his difficulty privately, and is praised for being mature, strong, composed, or *not a burden*. The praise is genuine, and it is the most efficient instruction in concealment that a household can supply. Its adult form is the man of Chapters 20 and 31 who cannot accept recognition for anything except endurance.

**The response of ridicule for expression.** The boy cries, states a fear, or asks for help, and the response is laughter, a nickname, an imitation, or a report of the disclosure to other parties. The learning is rapid, general, and durable. Its specific feature is that it generalises beyond the party who supplied it: the boy who is ridiculed by a cousin learns not to speak to adults, and the boy who is ridiculed by a peer group learns not to speak at all.

**The response of punishment for expression.** The response is a beating, a withdrawal of privilege, an accusation of weakness, or a threat. The learning is the strongest of the four, and its adult form is frequently the most difficult to address, because the man has learned not only that expression is unrewarded but that it is dangerous.

**The response of indifference.** The boy discloses and nothing happens: no ridicule, no punishment, and no response. The learning is distinctive and frequently overlooked. The boy concludes not that expression is dangerous but that it is pointless, and the conclusion is harder to reverse than fear, because fear can be addressed by safety and pointlessness cannot be addressed by anything except a response that demonstrates otherwise.

The reader should note the asymmetry that the case material records. **The responses that suppress expression do not need to be frequent to be effective.** A single punishment at a formative moment can govern a man's conduct for forty years, and the men in this book's case material frequently identify it precisely: an incident at eight, at eleven, at fourteen, in which the boy's expression was met with a response that he has organised his adult conduct to avoid repeating. The practical consequence is that the reader who is conducting this chapter's programme does not need to construct a perfect environment. He needs to ensure that the boy's disclosures are met, consistently, with the response described in Section 43.5.

### Channel Four: The Observation of Consequence

The fourth channel is the boy's observation of what happens to other people who express what they feel. It extends the third channel beyond the boy's own experience, and it is the channel through which a household's treatment of its adult members becomes the boy's curriculum.

The boy observes his father's disclosure and its consequence; his mother's disclosure and its consequence; his uncle's drinking and the household's response to it; his teacher's account of his own difficulty and the staff room's response; the older boy at school who cried and the peer group's treatment of him; the man in the community who sought treatment and the community's account of him. He draws conclusions from these observations that are frequently more influential than anything said to him, because they are conclusions about the world rather than about himself.

Three consequences are frequently observed and should be stated because each one teaches a specific lesson.

**The consequence of exposure.** A man in the boy's observation discloses a difficulty and is treated as diminished: his standing is reduced, his competence is questioned, his disclosure becomes the community's material. The boy learns that disclosure is dangerous. The lesson is frequently accurate about the environment in which the boy lives, and this chapter's material does not deny it. The remedy is not to persuade the boy that disclosure is safe in an unsafe environment. It is to supply an environment in which a specific disclosure to a specific party is safe, and to conduct the boy's observation of that environment.

**The consequence of concealment.** A man in the boy's observation conceals a difficulty and the concealment is successful: the position holds, the household does not learn, and the man continues. The boy learns that concealment works. The lesson is frequently accurate in the short term, and it is false in the long term, since the case material of Chapters 22, 23, and 42 establishes the position's eventual cost. The remedy is the boy's observation of the later stage, which households rarely supply because they conduct the concealment successfully and the collapse occurs after the boy has left.

**The consequence of repair.** A man in the boy's observation conducts a difficulty badly, damages a relationship, and then repairs it: he names what he did, states its effect, and conducts the repair over a period. The boy learns that failure is survivable and that repair is available. The finding in the case material is that this observation is the single most valuable item in the curriculum, and that it is frequently absent from the households that most require it, because the adults conduct their failures privately and the repairs, where they occur, occur outside the boy's observation.

The reader should note what the four channels imply for the chapter's programme. The programme does not consist of a conversation. It consists of an arrangement in which the adult's conduct, the household's instruction, the responses to the boy's expression, and the consequences the boy observes are aligned, over years, in the direction of the four competences. This is a slower programme than the reader expects, and it is the only one the material supports.

### The Relative Weight of the Channels

The reader will reasonably ask which channel he should address first, and the chapter's answer is drawn from the case material and stated as a working guide rather than as a measurement.

**The channel of consequence is the most influential and the least addressed.** The boy's conclusions about the world are formed from what he observes happening to people who express what they feel, and the household frequently has more control over those observations than it believes. The specific practice is the deliberate conduct of a repair or a disclosure in the boy's observation, and the deliberate absence of the community's treatments that the boy would misread.

**The channel of reinforcement and punishment is the most rapid and the most reversible.** A household that changes its response to the boy's expression will see a change within weeks, and the change will be more durable if the response is consistent across the adults in the boy's life.

**The channel of modelling is the most durable and the slowest.** It is changed by the adult's own conduct over a period of years, and it is the channel that the reader's own work in Parts X and XI addresses directly.

**The channel of instruction is the least influential and the easiest to mistake for the whole.** It is the channel that books, sermons, assemblies, and posters address, and its frequent failure is not a reason to abandon it. It is a reason to conduct it as a supplement to the other three, and to conduct it in the form that names what the boy has already observed.

## 43.2 What Is Actually Transmitted

The previous section described the channels. This section describes the content, because the content is frequently misidentified by the adults who transmit it, and the misidentification is the reason their corrections fail.

The adults in the case material generally believe that they are transmitting one of two things: a standard of conduct, or a preparation for hardship. The boy receives something different. He receives a set of five lessons about the relationship between his interior life and the world, and each lesson is stated below in the form in which it is learned rather than the form in which it is taught.

**The first lesson is that a state which is not expressed does not exist.** The lesson is learned from the household's treatment of its own difficulties: the father's exhaustion that is never mentioned, the mother's grief that is conducted privately, the financial difficulty that the children are not told about. The boy concludes that the household's account of itself is the account of what is real, and that his own states, which do not appear in that account, are therefore not real. Its adult form is Chapter 3.2's alexithymic pattern and Chapter 21.4's man who cannot answer the question *how are you*.

**The second lesson is that expression is a request, and a request is a debt.** The boy learns that stating a difficulty imposes a burden on the party who receives it, and that the burden must be repaid. He therefore states a difficulty only when he can state it as a settled matter, with the solution already in hand, and he does not state it when it is unresolved. Its adult form is Chapter 14.3's disclosure that arrives too late to be useful, and Chapter 27.4's man who cannot call anybody because a call would be an imposition.

**The third lesson is that the audience determines the permitted content.** The boy learns that certain states are permitted in certain rooms and not others: fear is permitted before his mother and not before his father; failure is permitted before his friends and not before his uncle; grief is permitted at a funeral and not at a workplace. He learns the mapping with precision, and he learns it as a set of rules rather than as a set of preferences. Its adult form is Chapter 18.4's compartmentalisation and the man who is a different person in each of his settings.

**The fourth lesson is that the cost of expression is paid by the party who expresses.** The boy observes that the person who discloses in his household is the person who is subsequently treated as diminished, blamed, or required to manage the household's reaction to the disclosure. He concludes that expression is expensive and that the expense falls on him. Its adult form is Chapter 35.3's accounting of the cost of asking, and the man who has calculated that help is more expensive than endurance.

**The fifth lesson is that the position is the man.** The boy learns that his family's standing, his father's standing, and his own standing are the household's principal asset, and that anything which reduces the standing reduces the man. He learns to protect the account rather than the position, which is the material of Chapter 42. Its adult form is the entire architecture of Part XII's preceding chapters.

The reader should note the relationship between these five lessons and the four competences of Chapter 39.4. The lessons are the inverse of the competences, item by item. The first lesson removes the knowledge of a state; the second removes the statement of a need; the third removes the appropriate conduct in the setting; the fourth removes the acceptance of a party's participation; and the fifth removes the distinction between the man and his position, which is the condition on which all four competences depend.

This exact inversion is the reason the chapter is placed here rather than in a book about parenting. **The adult's work in Parts X and XI and the boy's curriculum are the same work at different points in the life course**, and the reader who has conducted the earlier chapters is therefore already in possession of the material he requires. He is not learning a new subject in this chapter. He is learning to teach the subject he has been studying, to a pupil who is watching him conduct it.

### The Fifth Lesson and the Boy's Standing

The fifth lesson requires a separate treatment, because it is the one that the adults in the case material most frequently transmit without intending to, and because it explains why the boy's silence survives the household's attempts to address the other four.

The lesson is transmitted by the household's ordinary conduct: the emphasis on the family's name, the correction of the boy's behaviour in public, the instruction not to disgrace the family, the pride taken in the boy's performance at school, the comparison with the neighbour's children, the account of the boy given to relatives. None of these is abusive, and their cumulative effect is a specific structure: the boy's worth is located in an external account, and the account is conducted by other people.

The consequence for the boy's expression is direct. A difficulty that is stated is a difficulty that enters the account, and the account is the household's asset. The boy therefore does not state the difficulty, not because he fears punishment, but because he has understood the household's economy and he is protecting its principal asset. The finding in the case material is that this structure is frequently strongest in households that have experienced a loss of standing: a business failure, a demotion, a migration, a public humiliation, a period of poverty. The household has learned that standing is fragile, and it transmits the lesson with the urgency of experience.

The remedy is not to abandon the standard. A boy requires a standard, and the material of Chapter 41.6's working definition supplies one that does not require the inversion. The remedy is to separate the standard from the account: to praise the boy's conduct rather than his rank, to state the household's difficulties rather than conducting them as secrets, to treat a failure as information rather than as a reduction of the family, and to conduct the adult's own standing at its true level, which is the material of Chapter 42.6's sixth step.

A household that conducts this separation for two or three years produces a boy with a specific and identifiable characteristic. He can state a difficulty without believing that he has damaged his family. He can accept a correction without experiencing it as a reduction of his worth. He can observe another person's failure without concluding that the person is finished. These three capacities are the chapter's product, and they are measurable, which is the subject of Section 43.8.
