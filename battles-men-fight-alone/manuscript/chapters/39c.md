## 39.3 What Coldness Costs

The costs are five, and the first three are borne by other people, which is the reason the chapter presents them first.

### Cost One: The Marriage or the Partnership

The material is consistent across the case material of this book and the research on marriage.

**The spouse's position.** She lives with a man who is present, reliable, and unreachable. The household's practical arrangements are conducted; the marriage's own content is absent. The specific description, reported by spouses in the family practice material, is that the marriage becomes administrative: the man is consulted about the matters of consequence and is not available for the matters that constitute the relationship.

**The specific forms.** The conversations that end within a minute because the man has answered; the man's attention which is present but not engaged; the absence of the man's own material, so that the wife carries hers without reciprocity; the absence of his interest in hers; the physical dimension, which reduces without a decision; and the wife's own conclusion, which the case material records in a consistent form: *I am not unhappy about the money or the house. I am alone in the house.*

**The outcome.** The research on marital satisfaction reports the association that the case material illustrates: the man's emotional availability is among the strongest predictors of the wife's satisfaction and of the marriage's stability. The specific finding for this chapter is that the marriages that end in the case material of men who are not unfaithful, not violent, and not financially inadequate most frequently end on this ground, and that the men describe the ending as incomprehensible to them.

**The finding the chapter states plainly.** A man's provision does not substitute for his presence, and a man's reliability does not substitute for his being known. The wife who has everything the man supplies and does not have the man is the position that the case material reports most frequently among the marriages of men who are functioning well by every external measure.

### Cost Two: The Children

The material of Parts III and V applies, and its specific form for a cold father is as follows.

**The children select.** The children learn what can be brought to the man and what cannot, and the selection becomes the household's arrangement. The finding: the children of a cold father describe the same childhood in later years, and the description is generally that he was a good man and that they did not know him.

**The sons learn the pattern.** The specific and most consequential mechanism: the son of a cold man acquires the man's technique as the model of strength and reproduces it. The finding from the case material is that this is the most frequently reported transmission in this book: the man's father was cold, the man is cold, and the man's son, in his own account, is beginning to be. The chapter's instruction is that the pattern's interruption requires the man's own conduct and not his instruction: the son learns from what the man does with the material of his own life.

**The daughters learn something else.** The material on the daughters of unexpressive fathers is reported consistently: the daughter's pattern of relationship with men, her own assessment of what she can expect, and the specific difficulty reported in the material where the daughter is required to interpret a man's silence as ordinary conduct. The finding is included because men report the discovery of it at their daughters' marriages and frequently with significant effect.

**The grandchildren.** The pattern's continuation is the material of Chapter 43, and the chapter's instruction is that the interruption is conducted at the man's own level: the grandchildren's knowledge of their grandfather is determined by the man's children's account of him, which is determined by what the man did in their childhoods.

### Cost Three: The People He Leads and the People Who Depend on Him

The material of Part IX applies, with a specific addition: the cold man's organisation does not bring him the information.

**The consequence for the information.** The material of Chapter 31.1's second effect is magnified: the staff of a cold leader do not report the difficulties, the morale, the errors, or the man's own conduct. The organisation's information is therefore shaped by the man's temperature, and the man's decisions are made on a selected account.

**The consequence for the people.** The subordinates, the staff, the household's members, and the community's people are conducted by a man who is reliable and unmoved. The specific finding from occupational practice: the men who work for a cold leader describe the absence of anything to be loyal to, and the finding is not that they resent the man's severity; it is that there is nothing to be loyal to.

**The consequence for the man's own development.** The material of Chapter 32.2: the cold man's learning is arrested, because the asking requires an exposure the man has arranged not to conduct.

### Cost Four: The Man's Own Perception

The cost that is borne by the man and that he reports last, because it is the one he has arranged not to notice.

**The loss of the capacity to register states.** Section 39.2's fourth stage. The man cannot identify his own position accurately: he reports tiredness, busyness, and *nothing*; the states that would supply him with the information about his situation are not available to him. The specific consequence for the reader's own position is that his assessment of himself is the least reliable evidence in this chapter, and that the exercise at the end of it should be conducted with the household's material and not only with his own.

**The loss of the capacity to be moved.** The man's response to what would ordinarily move a person — the child's achievement, the death of a friend, the beauty, the music, the occasion — is reduced or absent. Men report the finding precisely and generally late: *I was at his graduation and I knew that I should feel something and I did not, and I stood there and I clapped.*

**The loss of the aliveness.** The final form: the life's content. The interests, the play, the pleasure, the anticipation, the curiosity, the desire, and the satisfaction are reduced, and the man's account of his own life is that it is a set of requirements that are being discharged. The material is the case material of Chapter 37.3's fourth and fifth stages and of Chapter 24's empty season.

### Cost Five: The Loss of Judgement and of the Means of Knowing What He Wants

The fifth cost, and the one that most frequently affects the man's actual decisions.

**The mechanism.** The material of Chapter 27.3's isolation applies in an additional form: the man who cannot register his own states cannot use them as information. His preferences, his resistances, his satisfactions, and his unease are the means by which a person knows what to do with his life; a man who has arranged not to feel them decides from an incomplete account, and the decisions he makes are visible to others — the position he takes because he cannot feel his own objection, the marriage he remains in because he cannot register that he has left it years earlier, the business he pursues because he cannot feel the drag of it.

**The specific application.** The chapter's material on men's requests for advice applies: the man who reports that he does not know what he wants generally has a position and cannot read it. The finding from the case material is that the capacity returns with the work: the men who conduct the practices of Section 39.5 report that their preferences return, generally first in the physical and practical domains and then in the larger matters.

## 39.4 What Emotional Strength Actually Is

The definition and the competences. The chapter supplies them in operational terms, because the word *strength* has been used to license conduct that is not strength.

### The Definition

**Emotional strength is the capacity to register one's own states and other people's accurately, to regulate them, to state them where stating them is useful, to hold them where holding them is required, and to act well in the settings for which one is responsible — with all of it available at once.**

Three elements of the definition matter.

**It is a capacity, and capacities are practised.** The definition supplies competences rather than a state of character, and competences are developed by repetition. This is the reason Section 39.5 supplies practices.

**It includes holding.** The definition does not require the man to express everything he feels in every setting. It requires accurate registration and appropriate conduct: the man who holds his fear through a briefing and states it to his wife that evening has conducted both parts of the competence.

**It requires action.** The definition's last element is the one that distinguishes the chapter's position from the opposite error — the man who is expressive and ineffective. The competence is not feeling; it is feeling plus conducting correctly in the position for which the man is responsible.

### The Four Competences

**Competence One: Registration.** The capacity to identify what is present in the man and in the setting: the state, its intensity, its trigger, and its likely effect on his conduct. The practice is Section 39.5's first, and its instrument is language: the man who can name the state has it available; the man who has only *nothing*, *fine*, and *tired* does not.

**The specific difficulty for men.** The vocabulary. Men in the case material report that they lack words for states that they can nonetheless identify when supplied with a list. The practice therefore employs a list, and the men report that the access to the list is the element that makes the competence learnable: fear, anger, grief, shame, guilt, humiliation, longing, tenderness, satisfaction, pride, weariness, anxiety, disappointment, contempt, envy, gratitude.

**Competence Two: Regulation.** The capacity to hold a state without either suppressing it or being conducted by it: the breath, the physical channel, the phrase used in the practice material, the pause before the response, and the arrangements of Chapters 22 and 37. The competence's marker is the man's conduct: the state present, the man's response appropriate, and the man's recall of the state intact afterwards.

**The specific note.** Regulation is not suppression. The distinction in practice: suppression removes the state's availability and produces the costs of Section 39.3; regulation keeps the state's availability and removes the automaticity of the response.

**Competence Three: Expression.** The capacity to state the material in the settings where stating it is useful, at the level appropriate to the setting, in the man's own form. The forms are those of Chapters 4.6, 27.5, and 34.6: the small delivery, the specific statement, the four-part disclosure, the unit of conduct. The competence's marker: the man has at least one setting in which his states are stated and are received.

**Competence Four: Endurance.** The capacity to carry the material that has not been resolved, through a period, without either the suppression that removes it or the discharge that dumps it on somebody else. The competence's substance is the material of Chapters 8, 13, and 29.6: the carrying with accuracy, the stated position on self-punishment, the arranged place for the material, and the conduct that corresponds to it.

**The finding for the reader.** The four competences are ordered and cumulative in practice: registration is the condition of the others, and the case material records consistently that men who attempt expression without registration produce either the discharge or the performance of feeling, and neither is the competence the chapter describes.
