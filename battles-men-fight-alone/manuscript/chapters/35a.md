# The Courage to Ask for Help {.chapter num="35" short="The Courage to Ask for Help"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the specific barriers that prevent men from asking for help, and distinguish between the barriers that reflect a real cost and the barriers that reflect an anticipated one.
2. Assess the actual costs of asking, in the four categories men report, and compare them with the costs the man has already been paying without asking.
3. Identify the map of available help, including the forms men actually use, and match his own need to the appropriate source.
4. Apply the four-part form of a request, and use the practice forms for asking a friend, a professional, an employer, a household member, and a man in a position to assist.
5. Ask for what he actually requires rather than for what he believes is available, and recognise the pattern of the reduced request that is refused in the form in which it was made.
6. Receive another man's request in the form that keeps the channel open, using the four rules for the man who is asked.
:::

## Introduction

This chapter concerns the act that the whole of this book has been approaching: a man's asking for help.

The preceding chapters have established why the act is difficult. Men's arrangements are built on provision and competence; the requirement to have answers is imposed by positions and reinforced by reward; the role of everyone's solution is acquired rather than chosen; the instruction that a man should manage unaided is delivered by the culture, by the household, and by the man's own account of himself; and the material that most requires an arrangement is frequently the material that is most costly to disclose. Every one of those arrangements is real, and none of them is a prejudice that can be corrected by instruction.

The chapter's approach has five commitments.

The first is that a man's reluctance to ask is not irrational and is not primarily a matter of pride. It rests on an assessment of costs that is frequently accurate and that the chapter does not dismiss: the standing that a man loses in some settings when he asks, the dependence he establishes, the obligation he incurs, the exposure of a material he has decided to carry. The chapter's argument is not that these costs are imaginary. It is that they have been assessed incompletely, and that the assessment has omitted the other side of the ledger: the costs the man is already paying by not asking, which are generally larger and are generally invisible because they are paid in instalments.

The second is that help is available in more forms than men recognise, and that the form the man rejects is frequently not the form he needs. The man who will not attend a therapy appointment may use a general practitioner's consultation, a trade association, a lawyer, a debt counselling service, a peer group, a professional supervisor, an apprenticeship, a cooperative, or a single conversation with a man who has been through the same difficulty. Section 35.3 supplies the map, and its purpose is that the reader should be able to match the need to the source rather than concluding from his refusal of one form that no form exists.

The third is that asking is a technique. The chapter supplies the four-part form and the practice scripts, and their purpose is that the act ceases to be an all-or-nothing declaration of the man's inadequacy and becomes a specific, bounded request that most recipients can answer. The finding from practice is consistent: the request that fails is generally vague, unlimited, apologetic, or framed as an announcement of the man's collapse; the request that succeeds is specific, bounded, and made in the language of a task.

The fourth is that the man's first request is generally not the one he believes he is making, and that this is not a failure. The first contact is frequently about a presenting complaint, a practical matter, or somebody else's difficulty, and the material arrives later. The staged material of Chapter 27.5 applies, and the chapter supplies the forms that allow the first step to be made before the man is able to name what he needs.

The fifth is that the chapter's material concerns both directions. Section 35.6 supplies the four rules for the man who is asked, since a man who asks and is badly received will not ask again, and since the men who read this book are frequently the men that other men bring their requests to.
