## 13.7 Practical Exercise: The Relationship Grief Inventory

This exercise takes two sittings of about an hour. It is intended for the man who has lost a relationship and who has not processed it, whether the loss occurred two months or twenty years ago. Men frequently find that the exercise is more useful for losses of long standing than for recent ones.

### Part One: The Record

Write out, in about 400 words, the account of what happened, without evaluation. Include the sequence of events, what was said, what you did, and what you were told.

Then answer four questions:

- How long ago did this happen?
- Who have I told about it, in detail, and how many people is that?
- What have I never said aloud about it?
- If this relationship ended again tomorrow, with the same circumstances, what would I do differently?

### Part Two: The Losses

List separately what was lost, using the six components from Section 13.1: the person, the future, the role, the household structure, standing, and the assumption of permanence. For each, write one sentence about what it has cost you. Men who complete this part frequently discover that the largest loss is not the first item on the list.

### Part Three: The Verdict

Write out the judgement you formed about yourself as a result of the loss, in your own words, as a single sentence. Examples, from men's accounts: *I was not enough.* *I chose badly.* *I am not capable of keeping anyone.* *I am the kind of man women leave.*

Then test the verdict using the method from Chapter 8.

- What is the specific content of the claim, rather than its general form?
- Which parts of it are supported by evidence, and which are inferences?
- What would I say to a friend who described himself in these terms?
- What evidence would be required to disprove the claim, and does that evidence exist?

### Part Four: The Contribution

Write out, honestly and specifically, what you did and did not do in the relationship. This is not an exercise in blame, and it should be completed without either self-flagellation or the transfer of all responsibility. The purpose is practical: to identify the behaviours worth changing so that they do not recur in the same form.

The question that organises this part is: what did I do, or fail to do, that a reasonable observer would identify as having contributed to the difficulty?

### Part Five: The Unfinished Item

Write the sentence that you have never said to the other person and that you carry. Then decide, deliberately, what to do with it: say it to the person where that is possible and appropriate, say it to a counsellor or a trusted person, write it and destroy it, or acknowledge that it will remain unsaid and that the cost of carrying it is acceptable.

What is not recommended is to carry it indefinitely without having made the decision.

### Part Six: The Current Position

Answer three questions, each with a short written answer.

1. **Where am I in the sequence?** Stabilisation, understanding, or re-engagement — and on what evidence have I placed myself there?
2. **Which of the two destructive responses, retaliation or entrenchment, has been my pattern?** What has it cost me?
3. **What is the next item of work?** One specific thing, with a date. It may be a conversation, an appointment, a written account, a scheduling decision, or the removal of an object. One item rather than a programme.

## 13.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"It has been three years and I still think about her. Is that abnormal?"**

Thinking about somebody after a significant relationship is not abnormal at any duration. What matters is the quality of the thought. Thought that has become settled and factual — *that happened, this is what it was, this is what I learned* — is ordinary memory. Thought that reopens a decision, replays arguments, monitors her life, or activates the same judgement about yourself is unfinished grief, and it is worth treating as such. The inventory above is designed for exactly this case.

**"Should I tell my children what happened?"**

Where they are adults, yes, in an age-appropriate and non-partisan form: what occurred, in factual terms, without characterising their mother. Where they are children, the guidance in Chapter 17 applies: they should be told what concerns them — where they will live, when they will see each parent — and not the detail of the adult dispute. What should never be done is to use them as messengers, as allies, or as instruments of information.

**"How do I know whether to try to reconcile?"**

Four questions are decisive: is the person willing to participate in a genuine repair rather than a return to the previous arrangement; is the conduct that caused the separation capable of change, or is it a settled pattern; can you live with the requirement for verification over the coming months; and would you choose the relationship again if it were presented to you today as it actually was, rather than as you remember it? Chapter 15 provides the framework.

**"My friend is going through this and has stopped speaking to anybody. What do I do?"**

Keep contact without requiring conversation. Practically: a regular call with no agenda, a standing arrangement to eat or walk, and the absence of pressure to discuss anything. Men in this position frequently return to conversation on their own timetable, and the people who are available when they do are the ones who helped. Where there is any indication of self-harm or of serious depression, the guidance in Chapter 6 and Appendix B applies, and professional involvement should be sought.

**"I cannot seem to trust anyone now. Will that change?"**

It can, and the mechanism is ordinary experience rather than decision. Trust is rebuilt by the accumulation of small interactions in which a person proves reliable, and this requires exposure, which is precisely what the betrayed man avoids. The practical approach is graduated: small commitments to people who have been reliable in the past, extended slowly, and the maintenance of at least one relationship in which trust was never broken. The work is slow and it is possible.

**"How long before I should consider another relationship?"**

There is no fixed period, and the useful test is the one in Section 13.5: can you describe the previous relationship fairly, without requiring anybody to take sides; do you have a life that does not require a partner; and have you identified and addressed the pattern that contributed to the previous difficulty? Where the answer to all three is yes, the timing is a matter of preference. Where any of them is no, the new relationship is likely to reproduce the previous one.
