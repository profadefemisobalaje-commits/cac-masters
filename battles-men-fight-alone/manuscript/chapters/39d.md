## 39.5 The Six Practices

The practices are set out in the order in which they are conducted, and each is specific. Their purpose is the four competences of the previous section, and their form is adjusted for men: each is brief, each is conducted on a fixed occasion, and none of them requires the man to speak about his feelings in a conversation he has not chosen.

### Practice One: The Daily Registration

**What it is.** Once each day, at a fixed time, the man writes one sentence: what was present in him during the day, named from the list in Section 39.4, together with the occasion that produced it. The practice takes two minutes.

**Why it works.** The competence's instrument is language, and the practice supplies the language without requiring a conversation. Men report the finding consistently: in the first weeks the sentence is *tired* and *nothing*, and by the fourth to sixth week the man is identifying states that he had not previously registered, generally beginning with irritation and anxiety and proceeding to the material of Chapters 28 to 30.

**The specified form.** The sentence should contain the state's name, the occasion, and the intensity from one to five. The intensity scale is not an assessment of the man's state; it is the beginning of his ability to read it.

**The difficulty.** The practice's first weeks are unproductive, and the man's conclusion is generally that he does not have states. The finding from the case material is that the practice should be continued for at least six weeks before it is assessed, and that the assessment's measure is whether the man's vocabulary has increased and not whether his states have altered.

### Practice Two: The Physical Channel

**What it is.** A physical arrangement conducted regularly, in which the man's states are given their physical expression: the exercise, the run, the weights, the walk, the manual work, the labour, the drive, the swim.

**Why it works.** Two mechanisms are documented. The physiological: exertion discharges the physiological component of the stress response, which is the component that suppression leaves in the body, as Chapter 34.2 established. And the practical: the physical arrangement supplies the man with a setting in which his states are present without the requirement that they be discussed, which is the form that men in the case material maintain most reliably.

**The specified form.** The arrangement should be regular rather than occasional, and it should be of an intensity at which the man's attention is occupied by the physical matter. The finding: the walk in which the mind continues on the position is not the practice; the exertion that occupies the man's attention is.

### Practice Three: The Deliberate Non-Numbing

**What it is.** One occasion each week in which the man declines the numbing and allows the state to be present: the evening without the drink, the drive without the radio, the hour without the screen, the period in which the man sits with what is present.

**Why it works.** The competence of registration requires occasions on which states are permitted, and the numbing supplies the opposite in every hour. The practice's purpose is not the man's discomfort; it is the identification of what is actually present, which the practice material reports is generally less severe than the man's fear of it and considerably more informative.

**The specified form.** The occasion should be bounded — an hour, one evening, a stated period — and the man should record what was present, using Practice One's form. The finding: the first two or three occasions are the most difficult, and the material that arrives is frequently the material of Chapters 29 and 30.

**The safety note.** Where the material that arrives includes any item from Chapter 34.4's first group, the safety plan of Chapter 34.5 is conducted, and the practice is conducted with the professional arrangement in place. The chapter states the position without qualification: the presence of the material is not to be conducted alone when the material is of that kind.

### Practice Four: The Repair After Rupture

**What it is.** The specific, brief, and conducted form of the repair after the man's state has escaped into his conduct: the harsh remark, the disproportionate response, the silence through an evening, the withdrawal.

**Why it works.** The competence's most consequential application for men, and the one that determines the household's experience. The mechanism: the man's temperature is conducted where it is felt, and the household accumulates the material; the repair conducts the account and thereby prevents the household's construction of it, which is the material of Chapters 17 and 34.6.

**The specified form**, in four parts and stated within the day where possible: what I did;' what it was actually about; the correction, where one is required; and what the household should understand about it. The men in the case material describe its effect in consistent terms: *I started saying, that was not about you, it was about the office, and the household changed within a month.*

**The finding for men who cannot name the state.** The practice's third part is the one that matters, and it is available to the man who cannot yet name what was present: *I do not know what it was. It was not about you and I am sorry.* The statement is accurate, it is sufficient, and the case material records its effect on the household's arrangements.

### Practice Five: The Arrangement in Which He Is Known

**What it is.** At least one setting in which the man's states are stated and received: the wife, the friend, the group, the professional, the brother. The material of Chapters 4, 26, 27, and 35 applies, and the chapter's requirement is that the setting exists, since the competence cannot be maintained in a vacuum.

**Why it works.** The competence is practised in a setting and not in private. The finding from the case material is the one that Chapter 35 established: the man's states become available to him in proportion to their availability to one other person.

**The specified form.** The arrangements of Chapter 27.5, at the level the man can maintain: the one person, the group, the professional arrangement, the standing occasion. The practice's measure is the competence's fourth element, endurance, which is the capacity to carry the material through a period, and the capacity requires the setting as the material of Chapters 29.6 and 30.6 requires it.

### Practice Six: Warmth as a Discipline

**What it is.** The deliberate conduct of the specific acts that constitute warmth, conducted whether or not the man feels them, at a stated frequency. The acts are supplied in Section 39.6.

**Why it works.** The chapter's practical centre, and the reason it is placed last: a man who cannot yet register or name his states can conduct warmth as a discipline, and the case material records consistently that the feeling arrives with the conduct. The mechanism is the one the whole of this book has established: the arrangement precedes the state, and the man's conduct in the arrangement supplies the state rather than the reverse.

**The specified form.** A written list of specific acts, with the frequency at which each is conducted, and, where the man wishes, a statement to his wife and children that he is conducting it. The statement's form in the case material: *I have been present in this house without being in it, and I am going to do specific things rather than promise to change.* The finding from the case material is that the statement and the acts together altered the household's arrangements within months.

## 39.6 Warmth as a Discipline

The acts. The following register is the one used in the case material and in the practice literature on marriage and fatherhood, and the reader should select from it rather than adopting all of it.

### The Twelve Acts

**1. The greeting.** Conducted on arrival, with the man's attention as well as his presence: the man stops, looks, and speaks. The act's specific finding: the household's experience of the man's arrival is determined in the first thirty seconds.

**2. The question about the day, asked and then waited for.** The act requires that the man not fill the pause, and the finding from the case material is that this is the single act that most alters a household's arrangements.

**3. The appreciation that is specific.** Not *thank you for everything*, but the specific statement: *the way you managed that matter with the school was better than I would have done it.* The specific form of the appreciation is the element that makes it received.

**4. The appreciation that is spoken rather than assumed.** Men report that their appreciation exists and is not spoken, on the basis that it is known. The finding from the spouses in the case material: the appreciation that is not spoken is not received, and the household's account of the man as unappreciative is constructed in the absence of the statement.

**5. The touch that is not an approach.** The hand, the arm, the shoulder, the brief and ordinary contact. The act's finding: the men who reduced their physical contact to the sexual dimension report, on conducting it, that the household's temperature alters within weeks.

**6. The presence at the occasions that matter to the other person.** The school event, the relative's occasion, the friend's funeral, the thing the child is doing. The act's finding: the occasions that matter to the household's members are frequently not the ones the man had identified, and the household should be asked.

**7. The remembering.** The birthday, the anniversary, the date the man's wife mentioned the matter she was worried about, the child's examination. The act requires an instrument — the written list, the calendar, the note in the telephone — and the finding is that the instrument is not a diminishment of the act but its mechanism.

**8. The protection of the other person's time.** The act of taking the household's arrangements off somebody: the evening with the children, the errand, the period in which the man holds the household so that a member may rest. The material of Chapter 37 applies to the household's members as to the man.

**9. The apology that is not qualified.** The material of Chapter 29.5's rules. The act's finding: the qualified apology is not received and the household's account is not corrected.

**10. The play.** The act that men most frequently set down and that the household's members most frequently identify as its absence: the game, the joke, the foolishness, the laughter, the nonsense. The finding from the case material is that the sons and daughters of the men in this chapter describe the play's absence before they describe anything else.

**11. The statement of the man's own position.** The specific act of telling the household what is present in him: the difficulty, the fatigue, the worry, the satisfaction, the pride. The material of Chapters 34.6 and 39.5's Practice Four applies, and the act's function is that the man becomes a person in the household rather than its provision.

**12. The question about the other person's actual material.** Not *how are you* but the question that requires an answer: *how has the matter with your mother been this week*; *what is the thing you are not telling me about the school*. The finding from the case material: the household's members will answer this question and will not answer the general one.

### The Two Rules

**Rule One: The acts are conducted at a stated frequency, and they are not conditional on the man's state.** The rule is the chapter's practical centre and its reason is the one that governs the whole of this book: the arrangement precedes the state. The men who conducted the acts in their worst periods report the finding consistently: the act was available when nothing else was, and the household's arrangements altered while the man's state remained difficult.

**Rule Two: The acts are stated to the household, once, in the man's own words.** The statement's form is supplied in Practice Six, and its function is that the household should know what is being conducted, since the household's members otherwise interpret the man's changed conduct as a temporary matter or as a tactic.

## 39.7 Illustrative Case: The Man They All Respected

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family practice, marriage counselling, and counselling of men.

**Alhaji Musa, called by his title, is sixty-one, a retired deputy director of a state ministry, married to Hadiza for thirty-four years, with five adult children and two grandchildren.**

The man's standing in his community, in his profession, and in his family was uniform across every account obtained in the case, including the accounts of his children. He was, in the words used by three separate parties and recorded in the case, *a straight man* and *a man of his word*: he had served thirty-four years without a stain, he had built the family house, he had educated five children, he had carried his siblings' obligations, and he had been the person the community brought its disputes to.

The material at the centre of the case was described by his wife, Hadiza, in the family consultation, in the following terms: *I have been married to the most respected man in this area for thirty-four years and I have been alone in the house for thirty of them. He has never raised his voice. He has never been late with anything. He has never once asked me how I was. When my mother died, in 2009, he arranged everything, and he sat in the front and he did not put his arm around me, and in the evening he asked me about the accounts of the burial, and I went into my room and I put my hand over my mouth and cried so that nobody would hear it in the house, and I have never told him that. There is nothing wrong with my husband. My husband is not there.*

The accounts of the five children, obtained separately, were consistent and each contained a specific detail.

The eldest son, aged thirty-three, an engineer: *I have never in my life had a conversation with my father of more than four minutes. I can tell you the principles. I could recite his principles in my sleep. I do not know one thing that he is afraid of, except that I know, because I was twenty-nine and my business was in trouble, and he gave me the money and he said nothing about it ever again. I have not been able to say to him that I was frightened in that year. He was not interested in that. He was interested in the money.*

The second child, a daughter aged thirty, a pharmacist: *My father has never touched me. I do not mean that anything happened. I mean that in thirty years he has never put his hand on my head. When I got my registration, he said, you have done well. I have that sentence. I have counted the sentences.*

The third child, a son aged twenty-seven who had left the country: *I moved to another continent and I called him and I told him and he said, that is good, do not forget where you come from, and the call was four minutes. When I told my mother she asked me how I was feeling about leaving and she cried on the telephone. I do not call my father. There is nothing to say. I love him and there is nothing to say.*

The fourth child, a son aged twenty-four, described as the closest to the man: *He is my best teacher and I am his project, and I do not know how to be with him when I am not doing something he can approve of. In January I lost my job and I did not tell him. I told my mother. My mother said, tell him. I have not told him. I would rather tell him that I am dead than that I am not working.*

The fifth child, a daughter aged twenty-one: *My father is a good man and I do not know him and I have stopped expecting to.*

The position was discovered through a specific event in the man's sixty-first year. Hadiza, after a period of four months in which the man had retired and had organised his days with the same discipline, told him in the household's parlour that she intended to visit her sister for a period of about six weeks, and gave him a written statement, which he produced in the case. Its central passage: *You have retired and you are now in this house all day and I have to tell you something that I should have said thirty years ago. I am not able to be in this house with you all day. You are not a bad husband. You have never asked me one question about myself and I do not think that you know anything about me, and I have a sister and I am going to her house.*

The work, conducted over about two years, began with the man's attendance at a general practitioner for a sleep difficulty and proceeded through counselling, and had five elements.

**The registration.** The counsellor gave the man the list of states and the daily sentence practice. His account of the first six weeks: *I wrote nothing for eleven days. I could not find a word. The counsellor asked me what I had felt in the meeting that day and I told her that I had felt that I needed to write something. She said that is not a feeling. I sat there for four minutes and I said, I felt that I was going to be told off, and she wrote it and said, that is fear, and I said, no. And she said, what is it. And I said, that is fear, and I was sixty-one years old and I had not said that sentence in my life.*

**The account.** The material of Chapter 29.6's first element, conducted over three months, in writing, of the man's own account of his life: his father, who had been a railway clerk and who had died at fifty-eight, whose conduct Musa described in the case document as *he never said one word to me in my life except instructions*, and the man's own sentence, recorded in the document: *I became the man my father was, and I did it deliberately, because I decided at fourteen that I would be a man whose word was his bond, and I did not know that the bond had no person behind it.*

**The repairs.** Musa conducted the disclosure to Hadiza in the fourth month, over two sittings, in the four-part form, and it contained the material most frequently reported in cases of this kind: the man's own statement that he had not known that she had been alone; that he had provided what he had understood to be the requirement; and that he had not asked her a question in thirty-four years because he had not had any such question in his own household and had not known they existed. Hadiza returned from her sister's house in the fifth month. Her account, given a year later: *He asked me how I was on the first evening. I told him that he had never asked me in thirty-four years and that I did not know how to answer him. He said that I should take my time. He asked me again on the following evening, and on the third evening I told him about my mother and I spoke for about two hours and he did not move.*

**The repairs with the children.** Conducted individually over about ten months, and the man's description of the sequence is the element most worth recording: *I began with the fourth. He had told me that he had lost the position and he had not told me, and my daughter had told me that. I went to him and I said four sentences. I said that I had heard, that I was sorry, that I was not disappointed, and that I did not know how to say anything else. He cried, and I sat there, and then I put my hand on his shoulder and I have never done that to any of my children. He said, in about ten minutes, that he had been afraid of telling me for seven months. I have a son who was afraid of me for seven months and he was in the same town.*

**The warmth as a discipline.** The final element, and the man's own selection within it: the greeting, the specific appreciation, the question about the day with the pause, the touch, and the play. He described the practice's difficulty and its outcome: *The counsellor told me to write the five things and do them whether I felt them and I told her that was dishonest. She said, you have been doing things you did not feel for thirty-four years, except you were doing the wrong ones. In the first month I asked my wife about her day eleven times and every time I wanted to leave the room. In the fourth month I was sitting with her in the evening and I realised that I was enjoying it, and I said the sentence out loud, and she laughed at me for about a minute.*

Musa's summary, two years after the parlour conversation, was: *I was known in this town as a man of his word and I am not giving that up. I have added one thing. I have grandchildren and one of them is three and she runs to me when I come in and I sit on the floor with her, and my eldest son watched me doing that and he said the sentence that I have not forgotten: that his children would run to him. I have been a wall for thirty-four years and I have been told by my own daughter that she has counted my sentences. I cannot recover those years. I can be a person in the years that are left, and I am, and my son is watching me do it.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The man's standing was authentic and the coldness was not a failure of character.** By every external measure — his service, his provision, his conduct, the community's assessment — Musa had discharged his obligations completely, and the case is included because the men in this position are the ones least likely to be identified and least likely to be presented with the material. The household's accounts were uniform in their respect for him and uniform in their description of his absence.

**The material was a specific inheritance, identified by the man himself.** His father, who spoke only instructions, and his own decision at fourteen. This is the fourth mechanism of Section 39.2 and the case is included because the reader's own pattern is generally traceable in the same way, and because the naming of it as the father's pattern is what makes it available to be altered.

**The coldness was maintained in the absence of any awareness of the household's experience.** The man did not know that his wife had been alone, that his daughter had counted his sentences, that his son had been afraid for seven months of a job loss. The case records the mechanism of Section 39.3's third cost in its household form: the household's members conduct the selection described in Chapters 17 and 18, and the man's account of his family's life is therefore constructed from what was brought to him.

**The registration was the first competence and it was the hardest.** Eleven days without a word, the counsellor's refusal of *I felt that I needed to write something*, and the identification of fear at sixty-one. The case is included because the finding is reported by men in this material consistently: the first competence is the gate, and the vocabulary is the instrument, and the reader who concludes that he does not have states is at the stage the practice's first weeks supply.

**The warmth was conducted as a discipline and the feeling followed.** The five acts conducted in the first month with the man wishing to leave the room, and the fourth month in which he identified enjoyment and stated it aloud. This is the chapter's central practical claim — that the arrangement precedes the state — and the case's final element is the one that makes it: the son watching the man sit on the floor with the grandchild and stating that his own children would run to him.
