## 37.1 Why Rest Is Difficult for Men

The difficulty has four sources, and they reinforce one another. Each is described here in the terms men use.

### Source One: The Man's Account of Himself

For a large proportion of men, the answer to the question of who they are is composed substantially of what they do and provide. Chapter 24's material applies: the identity assembled from the roles, and the account held in the form of the sentence used to describe himself — the position, the trade, the provision, the responsibility.

The consequence for rest is direct: setting the work down is not experienced as a pause in the man's activity. It is experienced as a pause in his standing. The man's uneasy sense when he is not working is not laziness; it is the interruption of the arrangement through which he knows who he is. The finding from the practice material is that this source is the strongest of the four in men above fifty and in men whose positions carry weight, and that it is the source that remains after the others have been addressed.

### Source Two: The Measure of Worth by Output

The second source is the rule rather than the identity: **a man is worth what he produces, and the accounting is periodic.** The rule is delivered by the environment and adopted by the man, and it is examined in Section 37.4. Its operation on rest is that rest is unaccounted time, and unaccounted time is registered as a loss.

The rule has a specific feature that men describe precisely: it applies to the current period rather than to the accumulated record. A man who has worked for thirty years and provides for eleven people does not, when he rests on a Saturday, register the thirty years; he registers the day. The arrangement is the one described in Chapter 25.5: the results-based measure, applied at short intervals, in which the man's standing must be re-established continuously.

### Source Three: The Household's Requirement as the Man Understands It

The third source is the man's model of what his household requires, and it is frequently more demanding than anything the household has stated. The man's position is that his household's security depends on his continuing, that his availability is one of the things he supplies, and that his stopping would be a visible reduction in that supply.

The model is partly accurate: a household's arrangements do depend substantially on the man's provision, and the men in the case material who cannot rest are frequently the men whose households require what they provide. What is inaccurate is the conclusion drawn from it — that the requirement is continuous and that any pause is a risk. Section 37.3 supplies the evidence on the alternative's cost, and Section 37.5 supplies the reframing.

### Source Four: The Environment's Reinforcement

The fourth source, and the one that is genuinely external. The environment rewards continuous work in specific and observable ways: the promotion, the selection, the standing, the deference, the invitations, the requests, the competition among men, and the specific approval that the culture extends to the man who is always working. It penalises rest in subtler and equally observable ways: the man who declines the additional engagement, who leaves at the stated hour, who is unavailable at the weekend, who takes the leave, is recorded in the organisation's informal account before it is recorded in any formal one.

The source's practical consequence is that the man's decision about rest is a decision against a considerable gradient, and that its correction requires an arrangement rather than a preference. The men who rest successfully in the case material are not the men who decided to; they are the men who constructed a specification — the boundary of Chapter 31.5 — and who held it against the environment's pressure, generally with the support of one person who noticed.

### The Two Forms of the Prohibition

The four sources produce two distinct prohibitions, and the distinction matters because the corrections differ.

**The prohibition on stopping.** The man cannot set the work down: the extended hours, the weekend, the leave not taken, the retirement deferred, the telephone maintained through the holiday, the work conducted at the family occasion. Its correction is the arrangement of Section 37.6, and specifically the specified boundary with dates.

**The prohibition on being restored.** The second form, and the one men report less frequently because it is less visible: the man stops and is not restored. The rest is filled with the administration of the position — the calls, the messages, the planning, the reading, the worry, the household's tasks accepted as demands. The man's report of the position is precise: *I have not worked less in ten years; I have worked in different places.* Its correction is the definition of Section 37.2 and the selection of the specific categories of recovery that the man's position requires.

## 37.2 What Rest Actually Is

The chapter defines the term, because the definition determines the arrangement.

### The Definition

**Rest is the conduct that restores the man's capacity. It is not the absence of work, and it is not sleep.**

The distinction has three parts.

**Rest is not the absence of work.** A day in which the man does not work and in which his attention remains on the position — the calls, the worry, the planning, the telephone — is not a day of rest; it is a day of work conducted in another location, which is the second prohibition of Section 37.1.

**Rest is not sleep.** Sleep is one route to restoration and it is the most important single route; the man's position after a week of insufficient sleep cannot be corrected by an afternoon, and the material on sleep and judgement is unambiguous. But rest includes the restoration that sleep does not supply: the physical, the sensory, the mental, and the social categories below.

**Rest is defined by its effect rather than by its form.** The measure is not what the man did but what it produced: the man's capacity, his judgement, his mood, his patience, and his household's experience of him. The measure is checkable and it is applied in the exercise at the end of the chapter.

### The Six Categories of Recovery

The research and the practice literature on recovery from work identify categories that are distinct, and the man's requirement frequently spans more than one. The categories are set out with their characteristic forms.

**1. Physical recovery.** The sleep, the exercise, the food, and the restoration of a body that has been used. The category is the one with the most documented effect on mood, cognition, and stress physiology, and the men in the case material who adopt a single measure generally adopt this one, frequently in the form of the walk, the run, the cycle, or the gym.

**2. Mental recovery.** The disengagement of the attention from the work's material: the task that occupies the whole of the man's attention with something else, the engrossing pursuit, the practical work with the hands, the game, the reading, the learning. The category's mechanism is documented: the attention cannot be disengaged by instruction, and it is disengaged by the substitution of an absorbing activity.

**3. Sensory recovery.** The reduction of the input: the quiet, the dark, the absence of the screen's light and the noise of the day, the silence, the natural setting. The category is the one that men most frequently overlook, and its neglect is associated in the case material with the form of exhaustion that the man describes as *I cannot hear myself think*.

**4. Social recovery.** The company that requires nothing of the man: the friend without the purpose, the group without the agenda, the household's ordinary evening, the conversation without the outcome. The category is distinguished from the work's company and from the household's administrative meetings, and the material of Chapters 26 and 27 applies to its construction.

**5. Creative recovery.** The making, building, writing, drawing, playing, gardening, cooking, repairing, and craft. The category's specific value for the men in the case material is that it supplies the engagement that mental recovery requires while producing a result, which addresses the measure problem of Section 37.1's second source directly.

**6. Purposeful recovery.** The conduct of the man's own choice that is neither work nor obligation: the interest, the pursuit, the involvement with a group or a cause, the arrangement in which the man is not the provider. The category overlaps with the fifth arrangement of Chapter 31.5 and its function for men is that it supplies the recognition and the engagement that the position supplies in a form the man has chosen.

### The Four Distinctions

**Rest is not a reward.** The arrangement in which rest is earned by production is the arrangement of Section 37.1's second source and it produces the pattern in which the man rests only when he has collapsed or when the measure is satisfied, which is never. The chapter's position is that rest is a component of the man's obligations: the people who depend on his judgement require it to be maintained.

**Rest is not uniform.** Different men require different categories, and the same man requires different categories in different periods. The man's own position is the criterion, and the exercise supplies it: what restores the man's capacity is identified by the effect, and the man should record which category produced which change.

**Rest is mostly small.** The research on recovery identifies short frequent episodes as effective, and the practice material records that men generally seek restoration in the annual holiday and neglect the daily and weekly measures. The specific finding is that a fortnight's leave taken in the absence of any daily recovery produces less restoration than the same fortnight taken with the daily measures in place.

**Rest is mostly unimpressive.** The categories are unremarkable: the sleep, the walk, the quiet, the friend, the afternoon's practical work, the Sunday, the evening without the telephone. The chapter notes this because the men in the case material describe their own measures as trivial, and because the effect of the ordinary measure is generally out of proportion to its appearance.
