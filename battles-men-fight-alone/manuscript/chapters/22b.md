## 22.3 Why Willpower Alone Fails

The most important practical fact about personal weaknesses is that the method men apply to them — resolve — is the method with the worst record. Understanding why is not an academic exercise; it is the reason the alternative methods work.

### The Mechanism of a Habit

Four components operate together, and each is a point of intervention.

**The cue.** A specific situation that initiates the behaviour: a time of day, a place, a person, a state of feeling, an activity that precedes it. The cue is frequently invisible to the man because it has been established for years and because the behaviour begins before he has registered the decision.

**The routine.** The behaviour itself, performed with varying degrees of awareness.

**The reward.** What the behaviour supplies: relief, stimulation, company, oblivion, a sense of competence, or the discharge of tension. This is the component that keeps the behaviour in place, and it is the one that men most frequently do not identify, because they assess the behaviour by its cost rather than by its function.

**The repetition.** The behaviour's establishment owes more to the number of repetitions than to the strength of any single urge. A weakness performed four hundred times over six years is not a disposition; it is a path that has been worn into the ground.

### Why Resolve Fails at the Four Points

**Resolve fails because it operates against the cue rather than on it.** A man who decides, in the morning, not to repeat the behaviour that occurs at eleven o'clock at night has made a decision at a moment when the cue is absent. By the time the cue arrives, the decision is a memory and the situation is present. The method that works reverses this: the cue is changed in advance, when the man has capacity, rather than resisted at the moment when he does not.

**Resolve fails because it does not address the reward.** A man who removes a behaviour without supplying what the behaviour provided has removed his own method of managing a state, and he will reinstate the behaviour in order to restore the method. This is the reason that the instruction to stop is frequently complied with for two or three weeks and then abandoned.

**Resolve fails because it is finite and it is being spent on everything.** The research on self-regulation is consistent on this point: the capacity for active resistance is a limited resource that is depleted by the ordinary requirements of a working day. A weakness that must be actively resisted at six o'clock in the evening is being resisted by a man whose capacity has been spent since eight in the morning. Chapter 12's material on decision fatigue is the same finding applied elsewhere.

**Resolve fails because it is stated in the form that produces the most shame.** *I will not do this again* is an absolute, and each failure is therefore a complete failure, which produces the shame described in Section 22.2, which produces the behaviour.

### What Replaces Resolve

Four mechanisms, and they operate together. They are set out in Section 22.5 and their logic is summarised here.

**The cue is altered in advance.** The behaviour's trigger is removed, changed, or interrupted before the situation in which it occurs. This is done at a time of the day when the man has capacity.

**The reward is supplied by something else.** A substitute is arranged that performs the same function, chosen to fit the function rather than to be virtuous.

**The behaviour is measured rather than resisted.** A record of occurrences, in place of a prohibition, converts the matter from a question of character into a question of data, which is a change in register that men find substantially easier to sustain.

**The isolation is ended by one person.** Accountability removes the private context in which the behaviour occurs, and it provides the correction to the man's account of himself that shame prevents.

## 22.4 Understanding Your Patterns and Triggers

The work of this section is diagnostic and it should be conducted in writing. Its purpose is to convert a general struggle into a specific pattern, because only specific patterns can be changed.

### The Trigger Map

For the weakness selected in Section 22.1, complete the following over two to four weeks, recording each occurrence at the time rather than reconstructing it later.

Four patterns emerge from this record with considerable reliability.

**The timetable.** Most weaknesses occur at defined times. The man who believes his anger is unpredictable will frequently find that it occurs in the two hours after work, on the days when the household's demands arrive before he has had a period of transition.

**The state.** Hunger, exhaustion, pain, anxiety, shame, boredom, and loneliness are the states most frequently preceding the behaviour. This finding is important because it means that a substantial proportion of the occurrences can be addressed by changes in sleep, food, and the structuring of the day, which are simpler interventions than the man expects.

**The company and the place.** The specific individuals and environments associated with the behaviour. The identification frequently produces an uncomfortable finding about a friendship or an arrangement.

**The expectation.** What the man expected the behaviour to produce. This is the field that identifies the function, and men who complete it report with consistency that the function is not the one they would have named: the drinking that was expected to produce relaxation was being used to produce sleep; the anger that was expected to discharge tension was being used to produce a sense of control; the gambling that was expected to produce money was being used to produce excitement in a period of numbness.

### The Function Test

Write the sentence: *I do this in order to ____.* Then test it against four questions.

1. **Does the behaviour actually produce the stated outcome?** Frequently not, or not beyond the first thirty minutes.
2. **What would produce the same outcome without the cost?** The answer to this question is the substitute, and it should be specific.
3. **What would happen if the state the behaviour manages had no management at all?** This question is uncomfortable and it is the one that identifies what the man believes he cannot survive.
4. **Who else's behaviour participates in the pattern?** Frequently another person's conduct is required for the man's weakness to operate: the person who supplies the alcohol, the colleague who invites the bet, the relative whose conduct produces the anger. The pattern is frequently not the man's alone.

### The History

A further diagnostic step, and it is frequently the one that produces the most useful material: identify the period in which the weakness began. Men report, with consistency, that the behaviour has a start date and that the date is attached to a situation.

The situations named most frequently are: a period of financial difficulty; a job or a role the man could not refuse and could not perform; a loss — a bereavement, a separation, a business failure; a period of illness; a marriage's deterioration; and a period in which the man was required to carry something he had never disclosed. In a substantial proportion of cases, the weakness is a method of managing a situation that has never been addressed, and its treatment is therefore the treatment of the situation.

The practical implication is direct: a weakness that began in a specific period is frequently not addressable without addressing that period's material. Men who attempt to remove the behaviour while leaving the situation untouched report the pattern described in Section 23.6 — recovery that does not hold — and the case in Section 22.7 illustrates it.
