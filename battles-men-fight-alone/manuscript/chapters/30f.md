## 30.8 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Conducted Both Funerals

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in bereavement services, primary care, and family practice.

**Sunday is fifty-one, a regional manager for a distribution company, married to Beatrice for twenty-three years, with two children aged nineteen and fifteen.**

In a period of five weeks in 2019, two people died. Sunday's father died at the age of seventy-eight after a period of illness. Twenty-three days later, in the same month, Sunday's closest friend, Mark, died at forty-nine of a stroke, in a hotel in a city where he had been working.

Sunday's account of the five weeks and the two years that followed supplies the case.

The father's death was managed with complete competence and it supplied the man's whole position in the household. Sunday arranged the hospital's formalities, the family's notifications, the funeral's arrangements, the burial, the reception, the accounts, and the subsequent attention to his mother's circumstances. His description: *I was the eldest son. I did the whole thing, and I did it well. Everyone said so. My mother still says so.*

Mark's death was managed in six hours. Sunday was informed by telephone on a Tuesday morning, and he attended the funeral in another state on the Thursday: a four-hour journey each way, a night in a hotel, and a return to work on the Friday. His description: *Nobody knew Mark in my family. He was my friend for twenty-two years. My wife met him twice, at functions. I told my office that I was attending a funeral and I told my house that a friend had died. My son asked me if I was all right and I said I was fine, and I went to work on the Friday because the week's report was due.*

The household's arrangement of the period is worth recording exactly. The family's attention was on the father's death. The mother required support. The relatives required the arrangements' details. Sunday's own position was that he had lost his friend, and nobody in the household had known Mark, and Sunday described the discovery of what that arrangement produced: *There was no day on which anybody was thinking about Mark except me. He was in my head and nowhere else in the world. It sounds like a thing a man would exaggerate. It is not. My father was being mourned in four houses. Mark was being mourned in one head, and I was taking the children to school.*

The first year was silent. Sunday described himself as busy and functional, with three items he identified later: his temper, his sleep, and his Saturdays. His temper, which his wife raised twice in that year, was short with his son and with his staff, which he attributed to the position's demands; his sleep was five hours, with the early waking that he described as *the hour in which I would think about everything at once*; and the Saturdays, which he had spent with Mark for eleven years — the workshop, the vehicle, the market — became unsettled, and he filled them with work and with an additional weekend engagement at the company.

The material arrived in the second year, triggered by a vehicle. Sunday was at a market and approached a man selling spare parts of the kind Mark had used, and he described the event precisely: *I stopped in front of the stall and I could not speak. I stood there for about a minute. The man thought something was wrong with me. I walked away and I sat in the car for twenty minutes and I cried properly for the first time, in a car park in the middle of the day, and I have not told anybody about the car park.*

The following six months produced three of the presentations in Section 30.3: the drinking, which had increased from occasional to four or five evenings a week; the presentation to his general practitioner with chest pain and palpitations, which was investigated and found to be without a cardiac cause; and the discharge into the household, which took the form of Sunday's sustained severity toward his son about arrangements and money, which he acknowledged in the case account as *not about my son*.

The work Sunday conducted, beginning in the third year, had five elements and he identified the order as important.

**The consultation.** Sunday attended a counselling arrangement through the hospital where the chest had been investigated. He identified the point of entry accurately: *I went about the chest. In the second appointment the counsellor asked me whether anybody close to me had died in the previous two years, and I said my father, and she waited, and I told her about Mark. She asked me why I had mentioned my father first. I said because he died first. She asked me how many days apart. I said twenty-three. She asked me how many people came to my father's funeral and how many came to Mark's. I did not answer her for a long time.*

**The account.** Over three sittings, Sunday wrote the record of Mark: what he was, the twenty-two years, the Saturdays, the eleven years of the workshop arrangement, what Sunday owed him, and what he had done in the three years since the death. The document included one item that he identified as the centre of the whole matter: *Mark had asked me, eight months before he died, to go with him to his hometown to see his mother, and I had said I would go in the following month, and in the following month the company's audit was held and I did not go, and I never met his mother and I did not go to the house after the funeral.*

**The visit.** In the fourth month of the work, Sunday travelled to Mark's hometown, met Mark's mother, and told her what Mark had been to him, and remained for two days. He described it as the most difficult and most useful act of the whole process: *I told her I had been going to come with Mark eight months before he died and that I did not come. She said he had told her and that she had been waiting to meet me. She is eighty-four. I now go twice a year and I take her provisions and I sit with her and she tells me what he was like as a boy. There is nobody else in the world who does that for me. My own father has four houses in which he is remembered. Mark has one old woman and me.*

**The date.** Sunday established the date of Mark's death in the household's calendar. He told Beatrice about Mark, the twenty-two years, the funeral that he had attended and returned from in three days, and the car park. The instruction he followed was that from Section 30.6: the disclosure does not require the content, and Sunday's form was: *Mark was my best friend for twenty-two years and I did not mourn him because there was no room for it in that month, and I have been carrying it since and it has been coming out in my temper and in my Saturdays. I am not all right about it and I am going to give it a day.* Beatrice's response, which he recorded: *she said she had known that something had been happening for two years and that she had thought it was a woman.*

**The conduct.** Sunday established two practices in Mark's name: the annual contribution to the education of two of Mark's grandchildren, arranged with Mark's daughter, and the maintenance of the workshop arrangement with Mark's nephew, which Sunday continued for the following four years as an arrangement rather than as a favour.

Sunday's summary, four years after the second death, was: *I organised my father's funeral for about two hundred people and I have been thanked for it by nearly all of them. I buried my friend in three days and nobody in my house knew his name. For two years I thought I was tired. The counsellor asked me how many days apart they died and I understood everything at once. There is no arrangement for a man whose friend dies. There is one now in my house.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The contrast between the two losses was total, and it was structural rather than emotional.** Two hundred mourners and one memorial service; nothing and nobody. This is the material of Section 30.1 in its clearest form, and the case is included because the difference is not a measure of the man's feeling but of the audience available to it: the father's death had arrangements and the friend's death did not.

**The man's competence at the first loss supplied the mechanism that delayed the second.** The competence was real, was required, and was acknowledged. It also occupied precisely the weeks in which he could have mourned Mark, and it supplied the household's account of the period in which his own loss did not appear. The case is included because the pattern is extremely common and is not a failure: the man did what the situation required and the material was left without arrangements.

**The material arrived in the body and in the temper.** The chest pain and the palpitations were investigated and found not to have a cardiac cause; the severity toward the son was acknowledged in the man's own account as unrelated to the son. These are the third and ninth presentations of Section 30.3 and the fourth and sixth from Section 30.2's perspective, and the case is included because the presentation is what brings such men into contact with a service, if anything does.

**The entry point was a question on a form.** The counsellor's question about a death in the previous two years, asked in a consultation about a chest symptom. The case is included because it demonstrates the point made in Chapter 27.6 and repeated in Section 30.7: the man's route into the material is generally through a presentation he is willing to make, and the arrangement's requirement is that the presenting complaint be accepted.

**The reconstruction was external and specific.** A visit to a mother in a hometown, two grandchildren's education, an arrangement with a nephew, a date in the year. The case is included because the reconstruction was not the expression of feeling, which the man found difficult, but the construction of arrangements through which the material had somewhere to go, and because its effect is described accurately in the man's own words: *there is no arrangement for a man whose friend dies; there is one now in my house.*
