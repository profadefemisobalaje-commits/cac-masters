# Building a Life That Does Not Depend on Pretending {.chapter num="42" short="Building a Life That Does Not Depend on Pretending"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define what pretending means in this book, and distinguish it from privacy, from discretion, and from the ordinary management of a man's account of himself.
2. Identify the six domains in which pretences are conducted, and locate his own in each.
3. Analyse the five costs of a life that depends on pretending, including the cost that is paid before anything is discovered.
4. Describe the four mechanisms by which a pretence is maintained, and the seven signs that it is failing.
5. Construct the supports of a life that does not depend on pretending, in the six domains.
6. Conduct the dismantling in the order that the practice material supports: the controlled disclosure, the reduction, the negotiation, and the reconstruction at the true level.
:::

## Introduction

The previous chapter examined the definition of manhood a man holds. This chapter examines the life he has built on it — and specifically the part of that life whose continuation depends on other people continuing to believe something that is not true.

The position is common enough that it must be stated precisely at the outset, because its boundary is not obvious and the reader's first response is generally to deny that the material applies to him.

**A man's privacy is not a pretence.** A man is entitled to conduct his own affairs without reporting them; the material of Chapters 18.6 and 20.6 established the distinction between the household's right to know and the man's general right to conduct his own matters. A man's discretion about his income, his savings, his dealings, his past, and his own interior is not the subject of this chapter.

**A man's ordinary presentation is not a pretence.** A man who is composed at a meeting when he is exhausted, who conducts a negotiation while his household's position is unsettled, who does not state his grief at a professional occasion, or who presents a confidence he does not wholly feel is conducting the ordinary management of his own conduct. This is the material of Chapter 39.4's definition and it is a competence.

**A pretence, in this chapter's sense, is a state of affairs in which the man's position can continue only for as long as specified parties do not learn specified facts.** The man's income, his debts, his employment, his competence, his marriage, his health, his household's arrangements, or his professional position is represented to be something other than what it is, and the representation's continuation is a **requirement** of his position rather than a choice about its presentation.

The distinction has a mechanical test, and it is the one the chapter supplies at the outset because it determines whether the reader can conduct the work: **a man is conducting a pretence where he is arranging his conduct so that a particular party does not learn a particular fact, and where his arrangements would require alteration if that party learned it.**

The chapter's concern is not the lie's morality. It is the structure: a life that requires specific parties to remain uninformed has a shape, and the shape has five costs. The cost that the case material establishes first, and the one that men report as the most significant, is the cost paid before anything is discovered: the constant vigilance that the position requires, the anticipation, the arrangements made to avoid the meeting of parties, the accounts not seen, the questions not answered, the telephone calls taken outside the room, and the specific exhaustion of a man who is conducting two accounts at once.

The chapter's approach has five commitments.

**The first is that the material is not a moral lecture.** The men in the case material did not build their positions out of vanity. They built them in the ordinary conduct of the obligations of Parts III to V: the school fees that had to be paid, the household that could not be told, the standing that was the instrument of the business, the occasion that could not be declined, the money that was borrowed to cover, and the covering that then required further borrowing.

**The second is that the outcome is not the exposure and its shame; it is the restoration of the man's position at its true level.** Section 42.5's material is constructive, and the case material's finding is consistent: the man's position, conducted at its true level, is generally more secure, cheaper to conduct, and more productive than the performance it replaced. The specific finding: **the performance has costs that the true level does not have** — the interest, the avoided arrangements, the occasions that must be conducted, the parties who must not meet, the hours of vigilance, and the deals made on false accounts whose terms the man cannot enforce.

**The third is that the disclosure is cheaper than the exposure.** The central mechanical finding of the chapter, and the one that supplies its urgency: a controlled disclosure, conducted by the man, at his own timing, in the order he chooses, with the arrangements he has prepared, costs the man a fraction of what an uncontrolled exposure costs. The case material records the difference consistently: the men whose positions collapsed in a discovery lost their standing, their relationships, and frequently their households; the men who conducted the disclosure lost the performance, which was the thing that had to end in either case, and retained their households' participation.

**The fourth is that the dismantling is conducted in an order.** The seven errors of Chapter 38.3 apply directly here, and the most consequential of them for this material is the attempt to restore the position immediately, generally by a further borrowing, which is the mechanism by which the pretence escalates into a collapse.

**The fifth is that the man's purpose is the substance and not the conduct of the work.** The chapter is placed in Part XII because its subject is the construction of a life; the reader's product is not the removal of a set of pretences but the establishment of arrangements that do not require them, and Section 42.5's six supports are supplied as the constructive material.
