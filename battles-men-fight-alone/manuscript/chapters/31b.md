## 31.1 What a Position of Authority Does to Relationships

The first section describes the mechanism. Three structural effects operate in every setting, and each has consequences that the man experiences as personal.

### Effect One: The Boundary of Rank

Authority establishes a difference between the man and the people he leads, and the difference is not a matter of personal attitude. It operates through six channels.

**The consequence of the man's words.** What the man says in a meeting, in a corridor, or in jest is received as a statement of direction, preference, or assessment. A remark that a colleague would treat as an opinion is received as a position; a suggestion is received as an instruction; an irritation is received as a warning. Men describe the discovery of this channel in almost identical terms: the moment they understood that they could no longer speak in the way they used to.

**The consequence of the man's mood.** A leader's mood propagates. His flatness in the morning is read as a problem, his silence as disapproval, his fatigue as an assessment of somebody's work. The leaders who understand this frequently adopt a deliberate constancy — composed, level, and unrevealing — and the constancy is a genuine requirement of the position. Its cost is that the man's actual state no longer appears anywhere.

**The consequence of intimacy.** Friendship with a subordinate is genuinely difficult, and the difficulty is not the leader's fastidiousness: it imposes on the subordinate a position in which he must manage both the relationship and the reporting line, and it imposes on the man's other subordinates the suspicion of favouritism, which the leader's own conduct cannot remove. The result is that the man's friendships are progressively confined to other people of comparable authority, and those people are generally his competitors or his equivalents in other organisations.

**The consequence of dependence.** The people around the man need things from him: approval, resources, a decision, a defence, a position, a word. The need is legitimate and it makes the relationship one-directional. Men describe this as the specific and slowly realised feature of the position: *I could not remember the last conversation I had had in which the other person did not want something from me.*

**The consequence of the man's own requirement.** The man in the position is aware of all of the above, and he manages it, which requires constant attention. The management is part of the work, and it consumes the capacity that would otherwise be available for the ordinary conduct of relationships.

**The consequence of the man's history.** A man in a position of authority has generally acquired it by a period in which he was assessed, evaluated, promoted over others, and required to demonstrate performance. Some of the people around him were passed over, and the history remains in the arrangement whether or not anybody mentions it.

### Effect Two: The Information Asymmetry

The man in the position receives information in a form that has been processed before it reaches him, and the processing is universal.

**What he is told is selected.** The subordinate reports what he judges to be relevant, what reflects well on him, what he has already addressed, and what he believes the man wishes to hear. The selection is generally not dishonest; it is the ordinary conduct of an intelligent person reporting upward.

**What he is not told is frequently the material.** The problem that has not been solved, the colleague who is struggling, the client who is dissatisfied, the mood in the team, the matter that will become a crisis in three months. Men describe the position accurately: *I am the last person in the building to know.*

**The man's own questions determine what arrives.** The organisation supplies what the man asks about, and the questions he asks are shaped by his own model of the work. A leader who is oriented to financial performance will be told about financial performance and not about morale; a leader who is oriented to output will be told about output and not about the fatigue behind it. The arrangement is self-reinforcing, and the leaders with the most accurate information are generally those who ask a wider set of questions deliberately.

**The man's own state is not reported to him by anybody.** The most consequential asymmetry. Nobody tells a leader that he has become short, that his attention is elsewhere, that he looks exhausted, that he has been less clear than usual, that his conduct at the last meeting was unusual. The information is available to everybody in the organisation and is withheld from the person it concerns, because the subordinate who reports it takes a risk and the peer who reports it incurs a cost.

### Effect Three: The Position as the Container of Other People's Anxiety

The third effect is the one that men report most heavily and describe least precisely, and it concerns the emotional function of the position.

In every group, the leader is the person to whom the group's uncertainty is brought. When the market moves, when the budget is cut, when a client leaves, when the future is unclear, the group's anxiety is delivered upward: to the man who is expected to know, to decide, and to hold. The function is performed whatever the man's state. A leader who is uncertain simply receives the anxiety and holds it, which produces the specific and well-documented condition that practitioners describe in leadership work.

The man's own anxiety is not delivered anywhere. It is added to the load rather than distributed, and the arrangements that would ordinarily receive it — the peer group, the supervisor, the spouse, the friend — are each affected by the position. His spouse requires his stability at home as much as the organisation does; his supervisor, where he has one, requires his performance; and his peers, as Effect One described, are generally his competitors.

::: keypoint
A position of authority has three structural effects on a man's relationships: the boundary of rank, through which his words, moods, intimacies, and needs acquire a weight determined by the hierarchy rather than by the relationship; the information asymmetry, through which he receives selected information and is not informed of the matter that most concerns him — his own state; and the position as the container of other people's anxiety, into which the group's uncertainty is delivered and from which nothing of the man's own is returned. None of the three is a personal failing, and all three require an arrangement rather than an adjustment of character.
:::

## 31.2 The Isolation of the Position

The previous section described the mechanism. This section describes the positions in which it produces the specific condition this chapter concerns: a man who is surrounded by people and has no peer, no confidant, and no arrangement in which he is not the leader.

### The Five Forms

**1. The man with nobody above him.** The proprietor, the founder, the chief executive, the commander, the bishop, the head of a household in which no adult has standing. He has no supervisor, so nobody has the authority to require an account of him, to question his judgement, or to tell him that he is wrong. The form's characteristic consequence is that the man's judgement is unbounded: there is no correction available, and the material of Chapter 27.3 on the damage isolation does to decision-making applies in its most complete form.

**2. The man whose nearest peer is his competitor.** The two people who hold equivalent positions in the same market, sector, or family. They can talk to one another, they generally do not, and the matters that most require discussion are the matters they cannot discuss.

**3. The man promoted from the group.** The man who was one of the team, who was promoted over his friends, and who must now lead them. He has lost the peer group and acquired a subordinate one. His friendships from before the promotion are not gone in name and they have changed in substance, and men describe a specific loneliness in this position that people outside it do not anticipate.

**4. The man in the small setting.** The leader of the small enterprise, the school principal, the officer of the association, the head of the department in a small town, the man who runs the family business. His position does not supply a leadership network, a professional community, or a peer group, and the handful of other leaders in his setting are frequently people with whom he has an interest in common: customers, contracts, political positions, or family matters.

**5. The man whose position is not recognised as leadership.** The man who leads by default rather than by appointment: the eldest brother, the man who holds the family's finances, the man who is consulted because he is the one who is competent, the man in the household who has become its decision-maker without a title. The position carries all three structural effects and none of its compensations, and this form is largely invisible, including to the man himself, who would not describe his position as leadership.

### The Four Things the Position Makes Impossible

**It makes complaint impossible in the ordinary direction.** The man cannot put his difficulty to the people he leads; the position forbids it, and where he attempts it the consequence is a loss of the confidence that the group requires in order to function. The prohibition is not a cultural prejudice; it is a functional requirement of the arrangement, and its consequence for the man is that a whole category of material has no audience.

**It makes uncertainty visible.** The man who expresses doubt about a decision that he has made is read as having changed his mind, and the organisation will begin to implement two directions at once. The man is therefore required to hold his uncertainties internally, which is exactly the material that requires a recipient.

**It makes friendship reciprocal with difficulty.** A relationship of equals requires two people who can supply one another. In the man's relationships with subordinates the material flows one way; with peers it flows through competition; with superiors it flows through performance. The man who has a genuine reciprocal friendship from before his position, or from outside his field, is the exception rather than the rule, and men who have one consistently identify it as the arrangement that has held.

**It makes the man's own state unavailable to him.** The fourth and most important impossibility. The man in the position does not receive the information about himself that everybody else receives, and he is generally the person about whom the least is known by the people closest to him in his working life. The condition this produces is described in the literature on leadership as a specific form of isolation, and its principal consequence in men's accounts is the loss of calibration: the man has no means of knowing whether his mood, his conduct, his workload, or his judgement has altered, and he therefore discovers the alteration only at the point at which it produces a consequence he cannot ignore.
