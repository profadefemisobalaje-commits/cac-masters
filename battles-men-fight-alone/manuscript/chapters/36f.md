## 36.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I have managed for thirty years. Why would I open this now?"**

Because the material is operating whether or not it is opened, and because the marks of that operation are generally available to the reader in his own conduct: the sleep, the temper, the avoidance, the drinking, and — the element men report most heavily on examination — the household's arrangements, which have been constructed around a state that the household cannot explain. The case material in this chapter and in Chapter 34 records the outcome of the work in men of this age and older, and the finding is consistent: the material is addressable, the arrangements are constructible, and the household's position changes.

**"What if the treatment makes it worse?"**

The concern is legitimate and the evidence addresses it: the treatments with the strongest record for traumatic material are conducted in a structured sequence in which the material is prepared for before it is addressed, and the preparation is the reason the treatment is effective. The risk that the material will worsen is associated with surfacing it without the arrangements, which is the reason the chapter places the stabilisation first and the reason it directs the traumatic material to a professional rather than to private work.

**"I do not want to talk about my childhood."**

Then the material of the childhood may be conducted in a form other than the narrative: the written account, the work with a professional who sets the pace, the structured treatments that address the man's present difficulties and his patterns rather than the childhood's narrative, and the arrangements of Chapter 33 where the material concerns obligations. The chapter's position is that the man's own account does not have to be delivered to anybody, but that the material's operation must have an arrangement.

**"My household has been living with this for years. Can I say anything now?"**

The material of Chapter 34.6 applies directly, including the four-part form and the handling of the recipient's response to the delay. The finding from the case material is that the household's account of the period is generally inaccurate and generally worse than the material, and that the disclosure is frequently received as an explanation the household has been waiting for. The man should also expect the household's own position to require its own arrangements, which is the material of Chapter 36's Condition Four.

**"Is my material serious enough for treatment?"**

The question is answered by Section 36.5's six conditions rather than by any comparison of the man's material with somebody else's. Where any of the six applies, the assessment is required; where the man is uncertain, the general practitioner is the route; and where the man's material is less severe than he had assumed, the assessment will have established that and supplied the arrangements that are appropriate.

**"How long does recovery take?"**

The first change in three to six months; the substantial change in one to three years; the man's own account of the whole, in retrospect, in decade terms. The finding that matters for the reader is the one at the end of Section 36.4: the first measure of progress is the existence of the arrangements rather than the state of the man, and a man who has established the recipient, the professional, the group, the account, and the household's arrangements has conducted the work's first stages regardless of how he feels in the month in which he reads this chapter.

::: summary
**Chapter 36 in brief.** Healing means that a wound has been given an arrangement in which it is held and that its operation in the man's life has been reduced. It does not mean that the wound is removed. The consequences of the definition are that the standard is achievable, that it is measurable in the man's sleep, temper, drinking, avoidance, judgement, and conduct toward his household, that it requires arrangements rather than resolve, and that it takes years while beginning within weeks.

The wounds men carry fall into ten categories: childhood, violence, humiliation, betrayal, loss, failure, the position, the household, the body, and the man's whole account of himself. The inventory's function is to identify what is still operating, which categories determine the work, the sequence in which the work proceeds, and what requires a professional. Seven misconceptions obstruct the work — that healing is forgetting, that time heals, that it requires a different kind of man, that it requires forgiveness, that a single breakthrough will supply it, that it requires the permanent reduction of the man's capacity, and that the man must be grateful for what the wound taught — and each sets a standard the work cannot meet. Five measures are supplied instead, in place of those standards.

Four conditions are necessary, and they are arrangements rather than attitudes: safety and stability, which comes first because material should not be surfaced without somewhere for it to go; the account, which must be described in a form the man can produce and available to at least one recipient, and in which the description of what occurred rather than of the man's feelings is the operative element; mourning and the conduct of what was lost, without which the material remains invested in an account in which the loss has not been counted; and reconnection, in which the man takes up something that requires him and that is not the material. The work proceeds in five stages — stabilisation, the account, mourning and repair, reconstruction, and reconnection and maintenance — and the stages are conducted in parallel as well as in sequence. The sequence is frequently reversed by circumstance, and the return to the first stage is normal and becomes shorter each time.

Professional treatment is required for six conditions: traumatic exposure with continuing symptoms, depression that has not lifted, anxiety and panic and the physical presentations, substance and behavioural dependence, the consequences of severe or prolonged adversity in childhood, and any material from the first category of Chapter 34.3, at any point. The treatments consist of structured talking treatments with an established evidence base, the specific trauma-focused treatments, medication where the practitioner recommends it, and the specialist services available for specific matters; and the chapter records, as its final note, that the conditions are ordinary, that they respond to treatment, and that the barriers men report are the barriers the arrangements of this part of the book are designed to address.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the inventory. Which category is operating most strongly, and where exactly is it operating — the hour, the setting, the person, the conduct?
2. Which of the seven misconceptions do you hold, in your own words? What standard have you been measuring yourself against?
3. Which of the four conditions of recovery is absent in your case, and what is the first arrangement that would supply it?
4. Where in your history is a conclusion about your own conduct that nobody has ever asked you to describe? Who could ask you the question, and what would the question be?
5. What is the household's account of your state, and what does it contain that is inaccurate? Which of the four parts of Chapter 34.6's statement would correct it?
6. Complete the plan in Part Four, with dates. Which arrangement is the one you are least willing to construct, and what does that indicate?
7. Write the three relapses in your own terms and the handling for each. What will you do on the day you do not wish to attend?

## Further Reading

- **On recovery from trauma:** the research and practice literature on traumatic exposure and its treatment, including the findings on the symptoms of post-traumatic stress in men, the evidence on the treatments with the strongest record, and the material on the sequence in which treatment is conducted.
- **On recovery from adverse childhood experiences:** the research on the long-term effects of adversity in childhood, including the findings on the conditions that follow, the evidence on treatment in adulthood, and the material on the factors that predict better outcomes.
- **On the process of recovery generally:** the literature on the process of change and of recovery in mental health, including the findings on the role of social connection, meaningful activity, and the person's own account, and the evidence on the stages and the durations of change.
- **On treatments and outcomes:** the evidence base for cognitive behavioural therapy, for trauma-focused treatments, for interpersonal treatments, and for medication in the conditions identified in Section 36.5, including the findings on effectiveness, on the working relationship, and on the factors that predict response.
- **Practical resources:** general practice and mental health services; specialist services for trauma, bereavement, addiction, and family matters; employee assistance and occupational health services; crisis lines; men's groups; and the material in Appendices B, C, K, M, Q, and R.
