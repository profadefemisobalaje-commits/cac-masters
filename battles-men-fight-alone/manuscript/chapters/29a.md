# Living With Regrets Nobody Knows About {.chapter num="29" short="Living With Regrets Nobody Knows About"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Distinguish regret from remorse, and explain why the distinction determines the correct treatment of each.
2. Identify the categories of regret that men carry privately, and assess which of them are repairable, partially repairable, or irreparable.
3. Explain the mechanisms by which an undisclosed regret continues to operate: rumination, the counterfactual loop, and the private standard that others do not know is in force.
4. Recognise the difference between regret that supplies a correction and regret that has become a punishment, and apply the criteria that separate them.
5. Construct a repair where repair is possible, in the form required by the material — acknowledgement, restitution, and the statement of what will be different.
6. Conduct the work required for an irreparable matter, including the position on self-punishment, the limits of what a man may legitimately require of himself, and the redirection of the material into conduct.
:::

## Introduction

There is a category of material that men carry privately in a form the previous chapters have not fully addressed. It is not a failure of provision, not a loss, and not a concealed weakness. It is a record of a specific past act or omission for which the man holds himself responsible and which he regards as something he should not have done.

This chapter concerns those regrets, and specifically the ones nobody knows about.

The distinction in the chapter's title matters. Almost every man's regrets include items that are known: the household knows about the business that failed, the family knows about the years of absence, the wife knows about the money that was lost. Those regrets are conducted in company, in whatever form the company provides, and their material is addressed in Parts III to VI. The regrets nobody knows about are a different class, because they have no audience and therefore no correction.

They are frequently not large events. In men's accounts they are unremarkable acts that occurred in a moment of pressure, tiredness, fear, or convenience and that the man has never described to anybody: the thing said in anger that a man's wife has been told about but nobody else has; the decision made at a critical moment in a way that failed a child, a brother, a friend, or a colleague; the money that was not repaid, the visit that was not made, the letter that was not sent, the chance that was not taken on somebody's behalf, the person who was not stood up for when standing up for him was possible; the dishonesty that was never discovered; the act of cowardice in a situation in which a young man believed he would have performed better; the treatment of somebody who could not answer back.

Three observations structure the chapter.

The first is that regret and remorse must be distinguished, and the distinction is not a matter of vocabulary. Regret concerns an outcome that a man wishes had been different. Remorse concerns conduct that the man judges to have been wrong, and it carries an obligation: something is owed. Men frequently treat one as the other, and both errors have costs. A man who treats an error of judgement, a decision that turned out badly, or an available choice he made under conditions he could not control as though it were a moral failure will carry an obligation that cannot be discharged, since nothing was owed. A man who treats a genuine wrong as an unfortunate outcome will never conduct the repair, and the material will remain in the form described in Section 29.3.

The second is that the correct response to a regret is determined by whether the matter can be repaired and not by how the man feels about it. This is the reason the chapter devotes a substantial section to the classification of regrets, and it is the practical core of the material: repairable matters are repaired, and irreparable matters are conducted in a manner that the chapter specifies precisely, because the alternatives — indefinite rumination or an artificial, cheap absolution — both fail.

The third is that a man is not required to be at peace. The chapter does not propose that the work will remove the material or that a properly conducted reckoning leaves nothing behind. Some regrets do not close. What the work supplies is that the material ceases to operate as a private punishment and is converted into conduct: a matter that is carried with accuracy, that contributes to what the man does, and that no longer consumes the attention it consumed before.
