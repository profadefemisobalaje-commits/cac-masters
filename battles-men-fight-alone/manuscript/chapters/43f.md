## 43.8 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Learned to Speak in Front of His Son

The case is composite and illustrative. It is constructed from the case material of this book and is presented as an example of the chapter's material rather than as a report of a particular person.

### The Presenting Event

Segun Alabi was forty-six years old and had been the production manager of a food-processing plant for nine years. He was married to Funke, a pharmacist, and he had two children: Damilare, thirteen, and Moyosore, nine.

The event that began the case was a telephone call from Damilare's form teacher. The teacher's account was specific. Damilare had stopped answering questions in class. His written work was unchanged and his marks had not fallen, which was the reason the school had not called earlier. He had withdrawn from the football team without stating a reason. He had been found, twice, sitting in the corridor during the break rather than in the yard. The teacher's question was whether anything had happened at home.

Segun's first response was the one the reader will recognise. He conducted a review of the household's recent months and found nothing: no bereavement, no financial difficulty, no illness, no dispute. He then conducted a review of Damilare's conduct and found a set of small changes that he had observed and had not registered: the boy had stopped asking to be taken to his cousin's house; he had begun going to bed early and sleeping badly; he had stopped reporting anything about school; and he had begun answering every question with the word *fine*.

The last observation produced the case's first difficulty, because Segun recognised the word. It was his own.

### The Household's History

Segun's father had been a quantity surveyor in a state ministry. In the year Segun was fifteen, his father had been implicated in a procurement irregularity that he had not committed, and had been suspended. The household had conducted the period in complete silence. Segun's father had left the house at the usual hour each morning and returned at the usual hour each evening, and he had not told his wife or his children what had happened. Segun had learned the facts eighteen months later, from a cousin.

His father had been reinstated, and had died six years afterwards of a stroke at the age of fifty-eight. At the funeral, the household's account of him was that he had been a strong man who had carried a great deal and had never complained. Segun had repeated the account for twenty years. In the week of the teacher's call, he found himself unable to repeat it without hearing the sentence differently: *he never complained, and nobody knew what he was carrying, and he died at fifty-eight.*

Segun's own conduct had reproduced the pattern with a precision that he had not examined. He had concealed a period of unemployment at thirty-two. He had concealed a hypertension diagnosis at forty-one for eight months. He had conducted the household's finances alone, on the principle that Funke should not be burdened. He had not told his wife about the suspension he had received at forty-four following a dispute with a supplier, and he had conducted the resulting reduction in his standing at the plant without stating it at home.

His account of these concealments, given later, was the one this book has recorded in Chapters 42 and 41: *I did not want to add to what they were carrying.* The account was sincere, and the case's finding is that it was also the mechanism by which his son had learned that a difficulty is a thing a man conducts alone.

### The First Attempts and Their Failure

Segun's first attempt followed the pattern that Section 43.4's practices describe. He told Damilare that he could talk to him about anything. He asked, at intervals, whether anything was wrong. He received the word *fine* on each occasion, and he interpreted the word as evidence that nothing was wrong.

The second attempt was an interrogation. He asked Damilare, on a Saturday morning, what had happened at school. The boy stated that nothing had happened. Segun stated that the teacher had called. The boy stated that the teacher was mistaken. Segun's response was an increase in volume, and the exchange ended with the boy leaving the room and Segun conducting a two-hour review of his son's ingratitude.

The third attempt was conducted by Funke, who spoke to Damilare for forty minutes and reported that he had said very little and had cried once and had asked her not to tell his father. The report produced the case's second difficulty, which was Segun's shame: his son had identified him as the party from whom the material had to be concealed.

The finding that the case records at this point is important and should be stated plainly. **Segun's three attempts were not failures of technique. They were the household's curriculum operating exactly as it had been transmitted.** The household's permission had been stated in words and contradicted by the father's conduct for thirteen years, and the boy had learned the conduct. No sentence that Segun could speak in a Saturday morning conversation was going to outweigh thirteen years of demonstration.

### The Turning Point

The turning point was not a conversation with Damilare. It was an event at the plant.

In the sixth month of the case, Segun approved a production change that resulted in a batch of approximately four hundred thousand naira of product being condemned. The error was his. He conducted the following two weeks in the pattern of Chapter 42: he prepared an account that attributed the loss to a supplier's specification, he delayed the report, and he conducted the plant's morning meetings with the vigilance that the position required.

On the fourteenth day, he stated the position to his production supervisor, in full, with the figure and with his own responsibility. The supervisor's response was not the response Segun had predicted. He stated that the loss was recoverable, that the specification would be changed, and that he was relieved to have the accurate position. He then asked Segun a question that Segun had not anticipated: *why did you wait two weeks?*

Segun's answer, given in the supervisor's office, was the case's turning point. He said: *because I did not know how to say that I had got it wrong without it meaning that I was finished.*

He went home that evening and conducted the same statement in his household. He stated the error, the figure, and his responsibility, in the presence of Funke and both children. He stated that he had concealed it for two weeks and that he had been afraid. He stated that he had been wrong to conceal it, and he stated what he had done to correct it.

The statement took four minutes. The case's record of the household's response is specific. Funke asked two questions about the correction and then stated that she was glad he had told her. Moyosore, who was nine, asked whether they would have to leave the house, and was told that they would not. Damilare said nothing during the statement and, at the end, asked whether his father had been afraid of losing his job. Segun said yes. The boy nodded and left the room.

The case's finding is that the four minutes did more than the three preceding months of attempts. The finding is consistent with Section 43.1's first channel: the boy had observed his father conduct a difficulty, state it, repair it, and survive it, and the observation was the curriculum.

### The Programme

Segun conducted a programme over the following nine months. It is described here in the form in which the case material records it, because the reader will find it more useful than a set of principles.

**The first component was the household's ten minutes.** The household began a practice of ten minutes on Sunday evening, after the evening meal, in which each member stated one thing about the week that had been difficult and one thing that had been good. The rule was that no member's statement was to be commented on, advised on, or reported outside the household. The practice was conducted for nine months, and it was missed on four occasions.

**The second component was the vocabulary.** Segun and Funke conducted a deliberate change in the household's language. The word *fine* was replaced, where it was inaccurate, with a more precise word, and the parents conducted the replacement in their own statements first. The household's working list, written on a card in the kitchen, contained eleven words: tired, worried, ashamed, disappointed, angry, frightened, lonely, relieved, embarrassed, overwhelmed, and sad.

**The third component was the father's own practice.** Segun conducted three statements of his own difficulty in the nine months, each of them brief and proportionate. The first was the statement of the plant's error. The second was a statement, in the fifth month, that he had been to the clinic and that his blood pressure was being treated. The third was a statement, in the eighth month, that he had been afraid that Damilare's withdrawal was his own fault. The third statement was the one that the case material records as the most significant, because it supplied the boy with the sentence he had not been able to supply for himself: *my father thinks it might be his fault, and he has said so.*

**The fourth component was the named person.** Segun identified a party outside the household to whom Damilare could speak: a family friend, Mr. Nwachukwu, a widower of sixty-one who repaired radios in a shop near the school and who had known Segun since his own apprenticeship. The arrangement was stated to Damilare in the language of Section 43.6's script, and Damilare used it in the fourth month. The case material does not record what was said in the shop, and it records that the boy returned from the shop on four further occasions.

**The fifth component was the school.** Segun met the form teacher and the school's guidance counsellor, stated the position accurately, and asked for two things: that Damilare should be given a named adult in the school, and that the school should not conduct any arrangement that identified him to his peers as a case. The school supplied both. The teacher's report in the seventh month recorded that Damilare had begun answering questions again, and that his answers were shorter than before and accurate.

**The sixth component was the setting.** Segun enrolled Damilare in a Saturday workshop at the plant, where he spent three hours each week with the maintenance team under a supervisor named Mr. Bello. The choice was deliberate. The workshop supplied the boy with a setting in which competence was demonstrated, error was corrected, and a limit could be admitted, and it supplied him with three young men between eighteen and twenty-two whose ordinary conversation was less governed by the propositions of Section 43.4 than his school's peer group. The case material records that Damilare's first statement of a difficulty to his father, in the seventh month, concerned a mistake he had made at the workshop and had been afraid to report. Segun's response was the one he had rehearsed: *thank you for telling me; what would help?*

### The Outcome and Its Measure

The case's outcome is stated with the restraint that the material requires. Damilare did not become a boy who spoke about everything. He became a boy who could state a difficulty to two parties, and who had begun to distinguish between the states that he had previously conducted as *fine*.

The case material records four measures.

The first is the household's own count: in the nine months, Damilare made eleven statements of difficulty to his parents, of which four concerned school, three concerned the workshop, two concerned a peer, and two concerned his own states without an external cause. The count before the programme was zero.

The second is the school's report: attendance at the football team resumed in the eighth month, and the teacher's report recorded that the boy had begun to ask questions in class.

The third is the boy's own statement, recorded at the end of the nine months: *I used to think that if I said something, it would make it worse for everybody. Now I say it and it is usually not as bad as I thought.*

The fourth is the father's measure, which the case material treats as the most significant. Segun recorded, at the end of the nine months, that he had conducted three disclosures of his own that he would not have conducted before the case; that he had begun treatment for his blood pressure; that Funke had become a party to the household's accounts; and that he had begun to conduct the plant's morning meeting differently, in a specific and observable respect: he had begun to state his own errors at the meeting rather than conducting them privately.

The case's finding is that the father's change was the programme's principal component, and that the components which the reader will find most attractive — the vocabulary card, the Sunday ten minutes, the workshop — were effective because they were conducted by a man whose own conduct had changed. The finding is the chapter's central claim in its case form.

### The Relapse

The case material records a relapse in the fourteenth month, and it is included because a case that records only the improvement is not useful to the reader.

In the fourteenth month, Segun's plant conducted a restructuring, and his position was changed. He conducted the change in the pattern of Chapter 42 for eleven days: he stated nothing at home, he conducted the household's arrangements from the previous position, and he slept badly. On the twelfth day, Funke asked him what was wrong, and he stated that nothing was wrong.

Damilare, who was then fourteen, stated at the Sunday ten minutes that his father had been *doing the thing he said he used to do*. The statement was made without accusation, in the household's ordinary form, and it produced the case's most important exchange. Segun stated that the boy was right, that he had been conducting the change alone, and that he had been afraid. The household then conducted the change as a household: Funke stated the household's expenditure, Segun stated the new income, and the three of them conducted a set of reductions over the following month.

The case's finding on the relapse is stated as the chapter's closing claim about transmission. **The boy had learned the curriculum well enough to identify its interruption in his father, and the household had learned it well enough to conduct the interruption as a household rather than as a failure.** The pattern was not abolished. It was made visible, and the visibility was the change.

## 43.9 The Programme

The section supplies the programme in a form that the reader can conduct, in three settings: the household, the school or workshop, and the community. It is deliberately small, because Section 43.7's finding is that the ordinary arrangement is more effective than the extraordinary one.

### The Household Programme

| Element | Conduct | Frequency | Measure |
|:------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:--------------|:------------------------------------------|
| The ten minutes | Each member states one difficulty and one good thing; no advice, no reporting | Weekly | Whether it was conducted; the number of members who spoke |
| The vocabulary | The household's card of state words, used by the adults first | Daily | The number of precise words used in a week; the reduction of *fine* |
| The adult's statement | One proportionate statement of the adult's own difficulty, followed by action | Monthly | The statement made; the action taken |
| The repair | The adult's acknowledgement of a specific act, its effect, and the repair | As required | The repair completed; the boy's observation of it |
| The named person | One adult outside the household, stated and available | Stated quarterly | Whether the boy can name the person; whether he has used the arrangement |
| The rehearsal | One competence rehearsed in low stakes | Weekly | The competence conducted without prompting |
| The application | One competence conducted in real stakes, with the adult available | Monthly | The boy's report of what happened |

### The School and Workshop Programme

The institutional programme has five elements, and each one is attached to a role rather than to a person.

**The first is the named adult.** Every boy should be able to name an adult in the institution. The role is stated in the institution's ordinary communication, the adult is trained, and the arrangement is reviewed each term.

**The second is the classroom's response to error.** The institution should state and conduct a standard: error is information, it is corrected, and it is not punished by ridicule. The standard is conducted by the teachers' own practice, and it is measured by the boys' willingness to attempt.

**The third is the curriculum's ordinary inclusion of difficulty.** Each subject supplies one occasion per term on which the private cost of a public matter is examined: the scientist's failure, the character's fear, the statesman's grief, the engineer's correction. The occasion is not a lesson on emotions. It is a lesson in the subject that includes the material.

**The fourth is the setting.** The institution should supply at least one setting in which a boy's capacities are useful and his standing is not conducted by dominance: the workshop, the team, the choir, the committee, the repair group.

**The fifth is the disclosure's conduct.** The institution should have a stated process for a disclosure: the named person, the confidentiality statement, the professional route, the follow-through, and the review. The process should be tested once a year with a scenario, in the way that a fire procedure is tested.

### The Community Programme

The community programme is the six principles of Section 43.7 attached to the five institutions, and it is conducted in the following order.

**The first step is the audit.** The reader should establish which of the five institutions in his community already possess the five features of Section 43.3's closing material: a vocabulary, a permission, a response, an observed consequence, and a repetition. The audit is conducted by asking five questions of each institution, and it takes an afternoon.

**The second step is the named person.** The cheapest and most effective intervention in any institution is the appointment of a trained, known, available person. The reader should establish whether such a person exists and, where he does not, propose the role at the institution's next ordinary meeting.

**The third step is the leader's disclosure.** The reader should identify one respected person in the institution who is willing to conduct a proportionate disclosure followed by an action. The disclosure need not be dramatic and should not be conducted as a testimony.

**The fourth step is the routine.** The institution should adopt one recurring arrangement: ten minutes at the meeting, a termly assembly, a weekly conversation at the workshop. The arrangement should be attached to the agenda rather than to a person.

**The fifth step is the review.** The institution should review the arrangement annually, record what it did after a disclosure, and adjust. The review is the feature that distinguishes the arrangement from a gesture.

### The Measures

The programme's measures are stated because the reader will otherwise evaluate it by his impression, and the case material's finding is that impressions are unreliable in this material.

**Measure One: The boy's vocabulary.** The number of precise state words the boy uses without prompting, recorded over a week.

**Measure Two: The boy's statements.** The number of statements of difficulty the boy makes to a party who has a right to know, recorded over a month, together with the party and the response.

**Measure Three: The boy's askings.** The number of specific requests for an instrument, recorded over a month.

**Measure Four: The boy's repairs.** The number of occasions on which the boy acknowledges an act, states its effect, and conducts a repair.

**Measure Five: The adult's own conduct.** The number of the adult's own statements, repairs, and askings conducted in the boy's observation, recorded over a month. The measure is the programme's leading indicator, and the case material's finding is that the other four follow it.

**Exercise 43: The Curriculum Audit and the Thirty-Day Conduct Plan**

The exercise has four parts. It should be conducted in writing, and it should be conducted over two sittings rather than one.

**Part One: The boys in your observation.** List every boy and young man between the ages of five and twenty-two whose conduct you can observe: sons, nephews, students, apprentices, neighbours, members of your association, younger colleagues. For each, record four items: his age; the adult he would go to if he were in difficulty, if you know it; the last occasion on which you observed him state a difficulty; and the channel of Section 43.1 through which you have most influence on him. Where you cannot complete an item, record the fact that you cannot.

**Part Two: The audit of one institution.** Choose one institution in which you have standing: your household, your school, your workshop, your association, your team. Answer the five questions of Section 43.3's closing material, with evidence rather than impression. (1) What vocabulary does the institution supply? (2) What permission does it state, in whose name, and in what words? (3) What was the response to the last disclosure, and what did it teach? (4) What consequence can a boy observe in the institution: a repair, a disclosure, an admission of a limit? (5) Where is the repetition, and how often does it occur? Record a score of zero to five, and record the weakest feature.

**Part Three: The adult's own audit.** Record, for the last thirty days: the number of occasions on which you stated a difficulty to a party who had a right to know; the number of occasions on which you conducted a repair; the number of occasions on which you asked for a specific instrument; the number of occasions on which you admitted a limit in a boy's observation; and the number of occasions on which you said *fine* when it was inaccurate. The counts are the curriculum your boys are receiving.

**Part Four: The thirty-day plan.** Write four commitments, each with a date, a party, and a measure. (1) The statement: one proportionate statement of your own difficulty, in the presence of a named boy, followed by an action. (2) The permission: the statement of availability to one boy, in the language of Section 43.6's script, with the confidentiality statement. (3) The rehearsal: one competence rehearsed with a boy in low stakes, at a stated time. (4) The institution: one proposal to one institution, at its next ordinary meeting, for a named person or a recurring arrangement. At the end of thirty days, record what was conducted, what was not, and what the boy did in response. Bring the record to the next thirty days.
