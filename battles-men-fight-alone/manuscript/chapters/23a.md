# The Secret Struggle With Addiction {.chapter num="23" short="The Secret Struggle With Addiction"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define addiction in behavioural and physiological terms, and distinguish between dependence, harmful use, and habits that are not addictive.
2. Identify the substances and behaviours to which men most commonly become addicted, and recognise the specific features of each.
3. Explain why addiction is concealed, and describe the progression from private use to private dependence.
4. Describe the effects of addiction on the man, his household, his work, and his finances, including the effects that are not visible from outside.
5. Evaluate the routes into treatment, including self-help, professional treatment, medical assistance, and residential programmes, and identify the appropriate route for a given situation.
6. Apply a structured method for assessing his own use, and use the exercise at the end of this chapter to construct a plan.

## Introduction

The subject of this chapter is the most thoroughly concealed of all the struggles in this book, and the concealment is the reason it is included in a book about men who fight their battles alone. A man with a drinking problem, a gambling problem, a dependence on a substance, or a compulsive behavioural pattern is frequently the last person in his household to acknowledge it, and the concealment is maintained by a set of mechanisms that are entirely rational from the inside: the fear of losing the standing on which everything depends, the fear of the household's reaction, the belief that the matter can be managed without assistance, and the specific and powerful belief that disclosure would make the problem permanent by making it official.

This chapter is written with two audiences in mind, and it addresses them differently.

The first audience is the man who is concerned about his own use. What is offered to him is a set of specific assessments, an explanation of the progression by which ordinary use becomes dependence, an account of the treatment routes with an honest description of what each involves, and the practical guidance for the period between the recognition and the treatment, which is frequently the most dangerous period of the entire process.

The second audience is the man who is not the person with the condition: the man whose brother, son, colleague, or friend is using heavily, whose wife has a dependence, or who occupies a responsible position — a manager, a head of a family, a professional — in which the situation has to be handled. For him, what is offered is a set of principles for intervention, an explanation of the effects that other people's intervention reliably produces, and the guidance on the boundaries that a person living with or managing an addicted man must establish.

Two general points should be stated at the outset, because they determine everything else in this chapter.

**Addiction is a condition, not a failure of character.** This is now established in the clinical and research literature with respect to both substances and behaviours. It has a physiological component, a psychological component, and a social component; it responds to treatment; and it is associated with relapse in a substantial proportion of cases, in the same manner as diabetes and hypertension, whose management is also imperfect and whose relapse is also not treated as proof that the patient is a bad person.

**Disclosure is the pivot.** Almost every route out of an addiction runs through a conversation that the man has not been able to hold. The reason is described in the sections that follow: the condition is sustained by isolation, and isolation is maintained by shame. The chapter's practical objective is therefore specific: to make that conversation possible and to identify where it should be directed.

## 23.1 Understanding Addiction

The word is used loosely in ordinary conversation to describe any strong enthusiasm or any habit, and the looseness makes the subject harder to think about. A precise set of terms is therefore necessary.

### The Three Levels

**Harmful use.** Use that produces damage to health, relationships, work, or finances, without the features of dependence. The person retains control of the quantity and the frequency, and the damage is the reason the pattern is a problem. The appropriate response is a reduction in use, obtained by the methods described in the previous chapter.

**Dependence.** A specific clinical pattern with a defined set of features. Its core elements are: a compulsion to use; difficulty in controlling the quantity or duration; a physical withdrawal state when use stops; tolerance, where increasing quantities are required; the neglect of other interests and obligations; and continued use despite clear evidence of harm. Dependence is a condition, and its management is a clinical matter.

**Addiction in the behavioural sense.** The same pattern applied to behaviour rather than to a substance: gambling, sexual conduct, gaming, internet use, shopping, work. The clinical literature on behavioural addictions has developed substantially, and the pattern for gambling in particular is established as a condition with mechanisms closely comparable to those of substance dependence.

### The Mechanism, in Ordinary Language

Four features of the mechanism are worth setting out, because men frequently reject treatment on the grounds that they should be able to manage the matter themselves, and the rejection is based on a misunderstanding of what is happening.

**The reward pathway is being directly engaged.** Substances and certain behaviours produce their effects through the brain's reward system, and repeated engagement produces a change in the system itself: the same quantity produces less effect, and the system's baseline state changes so that ordinary activity produces less pleasure and the substance or behaviour becomes the principal source of it.

**Tolerance and withdrawal create their own logic.** Once dependence has developed, the substance is required not to produce pleasure but to restore a tolerable state. The man is no longer using in order to feel good; he is using in order to feel normal, and the distinction is frequently the first thing that a man recognises when the pattern is explained to him.

**The triggers are learned.** The situations associated with use — the time of day, the place, the company, the emotional state, the argument — acquire the capacity to produce the urge by themselves. This is why the environment and the situation are part of the treatment rather than incidental to it.

**The self-management is impaired.** A central feature of dependence is that the capacity to govern one's own use is precisely what the condition damages. This is the reason that the instruction to exercise self-control, which is the standard response of families and employers, fails at the point where it is most needed: the capacity has been affected by the condition the instruction is intended to correct.

### What Addiction Is Not

**It is not a moral failure, and describing it as one does not help.** The moral account has a specific and measurable cost: it produces concealment, and concealment is the principal reason that men do not obtain treatment.

**It is not a permanent identity.** A man who has been dependent is not defined by it, and the increasing social acceptance of treatment has made the disclosure of recovery substantially less costly than the fear suggests.

**It is not, in most cases, immediate.** The progression from ordinary use to dependence occurs over a period whose median length is measured in years rather than months, through a sequence of stages: use for a purpose, use in a pattern, use with increasing frequency, use with consequences, use with concealment, and use as a requirement. This matters practically because it means that the earlier stages are identifiable, and because the man who recognises his own position in the sequence at stage three faces a much cheaper intervention than the man who reaches stage six.
