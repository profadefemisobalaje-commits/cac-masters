## 39.1 The False Choice

The choice offered to men is stated in many forms, and its structure is the same: **the man may be strong or he may be warm, and the two are alternatives.**

### The Eight Forms of the Offer

1. *A man who cries in front of his wife will not be respected in his own house.*
2. *You cannot carry that position and be soft about it.*
3. *Feelings are for women; men deal with facts.*
4. *If you let them see that it affects you, they will use it.*
5. *A strong man does not complain.*
6. *Do not bring your work home and do not take your feelings to work.*
7. *He is too emotional for this role.*
8. *He has become a different man since the loss — he used to be dependable.*

The offers are delivered by the man's environment, generally with goodwill; they are frequently supported by the man's own experience; and they are supported by the consequences described in the previous chapters of this book, in which the disclosure of material did carry costs and in which the composed exterior did preserve the man's position.

### The Three Errors of the Offer

**The first error: it treats the choice as a matter of degree rather than of kind.** The offer assumes that strength and warmth lie at the two ends of a single scale, and that a man's movement toward one is his movement away from the other. The position is inaccurate, and the research on emotional competence identifies the pattern that the offer obscures: the capacity to register, to regulate, and to express states appropriately, and the capacity to act reliably under pressure, are separate competences. They are associated with each other in most people, and the man who loses one does not thereby acquire the other; he loses both, and the strength he has constructed is a specific and narrow competence — the management of his own conduct under pressure — purchased at the cost of the others.

**The second error: it treats the composed exterior as strength rather than as an arrangement.** The composed exterior is a technique. It performs the function of protecting the man's position and his standing, and it is genuinely effective for that purpose. The offer converts the technique into an identity: what was a conduct in a setting becomes a description of the man, and the arrangement is then maintained in settings in which it has no function — including the marriage, the household, the friendship, and the man's own account of himself.

**The third error: it omits the costs.** The offer is stated as a trade of warmth for strength, and the trade's actual terms are different. What is surrendered is the capacity to register states, in himself and in other people; the capacity to regulate; the capacity to be known; the capacity to perceive what is happening in the settings where he is responsible; and, in the case material, the capacity to know what he wants. The position is not a trade of a small good for a large one. It is a trade of a large good for the appearance of one.

### The Evidence for the Position Between the Two

The chapter establishes the position with three findings, and each is available to the reader in the material of this book.

**The research on men's emotional competence and its outcomes.** The literature on emotional awareness and regulation reports consistent associations with mental health, relationship satisfaction, occupational effectiveness, and physical health, and the associations are in the direction of the higher competence producing better outcomes in each. The competence is not opposed to strength; the men with the highest measured capacity for reliability under pressure are not the men with the lowest emotional awareness.

**The material on men's relationships.** The findings in Chapters 26 and 27 establish that the disclosure function is the strongest single predictor of men's resilience, and that the men who lack it are the men who cannot be reached. The composed exterior does not produce the arrangements that protect men; it removes them.

**The case material of this book.** The men described in these chapters who are functioning well in their households are, in the accounts of their own families, men who are both reliable and known. The reader who considers his own household will find the same finding in his own material: what the household requires of him is not warmth instead of strength and not strength instead of warmth, but his presence as a person in the arrangements he is conducting.

### The Third Position

The chapter's subject is a third position, and it is not the midpoint of the scale. Its description, in the terms the case material supports:

**A man who can register what he feels, who can regulate it, who can state it in the settings in which stating it is useful, who can hold it privately where holding it is required, and who is fully available to the people who are entitled to him — while acting reliably, making decisions, and carrying what he carries.**

The position is unremarkable, it is held by a substantial proportion of men, and its components can be described and practised. The chapter's task is their description.

::: keypoint
Men are offered a false choice: that a man may be strong or he may be warm. The offer treats the two as ends of a single scale, converts a technique of conduct into an identity, and omits the costs of the trade. The position between the two is a third thing rather than a compromise, and it is composed of competences that can be described: registering what one feels, regulating it, stating it where stating it is useful, holding it where holding it is required, and remaining fully available to the people who are entitled to one — while continuing to act, decide, and carry.

## 39.2 How a Man Becomes Cold

The process is consistent across the case material, and it has six stages. The reader should locate his own position before conducting Section 39.5's practices, because the practices that apply at the third stage are not the practices that apply at the sixth.

### The Six Stages

**Stage one: the man's states are met with a consequence.** The childhood in which the expression of fear, grief, or need was met with ridicule, punishment, or the parent's inability to hold it; a household in which the man's father was cold; an environment in which boys who showed distress were treated as weak. The stage's finding is the man's first lesson: the expression of a state produces a worse outcome than its concealment.

**Stage two: the man acquires the technique.** He learns to present the composed exterior: at school, at the training, in the trade, in the household, in the profession. The technique is effective and it is rewarded, and the stage's finding is that the man's competence increases and his standing improves. The reader should note that this stage is the one at which the arrangement is deliberate and effective, and that the process's problem is not the technique but its extension.

**Stage three: the technique is extended to the settings in which it has no function.** The marriage, the friendship, the household, the man's own relationship with himself. The extension generally occurs without a decision: the man's conduct in the settings that require the technique is the conduct he has, and no occasion arises at which he is required to distinguish. The stage's marker is the man's own report in later years: *I could not switch it off. I did not know that it was on.*

**Stage four: the man stops registering the states.** This is the point at which the process becomes self-maintaining, and it is the stage the chapter's practices primarily address. The man who has not expressed a state for years ceases to identify it accurately: he reports tiredness instead of grief, irritation instead of fear, busyness instead of avoidance, and *nothing* instead of a state that he cannot name. The finding is documented in the research on the effects of the suppression of emotional experience, and men describe the stage in the material's own terms: *I did not know what I was feeling. I knew what I had to do.*

**Stage five: the man's states are discharged elsewhere.** The material of Chapters 29 to 34: the temper at home, the drinking, the risk, the work, the silence, the severity about small matters, the withdrawal. The stage's specific feature is that the man's states have not been removed; they have been given a different outlet, and the outlet is generally the household, because the household is the setting in which the technique is not required and in which the consequence is the cheapest.

**Stage six: the man becomes the exterior.** The final stage, and the one described in Chapters 24 and 37: the account of himself is the position, the technique, the function, and the provision. Nothing remains outside it; the interests, the play, the softness, and the connections have been set down, and the man's own state is available to him only in the negative — the flatness, the exhaustion, and the specific conclusion about his life that arrives in the early hours.

### The Four Mechanisms

The process is driven by four mechanisms, and each should be identified separately.

**The immediate relief of the numbing.** The suppression of a state produces the removal of the state's discomfort, and the removal is immediate. The mechanism is the strongest of the four and it is the same mechanism as Chapter 22's: the arrangement is reinforced by the relief it supplies in the moment, and the arrangement's costs are paid later and in other currencies. The research on numbing identifies its central finding: **the numbing does not select.** The man who arranges not to feel the grief, the shame, or the fear arranges also not to feel the satisfaction, the tenderness, the pleasure, and the connection, since the arrangement operates on the man's capacity to register states and not on a list of them.

**The environment's reinforcement.** The composed exterior is rewarded in the settings in which it is required, as Section 39.1 established, and the reward is consistent. The mechanism's specific feature is that the environment cannot see the extension of the technique into the household, and so it cannot correct it.

**The absence of an occasion.** The fourth stage proceeds without a decision, because a man is not ordinarily required to distinguish between the settings in which the technique is required and the settings in which it is not. The absence of the occasion is the reason that a man's coldness is frequently discovered in a specific event: the wife's statement, the child's withdrawal, the bereavement that the man cannot respond to, or his own conclusion in the early hours.

**The example of the man's own father.** The inherited form: the son of a cold man becomes a cold man, generally with the same absence of a decision and frequently with a greater determination to provide what the father provided. The mechanism is the material of Chapter 32.1's fourth source, and the reader should identify it explicitly, because the finding from the case material is that the pattern's interruption requires it to be named as the father's pattern rather than accepted as the man's nature.
