## 9.7 Illustrative Case: The Man With Two Bank Accounts

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in financial counselling, family mediation, and consumer credit services.

**Bello is forty-five and works as a senior officer in a state agency.** He has been married for sixteen years, has four children, and is widely regarded as a careful man. He keeps two accounts. His wife, Halima, knows about one of them.

The second account exists because of an obligation that Bello has never discussed. Nine years ago, his younger sister's husband died suddenly, leaving her with three children and a house that was unfinished. The family agreed that Bello, as the eldest surviving son, would take responsibility for completing the house and supporting the children's education. He accepted, as everyone expected him to, and has done so since then.

The obligation is not in itself the problem. Bello's salary has risen over the years, and the support has been manageable in most years. The difficulty arose four years ago, when his employer undertook a restructuring and his monthly allowances — a substantial part of his real income — were reduced and then, in the second year, stopped entirely for eight months. His base salary continued. The support did not stop, because stopping it would have produced a conversation that Bello was not prepared to have with his sister, his mother, and the extended family.

To bridge the gap, Bello took a loan from a cooperative society at a rate of four per cent per month. When that loan's servicing began to exceed his capacity, he took a second loan from a loan application service with a higher rate, and used part of it to service the first. He also, in the third year, delayed payment to two of his own creditors — a school and a hospital — in order to maintain the family support.

Three things happened in the fourth year. His sleep deteriorated, and he began to wake at three and spend an hour doing arithmetic. He became sharp with his children, particularly the eldest, whose university fees were due. And he stopped attending the monthly meeting of the family's development association, offering the cost of transport as a reason; the real reason was that at the last meeting he had been unable to contribute at the expected level and had been asked, in front of others, whether things were difficult.

He told nobody. Halima knew that something was wrong — she described later that he had become *a man who was present but not there* — and asked twice. On both occasions he answered that the pressure at work was heavy and that she should not worry.

The disclosure came about through bureaucracy rather than crisis. A loan application required a guarantor who was a civil servant, and Bello asked a colleague. The colleague, who had known him for eleven years, asked a single question: *Bello, how many of these are you servicing?* Bello told him. The colleague said he would guarantee the loan on one condition: that Bello tell his wife, that same week.

The conversation with Halima lasted three hours and was, according to both, the worst evening of their marriage. What emerged was not the debt, which was large but manageable once restructured through a staff cooperative at a lower rate, but the nine years of silence, which Halima experienced as a verdict about her place in the marriage.

The financial restructuring took eight months. The loan balances were consolidated, the interest cost was substantially reduced, the family support was renegotiated — with some difficulty and some loss of face, and with the sister's surprising and immediate acceptance when the situation was explained — and the household standard was reduced in three named categories. The relational repair took considerably longer, and its central element was a weekly conversation, at a fixed time, in which Bello reported the position: what had been paid, what remained, and what had changed.

Two years later, in a review with the cooperative officer who had helped with the restructuring, Bello described what he had learned in a sentence that is worth recording: *The debt was never the problem. The debt was arithmetic. What I did to my wife was keep her outside the most important thing in our family for nine years, and tell her that the reason was that she should not worry.*
:::

The case illustrates the chapter, and four features are worth isolating.

**The obligation was honourable, and it was not disclosed.** Bello's decision to support his sister's household was consistent with his responsibilities and would have been defended by anybody who knew the circumstances. The difficulty was not the commitment; it was the decision to carry it as a private matter of honour rather than a family decision with a budget attached. This is the most common configuration of concealed financial difficulty in extended-family systems, and it is the subject of Chapter 10.

**The immediate trigger was external, and the response was a loan at a punitive rate.** The reduction of allowances was not in Bello's control. His response to it — bridging the gap with high-cost credit rather than renegotiating the support obligation — was the point at which a manageable shortfall became a structural problem.

**The symptoms were present and were attributed elsewhere.** Broken sleep, irritability with the eldest child, withdrawal from the association, and the characteristic experience of being *present but not there*. He attributed all of them to work, and his wife was given the same explanation twice.

**The disclosure came through a question about a specific practice rather than an invitation to talk.** The colleague asked how many loans were being serviced, having inferred the position from the request for a guarantor. This is the same mechanism that appeared in the cases in Chapters 5 and 8, and it is the reason this book repeatedly recommends that men learn to ask direct questions about observable behaviour rather than general questions about feelings.

## 9.8 Practical Exercise: The Position Paper and the Disclosure Plan

This exercise takes approximately two hours, and it produces two documents. Both should be written, kept, and updated monthly. Readers who find the exercise emotionally difficult should note that this difficulty is itself evidence of the material's weight, and that Appendix D provides a structured alternative if the free-form version is too much to complete at once.

### Document One: The Position Paper

Complete each section, using figures rather than estimates wherever you can obtain them.

**Section A — Income.** All sources, with amounts, frequency, and reliability. Mark each as secure, variable, or at risk.

**Section B — Obligations.** For each: the creditor, the amount outstanding, the rate, the monthly payment, the security, and the consequence of default. Include obligations that nobody else knows about. This is the section men most often omit, and it is the section that determines whether the plan works.

**Section C — Assets.** For each: the asset, its realistic sale value, and whether it produces income. Mark the productive assets distinctly.

**Section D — The Arithmetic.** Total monthly income, total monthly obligations, the difference, and the number of months for which the current position can be sustained. Write the number down. It is the most useful figure in the document.

**Section E — The Reductions.** Three lists: immediate, negotiated, structural. Against each item, the amount saved and the date on which it will take effect.

### Document Two: The Disclosure Plan

**Part One — Whom to tell.** Choose one person. Where there is a spouse or partner, the plan should include her, although it may be appropriate to speak to a professional adviser first in order to have the facts in order. Where there is no partner, choose the person with capacity, discretion, and no leverage over you.

**Part Two — What to say.** Draft the account in six parts, following Section 9.5: introduction, position, amount, plan, what you are already doing, and the fallback. Write it out in full. The most important of the six is *what you are already doing*, and it should be completed before the conversation, not promised in it.

**Part Three — What you are asking for.** Write one of the following: to listen and help you think; practical assistance in the form of a specified amount; involvement in the plan as a monitor; or accompaniment to an appointment with an adviser. Specify which, and specify what you are not asking for.

**Part Four — The date.** Set the date and time. Men who intend to disclose within a fortnight but do not fix a date frequently do not disclose. Write it in a calendar rather than holding it in mind.

**Part Five — The review.** Set the date of the first review conversation and record what will be reported at it: amounts repaid, reductions effected, and any change in the position.

### A Note for the Man Whose Position Is Severe

If, having completed the Position Paper, you find that the position cannot be sustained for more than three months, or that legal consequences are imminent, the appropriate next step is professional advice before any disclosure to family: an accountant, a lawyer, a credit adviser, or a licensed insolvency practitioner, depending on the jurisdiction and the nature of the obligations. This is not an admission of defeat; it is the same decision a man makes when he takes a serious physical symptom to a physician rather than treating it himself.
