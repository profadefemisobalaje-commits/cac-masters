## 42.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I am not lying. I simply have not told anybody. Is that not different?"**

The chapter's test resolves the question and not the man's description of it. The test: is the position's continuation dependent on specified parties not learning specified facts, and would the man's arrangements require alteration if they learned them? Where the answer is yes, the position is the one Section 42.1 describes, whatever the man calls it, and the costs of Section 42.2 are being paid. Where the man's position would not require alteration, the material is privacy and the chapter does not apply to it. The case material records the finding that the men who described their position as *simply not having told anybody* answered the test's two questions in the affirmative.

**"If I tell my wife, she will leave."**

The prediction is recorded in the case material frequently and the outcome is recorded less frequently as the man feared it. The findings: the party's response is generally to the arrangement rather than to the fact; the party's response to the concealment is frequently the more severe of the two; and the party's own position is frequently more capable than the man had assumed, which is the material of Chapter 42.7's case in Grace's account. The reader who holds this question should put it to the professional route and to the one person of Section 42.5's second support before acting on his own prediction, which is the material of Chapter 30.4's rule.

**"I cannot reduce the school fees. The children's education is not negotiable."**

The instruction is not that every item of the third layer is released. The instruction is that the reductions are conducted in layers, from the least visible to the most, and that the third layer's items are conducted last, with the household's participation and the professional route, after the first two layers have produced what they can. The case material records the school's arrangements being renegotiated rather than abandoned in a substantial proportion of cases, as in Chapter 42.7, and records the finding that the concealment of the fees was a greater difficulty for the household than the fee's level.

**"My business runs on credit and reputation. If I disclose my position, I lose the work."**

The concern is legitimate and it is the one the chapter addresses in Section 42.3's fourth mechanism. The distinction the chapter supplies: the position's accurate statement to the professional, the household, and the one person is not the position's publication to the market. The case material records the finding that the men who conducted the disclosure with the arrangement and the advisers generally retained their professional standing and their credit, because the disclosure was conducted with a plan, and that the men who were exposed lost both. The reader's instruction: the disclosure's parties are selected, and the selection is a professional question.

**"What if I have already been exposed? The position is known."**

Then the chapter's later steps apply without the earlier ones, and the material of Chapter 38.5's stages is the sequence: the stabilisation, the income, the settlement, the standing, the relationships, and the new construction. The specific finding for this position, from the case material: the man whose exposure has occurred has lost the account of himself and has not lost the position's facts, which are the same facts that would have been addressed in a disclosure; his work is therefore the restoration of his account, which is conducted by the accurate statement, the repairs of Chapter 29, and the conduct over a period of years, and which the material of Chapter 38.5's fourth stage describes.

**"I have been doing this for twenty years. Is the position still recoverable?"**

The material of Chapter 38.5's indicative durations applies, and its finding for a long-maintained pretence is the one the case material records: the reductions available to a man whose position has been conducted on an appearance for twenty years are generally larger than he expects, because the appearance's cost is cumulative; the arrangements with the creditors are available at any stage; and the household's participation is generally available, as Chapter 42.7's case records, since the household has been participating in the position already and without being told.

::: summary
**Chapter 42 in brief.** Privacy is not a pretence and the ordinary management of a man's presentation is not a pretence. A pretence, in this chapter's sense, is a state of affairs in which the man's position can continue only for as long as specified parties do not learn specified facts; the test is whether he is arranging his conduct so that a particular party does not learn a particular fact, and whether his arrangements would require alteration if that party learned it. The six domains are the money, the work and the competence, the marriage, the household, the health, and the standing.

The costs are five: the vigilance, which is paid before anything is discovered and which increases with the pretence's age; the escalation, in which the pretence's covering produces further borrowing and increases the position's size while delaying its discovery; the reduction of the man's relationships to the parties before whom the pretence can be maintained, which removes the disclosure function; the reduction of his judgement, since his decisions are correct for the position he has described and wrong for the position he is in; and the discovery, whose additional cost beyond the loss is the loss of the parties' trust in the man's account of himself. The comparison the chapter requires: the disclosure and the exposure contain the same facts, and the exposure contains them in the form that costs the most.

The four mechanisms are the script, the avoidance of the meeting points, the borrowing against the future, and the maintenance of the appearance. The seven signs that the position is failing are that the man cannot state his position in figures; his explaining has become routine; he is avoiding a named party; his arrangements are conducted in the gaps of the day; he has withdrawn from the occasions; his sleep, drinking, or temper has altered; and he has begun to rehearse the discovery.

The six supports of a life that does not depend on pretending are: a position stated in figures, monthly, to the man himself; one person who knows the whole; the household's arrangements stated to the household; the reserve; the documented capacity; and the standing that does not require performance. They are cumulative and their order is not arbitrary. The dismantling proceeds in six steps and its order is mechanical: the statement; the one person; the household; the professionals; the reductions in three layers, from the items nobody sees to the items everybody sees; and the reconstruction at the true level. The position conducted at its true level is generally more productive than the position conducted behind the pretence, because the vigilance's cost is removed, the escalation stops, the decisions are taken on the actual position, and the man's relationships return to their actual parties.
:::

## Further Reading

- **On deception and its costs:** the research literature on deception, concealment, and self-presentation, including the findings on the cognitive and attentional costs of maintaining concealment, on the effects of concealment on relationships and health, and on the evidence concerning disclosure and its outcomes.
- **On financial stress and household debt:** the research and practice literature on household debt and financial stress, including the findings on the effects of unmanageable debt on health and relationships, on patterns of concealment within households, and on the outcomes of early engagement with debt advice.
- **On disclosure and its timing:** the literature on self-disclosure and on the disclosure of adverse information, including the findings on the effects of voluntary as against involuntary disclosure, on the role of the recipient's response, and on the conditions under which disclosure improves outcomes.
- **On business and reputation:** the practice literature on business failure, credit, and reputation, including the findings on the relationship between accurate representation and long-term commercial performance, and on the outcomes of reputation-management strategies conducted through misrepresentation.
- **Practical resources:** insolvency and debt practitioners; accountants; legal advice on guarantees, credit, and family property; financial counselling; clinical advice on alcohol use, sleep, and hypertension; and the material in Appendices D, M, and P.
