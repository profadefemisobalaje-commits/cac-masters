# The Loneliness of the Strong Man {.chapter num="4" short="The Loneliness of the Strong Man"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Distinguish between social isolation and the subjective loneliness that men experience in the middle of company and responsibility.
2. Explain the specific processes by which success and reliability produce isolation.
3. Describe what happens to male friendships under the pressure of work, family obligation, migration, and mid-life, and identify the stages of their disappearance.
4. Recognise the particular strain of being needed by everyone and enquired about by nobody, and describe the effects of that arrangement.
5. Explain the difference between emotional intimacy and sexual or romantic intimacy, and identify the forms of non-romantic closeness available to men.
6. Begin the practical work of rebuilding connection, using the stepwise method set out in this chapter and the structured plan in Appendix J.

## Introduction

There is a specific kind of loneliness that can only be understood by a man who has spent a Saturday in a house full of people who need things from him.

His daughter needs money for a school trip. His brother calls about a contribution for a funeral in the village. His wife has three items of domestic logistics that require his decision. A neighbour, having seen his car, has arrived to ask whether he can help with a delivery. His mother telephones about her medication. At four o'clock a young cousin arrives, because he is preparing for an examination and wants advice. By nine in the evening the house is quiet, the tasks are done, and the man sits in the dark for twenty minutes with a feeling that he cannot name and would not report to anyone: a sensation of being entirely alone in the middle of a full life.

This chapter is about that feeling. It is worth saying at once that the feeling is not self-pity, and that it is not a mood disorder. It is a structural feature of a life organised around responsibility, and it has been documented as a public-health concern as well as a personal experience: male loneliness is associated with poor mental health, with a higher risk of suicide, and with worse physical health outcomes, and it is now the subject of sustained attention by health agencies around the world. The purpose of this chapter is to explain how the arrangement is produced, and then to set out the practical route out of it.

## 4.1 Surrounded by People but Feeling Alone

The subjective aspect of loneliness that is important for this chapter is that it is not the same as isolation. **Social isolation** is an objective condition: few contacts, few interactions, limited practical support. **Loneliness** is a subjective condition: the perception that one's relationships are less satisfying or less intimate than one needs them to be.

The two can occur independently. A man can be isolated without feeling lonely — this is genuinely possible, and some men find the condition tolerable, at least for a time. And, much more commonly, a man can be lonely while being socially saturated. In fact, the second condition is arguably the more dangerous of the two, because it is invisible to everyone including the man himself.

Three features make loneliness in company harder to identify than loneliness in solitude.

**The evidence contradicts the feeling.** A man surrounded by dependants, colleagues, and contacts has every objective reason to conclude that loneliness is not his problem. When he does feel the thing, he tends to attribute it to a particular day, a particular tiredness, or a particular disappointment, rather than recognising it as a chronic condition of his arrangements.

**Nobody raises it as a possibility.** The people closest to a busy man do not ask whether he is lonely, because the question sounds absurd in his circumstances. When a man does mention loneliness, the most common responses — *But you have so many people around you* or *You have your family* — have the effect of closing the subject, since they contradict his experience and implicitly suggest that he has no grounds for complaint.

**The man has no word for his state.** Men frequently describe the experience in other terms: a sense of unreality, a feeling of being watched, disengagement, an inability to take pleasure in company, or a suspicion that they have become a spectator of their own life. The correct description — lonely — is often not available to them, for the reasons set out in Chapter 3.

The practical test is worth writing into the margin. **By what measure would you know that you are known rather than merely surrounded?** For most men, the answer comes in one of four forms: there is somebody who asks how I actually am and waits for the answer; there is somebody who has told me an unpleasant truth about myself, and I did not lose them; there is somebody to whom I have said the worst thing about my life; or there is somebody who keeps in touch when there is nothing to be gained.

::: keypoint
Being needed is not the same as being known, and being surrounded is not the same as being close. The two are routinely confused in the lives of men, because the first condition is visible and measurable while the second cannot be counted at all.
:::

## 4.2 Why Successful Men Experience Isolation

The mechanisms by which achievement and reliability produce isolation are not mysterious once they are named. Five of them do most of the work.

### Conversations Become Transactions

When a man is competent and available, he becomes a resource. Resources are approached for a purpose. This is not a moral failing in the people around him; it is what happens when a person reliably solves problems. His telephone fills with requests, and each request is legitimate. The cumulative effect is that almost every relationship in his life carries a function, and almost none of them operates without one.

Men describe the tipping point with precision: the day they began to notice that the only times they spoke to certain relatives was when there was a problem; or the realisation that the last four conversations with a close friend had each concerned a business or family matter. The discomfort this produces is difficult to name, because complaining that one is approached only for help sounds both arrogant and ungrateful. It is neither. It is an accurate report of a structural problem.

### The Audience Requires Maintenance

Once a man is known as steady, competent and unflappable, that identity becomes an asset that must be maintained. The maintenance is invisible work: choosing which difficulties to mention and which to omit; managing his expression in conversations where a problem is being discussed; deciding not to attend an event because he cannot currently fund the obligation it represents; and carrying the private weight of a reputation for having no problems.

The cost of this maintenance is the subject of Chapter 1, and for the purposes of this chapter the relevant effect is social. A man who is maintaining an appearance avoids precisely those situations in which intimacy develops: the unhurried conversation, the confession of weakness, the admission that he does not know what to do.

### Time Is Allocated to Obligation

Loneliness in men is frequently produced by nothing more complicated than scheduling. An audit of a typical mid-life man's week, drawn from the time-use studies and from men's own reports, produces a consistent picture: the hours available for paid work, commuting, household logistics, family care, community obligations, and the maintenance of property consume the week almost completely. What remains is a small residue, and the residue is usually committed to recovery — sleep, rest, and whatever is necessary to be fit for the following week.

The practical consequence is that friendship does not fail because men do not value it. It fails because it is the only category in the audit with no external claimant. Nobody sends a message saying that a man has not seen his closest friend for five months. And so it is the first thing to go.

### Comparison Replaces Conversation

An unintended effect of male success is that a man's peers become rivals. This occurs in every profession, but it is especially pronounced where positions are scarce: in competitive business environments, in politics and administration, in academic life, and in communities where the visible markers of status are closely watched. Where every conversation is a comparison, intimacy is impossible, because intimacy requires the ability to be seen without ranking.

The result is a particular phenomenon that men recognise immediately: groups of men who appear to be friends, who meet regularly, who assist one another in emergencies, and among whom no man has ever admitted to a serious personal difficulty.

### Migration and Relocation Break Existing Networks

The fifth mechanism is the most concrete and the least often discussed. Male friendship is often built on proximity and routine: men who grew up in the same area, worked in the same yard, attended the same gatherings, or walked home by the same route. Relocation — for work, for marriage, for education, for family reasons — severs the routine and, with it, the friendship. A man who moved for a better job at thirty-five frequently finds himself, at fifty, with professional contacts in abundance and no one with whom he can speak about anything that matters.
