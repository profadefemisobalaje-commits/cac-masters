## 28.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Kept the Papers

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in vocational counselling, mid-life review practice, and men's group discussion.

**Nathaniel is fifty-nine, a retired Customs officer of twenty-nine years' service, married to Grace for thirty-one years, with two adult children.**

Nathaniel's dream was agriculture. He had grown up on a family plot of about four hectares in a rural area, and at the age of nineteen, in his second year of a diploma in agricultural science, he had a plan that he could describe in detail thirty-nine years later: a poultry unit, a fish pond, and a mixed orchard on the family land, with the produce supplied to two markets in the state capital.

The plan ended in his third year, in a sequence he described precisely: his father's death, the family's dispute over the land, and the requirement that he obtain salaried employment to support his mother and his two younger sisters. He sat the examination for the customs service at twenty-four, was appointed at twenty-five, and worked in three cities in twenty-nine years.

The material he carried consisted of four items.

**The papers.** Nathaniel retained, from the age of nineteen, the handwritten plan, the drawing of the layout for the poultry unit, a notebook of prices and feed calculations, and a file of correspondence concerning a small grant he had enquired about in 1988. The file was kept in a folder in his box of documents and he looked at it, by his own account, *perhaps twice a year, generally in December*.

**The land.** The family plot had been resolved in favour of his uncle's branch of the family in the dispute, and about one and a half of the four hectares had passed to Nathaniel in the eventual settlement in 1996. He had never done anything with it and had, at one point in his forties, been approached by another man about selling it and had refused.

**The alternative life.** Nathaniel described a specific and detailed account of the life he would have had, including the income, the house he would have built on the plot, the position of his children, and — a detail he mentioned twice — the respect he would have received in his community as a man who had built something.

**The concealment.** Nathaniel had never told his wife the substance of the plan or the existence of the folder. His account of the concealment: *She knows the land is there. I told her once that I was going to farm it when I retired, and she said that it would be good to have something to do. She has never seen the drawings. I did not want her to know that I had planned all of this before I met her and that I gave it up.*

The position was discovered during the year before his retirement. Nathaniel had begun, from about the age of fifty-four, to make visits to the plot of an average of twice a year, and in the fifth year he spent a Saturday clearing a portion of it by hand. His account: *I stood in that place and I understood what I was doing. I was sixty years old and I was clearing land with a cutlass because I had decided in my head at twenty-five that I would come back and farm it, and I had forty hectares in my mind and one and a half hectares in front of me, and I did not want to do it. I did not want to keep chickens at sixty. I did not want to sell fish. I wanted the life I would have had if I had done it when I was thirty, and I had made a plan for a man I have never been.*

The work Nathaniel conducted over the following fourteen months had four elements, and he identified each separately.

**The accounting of the alternative.** With the help of a counsellor at his workplace's welfare office, Nathaniel wrote out what the farming life would actually have required and contained. The exercise, conducted on the version of the imagined life he had carried for thirty-nine years, produced a document of four pages with the following items: the failure rate of the small poultry and fish operations he had observed in the same area; the losses sustained by a former classmate who had farmed and whom he had envied in the 1990s; the years of the family support he would not have been able to provide, including his youngest sister's education, which had produced a nurse whose income had, subsequently, funded the household during his own mother's illness; and the fact, which he recorded himself, that his children had grown up in three cities and had received a schooling that no rural plot would have supplied.

He described the effect of that document in the following terms: *I had never asked what the farm would have failed at. I had only asked what I had lost. My sister, the nurse, paid for my mother's treatment for four years. I wrote that down and I sat with it and I stopped wanting my other life.*

**The disclosure.** Nathaniel told his wife Grace about the plan, the folder, the imagined life, and the visit to the plot. He described the conversation at length and identified its most important feature as the framing: *I told her first that I was not blaming anybody and that I would choose the same things again, and I said it before I told her anything else.* Grace's response, which he described as the reason the conversation went the way it did, was: *she said she had always known there was something in that box that I looked at, and that she had never touched the box.*

**The marker.** Nathaniel selected four items from the folder — the plan, the drawing, the notebook, and the 1988 correspondence — and placed them, with a written account of two pages entitled *What I did instead*, in a new file. He then took his son and his daughter separately to the plot, showed them the land, and told each of them the plan and the accounting. His account: *I wanted them to have the papers with the story attached, so that they would know what it was instead of finding a drawing of a poultry house in my box and wondering whether their father had been a disappointed man.*

**The step.** In the second year after the retirement, Nathaniel rented the plot to a cooperative of three young men on a two-year arrangement with an option to renew, and he visited the site, by his own account, *about once every three months, to look and to advise, and I enjoy it, and that is all it is.*

Nathaniel's summary, three years after retirement, was: *For thirty-nine years I carried a plan for a man who never existed. He had my land and he did not have my children, my sister's nursing certificate, my mother's last four years, or the three cities my daughters know. I kept his drawings in a box and I looked at them in December. The day I wrote down what he would have failed at was the day I stopped carrying him. The land is not wasted. The other two and a half hectares are still in the family's hands and I have stopped thinking about them.*
:::

Six features of this case deserve isolation.

**The dream was stored in physical form, and the storage maintained it.** A folder in a box, consulted twice a year. This detail is extremely common in men's accounts and it is the mechanism by which a dead dream remains available: the object is the dream's container, and its presence in the man's documents gives the dream a location in his actual life. The marker in Section 28.4 addresses this directly by giving the object a new context rather than by requiring its destruction.

**The imagined life was detailed and included standing.** The house, the income, the children's positions, and the respect of a community. The detail is characteristic and it is the reason the imaginary life is so difficult to compete with: it has been furnished over four decades and every item in it was chosen by the man.

**The concealment was from the wife, and the reason was the prehistory of the marriage.** *I did not want her to know that I had planned all of this before I met her and that I gave it up.* This is the specific form the concealment takes in this material and it is included because it is frequently misunderstood by the man's household when the disclosure eventually occurs: the withholding is not a judgement on the marriage, it is the man's refusal to present his wife with an alternative life in which she does not appear.

**The grounding happened in a physical place and produced the truth about what remained.** The man standing on one and a half hectares with a cutlass, having held forty hectares in his mind. This is the practical counterpart of the exercise at the end of this chapter: the dream must be brought into contact with the actual circumstances in order to be assessed, and the assessment is generally less terrible than the man's imagination and entirely different from it.

**The accounting was conducted on the alternative's failures, and it worked.** Four pages concerning the failure rate, the classmate's losses, the sister's nursing certificate, and the children's schooling. This is the element the chapter emphasizes most strongly and it is included despite its apparent ordinariness. The imaginary life is constructed from advantages only; the correction is not encouragement but information about what that life would have contained, and the information is generally available within the man's own knowledge.

**The closing transferred the material into the family's record rather than into silence.** The file entitled *What I did instead*, the visits with the two children, and the disclosure to the wife. The outcome is the one Section 28.6 describes: the dream is closed, the life that was built is counted, and the man's children inherit an accurate account of their father instead of a box of unexplained drawings.

### Part Three: The Reckoning (Sitting Three)

Count the life that was built, in the six domains of Section 28.6.

**1. The people who exist because of the decision.** By name.

**2. The capability accumulated.** What I can do now that I could not do at the age I set the dream down.

**3. The obligations discharged.** What was paid, maintained, funded, and kept afloat — taken from records, not from memory.

**4. The standing in the community.** Where I am consulted, relied upon, or expected.

**5. The competence of the man in the present.** What I now possess that the imagined man at twenty-two did not.
