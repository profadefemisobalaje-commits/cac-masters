::: summary
**Chapter 5 in brief.** Internal struggles are concealed by four mechanisms that operate together: competence, which supplies continuous evidence that a man is managing; the absence of vocabulary, which prevents him from reporting what he experiences; the masculine presentation of symptoms, which converts anxiety into tension, irritability, control behaviour, and drinking rather than into named fear; and shame, which makes the existence of the struggle itself feel like evidence of inadequacy.

Internal conflicts fall into eight recognisable families — between self and expectation, actions and values, desire and duty, worth and truth, the present and an unresolved past, the present and an anticipated collapse, identity and role, and need and capacity. What distinguishes ordinary conflict from a condition requiring assessment is intensity, duration, and impairment, not the presence of the conflict itself.

Anxiety in men frequently does not present as the standard picture. It presents as control-seeking, overwork, irritability, physical tension, and disturbed sleep, with the underlying subject being the loss of income, the failure of a business, or the exposure of a fault rather than the anticipated judgement of others. The result is that men delay identification and treatment, and that symptoms are attributed to work, age, or family, none of which can be addressed to good effect.

Persistent worry is distinguishable from ordinary concern by three features: it circles rather than concludes, it attaches to matters that cannot be resolved in the present, and it displaces the action that would address them. Intrusive thoughts are unwanted mental events that occur in almost everyone; the content does not indicate character, the secrecy causes more harm than the thoughts, and the phenomenon responds well to treatment, particularly cognitive behavioural approaches.

Shame and guilt must be distinguished. Guilt concerns an act and responds to acknowledgment, amendment, and restitution. Shame concerns the self and produces concealment, because a self cannot be amended by an apology. Shame converts readily into anger, which is why disproportionate hostility frequently follows exposure. The material men carry with most shame — failure to provide, sexual matters, business failure, harshness towards family, conduct in youth, harm done to a parent, debt, having been harmed, and having been ill — is material that other men carry too, and its disclosure is almost always accompanied by the discovery that one is not alone.

Unresolved memories persist, acquire associations, accumulate, and do not resolve by decision. They require a correction of meaning rather than an act of will, and in some cases professional support.

Professional assessment is warranted by duration, intensity, impairment, and compensatory behaviour, and immediately in the presence of any suicidal or self-harm thinking, symptoms of possible medical origin, or a household crisis. An assessment has four components — account, history, risk, and formulation with a plan — and the reader is entitled to ask what is happening, what the options are, and what would indicate deterioration. The available routes extend well beyond private psychiatry, and include physicians, counsellors, hospital services, telephone and online support, and peer groups.

Invisible pain becomes treatable when it becomes describable. Descriptions that work are specific, temporal, and functional: what happens, since when, how often, and what it prevents. A written account prepared in advance converts a humiliating struggle for words into a conversation that starts with information.

## Reflection Questions

1. The four concealments in Section 5.1 are competence, vocabulary, symptom presentation, and shame. Which applies most strongly to you? If a physician asked you the standard screening question today, what would you actually answer, and how accurate would it be?
2. Of the eight families of internal conflict in Table 5.1, which two or three are present in your life? For each, write the sentence a man would say if he were to name it plainly.
3. Consider the anxiety markers in Section 5.2. Which of the five applies to you now, and for how long has it applied? If none applies, is there a man close to you to whom two or three clearly apply?
4. Section 5.3 distinguishes guilt from shame. Take one thing you carry and decide which of the two it is. If it is guilt, what would amendment or restitution require? If it is shame, what would a corrected evaluation sound like?
5. Intrusive thoughts are common, and their content does not indicate character. Has any thought frightened you sufficiently that you have told nobody? What would it take to mention it to a competent professional?
6. Section 5.6 lists three principles for describing distress — describe behaviour and body rather than feelings, attach everything to a date, and report consequences rather than causes. Compose three sentences about yourself using those principles.
7. What is your honest position on the criteria in Table 5.2: duration, intensity, impairment, and compensatory behaviour? If two or more apply, what is the date on which you will make contact, and with whom?

## Further Reading

- **On anxiety in men:** the clinical literature on anxiety disorders, together with the growing body of work on how anxiety presents differently in men and on the reasons for under-recognition. The self-help literature based on cognitive behavioural therapy is well developed, evidence-based, and accessible; it works best as a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, professional assessment.
- **On worry and intrusive thoughts:** the research on rumination and on the significance attached to unwanted thoughts, including the well-established finding that the meaning a person assigns to a thought is more consequential than its frequency.
- **On shame:** the psychological literature on shame and guilt, including the distinction developed in this chapter and the research linking shame-proneness to concealment, hostility, and poor mental health outcomes. The work of researchers who have studied shame across cultures is particularly useful for readers in Africa, where the management of shame involves both individual and communal dimensions.
- **On assessment and treatment:** the World Health Organization's guidance on mental health, including its materials on depression and anxiety, which are available at no cost and are written for a general readership. National health services also publish accessible guidance, and the mhGAP programme materials are widely used by non-specialist clinicians in low- and middle-income settings.
- **On men's health services:** the growing literature on how services can be designed so that men actually use them, including work on appointment systems, the wording of invitations, and the role of primary care.
- **Personal accounts:** published first-person accounts by men who have lived with anxiety, obsessive thoughts, or severe shame, which are frequently more instructive to a man in the early stages of recognition than any clinical description.
