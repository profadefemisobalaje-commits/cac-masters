## 6.6 Reclaiming Emotional Well-Being

Recovery from depression is not a moment of realisation. It is a process with a recognisable shape, and it is helpful for a man to know what that shape is, because the process itself produces discouragement at predictable points.

### The Shape of Recovery

The early phase of treatment frequently produces a change that is not obvious to anybody outside: the man begins sleeping better, or begins to leave the house on purpose, or notices that he is arguing less. Mood improves more slowly than behaviour, and this produces a common pattern in which families see improvement before the man feels it. That gap is normal, and it is worth naming in advance, because a man who expects to feel better before he behaves better will conclude after three weeks that treatment is not working.

The middle phase involves the restoration of activity, contact, and purpose, generally in that order. Men are usually advised to resume a substantial part of their previous activity before they feel able to, because the ability follows the activity rather than preceding it. This is counter-intuitive and frequently resented. It is also one of the best-established features of treatment.

The later phase involves consolidation: monitoring for the return of symptoms, maintaining the habits that made recovery possible, and identifying the conditions that made the episode likely. Many men discover that they need to make one or two structural changes — the removal of a specific ongoing overload, the repair of a particular relationship, the treatment of a physical condition — without which the depression tends to return.

### Relapse Prevention

Depression recurs, and a man who has had one episode has a materially higher chance of another. Four practical measures reduce the risk.

**Recognise your personal signature.** The early signs differ between people: the first to appear may be a change in sleep, a rise in irritability, a loss of appetite, or an inability to make decisions. Write yours down while you are well, because you will not be able to see it when it is happening.

**Keep one person informed.** Somebody — a spouse, a friend, a relative, a professional — should know the signature, and should be appointed, in advance, to raise it.

**Maintain the practical foundations.** Regular sleep, exercise, and the avoidance of heavy drinking are more protective than any technique. This is unglamorous and it is the most effective thing available.

**Plan the follow-up.** Ask the treating professional for an explicit plan: how long treatment continues, what the review arrangements are, and what should happen if symptoms return. A written plan of this kind is a substantial protection.

### What Recovery Does Not Require

A final comment is needed on two expectations that damage recovery.

Recovery does not require the man to be able to describe his feelings at length, to have a vocabulary equal to a professional's, or to become a different kind of man. Many men recover fully while remaining men of few words. What is required is not fluency but precision: being able to report sleep, behaviour, symptoms, and consequences accurately is sufficient for treatment, as Chapter 5 explained.

Recovery also does not require the disappearance of every difficulty. The debt may remain, the son may still be struggling, the bereavement will still be real. What changes is the man's capacity to carry these things and to function with them — which is the practical difference between an untreated and a treated depression, and is, for most men, the whole of what they are asking for.

## 6.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Whose Family Thought He Had Changed

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family medicine, occupational health, and counselling practice.

**Musa, forty-six, is a building materials trader in a market, married with four children.** He has been in business for nineteen years and is known in his line as hardworking and reliable.

Over a period of about eighteen months, four things changed and none of them was discussed with anybody. First, he became short-tempered, particularly with his two youngest children, and he began to speak to them in a tone he had never used before. His wife concluded that he was under pressure from the market, and she responded by keeping the children away from him, which he noticed and experienced as confirmation that he had become a difficult man.

Second, his sleep changed. He began waking at about three in the morning and lying awake until five, at which point he would leave for the market two hours earlier than necessary. His excuse was the traffic; his wife accepted it, and his absence from the house in the early mornings was added to the list of things that were quietly changing.

Third, he lost interest in his Sunday ritual of playing football with a group of men he had known for twenty years, offering the explanation that he was too old. The men did not insist. He also stopped attending the association of traders from his home state, of which he had been a member for a decade.

Fourth, he began to drink more in the evening — three or four bottles, most nights, with the stated purpose of sleeping. He did not tell his wife that the drinking had become necessary.

His business did not decline. In fact, he spent more hours in it, and his turnover held. Nobody in the family, the market, or the community identified that anything was wrong; the recognised diagnosis of the situation, in the words of his wife, was that *Musa has changed*.

The crisis arrived in the form of a trivial incident. A customer questioned a price, and Musa shouted at him in the shop in front of other customers, which was so out of character that two neighbouring traders came to ask if he was unwell. He apologised, went home early, and sat in his room. His wife, who had been managing the situation for eighteen months without a name for it, asked him one question: *What is happening to you?*

He did not answer that night. Two days later he told her that he had not felt like himself for more than a year, that he did not know what was wrong with him, and that he had begun to think that his family would be better off without him in the house. That last sentence caused her to insist — over his objection — that they go to the family physician together, that same week.

The physician asked about sleep, appetite, weight, and alcohol; about the changes in temperament; about the loss of interest in activities; and, directly, about thoughts of death. Musa described a private conviction, present for months, that he had become a person nobody could love, and a settled belief that his continued presence in the home was a burden. He was diagnosed with a moderate depressive episode with a risk of self-harm, started on medication the same week, referred for counselling, and asked to return in one week and then every two weeks. His wife was given an explanation of the condition, told what to watch for, and told explicitly to ask him about his thoughts if she was worried, rather than avoiding the subject.

Eight weeks later, his sleep had improved, the shouting had largely stopped, and he described his state as *like coming out of a fog.* Nineteen months later, in a review consultation, he said something that his physician considered worth repeating to other men: *I thought I had become a bad man. I had become an ill man, and nobody — including me — had used that word.*
:::

The case illustrates the central point of the chapter, and four features should be noted.

**Every symptom was present and every symptom was misread.** Irritability was read as strain; the early waking was read as traffic; the loss of football and the association was read as age; the drinking was read as a habit. None of these readings was unreasonable, and the sum of them concealed a condition for eighteen months.

**Function was preserved until the end.** The business did not decline; his hours increased. This is one of the most common reasons male depression goes unrecognised, and it is why family and employers should look at changes in temperament and participation rather than at output alone.

**The risk was disclosed only in response to a direct question.** Musa had thoughts that his family would be better off without him and had told nobody. The disclosure followed a question from his wife, which itself followed an escalation. This is why both families and clinicians must ask direct questions about self-harm: the information is generally available and generally not volunteered.

**Treatment worked and the family was included in it.** Medication, counselling, a follow-up schedule, and an informed spouse produced a recovery that was visible within two months. The intervention did not require unusual resources. It required that the condition be identified and named.
