::: summary
**Chapter 11 in brief.** The social definition of a successful man comprises five components — income and its visible signs, occupation and title, family formation, property, and community standing — each of which is legitimate as a value. The definition causes damage because of three properties: it is comparative rather than absolute, so success is a position rather than a standard; it is public, so a man's private assessment is continuously contradicted or confirmed by others' commentary; and its timetable was calibrated to an economy of stable employment and rising incomes that no longer describes the conditions in which most men work.

Ambition becomes destructive when it ceases to be an activity and becomes an identity. Three relationships to work can be distinguished: work as a means, work as a vocation, and work as an identity. The third is unstable in three ways — it fails when the work is lost, it requires continuous confirmation even when the work succeeds, and it erodes the alternative sources of identity that would supply self-regard in other circumstances. The substitution of achievement for identity is common in mid-life and is frequently mistaken for dedication.

Delayed achievement is distinct from failure because there is no event to mourn and no clean conclusion. It produces a chronic sense of being behind, compensatory activity, the loss of pleasure in the present, and a reluctance to celebrate. Its treatment depends on distinguishing temporary delay, produced by time-limited circumstances, from structural delay, produced by conditions that will not change on the current path. The diagnostic question is what the position would look like in five years on the present trajectory.

Unemployment damages men's mental health more severely than women's, through loss of income, structure, role, and social contact, and produces a collapse of routine, withdrawal, irritability at home, decay in the job search, and health deterioration. Underemployment is frequently more corrosive, because the conditions are experienced daily, the man attracts comment, and the difficulty appears optional. Lost opportunities are the most private of the three, are measured against an imagined alternative life, and are frequently attached to a specific person whose requirement caused the opportunity to be declined.

Constant comparison costs men intrinsic satisfaction, the quality of their decisions, generosity towards peers, the range of options they can consider, their health through continuous competitive effort, and the example they transmit to their children. The four effective measures are fixing a personal reference point, changing the reference group deliberately, reducing exposure to the comparison mechanism, and converting comparison into information.

A personal definition of success must be specific, verifiable, the man's own, calibrated to his actual capacity, and inclusive of what he is not willing to sacrifice. It covers seven domains — income and work, housing and assets, family and marriage, children, health, community and contribution, and rest and life — and it should include a ceiling, a boundary, and a timeline. It becomes useful as a standard for decisions, as a defence in conversation, and as a measure reviewed annually. The chapter recommends neither the abandonment of ambition nor the rejection of family expectation, but the substitution of an examined, personal standard for one assembled from comparisons and applied without consent.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. From Section 11.1, which of the five components of the social definition carries the most weight in your own assessment of yourself? Which of the three destructive properties — comparative, public, or borrowed timetable — has done the most damage in your life?
2. Which of the three relationships to work describes your current position: work as a means, as a vocation, or as an identity? What has changed in the last five years, and what would it take to move one position back?
3. Answer the diagnostic question in Section 11.3: if nothing changes in your circumstances and you continue on the present path for five years, will the position be different? What did you conclude, and what follows from it?
4. If you have experienced unemployment, underemployment, or a serious lost opportunity, which of the reported effects did you experience? Which of them is still affecting you, and what support was available that you did not use?
5. List the five costs of comparison from Section 11.5 and mark those you are currently paying. Which one is most visible to the people around you?
6. Complete Part Two of the exercise: what have you achieved that the inherited definition does not recognise? Read the list aloud before answering. What did you notice?
7. What is your boundary — what are you not willing to sacrifice — and is it compatible with your current working pattern? If not, which of the two will change first?

## Further Reading

- **On the social construction of success:** the sociology of status, respectability, and the evaluation of men, including historical and comparative work on how the markers of male success have changed and how they differ between societies. Work on the post-colonial transformation of status markers in West Africa is directly relevant.
- **On unemployment and mental health:** the extensive research literature on the psychological effects of unemployment, including the well-documented finding that job loss raises depression, anxiety, and mortality risk, and the studies showing that the effects are strongest in men and in those who are the principal earners of a household.
- **On underemployment:** the literature on the psychological effects of working below one's capacity, including research on over-qualification and its effects on job satisfaction, self-esteem, and well-being.
- **On social comparison and career:** the research on social comparison in the workplace, including studies of comparison with peers and its effects on motivation, performance, and satisfaction, and the work on reference group choice.
- **On career transitions:** practical literature on changing career paths at mid-life, including structured methods for assessing transferable skills, evaluating a change of path, and planning a transition while meeting existing obligations.
- **On financial independence and lifestyle design:** the practical literature on designing a life with a defined standard, including work on the concept of enough, on reducing obligations, and on the relationship between consumption and satisfaction. Readers may find that this material is frequently written to a different cultural context, and should adapt rather than adopt it.
