## 26.3 The Brothers of Adulthood: Family Loyalty and Its Failures

The betrayals that occur between brothers — and between siblings generally, where men are concerned — have a distinct structure, and they are frequently the most damaging of the losses described in this chapter, for three reasons: the relationship is permanent, the community's judgement is applied to it, and the material in question is frequently the family's property, its standing, and its history.

### The Five Forms

**1. The dispute over inheritance or family property.** The most common form globally, and the most destructive to families. Its typical features: an intestate estate or an estate whose distribution was never formalised; a brother who takes possession of a property, a plot, or a business; the use of a position of trust — the executor, the eldest, the brother who lives closest, the brother who holds the documents — to acquire an advantage; the circulation of a competing account within the family; and a dispute that lasts years and that is frequently concluded without any settlement at all.

The specific loss for the man is threefold: the material; the relationship with the brother; and, most significantly, the family's structure, since the dispute frequently divides subsequent generations, who inherit the enmity without having participated in the cause.

**2. The refusal of assistance at a moment of obligation.** A brother who does not contribute to a parent's care, a funeral, a sibling's medical costs, or a family obligation, while retaining the standing that the family's arrangements confer. The material is the whole of Chapter 20 in a family setting: the man who carries the obligation while the brother who does not appears at the ceremony.

**3. The alliance against the man.** A brother who supports an account of the man that is being circulated in the family; who supplies family information to an adversary in a dispute; who takes the side of a relative against the man in the expectation of an advantage; or who participates in the exclusion described in Section 16.4.

**4. The pursuit of the man's interest.** As in friendship, and frequently in a form that is not available to a friend: the brother who takes a position in a family business without the man's knowledge, who obtains a property that the man had been negotiating for, who makes an approach to the man's wife's family, or who succeeds to a relationship within the family that the man had relied upon.

**5. The disclosure of the man's history.** The brother holds a set of information that nobody else holds: the family's arrangements, the man's childhood, his early failures, and frequently his confidences. Its disclosure to the family, to the wife's family, or to the community is a betrayal of a different weight from the friend's, because the material is deeper and the audience more consequential.

### The Specific Difficulties of a Fraternal Betrayal

**The relationship cannot be ended by the man's decision.** A friend can be left; a brother is present at every family occasion, in every family decision, and in the family's records. The man's options are limited to the forms described in Chapter 16.5 — revised terms, a boundary with contact, and estrangement within a family he continues to belong to.

**The community does not recognise the injury.** Family disputes are frequently treated as ordinary, and the man who loses a case of family property is frequently advised to accept the position for the sake of peace. The advice is frequently correct in its practical consequence and it removes the man's permission to grieve.

**The man is frequently required to be the party who concedes.** Where the man is the elder, the more capable, or the more solvent, the family's expectation is that he will absorb the cost, and the expectation is frequently applied by the very relatives who have assessed the dispute.

**The material is frequently never resolved.** Unlike the friendship, which ends, a family dispute can continue for decades, conducted in the family's occasions and in the conduct of the next generation. The practical work of this section is therefore not the resolution of the dispute but the arrangement by which the man continues to function inside an unresolved situation.

### The Practical Handling

**Establish the facts and the documents.** The single most useful action, and the one most frequently omitted. Where a family matter is in dispute, the man should obtain: the will or the applicable customary position, the title documents, the bank records, the correspondence, and a written account of the family's decisions with dates. The records convert the dispute from a contest of accounts into a matter of fact, and they are frequently the instrument by which an allocation is corrected, as the case in Section 16.7 illustrates.

**Bring the matter to a body rather than to the family's conversations.** The available bodies: the family's own council, if it has a procedure; the customary authority, where applicable; a mediation service; the relevant government agency for property and title matters; and the courts, where the material justifies it. The practical finding from men's accounts is that a matter taken to a competent body, even where the outcome is partial, is substantially less damaging than the same matter conducted indefinitely in the family's conversations.

**State the position once and then continue to appear.** The pattern established in Chapter 16.5. The man's most effective response to a fraternal betrayal is presence: he continues to attend, to contribute at the level available to him, and to treat the brother with ordinary courtesy. The conduct is what alters the family's assessment, and it does so over years rather than months.

**Decide the boundary explicitly.** The available positions, in ascending order: ordinary contact with no cooperation on the disputed matter; ordinary contact with no dependence on the brother in any matter; the reduction of contact to the family's obligatory occasions; and estrangement. The choice should be made in writing, should be based on the assessment rather than on the man's immediate state, and should be reviewed annually.

**Protect the next generation.** The most consequential measure, and the one that determines whether the dispute outlives the participants. The rule: the man does not transmit his account of the brother to the children, and he does not use them as messengers or allies. Where the children observe the dispute, the explanation should be factual and brief: this is a disagreement about property between adults, and it does not change anything about your relationship with your cousins.

## 26.4 The Anatomy of a Betrayed Friendship

This section describes the course of a betrayed friendship, because the men who navigate it well do so by identifying the stage they are in and taking the appropriate action at each.

### The Six Stages

**Stage One: The act.** The disclosure, the pursuit, the failure of assistance, the financial loss. The man's immediate response is frequently disbelief, which is a defence: the material is inconsistent with the model of the relationship he held.

**Stage Two: The discovery's management.** The man decides, generally within days, what to do with the information. The options are the confrontation, the silent reduction of the relationship, or the attempt to continue as though the act had not occurred. The last of these is the most common and it is the most expensive, because it maintains the relationship while transferring the man's trust investment into a private account of resentment — the ledger described in Chapters 15, 17, and 20.

**Stage Three: The confrontation, where it occurs.** Two forms. The direct account — what the man knows, what it has cost him, and what he intends — which is effective and difficult; and the indirect account, comprising the general remark, the pointed observation, and the withdrawal, which is ineffective and produces a period of mutual manoeuvring. Men should note that the direct form does not require the friend's agreement; it requires the man to have stated the position, which is the same standard as in Chapters 20 and 25.

**Stage Four: The assessment of the relationship.** The stage most frequently omitted, and the one that determines the outcome. The questions: which term of the relationship was relied upon and was not held; was the failure a decision or an incapacity; is the conduct capable of change; is the relationship worth the revised terms; and what would the man require in order to continue? The questions are answered in writing, and the answers determine whether the outcome is repair, revision, or conclusion.

**Stage Five: The outcome.** Three possibilities. **Repair**, where the friend has acknowledged the act and the terms have been restated — a legitimate outcome, and the one that requires the most work from both parties. **Revision**, where the relationship continues on reduced terms: the friendship without the confidences, the association without the financial arrangements, the group without the reliance. **Conclusion**, where the relationship ends, either formally or by the ordinary lapse that follows a deliberate reduction of contact.

**Stage Six: The consolidation.** The stage in which the man's understanding of the event is fixed, and the stage at which the lessons are drawn. Section 26.5 addresses it directly, because the outcome of this stage determines whether the man's capacity to trust other men is preserved or reduced.
