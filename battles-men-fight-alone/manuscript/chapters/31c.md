## 31.3 The Weight Nobody Sees

The second section of the chapter concerns the load itself. Leaders' burdens are described in organisational terms — the targets, the budget, the staffing, the strategy — and the following four are generally not among them, because they are not recorded anywhere and because the man would not describe them.

### The Four Burdens

**1. The decision burden.** The number of decisions, and the fact that each of them is final.

A man in a position of authority makes a large number of decisions daily, of which a substantial proportion cannot be referred upward, deferred to a meeting, or made by consensus. The material on decision fatigue is relevant and its findings are consistent: the quality of decisions declines over a day of unbroken decision-making, and the effect is strong enough that practitioners in several fields have constructed routines to protect judgement at the hours in which it matters.

The burden's specific form for a man in authority is the number of people affected by each decision. A decision about a supplier affects the supplier's staff; a decision about a position affects a household; a decision about a procedure affects the people who must perform it. Men describe the arithmetic precisely: *I counted, one year, that my decisions had affected about six hundred people directly and their families indirectly, and after I counted it I could not sleep.*

Leaders also carry the decisions that were made wrongly, which never leave the record. The man knows which of his decisions were mistaken, which people were harmed by them, which of them are still producing effects, and there is no arrangement in which this material is processed. It accumulates.

**2. The example burden.** The requirement to be what the position requires, continuously.

The man in authority is a standard. His attendance, his punctuality, his conduct, his honesty, his treatment of people, his work rate, and his reaction to difficulty are all read, by everybody, as the standard of the organisation. The requirement is real and it does not have off hours: the man's conduct at a function, in a car park, in a period of his own difficulty, and in his household is part of the organisation's standard, and the men who lead well describe the requirement as continuous and as the burden they most frequently resent.

The cost is that the man's own conduct is never free — including, frequently, in his own household, which is the subject of Section 31.6.

**3. The morale burden.** The requirement to hold the state of the group while holding his own.

The leader is the person who holds the group's morale, and the holding is performed in specific ways: the level tone at the difficult meeting, the explanation when the news is bad, the availability in the period of a crisis, the presence at the occasion nobody wants to attend, the decision to absorb an anxiety rather than distribute it. Men describe this burden as the one that produces the most fatigue and the one that is least acknowledged, because its successful performance is invisible: a group that is steady because the man held it steady gives the man no credit, having never seen the alternative.

**4. The boundary burden.** The requirement to decide what the position will be allowed to consume.

The man's hours, his attention, his household's arrangements, his health, his friendships, and his own interests are all available to the position, which will take as much of them as the man permits. Nobody else in the arrangement has an incentive to set the limit: the organisation's requirements are genuine and expanding, the subordinates are not responsible for the man's household, and the man's own household is generally reluctant to make demands on him because his position is understood to be demanding. The boundary is therefore the man's own obligation, and the material on men's overwork in Chapter 18 applies directly here, with one addition: a man in authority who does not set the boundary frequently presents the absence of a boundary as a virtue, and the presentation is received as such.

### The Fifth Burden, and the Reason It Is Listed Separately

**The burden of carrying other people's personal material.**

This burden is not in the ordinary description of leadership and it is very commonly carried. The men in a leader's organisation bring him their material: the employee whose marriage is failing, the colleague whose child is ill, the subordinate who is drinking, the young man who is being mistreated by his supervisor, the member who has a debt, the relative of a member who has died. The man is brought these matters because he is the person with the standing to do something about them, and he does what he can, and the material does not leave him.

In the material reported in leadership work, the accumulation of this burden is among the most frequently described sources of the man's condition, and it is the category he is least likely to mention, because the others' difficulties are real and his own position appears privileged. The point should be stated plainly: a leader who receives the confidences of forty people is carrying forty pieces of material, and there is no arrangement, in most organisations, in which his own is received.

### The Two Effects of Carrying the Weight Alone

**The degradation of judgement.** The material from Chapter 27.3 applies in its full form to men in authority, and the finding should be considered by the reader in the specific terms of his own responsibilities: a decision made by an isolated man is made from one perspective; a decision made by an exhausted and uncalibrated man is made with degraded judgement; and the consequences of such decisions are borne by the people the man is responsible for. The arrangement described in Section 31.5 is therefore not a benefit to the man alone. It is a component of the man's responsibilities.

**The accumulation of the material in the household and the body.** The material that has no arrangement at work does not disappear. It presents at home as severity, absence, and unreachability; in the body as sleep disturbance, blood pressure, chest symptoms, and exhaustion; and in the man's conduct as the drinking, the risk-taking, and the shortening temper described in Section 30.3. Men in authority are frequently the men whose households absorb the cost of the position, and the man learns of it, if he learns of it, from a spouse, a child, or a medical practitioner.

## 31.4 When the Leader Has Nothing Left

The presentations of a man in authority who has reached the end of his capacity are recognisable and are frequently misread — by the man as a temporary period, by the organisation as a performance matter, and by the household as a change in temperament.

### The Nine Presentations

**1. Exhaustion that sleep does not correct.** The man sleeps and wakes unrefreshed, the pattern is sustained over months, and the ordinary remedies — a weekend, a holiday, the reduction of hours — produce less relief than expected and less each time.

**2. Decision avoidance.** The presentation characteristic of this group: the man who has always decided begins to postpone, to form committees, to require more information, to defer to others, or, in the most striking form, to cancel meetings at which decisions would be required. Men describe the experience precisely: *I could not make one more decision, so I moved the meeting.*

**3. The collapse of tolerance for other people's material.** The man who has spent his career receiving other people's difficulties begins to find them unbearable: the complaints, the requests, the confidences, the problems he is brought. He becomes short with the people who need him, which is not consistent with his history and which he frequently recognises afterwards with severe self-judgement.

**4. Cynicism.** The man's account of the work, the people, or the organisation becomes contemptuous, and the contempt is expressed in private. The presentation is documented in the literature on occupational burnout as one of the core components, and its specific danger for men in authority is that the man's private account of the people he leads affects his decisions about them before it appears in his conduct.

**5. The loss of the position's meaning.** The work continues, the performance is maintained, and the man no longer knows why he is doing it. He frequently describes the position in the terms of Chapter 24: the role has become his whole account of himself and the account has lost its content.

**6. The performance of performance.** A specific and deceptive presentation: the man's professional functioning remains intact while the interior of the work has emptied. Colleagues notice nothing, the results continue, and the man describes performing the role rather than holding it. Men in this state are frequently at their most admired.

**7. The body.** The blood pressure, the chest, the stomach, the headaches, the insomnia, the weight, the alcohol. This category is frequently the channel through which the man is eventually brought into an assessment, and it should be treated as information rather than as an unrelated matter.

**8. The conduct at home.** The severity, the withdrawal into the study or the phone, the inability to be present, the flatness at occasions, the short temper with the people he loves, the household's management of his mood. Households in this situation generally describe the man's condition accurately to a third party long before the man describes it himself.

**9. The unreachable conclusion.** The specific and most serious presentation: the man has concluded, privately, that he cannot continue — in the role, in the marriage, in his life. The conclusion is generally reached in the same isolation as everything else, it is frequently not communicated, and it is sometimes acted upon. Men in authority are a group with a documented elevated risk, and the chapter states the position directly: a man who has reached the conclusion that his situation is unendurable and that nothing can be altered should treat the matter as requiring professional assessment immediately, and should not rely on his own capacity to manage it privately, because that capacity is the facility that the condition has degraded.

### The Two Errors

**The man's error.** The presentations are interpreted as temporary, as related to a particular quarter, as the consequence of a difficult individual, as a matter of fitness, or as an indication that the man requires a holiday. The interpretation delays the response for a period that is frequently measured in years, and the delay is what allows an ordinary condition to become a serious one.

**The organisation's error.** The matter is read as a performance matter, and performance matters are managed with the measures available to the organisation: targets, monitoring, additional support on the immediate tasks, and, in the end, an exit. The man's conduct is then interpreted as evidence about his capacity rather than about his condition, and the corrective arrangement — the arrangement in Section 31.5 — is the one measure that is not among the available options.
