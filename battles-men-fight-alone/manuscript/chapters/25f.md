## 25.8 Practical Exercise: The Judgement Audit and the Personal Measure

This exercise takes about two and a half hours, spread over three sittings.

### Part One: The Audit of Judgements (Sitting One)

Write out the judgements that have been applied to you and that you have accepted. Be specific and, where possible, record the event, the person, and the date.

| The judgement | Who applied it | On what basis | Have I accepted it? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | |

Sources to consider: family comments at gatherings; the treatment you receive from relatives and neighbours; the institutional records (employment, credit, qualifications); the reduced consultations and invitations; anything said to you directly.

Then answer four questions.

- How many of the judgements on the list are based on results rather than on conduct?
- Which of them did I accept without noticing that I had accepted it?
- What have I stopped doing, applying for, or attempting, since accepting them?
- Which single judgement has done the most damage, and what is the evidence for it?

### Part Two: The Record (Sitting Two)

Compile the record described in Section 25.3. Write out, with as much specificity as you can:

- the work completed in the last five years, in units (installations, jobs, projects, client engagements, services rendered, students taught, cases handled, customers served);
- the skills demonstrated, including those for which you have no certificate;
- the people who have depended on your work and who would confirm its quality;
- the obligations discharged: what you paid, supported, funded, repaired, and maintained.

Then answer: how many of these had I ever counted before? What proportion of my effort has been recorded anywhere?

### Part Six: The Statement

Write the one-sentence accurate statement of your position, without apology and without elaboration. Examples of the form: *I lost the business in 2019 and I have worked as a technician since; I am supporting four children and I am two years behind on the fees.* Then rehearse saying it aloud, once, to somebody who is likely to ask. The purpose is the removal of the man's dependence on the audience's interpretation, which requires that the position be statable.

## 25.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not simply an excuse to say that the world judges by results?"**

The observation is a description, not an excuse, and the chapter is explicit that a man's circumstances should be described accurately and that effort should be made in full. What the observation establishes is that the world's assessment is not the whole of a man's worth, and that a man who has accepted it as the whole will stop making effort, which is the opposite of the effect intended by the people applying it.

**"I have lost everything. Everyone in my community knows. What do I say?"**

The statement in Part Six of the exercise, delivered once, accurately, and without apology. The record of men in this situation is consistent: the audience's interest in the failure lasts considerably less long than the man's fear of it, and the man's subsequent conduct is what the community actually assesses, because a community of long duration evaluates conduct in practice rather than results in the abstract.

**"My family treats me as the unsuccessful one. How do I change their view?"**

By not requiring the change. The practical sequence is the one in Section 25.6: state the position once, continue to attend and contribute at the level available to you, and allow the conduct to accumulate. Men report that the change in the family's assessment arrives, where it arrives, in the form of a specific occasion — an appointment, a consultation, a role on a committee — rather than in the form of a retraction.

**"Is it dishonest to hold a personal measure that lets me feel adequate when I am not?"**

Only if the measure describes the circumstances inaccurately, which Section 25.4 prohibits. The measure concerns the man's conduct and effort, over which he has control, and the accuracy of the measure is what makes it honest: a man whose obligations are unmet should record the shortfall in the measure, disclose it, and record what he did about it. What the measure refuses is the extension of the circumstances into a verdict about the person, which is not honesty but a generalisation.

**"What if I genuinely have not made the effort?"**

Then the measure requires it, and the honest assessment is the beginning of the correction rather than an account of the man's character. Men frequently discover, on completing Part Two of the exercise, that the effort has been made and that the environment has been inefficient; they also discover, in a minority of cases, that the effort has not been made, which is the more useful finding and which the measure addresses directly.

**"Does this chapter apply to men who have succeeded?"**

Yes, and frequently more urgently. The man whose results are adequate has the same audience and has usually invested more completely in the assessment it applies, which is why the loss of position in mid-life is as damaging to him as it is to the man who never obtained it. The material of Chapter 24 applies, and the measure in Section 25.5 is the protection against its collapse.

::: summary
**Chapter 25 in brief.** A man is measured by his results, and the measurement is applied through six mechanisms: the question *what do you do?* and the immediate answer it requires; the visible markers; the comparisons conducted by others in his presence; the institutional records that determine his future options; the evaluative vocabulary available for men; and the informal consequences, comprising invitations, introductions, memberships, and opportunities that never arrive. The measurement extends to the man's household, to his character, and to his family's history, and it is applied most sharply in settings where standing is determined by information available quickly, and most accurately in communities of long duration that assess conduct rather than results.

Being publicly assessed as a failure has nine features: the verdict is general, permanent until contradicted by results, socially administered, and answered by an account composed with the audience in mind; it produces withdrawal; it reduces the man's own conduct and ambition; it transfers to his household; it has no procedure for restoration; and, in its decisive form, it is interiorated and becomes the man's own assessment of himself.

Society has no place for four specific versions of success: the structural mismatch, where the economy does not contain the work for which the man is prepared; the credential mismatch, where competence exceeds certification; the recognition mismatch, where work of high quality attracts low status; and the path mismatch, where the man's chosen measure is not the community's. All four share three features: the man's effort is not the variable, the record is the decisive practical instrument, and a reference group of people who can assess the work is required.

Dignity is defined as the position a man holds toward himself when his conduct is consistent with his own standards and his circumstances are described accurately. It is not recognition, resignation, the denial of circumstances, or the maintenance of pride. Its practices are to state the circumstances accurately once without elaboration, to refuse the verdict while accepting the assessment, to keep conduct within the standard, and to hold one relationship in which the man is not assessed.

A personal measure must be verdict-free, available now, checkable, and the man's own. It is constructed in four domains — conduct and integrity, effort, obligation, and conduct toward his household — and it is supported by the technique of translating outcomes into efforts. Living without requiring approval involves four arrangements: the smaller audience identified by name, the reduced exposure to the delivery of the verdict, the stated standard, and the relationship in which the man is not assessed. It involves three prohibitions: do not argue with the audience, do not require the household to defend you, and do not construct a counter-audience in which the man is over-valued.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete Part One. Which judgement have you accepted without noticing that you had accepted it, and what have you stopped attempting since?
2. What is the record of your work over the last five years, in units? How many of those units had you ever counted before?
3. From the four mismatches in Section 25.3, which applies to you — structural, credential, recognition, or path? What does the corresponding treatment require, specifically?
4. Write your one-sentence statement of position, without apology. What is the sentence you would most dislike saying aloud, and why?
5. Which of the four practices of dignity in Section 25.4 do you currently operate, and which is absent? What would change in your week if all four were present?
6. Name the people whose assessments you treat as authoritative. Which of them does not meet the criteria of knowledge, honesty, and concern — and what would change if their assessment were treated as information?
7. Which of the three prohibitions in Section 25.6 are you currently violating: arguing with the audience, requiring the household to defend you, or maintaining a counter-audience? What has it cost you?

## Further Reading

- **On social evaluation and status:** the sociological literature on status, respect, and the evaluation of persons, including the work on how societies allocate standing and the consequences of low status for health and behaviour.
- **On the provider role and social standing in men:** research on the association between men's employment status and their social position, including the findings on the effects of unemployment on men's standing within households and communities.
- **On unemployment and mental health:** the substantial literature on the psychological effects of unemployment, including the consistent finding that the effects are stronger in men and stronger where the man is the principal earner of a household.
- **On dignity:** the philosophical and psychological literature on dignity and self-respect, including the accounts of dignity as a standing that does not depend on the recognition of others, and the distinction between self-respect and self-esteem.
- **On informal work and the mismatch between competence and credentials:** the literature on informal employment, on skills recognition, and on the labour market consequences of the absence of formal certification, including the evidence on the value of competency-based assessment.
- **Practical resources:** adult education and professional certification routes; business and technical support services for small enterprises and trades; men's groups and professional associations in occupational communities; and the material in Appendices I and J.
