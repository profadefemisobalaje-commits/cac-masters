## 3.6 Learning to Acknowledge Difficult Feelings

The remaining question is practical. A man who has recognised himself in this chapter — who has suppressed emotion for years, who cannot readily name what he feels, who has not cried since a particular year in the past — what does he actually do?

The answer is that he learns a skill, in the same way that he learned any other skill he now possesses: by starting with the elementary components, practising them deliberately, and tolerating being bad at it for a period. Four components are involved: naming the sensation, using a graduated scale, writing one honest sentence a day, and borrowing language from other men. Figure 3.4 summarises the sequence and notes on some ordinary male statements that are usually translations.

### Naming the Sensation Before Naming the Feeling

Most men who think they cannot identify feelings can identify sensations quite accurately, and this is the way in. Before attempting to answer *How do I feel?*, answer *What is my body doing?*

Sit still for sixty seconds and report, in writing: jaw, throat, chest, stomach, hands, shoulders, breathing, and overall level of restlessness. Then convert: a tight jaw usually accompanies anger held back; a closed throat accompanies grief or unshed tears; a heavy chest accompanies fear, dread, or sadness; a churning stomach accompanies anxiety; clenched hands accompany anger or the effort of restraint.

The technique works because sensation is concrete and feeling is abstract, and men are generally more practised with the concrete. After a few weeks of recording sensation, most men can make the translation reliably: *My chest is heavy and my throat is closed, therefore I am probably grieving or dreading something.* At that point the vocabulary has begun to exist.

### Using a Graduated Scale

A second aid is to rate the state rather than to describe it. On a scale from one to ten, how heavy is the chest? How strong is the urge to leave the room? How close am I to losing my temper? Scales feel artificial to many men at first, and their advantage over adjectives is precisely that they require no vocabulary and produce comparable data. Over weeks, a man can see whether the pattern is improving, and he can also notice that most states are not at nine out of ten — a discovery that converts terror into something manageable.

### One Sentence a Day

The single most effective exercise reported by men who have rebuilt emotional vocabulary is writing one honest sentence per day. It does not have to be profound. The requirements are that it be true, that it use a feeling word or a bodily state, and that it be written down (not merely thought) and kept private.

Examples of the form, drawn from the practice of men who have done this exercise:

- *I was frightened in the meeting this morning and I said nothing.*
- *I miss my father today and I did not expect to.*
- *I am angry with my brother and I have decided not to mention it again.*
- *I feel nothing about the house and that disturbs me more than grief would.*
- *I wanted to be comforted this evening and I went to the workshop instead.*

Two hundred such sentences, written over several months, constitute a document that most men find astonishing to re-read, because it produces a record of an inner life that they did not believe existed. It also tends to identify, without any interpretive labour, the two or three matters that actually require attention.

### Borrowing Language

Men frequently find their own words more easily in somebody else's mouth. There is a specific and beneficial effect in reading an account of an experience — in a book, in a recorded interview, in a supervised group, in a documentary — and hearing one's own state described by another person in words one would not have been able to supply. The technical term for the recognition is not important; what matters is the practical effect, which is that language is transferable. A man may take a sentence from a writer, a counsellor, or an older man who has been through something similar, and use it as his own until it fits.

This is one of the reasons that a group of men who talk honestly to one another produces improvements in vocabulary so quickly. Every man in such a group is being supplied with language by the others, and the supply is more effective than any list of feeling words, because it arrives attached to a real situation.

### What to Expect in the First Months

Three experiences are so common in the first months of this work that they should be described in advance.

**Feeling gets worse before it gets better.** When a man stops suppressing, the material he has been holding becomes available. This is not evidence that acknowledging feelings is harmful; it is evidence that the store was large. It is at this stage that having a professional to talk to is most valuable, and Appendix Q explains how to find one.

**Other people notice before he does.** Wives, children, and colleagues frequently observe the change — more patience, more directness, occasionally more irritability — before the man himself believes any change has occurred.

**Old losses come forward.** It is extremely common for a man beginning this work to encounter a grief from ten or twenty years earlier, sometimes with considerable force. This is not a side effect to be avoided; it is the work. Chapter 30 provides the structured method, and Appendix K is written specifically for it.

### When to Seek Professional Support

This chapter has described a method that works for men whose difficulty is primarily one of habit and vocabulary. It is not sufficient for every situation. Professional support should be sought without delay where any of the following applies: the distress is interfering with work, sleep, eating, or ordinary functioning for more than a few weeks; there are symptoms of depression or an anxiety disorder as described in Chapter 6 or Appendix B; there is any thought of harming oneself or another person; there is a history of trauma that is being re-experienced; or alcohol or other substances are being used to manage the feelings. In such cases, the work of building vocabulary continues, but it is done in company with a qualified professional, and the reader should not attempt it alone.

## 3.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Left Standing

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns commonly described in bereavement counselling, family systems work, and research on male grief.

**Yakubu was thirty-six when his wife, Aisha, died in childbirth.** Their daughter survived. He was the one who received the news at the hospital reception, the one who telephoned Aisha's parents, the one who arranged the burial, the one who answered the questions of nearly two hundred visitors over four days, and the one who, in accordance with his community's custom, was expected to remain composed because he was now solely responsible for two other children as well as the newborn.

He did not cry. He wept once, briefly, on the sixth day, in the bathroom, and then stopped himself because there were people in the house. After the mourning period he returned to work eleven days earlier than his employer had offered. Within four months he had reorganised the household: a cousin came to help with the children, he moved his eldest son to a school closer to home, and he took on additional weekend work to cover the school fees.

Over the next six years, Yakubu was described by everyone as an example. His daughter grew healthy; his eldest son gained admission to a polytechnic; he was promoted twice. Two things were happening inside him, and he did not connect either of them to Aisha's death.

The first was a dissociative blankness on the subject. He could not discuss his wife. When relatives mentioned her, he changed the subject within two sentences. He had not visited the grave since the burial, and he avoided a particular road, because the last hospital was on it. He believed this was appropriate: a man moves on.

The second was a creeping flatness in everything else. He no longer played football on Sundays. He stopped attending the association meeting to which he had belonged for years. He found that he did not enjoy his children's achievements as much as he wanted to, and this disturbed him privately, because he believed it meant that he had become a cold man. At a colleague's wedding, four years after the death, he found himself unable to speak to the couple for more than a minute, and he left early without explanation.

The turn came when his daughter, then six, asked him, in the simple way that children do, why they had never visited her mother's grave. He answered that it was far away. She said that it was not far away, because her grandmother had described the road. That night, he did not sleep. In the following weeks he spoke to a counsellor at a hospital for the first time, and what emerged over several months was the material that had been carried in silence since the day of the burial: that he had never been told that Aisha would die; that the decision to proceed had been made in his absence; that his last words to her, in the morning, had been about a missing rent receipt; and that he had wept for eleven seconds in a bathroom in order to remain a strong man in front of a house full of visitors.
:::

The case contains four elements worth isolating.

**The role assignment was made by the situation, and he accepted it as a duty.** Nobody told Yakubu not to grieve. He inferred it from the circumstances, from custom, and from the absence of any person whose function was to look after him while he did.

**The suppression was effective in the short term and destructive in the long term.** He met every criterion of functioning: work, children, promotion, household order. The costs appeared elsewhere — in the loss of pleasure, in avoidance of a road, in the inability to be present at a wedding, in a growing belief that he had become cold.

**The grief did not require an occasion to return.** It required a question from a child. Material of this kind waits, with patience, and it re-emerges when the environment permits.

**The intervention was available and not exotic.** He spoke to a hospital counsellor. Chapter 35 describes exactly how such an approach is made, and Appendix Q lists the routes available in Nigeria and elsewhere for men who do not know where to begin.
