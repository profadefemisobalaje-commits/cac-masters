## 11.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Twelve Years Behind

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in career counselling, family practice, and research on unemployment and its psychological effects.

**Chidi is forty-three, a graduate of a federal university in a science discipline, and he now runs a small borehole-drilling operation with two employees.** He has been married for eleven years and has three children.

At the age of twenty-five, Chidi's expectations were specific and widely shared by his family. He intended to obtain a postgraduate qualification, enter a research institution or an oil-sector company, and be established by his mid-thirties. His family held the same expectation, and it was expressed at every gathering in the standard form of encouragement and comparison.

Four events interrupted the plan, and each was individually reasonable. On completing his national service, he was required at home for two years because his father's health had failed and there was no income in the household other than his. He then obtained a job in a bank, which paid well and required everything, and which made further study impractical. In the fourth year, the bank restructured, and his department was closed. He then used his savings and a loan to start a business, which failed within eighteen months because a major client defaulted on a large contract. After the collapse, he spent three years in informal work — transport, supply, small contracts — before beginning the borehole operation, which has now been stable and profitable for four years.

At forty-three, Chidi supports his household, employs two men, has built a house in his home area with a mortgage from a cooperative society, and has educated his two older children to secondary level. His younger brother, who followed the original plan without interruption, works for an international organisation, and his eldest sister is a lecturer.

The family conversation continues, and Chidi's position in it is settled: he is the one who did not fulfil his potential. The intention of the family is affectionate and its assessment is entirely comparative. At the last funeral, an uncle said, with genuine warmth and in front of a group, *Chidi would have been a director by now if he had stayed in the bank.*

What the family does not know is what the sentence does. For six years, Chidi has carried a settled sense of having failed, built from three elements: the sense of being twelve years behind his own plan; the memory of the failed business, which he has never discussed with anybody; and the continuous comparison with his brother and sister, with whom he is affectionate and whom, in private, he has begun to avoid.

The consequences have been specific. He does not attend the family's development association meetings as regularly as before. He has become unusually sensitive about money in conversations with his wife. He declined an offer to supply equipment to a construction company that would have doubled the size of his operation, because the financing required a guarantee from his brother, and he was unwilling to be in a position where a failure would be known. And he has, for three years, resisted the suggestion that he should train his eldest employee to supervise works so that he can reduce his own hours, on the grounds that the work is not done properly if he is not present.

The turn came through an ordinary conversation with a customer, an older man who had himself restarted twice, and who asked him a question that he had evidently asked himself at some point: *What is your plan from here?* Chidi, who is not given to disclosure, found himself describing the last eighteen years in about four minutes, including the failed business and the brother he avoided. The customer's response was short: *You have spent eighteen years working and you have a business, a house, three children and a wife. Who told you that the only measurement is the one your family keeps?*

Chidi began, over the following months, to do three things: he wrote down his own standard for the next decade; he attended the association meeting with a contribution he could afford and decided not to comment on it; and he trained his employee to supervise works, which took nine months and resulted, in the following year, in his hours falling by about a fifth. He did not resolve his relationship with his brother, and he does not pretend that he has. What he said, in a conversation two years later, was: *The failed business cost me four years. Comparing myself with my brother has cost me fifteen, and he never asked me to.*
:::

Five features of the case deserve isolation.

**The plan was reasonable and the interruptions were not failures.** Chidi's original plan was coherent; the interruptions were a father's illness, an employer's restructuring, and a client's default. None of the four events was a consequence of his judgement, and their cumulative effect determined the course of his working life.

**The comparative assessment was delivered affectionately and continuously.** The uncle's remark was intended as a compliment about Chidi's capacity. The mechanism by which such remarks produce harm is worth noting: the harm comes not from malice but from repetition, from the presence of an audience, and from the fact that the assessment is made in the only terms available to the family.

**The costs appeared as opportunities declined, not as failures observed.** The declined equipment contract, the untrained employee, the avoided brother, and the three years of resistance to delegating are the real costs of the case, and none of them is visible to the family that produced the assessment.

**The intervention was a single question from an outsider.** The customer asked about the plan, which is the question that converts a vague sense of being behind into a specific consideration of what is next. The pattern recurs throughout this book, and it is the reason Appendix J and Chapter 27 place such emphasis on maintaining at least one relationship outside the reference group.

**The resolution was specific and partial.** Chidi wrote a standard, adjusted his participation, and delegated a task. He did not resolve his position in the family, and he did not reconcile fully with his brother. Recovery of this kind is usually partial and practical rather than complete and emotional, and men should expect it to be.

## 11.8 Practical Exercise: The Personal Definition of Success

This exercise takes between ninety minutes and two hours, and it is best completed in one sitting and then reviewed annually.

### Part One: The Inherited Definition

Write out, in about 300 words, the definition of success that you absorbed. Answer specifically:

- What did your family expect you to become by the age of thirty, forty, and fifty?
- What did your community treat as the markers of having done well?
- What did the men you admired in your early twenties have that you were expected to obtain?
- What has been said to you, at gatherings, about how you compare with contemporaries?

Do not evaluate any of this yet. The purpose of the first part is to establish what standard has been operating, in writing, in the man's own account rather than in his general sense of pressure.

### Part Two: The Audit

Answer five questions about the inherited definition.

1. **Which elements do I still hold deliberately, and which do I carry without having chosen them?**
2. **Which elements are calibrated to circumstances that no longer exist** — a career ladder, an economy, a family arrangement?
3. **What have I achieved that the inherited definition does not recognise?** This is the most important question in the exercise, and men almost always find that the list is longer than they expected.
4. **What have I sacrificed to meet the inherited definition, and was the sacrifice worth it?**
5. **Which element of it am I still failing to meet, and what is it costing me to keep the element running?**

### Part Three: The Personal Definition

Write your own definition across the seven domains in Table 11.1, in specific and verifiable terms, in no more than 400 words. Include three additional elements:

- **The ceiling.** The level at which you would consider yourself to have arrived: a figure, a state, a condition. A definition without a ceiling can never be met.
- **The boundary.** What you are not willing to sacrifice: health, marriage, children's regard, rest, integrity. Write it explicitly.
- **The timeline.** What you intend to have done in three years, and in ten.

### Part Four: The Application

Apply the definition to three live decisions in your life — an obligation you are considering accepting, a role or opportunity you are considering pursuing, and an expenditure or commitment you are maintaining. For each, answer: does this move me towards my own standard, or towards somebody else's ranking?

### Part Five: The Annual Review

Set a date, twelve months from today, and write into your calendar what you will review: the seven domains, the boundary, and the timeline. The review is the mechanism by which the definition becomes operational rather than ornamental, and Appendix I provides the extended version of this work, including the values and purpose exercises that accompany it.

## 11.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it wrong to want more than my father had?"**

No, and the ambition is frequently the origin of a family's progress. The distinction is between wanting more than a previous position, which is progress, and wanting more than a contemporary, which is comparison. The first has a ceiling and delivers satisfaction when it is reached; the second does not.

**"How do I deal with family members who constantly compare me with others?"**

By holding your own standard in the conversation. A single sentence, delivered calmly and repeated as necessary, does most of the work: *My plan is X, and I am on schedule for it.* You will not persuade them, and the objective is not persuasion; it is to prevent the conversation from being conducted exclusively in their terms. Where a specific relative's remarks are continuous and corrosive, reducing exposure to that person is a legitimate decision rather than a family failure.

**"I am forty-five and I have not achieved what I planned. Is it too late?"**

The answer depends on what the plan was, and the useful exercise is to test the plan's elements individually for feasibility rather than treating the plan as a single verdict. Men frequently discover that one or two elements remain entirely feasible, that one requires a different version of the same objective, and that one is no longer desirable. Chapter 28 addresses the revision of abandoned ambition directly.

**"Should I take on more work to catch up?"**

Only where the additional work has a defined purpose and an end date, and only where the costs are named in advance. Additional work without a defined purpose is the most common response to the sense of being behind, and it produces the collapse described in Chapter 12 without closing the gap.

**"What if my definition of success conflicts with what my family expects of me?"**

Then you will have to hold both, and the holding requires two things: a clear statement of your own plan, delivered without apology, and a deliberate decision about which expectations you will continue to meet. This is the same method as the boundaries described in Chapter 10, applied to status rather than money, and it is exercised in Appendix O.

**"I do not know what I want. How do I start?"**

Begin with the domains rather than with a life plan. Answer the seven questions in Table 11.1 for the next twelve months only. Men who cannot describe a life plan can almost always describe twelve months, and the annual iterations produce a plan.
