## 25.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Written Off

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in vocational counselling, family practice, and community affairs.

**Emeka is fifty-two, a refrigeration and air-conditioning technician, married to Ifeoma for twenty-four years, with four children between the ages of thirteen and twenty-two.**

Eleven years ago, Emeka was employed as a maintenance supervisor for a company that operated a chain of supermarkets. In the third year of that employment, the company was taken over, the maintenance function was outsourced, and Emeka was made redundant as part of a group of about sixty staff. He was forty-one, he had a school certificate and an apprenticeship, and he had no formal professional qualification.

Two events in the following period determined the course of the case.

The first concerned his efforts. Over the following two years, Emeka submitted approximately 190 applications, obtained four interviews, and received no offer. He worked in the interval as an air-conditioning installation contractor on a per-job basis, which produced an income of about half his former salary and which fluctuated substantially. He also completed two short technical courses, neither of which altered the position.

The second concerned the response of his environment, which he described precisely. At his father's funeral, in the second year after the redundancy, a relative observed, in the presence of the family, that Emeka was *the one who used to be with the big company*. In the fourth year, a relative who had previously sought his advice on property matters began to consult his younger brother instead. In the fifth year, the family's contribution to a development levy was reduced to a level set by the association without reference to him, which he subsequently learned had been described as an allowance for his circumstances. In the eighth year, a proposal concerning the family land was discussed and concluded without his being consulted.

Emeka's own account of this period identified three elements. He had, over about four years, stopped attending the association's monthly meetings, which he described as *not wanting to sit in the room where they have decided what I am*. He had reduced the household's expenditure and had withdrawn two of his children from a school that the household could no longer fund, which he regarded as the only failure about which he was certain. And he had, by the sixth year, formed the conclusion that he was *finished*, which he defined in the assessment as follows: *I meant that I had stopped expecting anything to change. I did my jobs. I did not plan.*

Two interventions, which came at approximately the same period and from different directions, produced the change.

The first came through a technical channel. In the seventh year after the redundancy, a large installation project in the city required a site supervisor with experience of commercial refrigeration, and the contractor, who had worked with Emeka fifteen years earlier, approached him. Emeka supervised the project for nine months at a day rate that was approximately two and a half times his contracting income. The project led to two further engagements, and by the second year he was supervising approximately four projects annually, with two apprentices working under him.

The second came through a professional association. On the recommendation of the same contractor, Emeka joined a national body for refrigeration technicians, which required an examination for membership and which provided, in addition to the credential, a network of practitioners in the trade. He sat the examination in the second year and passed. His account of the association's effect was specific: *In that room I am not the man who lost his job. I am the one who has done more cold-room installations than most of them, and they know it because they have been on those sites. Nobody in that room has ever asked me what company I work for.*

The reconstruction that resulted had four components, each of which he identified separately.

**The record.** Emeka compiled, on the association's advice, a written record of eleven years of completed work: the installations, the sites, the clients, the fault diagnoses, and the two apprentices trained. He described this as the single most significant action of the whole period, and its effect was specific: *I had eleven years in which I worked every week and I had come to believe that I had not done anything. I wrote down 340 jobs. I had never counted them.*

**The measure.** With the record as the basis, he wrote out his own standard, which consisted of five items: every installation commissioned properly or not paid for; no work concealed from a client; the apprentices taught properly; the household's obligations met to the extent possible and any shortfall disclosed rather than hidden; and no participation in any arrangement that misrepresents what he had done. He described the standard as the reason the position with his family changed, because he could state his position in a sentence without apology.

**The re-entry.** In the third year, Emeka resumed attending the association's meetings of his community's development association. He described his reasoning as follows: *I decided that I would attend whether or not they had decided what I am.* At the meeting of the fifth year, a proposal concerning the family land was raised, and Emeka stated his position on it and was heard. He was subsequently appointed to the association's works committee, and, in the seventh year, was its chairman.

**The disclosure to the household.** The final element, and the one Emeka described as the most difficult. He explained to Ifeoma and to the two children who had been withdrawn from school the position of the intervening years: the 190 applications, the conclusion that he was finished, and the reason he had become silent in the house. He described the outcome as follows: *The children did not know that I had applied for anything. They thought I had stopped trying. My daughter told me, at that meeting, that she had believed I did not care about her school, and she had been carrying that for eleven years.*

Emeka's summary, at the end of the case, was: *I lost a job at forty-one and I was written off, and I am not claiming that the writing off was my imagination. What I did not know was that I had agreed with it. For six years I carried their verdict and I did not count the 340 jobs. The record and the room full of technicians were worth more than every application I ever submitted.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The verdict was administered socially, in specific events that could be dated.** The remark at the funeral, the transfer of the consulting relationship, the reduced levy, the unconsulted discussion of the land. None of these was an act of cruelty and their cumulative effect was to relocate the man in the community's structure. This is the machinery of Sections 25.1 and 25.2 operating in its ordinary form, and the case is included because the mechanism is frequently invisible to the man's household and to the man himself.

**The man's interioration of the verdict was the decisive event.** Emeka's conclusion that he was finished was formed in his own assessment, and it produced the withdrawal, the reduction of his ambitions, and the silence in his household. The case's central teaching is the finding of Section 25.2, that a public judgement becomes a private identity when it is held without challenge, and the period between the verdict and the interioration was in this case about four years.

**The record was the instrument.** Three hundred and forty jobs, written down for the first time in eleven years. The instrument's function is described in Section 25.3: it converts a man's unrecorded effort into evidence, and the evidence is not subject to the audience's opinion. Men in comparable situations frequently describe the counting as the moment at which the position became visible to them, and the case is included for that reason.

**The recognition came from people who could assess the work.** The professional association's members knew the installations, the sites, and the quality of the work, and their assessment was therefore substantive rather than social. This is the reference group of Section 25.3 in operation, and the case illustrates its specific value: recognition from a competent audience is different in kind from recognition from a general one, and it is frequently available to men to whom the general audience has closed.

**The disclosure to the household corrected eleven years of misattribution.** The daughter's statement — that she had believed her father did not care about her school — is the cost of the concealment described in Chapters 9 and 17, and the case is included because this misattribution is systematic: in the absence of information, a man's withdrawal is interpreted by his children as a statement about them.
