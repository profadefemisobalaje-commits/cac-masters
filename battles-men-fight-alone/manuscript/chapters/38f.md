## 38.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I have not told my wife. Is there any way to avoid that?"**

The chapter's position is that the household's arrangements depend on the position, and that the household is constructing an account of the period in any case from the reduced expenditure, the man's state, and the changes it observes. The delay's cost is the material of Chapter 34.2: the account becomes more difficult to deliver, the household's arrangements are made on an incorrect basis, and the trust's restoration is a separate matter that requires the years. The men in the case material who delivered the disclosure with the statement and the plan report the household's response as oriented to the plan, and the response to the delay as directed at the concealment rather than at the position.

**"I cannot face the professionals. The bank, the practitioner, the creditors."**

The professional route is established to conduct exactly this work, and its function is that the man is not required to conduct the negotiations personally. The practice material records the finding consistently: the practitioner's engagement is generally followed within weeks by a reduction in the position's size and in the man's own state, and the element men most frequently identify as decisive is that the position became a matter of figures held by somebody who was not ashamed of it.

**"If I take the employment at half of my former income, everyone will see that I have failed."**

The audience will see what the man's conduct over the following years supplies. The material of Chapter 25.6's three prohibitions applies, and the case material is consistent: the men who took the reduced position and rebuilt describe the period of the reduced position as the point at which the recovery began, and the men who refused it describe the years of the attempt to restore the former form.

**"My family expects me to continue the contributions. I cannot."**

The material of Chapters 33.4 and 38.4's fifth section applies: the obligations are listed, the disclosure is made once with the arrangement, and the reduction is stated in specific terms. The case material records the finding plainly: the family's capacity is generally present and unexamined, and the collapse is frequently the occasion on which the family's contributions appear. Where they do not, the man has conducted the disclosure accurately and the obligation is a matter of the family's record rather than of his concealment.

**"How long does the reconstruction take?"**

The stages' indicative durations are supplied in Section 38.5: the stabilisation in three to six months, the income in six to eighteen, the settlement in one to five years, the standing in two to five, and the relationships and the new construction continuously. The men in the case material describe the whole in terms of four to seven years for the practical position and longer for the standing. The reader should note the finding of Section 38.1: the first three months govern the starting position, and that is the period in which the chapter's instruction is the most valuable.

**"What if the position is genuinely irrecoverable — the health, the capacity, the age?"**

Then the material of Chapters 24, 28, and 36 applies in its full form, and the reconstruction is conducted on the capacities that remain rather than on the capacities that were lost. The practice material records the finding in the cases of men whose capacity was removed by injury or illness: the reconstruction is constructed on a reduced position, on the man's remaining capacities, and on the arrangements of Parts VI and X, and the men's own accounts describe the outcome in the terms of Chapter 38.5's sixth stage. The chapter's instruction on the point is that the recounting of the capacities in Section 38.4's fourth section is not a formality, and that a man who has concluded that he has nothing should have the section reviewed by the ally and the professional before accepting his own assessment.

::: summary
**Chapter 38 in brief.** A total collapse is a loss of the man's standing arrangements in two or more domains at once, in a manner that has removed the structure through which he would ordinarily address any one of them. Its forms are the business collapse, the employment collapse, the household collapse, the health collapse, the legal or positional collapse, and the compound collapse. Its five features are that the instruments are gone, the obligations continue, the man's judgement is affected, the audience is real, and the shame is present — and its first three months govern the shape of the following years.

The first period has four tasks: safety, covering the man's life and his household's, with the safety plan of Chapter 34.5 where any item of the first group applies; information, the written statement of the position, with no decision of consequence taken before it exists; the triage of the obligations into the legally unavoidable, the negotiable, and the moral, with the moral obligations disclosed and scheduled rather than paid first; and one ally who knows the whole position and who is also told what the man is not to do. Seven errors must be avoided: concealment, panic borrowing, the rapid sale of assets, the isolated major decision, the revenge and litigation impulse, drinking and numbing, and the attempt to restore the former position immediately.

The reckoning is the written statement in six sections — the assets, the liabilities, the income and the requirements, the capacities, the obligations of standing, and what remains intact — completed with two sentences: the accurate description of the position, and the man's own account of the failure classified into the error, the wrong, and the tragedy. The reconstruction proceeds in six stages in a fixed order: the stabilisation of the position, the restoration of income, the settlement of the obligations, the restoration of standing, the restoration of the relationships and the household, and the new construction. The order is mechanical rather than temperamental, and its mechanism is arithmetic: the settlement requires the income, the standing requires the settlement, and the largest opportunities require the standing.

The reconstruction is conducted with the household and not around it. The household generally knows that something has occurred; it has its own losses; its members have their own conduct in the period and are frequently carrying more than the man knows; and the household's trust in the man's account of its affairs is a separate matter requiring its own restoration. The four arrangements are the disclosure with the statement and the plan, the household's own arrangements at the level the position permits, the household's role in the reconstruction, and the household's own people and rest. The two errors are the protective concealment and the reconstruction conducted as a private project. The reconstructed position is not the former position, and the men who have rebuilt describe its advantages in terms that they could not have described in advance: the accuracy of their account of themselves, their household's knowledge of them, the arrangements that did not exist before, and the reduction of the audience's authority over them.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the first-period audit. Which of the four tasks have you conducted, and which of the seven errors are you currently inclined toward?
2. Complete the statement's six sections. What did section one contain that you had forgotten, and what did section four contain that you had not counted?
3. Write the account of the failure in its three classes. Where have you been applying the wrong or the tragedy to a matter that was an error, or the reverse?
4. Complete the triage. Which Category C obligation have you been paying before Category A, and what has it cost?
5. Name the ally and record the sentence you have asked them to use with you. What is the decision you are inclined to take too quickly, and does the ally know it?
6. Write the household's four arrangements. What are the household's own losses, and what arrangements do its members require?
7. Which stage are you in, and what is the condition that must be met before the next stage's work is attempted?

## Further Reading

- **On insolvency and debt:** the practice literature on personal and business insolvency, on arrangements with creditors, and on debt advice, including the findings on which routes produce better outcomes and on the consequences of early as against late engagement with creditors.
- **On business failure:** the research on business failure and on the personal consequences for proprietors, including the findings on the financial and psychological effects, on the recovery of the proprietor's subsequent income, and on the factors that predict a better outcome.
- **On redundancy and unemployment:** the literature on job loss, including the findings on the effects on health and on the household, the evidence on the value of early re-employment even at reduced income, and the material on the reconfiguration of identity after a position is lost.
- **On rebuilding after disaster and adversity:** the research on recovery after major adversity, including the findings on the factors associated with recovery, on the role of the man's account of the event, on social support, and on the evidence concerning the reconstruction of identity.
- **Practical resources:** insolvency practitioners, debt advisers, and credit counselling; legal advice on guarantees, contract, and family property; employment services; family and relationship counselling; men's groups; and the material in Appendices D, P, and R.
