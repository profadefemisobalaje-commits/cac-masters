## 44.8 The Programme

The section supplies the chapter's material in a form that the reader can conduct. Its four instruments are the two ledgers, the review, the wisdom account, and the mentoring arrangement. Each is small, each is conducted in writing, and each is repeated rather than completed.

### Instrument One: The Two Ledgers

The instrument is a table with two columns, conducted for one significant event and repeated annually.

| The cost ledger | The value ledger |
|:---------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------|
| What the event took, stated specifically: the money, the years, the health, the relationship, the standing, the opportunity. | What the event supplied, stated with evidence: the information, the capacity, the party identified, the assumption that collapsed. |
| Who paid each item, and whether the payment was theirs to make. | What was done with each item, and what it produced. |
| What remains, and what would address it: treatment, accommodation, legal route, arrangement. | What has not yet been used, and what use would require. |

The instrument's rules are the four of Section 44.1's closing material. The ledgers are recorded separately; neither cancels the other; every entry in the value ledger is evidenced; and the remaining costs are accompanied by an action.

### Instrument Two: The Review

The review is conducted on one event, within the second to eighth week where possible, and it is written.

**Question One: The chronology.** What happened, in sequence, without adjectives: the dates, the decisions, the parties, the information available at each point.

**Question Two: The decisions.** What did I decide, and on what information? For each decision, record what I knew, what I did not know, and what I assumed.

**Question Three: The cost.** What did it cost, in figures where the cost is financial and in specific terms where it is not, and to whom?

**Question Four: The design faults.** What in my arrangements, habits, information, incentives, or household's structure made this possible? Record at least two, and record them as design faults rather than as character faults.

**Question Five: The alteration.** What will be different in the next twelve months? Record one structural change, with a date and a measure. Where the event was not a mistake, record the change in the form *what would reduce my exposure to this*, rather than in the form of a fault.

The review is conducted once, dated, and filed. Subsequent attention to the event is conducted as an application of the fifth question.

### Instrument Three: The Wisdom Account

The wisdom account is the chapter's central instrument, and it is conducted in four parts.

**Part One: The event, in eight sentences.** The account is limited deliberately. The case material's finding is that the limit forces the selection of the material that matters and removes the performance from the account.

**Part Two: What it cost, and what remains.** The cost ledger's summary, with the remaining cost and the action that addresses it.

**Part Three: What it taught, stated as instruments.** Five entries, each in the form: *I learned that [specific thing], and I have used it to [specific act].* Where an entry cannot be completed with a use, it is recorded as *learned and not yet used*, and the reader should note the entries in this category, because they are the chapter's most valuable unfinished work.

**Part Four: Who has used it.** The parties to whom the man has supplied the account or the instrument, and what they did with it. Where the answer is nobody, the reader should conduct Section 44.4's third domain within the following quarter.

### Instrument Four: The Mentoring Arrangement

The arrangement is stated in one page and contains six items.

**Item One: The party.** Who the man is mentoring, and how the arrangement arose.

**Item Two: The functions.** Which of the five functions of Section 44.5 he is supplying: presence, information, accompaniment, the limit, continuity. The reader should record the functions he is not supplying, since the case material's finding is that mentors frequently supply information and omit presence.

**Item Three: The boundaries.** The times, the frequency, the scope, the stated limits of confidentiality, and the review date.

**Item Four: The object.** What the arrangement is conducted around: the rebuilding plan, the qualification, the business's reconstruction, the marriage's repair, the return to work.

**Item Five: The mentor's own support.** Who the mentor speaks to about the arrangement, and when.

**Item Six: The prohibition.** The four practices of Section 44.5 that the mentor will not conduct, stated explicitly, because the case material's finding is that they are conducted by men with the best intentions and without any awareness that they are conducting them.

### The Four Tests

The programme's measures are the four tests of Section 44.4's closing material, and they are recorded annually.

The first is what the man does differently: a list of arrangements, decisions, or practices that did not exist before the event.

The second is what he can now do: a list of competences that the difficulty supplied.

The third is who has used it: the parties to whom the material has been transmitted and what they did with it.

The fourth is what it still costs and what is being done about that cost: an honest statement of the remaining cost and the treatment, accommodation, or arrangement that addresses it.

::: exercise
**Exercise 44: The Wisdom Account and the Bitterness Audit**

The exercise has four parts, and it should be conducted across two sittings of not less than one hour each. It requires paper, and it should not be conducted on a screen.

**Part One: The two ledgers.** Choose one event from your life that was significant, that was difficult, and that is at least two years past. Conduct the two-ledger table of Instrument One. Record the cost in specific terms, including the parties who paid it and whether the payment was theirs to make. Record the value in specific terms, with evidence, and record what has been used and what has not. Where an entry in the value ledger cannot be evidenced, strike it out and record the fact that you cannot evidence it.

**Part Two: The review.** Conduct the five questions of Instrument Two on the same event, in writing. Where the event was not a mistake, conduct the fourth question in the exposure form. Record the single alteration, with a date and a measure, and record the date on which you will review whether it was conducted.

**Part Three: The bitterness audit.** Examine your own record against the five components of Section 44.6's anatomy, with evidence. (1) What was the injury, and was it real? (2) Whose acknowledgement are you waiting for, and for how long? (3) What is on your ledger of the owed, and what is its total? (4) Which fields has your attention narrowed to, and what have you ceased to perceive? (5) What evidence has your own conduct produced that confirms your account, and who has been tested and failed? Record the component that is strongest in your record. Then state, in writing, the second step of the exit: what you are owed, and what you will do if it is not paid.

**Part Four: The mentoring arrangement.** Conduct Instrument Four for one young man or one man in difficulty whom you are already in a position to assist. Where no such party exists, conduct the instrument for the party you would be willing to assist, and state the arrangement to him within the following month. Record the functions you are supplying and the functions you are not, and record the prohibition of the four practices in your own words.

At the end of the two sittings, record three sentences: the cost that is still being paid, the instrument that the event supplied, and the party who will receive it.

## 44.9 Questions Readers Ask

**"My difficulty was not a mistake. Somebody did this to me. Does this chapter apply?"**

It applies, and the section that applies is the one on bitterness rather than the one on mistakes. The chapter's material does not require the man to have caused his difficulty, and the case of Section 44.7 is a case in which the injury was real and the dismissal was subsequently set aside.

The specific application for the reader whose difficulty was caused by another party is the following. The review's first, third, and fifth questions are conducted in full. The second and fourth are conducted in the modified forms supplied in Section 44.2's closing material: the second as a record of what was decided on the information available, which is frequently *I decided correctly*, and the fourth as a question about exposure rather than fault. The bitterness audit is conducted in full, because the anatomy of Section 44.6 applies most directly to injuries that were caused by other parties.

**"I do not want to be told that my suffering was worth it. Is that what this chapter is saying?"**

No, and the distinction is stated at the chapter's beginning. The cost ledger and the value ledger are separate accounts, and neither cancels the other. The chapter's position is that the cost was real, that it was frequently unjust, and that it is not repaid by anything the man learned. The value ledger is a separate account, it is available whether or not the cost was deserved, and its extraction is worth conducting because the alternative is to pay the cost and receive nothing.

The reader who is told by another party that his suffering was worth it should note that the statement is frequently made in order to close a conversation that the speaker finds difficult. The statement is not the chapter's position, and the man is entitled to say so.

**"I have conducted the review and I found nothing. What does that mean?"**

It means one of three things, and the reader should establish which.

The first is that the review was conducted too early, in the first days of the event, when the chronology is not yet complete and the emotion governs the material. The correction is to conduct it again in the second month.

The second is that the review was conducted as a verdict or as an exoneration, in the forms of Section 44.2's four errors. The signature is the presence of adjectives about the man or about other parties, and the absence of any description of a decision or an arrangement. The correction is to conduct the chronology as a list of dates and decisions, without evaluation, and to answer the fourth question with a design fault.

The third is that the event was genuinely one to which the man contributed nothing and from which he can extract nothing except the exposure information. This is a real category, it is smaller than most men believe, and where it applies the review's output is the exposure question and the alteration that reduces the exposure.

**"How do I know whether I am grieving or bitter?"**

The distinction is supplied in Section 44.6's closing material, and it is stated here in its practical form. Grief comes in waves, and the man's account of his present position remains accurate between them. Bitterness is steady, and the man's account of his present position is organised by the injury's categories.

The three questions that distinguish them are: does the difficulty arrive in waves, or is it present continuously? Does my account of the injury remain fixed, or does it alter as I learn more? Am I waiting for a party to acknowledge something, and has the waiting organised my conduct?

Where the answers indicate grief, the material of Chapter 30 applies, and the man should not attempt to conduct the grief as a decision. Where the answers indicate bitterness, the exit of Section 44.6 applies.

**"I have mentored three young men and all three have withdrawn. What am I doing wrong?"**

The pattern is common and it is frequently not a fault. The case material records three causes, and the reader should examine his own record against each.

The first cause is the mentor's timetable. A young man frequently requires a period before he can conduct a plan, and a mentor who supplies the plan in the first week is experienced as a demand. The correction is presence without agenda for the first month.

The second cause is the prescription. A young man who is told what to do and who cannot do it will frequently withdraw rather than report the failure. The correction is the question, which leaves the judgement with the young man and the information with the mentor.

The third cause is the arrangement's object. A relationship that exists in order to discuss the young man's difficulty has no other content, and it exhausts itself. The correction is the object of Section 44.5's third condition: the arrangement is conducted around a task, and the task supplies the relationship with ordinary material.

Where none of the three applies, the reader should consider the fourth possibility, which the case material records and which is not the mentor's fault: the young man's withdrawal is frequently a stage, and a proportion of young men return after a period of months or years. The mentor's task is to remain available without requiring the return.

**"Is it possible to become wiser without suffering?"**

Yes, and the proposition is not a paradox. A man acquires the material of this chapter through difficulty of some kind, and not every difficulty is severe. The ordinary difficulties of work, marriage, parenthood, and professional failure supply a great deal of material to a man who conducts the review. The case material records men who conducted the material on moderate difficulties and acquired substantial competence, and men who survived severe difficulties and acquired none.

The finding is that the variable is not the difficulty's size. It is the review's conduct, the attribution's accuracy, the conversion's application, and the mentoring's transmission. A man who conducts these on the ordinary difficulties of a professional life will acquire more than a man who survives a catastrophe and conducts none of them.

**"What if the party who injured me is dead?"**

Then the ledger cannot be settled by acknowledgement, and the exit's second and third steps apply without the first party. The case material records two practices for this position, and neither requires the dead party's participation.

The first is the written statement, conducted as a letter to the party, which is written and not sent. The practice is the material of Chapter 30.5's letters, and its finding is that the writing produces the separation of the claim from the man's conduct more reliably than any internal decision.

The second is the conduct of the injury as information for the living: the account supplied to the parties who can use it, which is Section 44.4's third domain. The finding is that the transmission frequently completes what the acknowledgement could not, because the man's experience becomes useful rather than owed.

**"I am still in the middle of the difficulty. Can I conduct any of this now?"**

Yes, and the chapter has stated the availability at each stage. In the first weeks, the chronology is conducted, because the record of dates, decisions, and information is easiest to make while the material is fresh. The cost ledger is begun and not concluded. The fourth test of proportion — what else is in my life at this moment — is conducted, because it is the test that the man in the middle of a difficulty most requires. The stabilisation material of Chapter 38.4 is conducted in full, and it precedes everything else.

The review's full conduct, the wisdom account, and the mentoring arrangement are conducted after the position has stabilised. The case material's finding on the timing is that men who attempt the full review in the first month frequently conduct it as rumination, and men who defer it entirely for a year frequently lose the material.

## 44.10 Chapter Summary

**Chapter 44 in brief.** Battles do not make a man wiser automatically; they supply material, and the material becomes wisdom through a craft. The cost ledger and the value ledger are separate accounts, and neither cancels the other: the cost was real and is not repaid by anything learned, and the value is available whether or not the cost was deserved. What adversity supplies is accurate information about the world, accurate information about the man, the identification of his actual parties, the reduction of a fear that was larger than the event, and competences that no other route would have supplied. What it does not supply is meaning, permission, wisdom, community, or virtue.

The five myths are that what does not kill you makes you stronger, that a man needs a hard life, that suffering reveals the real man, that time heals, and that a wise man does not need help. Each is partly true and each is harmful in a specific way, and the correction of each is a distinction rather than a denial.

The review has five questions: the chronology; the decisions and the information available; the cost and who paid it; the design faults in the man's arrangements; and the single alteration for the next twelve months. Its four errors are the review as a court, as an exoneration, as an avoidance, and as a rumination. The attribution separates what belongs to the man from what belongs to other parties, to circumstance, and to chance, and it is stated in conduct rather than in character. Where the event was not a mistake, the review is conducted in its exposure form, and the finding is that such events frequently produce the most valuable changes.

Perspective is exercised by four tests: the test of time, of the audience, of the alternative, and of the whole. Maturity has six markers: the capacity to state a state without dramatising or denying it; to be corrected without collapse; to hold two true things at once; to delay the account; to act without certainty; and to be generous from a position of loss. Composure, endurance, detachment, and seniority are not the markers.

The conversion is conducted in three domains: the man's own life, through the alteration of an arrangement, the correction of a standing pattern, the revision of his account of himself, and the conduct of his health; his household, through the proportionate transmission of the lesson, the alteration of the household's arrangements, and the conduct of his own difficulty in the household's observation; and other men, through the instrument rather than the account, and through the warning as well as the testimony. The failure mode is the conversion of the suffering into an identity, whose five signs are that the account does not change, that the difficulty is presented before the present competence, that present difficulties are referred to the past one, that the man resists the resolution of the consequences, and that the account requires other parties' guilt.

Mentoring has five functions — presence, information, accompaniment, the limit, and continuity — and four prohibitions: the prescription, the comparison, the rescue, and the testimony. Its conditions are the boundary, the confidentiality, the object, the mentor's own support, and the possibility of a good ending.

Bitterness is a settled account in which the man's real injuries are the central fact, the parties who caused them are permanently in debt, and the future is pre-emptively discredited. Its anatomy is the injury, the unacknowledged cost, the ledger of the owed, the narrowing, and the confirming arrangement. It is not a moral failure, and its exit has five steps: the accurate statement, the separation of the claim from the future, the closure by an act the man controls, the reversal of the narrowing, and the interruption of the confirming arrangement.

::: summary
**The chapter's central claim.** The difference between a man who is made wiser by his battles and a man who is made harder by them is not the battles. It is whether he conducts the review, states the attribution, applies the proportion tests, converts the material into arrangements and competences, transmits it to another man, and separates his claim on the past from his conduct of the future. Each of these is a practice, each is small, and each is available to the reader this month.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Which event in your life has supplied the most material, and what has been extracted from it in instruments rather than in attitudes?
2. Which of the five myths do you still hold, and what does it permit you to avoid?
3. Conduct the two-ledger table for one event. Which column was easier to write, and what does that tell you?
4. What is the design fault that your review has disclosed, and what is the single alteration that addresses it?
5. Which of the four tests of proportion do you most frequently fail, and in what circumstances?
6. Which of the six markers of maturity is weakest in your record, and where did you last see it fail?
7. What in your history have you converted into an identity, and what would the account look like if the instrument were placed in front of the difficulty?
8. Whom are you owed, and for how long have you been waiting? What would the second step of the exit require you to write?
9. Which young man or man in difficulty could use what you have learned, and what is the smallest arrangement you could supply him within the next month?
10. If a man who knows you were asked to state what your difficulties have made you able to do, what would he say?

## Further Reading

- **On post-traumatic growth and its limits:** the research literature on post-traumatic growth, including the findings on the conditions under which growth is reported, the criticisms of self-reported growth, and the evidence on the coexistence of growth and continuing distress.
- **On rumination, reflection, and their difference:** the research literature on rumination and reflective processing, including the findings on the effects of repetitive negative thinking on mood and problem-solving, and on the conditions under which reflection produces learning rather than recurrence.
- **On attribution and learned helplessness:** the established literature on attributional style, explanatory style, and learned helplessness, including the findings on the effects of stable and global attributions on subsequent conduct, and on the modification of attributional style.
- **On wisdom as a measurable construct:** the psychological literature on wisdom, including the findings on its components, its relationship with age and experience, and the evidence that it is not an automatic consequence of either.
- **On forgiveness and its outcomes:** the research literature on forgiveness, including the findings on its effects on health and wellbeing, on the distinction between forgiveness and reconciliation, and on the conditions under which forgiveness is and is not associated with better outcomes.
- **On mentoring:** the research and practice literature on mentoring adults and young people, including the findings on the characteristics of effective mentors, the risks of poorly bounded relationships, and the effects of mentoring on both parties.
- **Practical resources:** clinical psychology and psychotherapy for unresolved grief and trauma; employee assistance and career transition services; mediation and legal advice where a claim is capable of pursuit; peer support and supervision arrangements for mentors; and the material in Appendices H, I, K, and P.
