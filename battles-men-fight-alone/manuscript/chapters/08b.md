## 8.3 The Influence of Childhood Criticism

Most men who carry persistent self-doubt can identify its origin with little difficulty, once they are asked the right question. The question is not *Were you criticised as a child?*, because almost everybody was. It is: *Was there a period in which your performance determined the emotional temperature of your home?*

### The Five Patterns That Produce It

Research and clinical experience point to five patterns. They frequently overlap, and a man may recognise several.

**Performance-conditional affection.** The child's experience that approval is received for what he does and withdrawn for what he fails to do. The pattern is rarely cruel. It frequently consists of ordinary behaviour: congratulations for results followed by silence, or a mild disappointment visible in a parent's face after a poor report, or the practical rewards of success and the absence of rewards for anything else. The child draws a reasonable conclusion: *I am valued for results, and therefore my worth varies with my performance.*

**A chronically dissatisfied or critical parent.** A parent who is never satisfied — whose response to success is the identification of what could have been better, and whose response to failure is a verdict on the child's character — produces a durable internal critic. It matters less whether the criticism was frequent than whether it was unpredictable and whether anything was ever sufficient.

**Comparing siblings or peers.** Explicit comparison — *your brother is not like this; look at your classmate's result* — is common in many cultures and is intended to motivate. Its effect is to establish an external standard against which the child is permanently measured, and to teach that the comparison never ends.

**Humiliation at school or among peers.** An experience of public exposure — a mocking teacher, a failure in front of the class, a punishment for an error, a bullying episode organised around a perceived deficiency — can produce a durable expectation of exposure that generalises far beyond the original situation.

**Adverse family circumstances that required the child to be useful.** Where a father dies, a mother is ill, a family loses its income, or parents separate, an older child is frequently drawn into responsibility earlier than his development would suggest, and learns that he is valued for what he contributes. The experience produces competence and, frequently, the permanent sense that his place in the family depends on his usefulness. Chapter 19 examines this in detail.

### What the Material Becomes in Adult Life

The specific statements of childhood frequently persist into adulthood in forms that the man no longer recognises as inherited. Four transformations are common.

**The statement becomes a rule.** *You will never amount to anything* becomes an adult operating rule: never be idle, never be without a plan, never be vulnerable to a verdict. The rule is followed as though it were a sound principle, and its origin is forgotten.

**The voice becomes generic.** The critic's sentences become the man's own inner commentary, spoken without an identifiable speaker. Men frequently cannot say whose voice it is until they are asked to write it down and read it aloud, at which point the resemblance — a phrase, a tone, a particular word — becomes apparent.

**The standard becomes impossible rather than high.** A parent who is never satisfied teaches a child that no result is sufficient. In adult life, this manifests as a standard that moves with each achievement, producing the spiral described in Chapter 7.

**The material becomes self-verifying.** A man who expects inadequacy to be exposed behaves in ways that make exposure more likely: he works excessive hours and burns out; he avoids situations that would develop his capability; he accepts responsibilities beyond his capacity to demonstrate worth; and he interprets ordinary correction as confirmation. The behaviours then produce evidence, and the original belief appears to have been correct all along.

### The Two Necessary Corrections

Two corrections are required, and neither involves blaming the parents, which is neither useful nor accurate in most cases.

**The first correction is of meaning, not of memory.** The events happened, and many of them were ordinary rather than abusive. What requires revision is the inference the child drew: that his worth was conditional on performance. In the great majority of cases the inference was the child's, formed from limited information, and adopted because it made sense of the world at the time. The adult has more information — including the fact that his parents were themselves operating under standards imposed on them — and can therefore revise the inference without falsifying the history.

**The second correction is of method.** If the internal standard cannot be removed, the process by which a man evaluates himself can be changed. This is the substance of Sections 8.5 and 8.6 and of Appendix H, and it is the part of the work that produces results, because it does not depend on resolving the past.

## 8.4 Negative Internal Dialogue

The internal critic operates through speech. It produces sentences, and the sentences have identifiable forms. Learning to notice them is the practical entry point to the whole subject.

### The Six Standard Forms

The following six patterns account for the majority of the automatic statements men report. They are adapted from the well-established cognitive literature on thinking traps, and they are worth memorising, because recognition precedes revision.

**All-or-nothing judgements.** *If it is not perfect, it is worthless. If I did not lead the project, I contributed nothing. If I lose this client, I am finished.* The pattern converts a spectrum of outcomes into two categories, one of which is failure.

**Magnification of the negative.** A single error, a single critical remark, a single poor result is given a weight far exceeding its significance, and it is placed at the centre of the man's attention.

**Minimisation of the positive.** *It was not difficult. Anyone with my resources would have done it. He was only being polite. The circumstances helped.* The practice is so habitual that it is frequently invisible to the man performing it.

**Mind-reading.** *They think I am not up to it. He is disappointed in me. She has lost confidence in me.* The man infers other people's judgements without evidence, and the inferences are consistently unflattering.

**Catastrophising.** *If this goes wrong, the family is finished. If they find the error, I will lose everything. If I fail this examination, my life is over.* The pattern takes a specific risk and attaches to it a total consequence.

**Labelling.** From a specific event, a permanent identity is inferred: *I am a fraud. I am useless. I am the kind of man who cannot manage.* The label is then used as evidence in subsequent judgements, which produces a closed system.

### Recognising Whose Voice It Is

A practical technique that men frequently report to be decisive consists of writing down the ten or fifteen sentences that the internal critic produces most often, and then asking four questions about each.

1. **Whose voice does this sentence sound like?** A parent, a teacher, a supervisor, a former spouse, a peer group, or the man himself.
2. **How old is the sentence?** Do these words date from the present, or from a period that has ended?
3. **Would I say this to my son, my daughter, or a young colleague?** If not, what does the refusal reveal?
4. **What is the evidence, and what would a fair observer conclude?** This is the corrective step, and it is developed in Section 8.5.

The second question is frequently the most powerful. Men who discover that the sentence now producing their daily distress was composed by a person who has been dead for a decade, or by a teacher whose name they barely remember, consistently describe the discovery as altering their relationship with the sentence. It does not remove it, but it demotes it from truth to testimony.

### The Rule About Disputation

A caution is necessary, because the methods in this chapter are frequently misapplied. The objective is **not** to replace every critical thought with a positive one. Positive affirmations that contradict a man's evidence base are rejected by him immediately and produce additional failure experience.

The objective is **accuracy**. A man revising a catastrophic prediction does not tell himself that everything will be fine; he establishes what would actually happen if the feared event occurred — usually a difficulty rather than a catastrophe — and he replaces the total claim with a specific one. This is the difference between confidence as a posture and confidence as an accurate estimate, and only the second survives contact with reality.
