::: summary
**Chapter 3 in brief.** The inability to express pain is not a natural difference between men and women; it is acquired, and it can be revised. Four mechanisms account for it: the early extinction of crying through differential treatment of boys; the substitution of anger for sadness, which produces men who experience sadness as irritation; the absence of an emotional vocabulary, which leaves a man knowing that something is wrong without any means of saying what; and the rational adult calculation of social cost, usually based on two or three memorable experiences in which expression produced a bad outcome.

Emotional suppression takes two forms. Expressive suppression means feeling something fully and preventing it from being visible. Experiential avoidance means preventing it from being experienced — the more consequential form, and the one that men who believe they are self-controlled are most likely to have. Its documented consequences include elevated physiological stress, impaired attention and memory, difficulty being read by others, increased somatic symptoms, spill-over into the emotional life of the household, and rebound at unpredictable moments.

Suppression is also a bargain, and the bargain is worth seeing clearly. It protects calm in the short term, standing at work, freedom from questions, and the ability to keep functioning after a loss. What it costs — intimacy, sleep, judgement, friendships, and unfinished grief — arrives later, which is why the trade looks favourable at the moment it is made.

Self-control and avoidance must be distinguished. Self-control requires awareness, a chosen delay with a date, attention to the body, eventual discharge of the emotion, and honesty with others. Avoidance requires none of these, and produces tension, forgetting, and absence. The scale set out in this chapter allows a reader to locate himself without accusation.

Crying is not a requirement of healthy masculinity; the ability to cry, when the occasion calls for it, is another matter, and its loss removes a route both to discharge and to comfort. The fear that tears will not stop is the fear of accumulated material, and it is usually evidence that the store is large.

Two conditions follow long suppression. Unprocessed grief is a specific loss that has not been assimilated, and it returns in waves, dreams, and avoidance of reminders. Emotional exhaustion is depletion from prolonged emotional labour without recovery, and it presents as flatness, low tolerance, and absence of reserve. They require different responses, and the load must be reduced before the grief is opened.

Vocabulary is rebuilt by practice: naming sensation before feeling, rating intensity on a scale, writing one honest sentence a day, and borrowing language from other men who have described the same state. In the first months, feeling frequently gets worse before it gets better, and old losses come forward. That is the work, not a complication of it — but where functioning is seriously impaired, where trauma is present, or where substances are being used, the work must be done alongside a qualified professional.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Which of the four mechanisms in Section 3.1 — early extinction, substitution of anger, absence of vocabulary, or calculated social cost — best explains your own relationship with emotional expression? Can you name the specific incident behind the fourth mechanism, if it applies?
2. On the self-control scale in Section 3.3, where do you currently place yourself in each of the following domains: marriage, children, work, money, health, and your own history? Which domain's position surprised you?
3. Table 3.1 sets out the bargain of suppression. Write down the two rows of that table that apply most directly to you, and state the year in which you began paying the price on the right-hand side.
4. When did you last cry? If the answer is measured in decades, what happened in that year, and what have you been doing with the feelings since?
5. Section 3.5 distinguishes unprocessed grief from emotional exhaustion. Which of the two fits you better, and what does the treatment order imply for your next step?
6. What is your honest answer to the question, *What is my body doing when I am under strain?* What would another person notice about you before you noticed it yourself?
7. To whom could you say one true sentence this week? If the answer is nobody, what does that tell you about the work ahead?

## Further Reading

- **On emotion and suppression:** the psychological literature on emotion regulation, including the distinction between reappraisal and suppression and research on the differing consequences of each. The work on suppression is unusually clear and is accessible to general readers through mainstream psychology publications.
- **On crying:** the small but interesting research literature on emotional tears, including studies of their physiological composition and of the social signalling function of weeping.
- **On male grief:** the literature on gender and bereavement, and specifically work on the concept of disenfranchised grief — grief that is not publicly recognised as legitimate. This is directly relevant to men whose losses, such as a miscarriage, a stillbirth, a lost pregnancy, a divorce, or the death of a friend, are not socially acknowledged.
- **On alexithymia:** the clinical literature on the difficulty of identifying and describing feelings, including its relationship to physical symptoms and to depression in men. Readers should note that the clinical category is more specific than the general experience discussed in this chapter and requires professional assessment.
- **On emotional vocabulary:** practical works on feelings vocabulary, including the technique of building a personal lexicon, drawn from counselling practice and from acceptance- and commitment-based approaches.
- **On bereavement support:** the guidance issued by national health bodies and by professional bereavement organisations, together with accounts by men who have spoken publicly about losses they carried privately for years.
