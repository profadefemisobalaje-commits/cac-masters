## 36.6 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Still There

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in mental health services, occupational health, and family practice.

**Chukwuemeka, called Emeka, is forty-nine, a security supervisor at a large market complex, married to Nkechi for twenty-one years, with three children aged twenty, seventeen, and eleven.**

The material at the centre of the case occurred in 2005, when Emeka was thirty, and it consisted of an armed robbery at a filling station in which he had been working as an attendant.

Emeka's account of the incident, given in the professional arrangement, was as follows: four men arrived at about nine in the evening, one remained at the entrance, and two entered the office. Emeka was on the forecourt and was struck on the head with the butt of a weapon and left on the ground. His colleague, a man named Uche, aged twenty-three, who was in the office, was shot and died at the scene. Emeka was hospitalised for three days for the head injury, attended the funeral, was interviewed by the police, gave evidence at a trial two years later, and returned to work at a different filling station within a month.

His description of the period that followed: *I did not sleep in a bed for two years. I slept sitting up with the light on. I told my family that it was the injury. My mother told me to pray about it, and I stopped mentioning it, because I did not want to be a man who was crying about something that was finished. Uche's mother came to the house three times in the first year and after that she stopped, and I have seen her in the market twice in nineteen years and I have walked the other way both times.*

The material's pattern over nineteen years, in Emeka's own account and in the record of the service that eventually saw him, was as follows.

**The sleep.** The disturbed sleep, which persisted with variation for the whole period, with periods of relief. The man described the specific finding: *I could not sleep in the dark for the first three years, and for the next fifteen I could not sleep on the side of the bed nearer the door.*

**The hypervigilance.** The man's conduct at his work, which was in security and which meant that the pattern was organised into his professional life. He was, in his own words, *the best man the company ever had at the gate*: he knew the vehicles, the routines, the small details, and he was unable to relax during any period of duty. His account: *I have been watching doors for nineteen years. The company thinks it is the personality. I have never been able to sit with my back to an entrance, and I have not been in that filling station's road since 2010.*

**The avoidance.** The filling station's road, the two encounters with Uche's mother, the subject of the incident, the subject of Uche, and the funeral arrangements of colleagues, which the man avoided for years with explanations about the shift.

**The anger at home.** The specific pattern in the household, recounted by Nkechi in a family consultation: the man's sudden and disproportionate rages, triggered by the children's coming in late, by a door left open, by a noise in the compound at night. Her account: *He would be calm and in the next minute he would be shouting about the gate. The children learned to come in before six. He was never like that before we married and I have never understood it and I do not ask him anything about that time any more.*

**The alcohol.** The man's drinking, which began as a means of sleeping and which had, by the tenth year, become a nightly pattern of about four to six units, with the increase in the years of the trials and the anniversary.

**The presentation.** Emeka was referred by the employer's occupational health service at forty-nine after two matters: a period of four months of reduced sleep and increased alcohol following the death of a colleague in a road accident, and an episode at the market's gate in which the man had intervened in a confrontation between traders, had been struck, and had experienced a period afterwards of several hours in which he could not remember where he was and believed he was at the filling station. The second matter was the one that produced the referral, and Emeka's own description of it was: *I was twenty-nine years old in my own head. I was on the ground and I could hear the shot and I could not get up.*

The treatment, conducted over fourteen months, had five elements.

**The assessment and the diagnosis.** The assessment identified the symptoms of long-standing traumatic exposure, together with a moderate depressive episode and a harmful pattern of alcohol use. The practitioner's description, recorded with permission and used in the case with details altered, was: *A man who has been at the scene of a killing for nineteen years, who has organised his entire professional conduct around predicting the repeat, who has never described what happened to a single person, and who has been treating the alarm as a feature of his personality.*

**The stabilisation.** The first two months were directed to the sleep, the alcohol, and the safety arrangements of the working setting, together with the establishment of the treatment relationship itself. Emeka described the period: *I was expecting that he would want to talk about the night and the first two months were entirely about the sleeping and the drinking and my shift. In the ninth week I told him that I had not said anything about the night and he said, we are not there yet, and I understood that somebody was holding the thing.*

**The trauma-focused treatment.** The work on the incident was conducted over about four months in a structured form, with the man describing the event and its elements in a sequence, in the session, at a pace set by him, together with the work on the man's interpretations of his own conduct. Emeka described the element of the work that mattered most: *The man asked me, in about the fourth month, what I believed I should have done at the gate in nine seconds with a head injury and two men armed, and I told him that I should have reached Uche, and he asked me to describe how that would have been done. I could not describe it. I sat there and I could not describe it, and then he asked me whether anybody else at the filling station had reached the office. I said no. He asked me whether Uche had been in the office with the door closed. I said yes. She was inside and the door was closed and I could not have been inside in the time. I had never once said that sentence in nineteen years because I had never let anybody ask me the question.*

**The alcohol work.** Conducted in parallel with the structured work of Chapter 22: the removal of the cue, the substitution, and the arrangements. The man's sleep improved as the work progressed and the drinking reduced to occasional use by the eighth month.

**The repairs and the conduct.** The elements that the man identified as the ones that altered his life, and they were conducted in the ninth to the fourteenth months. He gave his account to Nkechi in full, in a conversation that occupied an evening, with the four parts of Chapter 34.6: *She said the thing I did not expect. She said, so it is not the children. She had been carrying nineteen years of believing that I had become a man who was angry with his family, and the explanation had been in front of her the whole time.* He then visited Uche's mother, in the eleventh month, with his account and with an arrangement: a monthly contribution and a visit. His description: *She said she had waited for me. She said she had not understood why I had stopped coming and that she had thought that I had blamed her son for the night, and I told her what had actually happened and that I had blamed myself and that I had not been able to sit in her house. I go every month now. She is seventy-four.*

Emeka's summary, at the end of the treatment, was: *I was thirty years old on that forecourt and for nineteen years I was still there. I could not have done it without the man in the room asking me what I had been certain about. I was certain that I could have reached her. I have said the sentence about the closed door and the nine seconds out loud and I know what happened now. I sleep in the dark and I sleep on the side near the door. I go to Uche's mother's house. My children come in at night and I do not shout about the gate. My wife knows what it is. I am forty-nine years old and I have been a guard for twenty years and I am still careful, and I am careful for the right reason now, which is the job and not the night.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The material had been carried for nineteen years without a single disclosure.** The man had attended the funeral, given evidence at a trial, returned to work, married, raised three children, and never described the event to anybody. The case is included because the material in this category is frequently held for decades and because the man's own conduct — the functioning, the competence, the household — supplies no indication that a disclosure is required.

**The symptoms had been organised into the man's professional life.** The hypervigilance, the watchfulness, the inability to sit with his back to an entrance, and the exceptional performance in a security role. The case is included because this is the specific mechanism described in the research on traumatic exposure in men: the symptoms are frequently functional in the man's occupation and are therefore rewarded rather than identified.

**The household had constructed an account and the account was wrong in the man's own conduct.** *He was never like that before we married and I have never understood it and I do not ask him anything about that time any more*, and the discovery that the household's explanation was that the man had become angry with his family. This is the material of Chapter 34.6's third part in its most consequential form, and the case is included because the correction of the household's account was, in the man's own order of significance, the most important element of the treatment.

**The premise at the centre of the material was a belief about the man's own conduct, and it was never tested.** *I could have reached her.* The case is included because this is the finding that the structured work on trauma and the work of Chapter 29 both identify: the material's persistence is frequently maintained not by the event but by a conclusion the man has drawn about his own conduct and has never been required to describe. The practitioner's questions — how would it have been done, who else reached the office, was the door closed — are ordinary questions about a conclusion that had been held for nineteen years without examination.

**The recovery was conducted with the arrangements and not by the man alone.** The treatment relationship, the practitioner's work, the alcohol treatment, the household's disclosure, and the repair with the bereaved mother. The case is included as the chapter's clearest illustration of the sequence and the conditions: the stability first, the account second, the mourning and the repair third, and the reconnection — the monthly visit, the household's arrangements, the sleep on the side near the door — as the last and continuing element.
