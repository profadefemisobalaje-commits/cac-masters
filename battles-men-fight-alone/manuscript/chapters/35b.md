## 35.1 Why Asking Is Difficult for Men

The research on men's help-seeking identifies consistent barriers, and the following nine cover the great majority of the material men report. They are arranged in the order in which men describe them, and each is stated in the man's own terms.

### The Nine Barriers

**1. The meaning of asking.** The first and most powerful. For a large proportion of men, the act of asking is not a request; it is an admission. The man's understanding of the exchange is that he is stating that he cannot manage, which is a statement about his standing as a man, a provider, a father, or a professional. The barrier's force is that it is not a prediction about the recipient's response but a conclusion about the act's meaning, and it therefore applies regardless of who is asked.

**The correction that applies:** the distinction between the man's whole capacity and the specific matter. A man does not fail to manage because he requires a competence he does not possess in a domain in which it does not reside. The distinction is examined in Section 35.2.

**2. The anticipated loss of standing.** The prediction that the recipient, or the setting, will regard the man differently afterwards. Men report the prediction in specific terms: the colleague who will regard him as unreliable, the wife who will regard him as reduced, the family which will use it, the employer who will note it, the professional who will record it.

**The correction that applies:** the evidence on the actual outcomes of disclosure, which is more favourable than the prediction, and the distinction between the settings in which the cost is real and the settings in which it is anticipated. The distinction is supplied in Section 35.2 and represents the chapter's principal practical material.

**3. The requirement to have answers.** The material of Chapter 32 in the form it takes in the act of asking: a man who is supposed to know does not ask a question in the setting in which he is supposed to know. The barrier is specific to the man's domain, and it is the strongest barrier reported by men in professional and technical occupations.

**The correction that applies:** the professional and peer arrangements of Chapter 31.5, which relocate the asking to settings in which the man is not required to know.

**4. The obligations of the position.** Men in positions of responsibility report that the act of asking is unavailable to them because the people who could help are the people they are responsible for. The barrier is accurate in the short term, and it is the reason that the arrangements of Chapter 31.5 are constructed outside the reporting line.

**The correction that applies:** the construction of the arrangement rather than the choice of a recipient from among the man's dependents.

**5. The absence of any experience of being helped.** The man who was never attended to as a child has no model of the exchange, and he therefore cannot imagine its form. The barrier is the first form of isolation in Chapter 27.1, and its feature is that the man does not experience it as a barrier at all: he reports that he has never needed help rather than that he cannot ask for it.

**The correction that applies:** the staged method of Chapter 27.5, and the deliberate adoption of the smallest available form of the exchange.

**6. The debt of gratitude and the reciprocity problem.** The man's assessment that accepting assistance establishes an obligation he may not be able to discharge, and that the relationship will be permanently altered by the imbalance. The barrier is frequently the strongest one in the material on family assistance, where the man has seen relatives made permanently subordinate by the assistance they received.

**The correction that applies:** the reciprocal arrangement of Chapter 27.5's third stage, the stated terms of Chapter 33.4, and the professional route, in which the exchange is a service rather than a favour.

**7. The fear of what will be found.** A specific and frequently reported barrier: the man's reluctance concerns not the act of asking but its outcome. The assessment will produce a diagnosis, the accounts will be examined, the position will be worse than the man has admitted to himself, and the arrangement entered into will require changes he does not wish to make.

**The correction that applies:** the information that the position is almost always better known than the man believes it to be, and that the assessment's function is to establish what is required rather than to deliver a verdict. Section 34.2's material on the rising cost of delay applies: the material's total is larger, not smaller, at the point at which the man finally addresses it.

**8. The specific barrier of the man who has asked before.** The man whose earlier request was refused, dismissed, forgotten, or used against him, and who has concluded from the outcome that asking does not work. The barrier is also the reason that the Staged material of Chapter 27.5 requires more than one attempt: the outcomes of requests vary by recipient, and the first outcome is information about that recipient rather than about asking.

**The correction that applies:** the principle of Chapter 26.5's revised reliance, applied to the asking itself: the reliance should be graded to the demonstrated record of the recipient, and the first request should be one the man can afford to have refused.

**9. The instruction that a man should not need help.** The barrier is delivered in childhood by households, by schools, by men's groups, by the culture of the trade, and by the man's own account. It is stated in the form of a rule rather than a prediction, and rules are resistant to evidence.

**The correction that applies:** the recognition that the instruction is a description of a role that was designed for particular circumstances — the man who must function in conditions where his failure is dangerous for other people — and that it has been applied beyond its useful range. The material on which the chapter relies is the arithmetic of Section 35.2: the instruction's costs are large, they are paid by the man's household, and there is no arrangement that requires them.

### The Common Structure

The nine barriers share a structure, and it is worth stating because it determines the correction.

Each concerns **the man's whole standing** rather than the specific matter; each is anticipated rather than experienced; each is therefore available to be tested; and each suppresses the request entirely rather than altering its form.

The consequence is that men who cannot make the whole request make no request. The chapter's method is the opposite: it supplies a form of request that is specific, bounded, and made in the language of a task, and its purpose is that the man is able to test the barriers with an act small enough to survive the outcome.
