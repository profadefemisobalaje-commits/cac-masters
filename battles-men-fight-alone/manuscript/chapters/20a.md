# The Pain of Being an Unappreciated Man {.chapter num="20" short="The Pain of Being an Unappreciated Man"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the experience of being taken for granted, and distinguish it from the absence of praise.
2. Analyse the mechanisms by which a man's contribution becomes invisible, including those the man himself operates.
3. Explain the difference between recognition, appreciation, and gratitude, and identify which of the three is actually being sought.
4. Describe the development of bitterness and resentment in men, including its identifiable stages and its effects on the man and his household.
5. Apply a structured method for stating his own value without complaint, demand, or scorekeeping, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Design the practices by which a household becomes one in which contribution is noticed, using the plan at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

There is a specific complaint that men make rarely, and that when they do make it, is heard badly: *I do everything that is required of me and nobody notices.* It is heard badly for three reasons. It sounds like a demand for praise. It sounds ungrateful, in a man who has more than many. And it sounds as though the man is asking for a service — recognition — that he should not need.

This chapter sets the subject out differently. What the man is describing is not the absence of praise. It is the absence of the ordinary acknowledgement that belongs to a person whose contribution other people depend on, and the experience of it is specific: the man is aware that the household's arrangements would not continue without him, and simultaneously aware that no one in the household has expressed that awareness in any form for years.

The subject has three components that are frequently confused, and separating them is the first work of the chapter. **Recognition** is the household's knowledge that a contribution exists. **Appreciation** is the household's valuation of it. **Gratitude** is the household's expression of that valuation toward the man. A man may have all three, or any combination, and it is the first that most commonly fails in the households examined here: the contribution is not valued less, it is simply not seen.

The final observation before proceeding is about the stakes. Being taken for granted does not, by itself, end marriages or damage children. What damages households is the second-order effect: the resentment that accumulates in a man who has been unnoticed for a decade, and the behaviours that resentment produces — withdrawal, contempt, the abrupt and disproportionate anger described in Chapter 15, the search for acknowledgement from somebody who will supply it, and, most destructively, the transfer of the arrangement to the next generation, in which the man's children learn to take a father for granted and to expect to be taken for granted themselves.

## 20.1 The Experience of Being Taken for Granted

The experience has a structure, and men in it describe a consistent set of features.

### The Seven Features

**1. The work is regular and invisible.** The contribution is provided on schedule, in full, and its regularity is why it is not noticed. An unusual contribution attracts attention; a monthly one does not.

**2. The contribution has no event attached to it.** Married couples acknowledge occasions: anniversaries, births, qualifications, promotions. Sustained provision has no occasion, and it is therefore never marked.

**3. The deficits are discussed and the surpluses are not.** A household will discuss the month in which the fees were late. It will not discuss the eleven years in which they were not.

**4. The man's own affairs are not discussed at all.** The household knows what he provides and not what it costs him to provide it. The material of Chapters 9, 12, and 17 applies in full.

**5. Other people's contributions are visible and are acknowledged.** The relative who assisted once with a hospital bill is thanked repeatedly for years; the man who paid it has not been mentioned. The asymmetry is not maliciousness; it is that the occasional contribution is a change in the arrangement and the man's contribution is the arrangement.

**6. Requests are made without acknowledgement of what has already been given.** A request for assistance is not unreasonable in itself. What registers with the man is that the request arrives without any reference to the previous ones, which is equivalent to saying that they were not contributions but the ordinary state of affairs.

**7. The absence of acknowledgement is not expressible.** The man cannot raise it without appearing to request praise, and he therefore does not raise it, and the arrangement continues. This is the mechanism that makes the condition durable, and it is the reason the chapter devotes a section to the way the subject can be stated.

### What the Man Is Actually Missing

It is worth being precise, because the imprecision is what makes the complaint unspeakable.

The man is not missing praise. Praise is a general commendation — *you are a good man, you are hardworking* — and it is frequently given and does not address the condition. What he is missing is the household's **knowledge** of what he carries: the specific awareness that the school fees took forty per cent of his income in a month in which he also paid for a funeral; that he declined the travel that would have advanced him; that he has not slept properly since April; that the vehicle is being maintained on credit.

Men describe the need in the same terms across countries and circumstances: not that anybody should thank them, but that somebody should know. The distinction is not sentimental. It is the difference between being a function of a household and being a member of one.

### The Man's Own Contribution to the Condition

Three of the mechanisms are operated by the man, and they should be stated before the household is assessed.

**He conceals the cost.** A man who provides in a manner that conceals what it costs him cannot be acknowledged for the cost, because it is unknown. The concealment is described in Chapter 9, is intended protectively, and produces precisely the invisibility the man then resents.

**He declines acknowledgement.** Where an acknowledgement is offered, men in this position frequently reduce it: *it is nothing; it is my responsibility; I am only doing what I should.* The reduction is performed to avoid the appearance of seeking credit, and its effect is to remove the acknowledgement from the record.

**He does not describe what he wants.** The requirements of a household are negotiated in part through complaint and request. A man who never complains and never requests has removed himself from the negotiation, and the absence is registered by the household as an absence of requirements.

::: keypoint
Recognition, appreciation, and gratitude are three different things. A household may value a man's contribution completely and still not know it, because the contribution's cost has never been described. The first practical step in this chapter is not a request for thanks; it is the supply of information that is currently missing.
:::

## 20.2 Sacrifice That Goes Unnoticed

This section examines the specific form of unnoticed contribution that produces the most bitterness in men: unnoticed sacrifice, as distinct from unnoticed work.

The distinction is as follows. Work is the performance of a required function; a man who works sixty hours a week performs what the household expects. Sacrifice is the giving up of something the household did not require him to give up, in order to meet an obligation the household does require; the abandoned position, the declined opportunity, the abandoned ambition, the friendship let lapse, the health neglected.

Four features make sacrifice uniquely invisible.

**It has no output.** Work produces a visible result: the fee paid, the food bought, the house built. Sacrifice produces an absence, and absences are not visible. The household sees the household's condition; it cannot see the degree that was not taken.

**It is indistinguishable from what the man would have done anyway.** A man with a modest income and intermittent work who declined a further qualification may have declined it for any of several reasons; the household's default assumption is that the qualification was not taken because it was not possible, not because it was a sacrifice.

**It is rarely announced, for a specific reason.** A man who announces a sacrifice creates an obligation in the recipient, and men avoid creating obligations. They therefore perform the sacrifice silently and wait, in a manner that is invisible even to themselves, for the recognition that never arrives because the information never did.

**The man himself reconstructs it as an ordinary decision.** The mechanism identified in Chapter 17.2: a sacrifice unspoken for fifteen years is eventually remembered as the natural consequence of circumstances, and the man then experiences an unfocused grievance without a subject — *I have done everything and there is nothing to show for it* — which is more difficult to address than a specific complaint.

### The Ledger, Again

The unspoken ledger, introduced in Chapter 15 and revisited in Chapter 17, appears in its most damaging form in this chapter. The features are worth restating because they are the reason this material belongs in a book about hidden struggles rather than in a book about manners.

A man who has sacrificed without acknowledgement accumulates an account. The account is not maintained deliberately; it is maintained by the same process that records any unmet obligation. Its effects are recognisable.

- **Selective attention to deficits.** The man registers every instance of ingratitude, every request made without acknowledgement, every arrangement that treats his contribution as the baseline.
- **Disproportionate reactions to small matters.** The response is calibrated to the account rather than to the event, as described in Chapter 15.5.
- **A progressive withdrawal of goodwill.** The man's voluntary contributions reduce: the additional assistance, the readiness, the small generosities. This is the point at which the ledger begins to produce damage that others can see, and it is usually interpreted by the household as a change in the man's character.
- **The construction of a grievance that cannot be raised.** The man's position — *I have given everything and nobody has noticed* — cannot be stated without reclassifying the giving as a request for recognition, and it is therefore carried until it is expressed as contempt, which is the subject of Section 20.4.
