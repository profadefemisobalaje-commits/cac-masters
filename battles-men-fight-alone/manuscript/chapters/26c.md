## 26.5 What Betrayal Taught You: Lessons Without Bitterness

The final substantive section is the one that determines the man's arrangements for the remainder of his life. Its subject is the conversion of the experience into usable knowledge, without the conversion of the man into somebody who cannot rely on anybody.

### The Wrong Lesson and the Right One

**The wrong lesson** is the general proposition: *men cannot be relied upon; I am better off alone; I will not give anybody that position again.* The proposition is false — the same men who betray also assist, and the evidence for reliability is available in the man's own history — and its cost is exact: it removes the arrangements that the research associates most strongly with men's resilience, and it produces the condition described in Chapter 27.

**The right lesson** is specific and technical, and it consists of four findings.

**1. Which term I relied upon without agreeing it.** The man's contribution to the outcome, stated without self-blame: he assumed a term that the other party did not hold. The finding converts the event from a general statement about trust into a matter of procedure.

**2. What the evidence was, and when it was available.** The man identifies the signals that were present before the act — the previous breach, the pattern in the friend's other relationships, the reluctance to be accountable, the accommodation that the man had made and not raised — and the time at which they became visible. The finding supplies the man's capacity to read such signals in future, and it should be conducted without the hindsight bias described in Chapter 16.5.

**3. What the arrangement should have included.** The practical correction: the documentation of a financial arrangement, the disclosure of the material to one other person, the test of a small reliance before a large one, the stated term. The finding is the substance of Section 26.6.

**4. What remains true about men.** The man should conduct this finding deliberately, because the previous three findings leave it open. The material: the men who have kept faith, the assistance that has arrived without being asked for, the relationships that have held through difficulty. Men who complete this finding report that the list is longer than they expected, and that its construction is the mechanism by which the general conclusion is avoided.

### The Three Rules of Revised Reliance

**Rule One: grade the reliance to the demonstrated record.** A man's trust should be extended in proportion to what the other person has demonstrated, in the domain in question, over time. The rule is not cynicism; it is the ordinary practice of every professional arrangement and it is applied in ordinary relationships more often than men acknowledge. Its application requires the man to ask, before a substantial reliance, what the person has demonstrated in that specific domain.

**Rule Two: state the terms of any material arrangement.** Where the friendship involves money, business, property, a position, or a confidence of consequence, the terms should be stated and, where the material justifies it, written. The rule is frequently resisted on the grounds that stating terms implies distrust; the evidence from men who have adopted it is that the statement of terms is the mechanism by which friendships survive material arrangements, and that the arrangements conducted without terms are the ones that end.

**Rule Three: maintain more than one confidant, and more than one source of assistance.** The concentration described in Section 26.1 is the structural vulnerability. The rule: a minimum of two men with whom the man's actual position is known, and a practical network of more than one person for each kind of assistance the man requires. The rule's function is to remove the single point of failure, and its adoption is the single most effective protection against the severity of the losses described in this chapter.

### The Question of Forgiveness, in the Friendship Context

The material in Section 16.5 applies, with two specifications.

**Forgiveness does not require the resumption of the reliance.** A man may release the account and continue the relationship on reduced terms, and this is the most common outcome among men who have handled the situation well. The reduction is not a failure of forgiveness; it is the accurate consequence of the other person's demonstrated record.

**The forgiveness, where it occurs, is frequently delayed and frequently arrives without the man's noticing.** Men describe the process in the same terms across cases: the material occupies the mind continuously for a period measured in months; its incidence reduces; a year or two later the man encounters the friend at an occasion and finds that the account has closed without a decision having been taken. The description is worth recording because it indicates that the outcome requires the practical steps rather than a decision about the past.

## 26.6 Choosing Better Men: Discerning Friendship

The final section converts the chapter into an instrument: the construction of a set of friendships that will hold, and the selection of the men to build them with.

### The Discerning Criteria

Six criteria, drawn from the accounts of men whose friendships have held over decades. They are properties rather than characteristics, which is to say that they can be observed rather than inferred.

**1. How the man speaks about people who are not present.** The most reliable single indicator. A man who speaks of his wife, his colleagues, and his friends with contempt in their absence will do so about any man in the group, including the reader. The criterion is applied to the man rather than to the conversation, and its predictive record in men's accounts is high.

**2. How the man behaves when he has an advantage.** Whether he takes the opportunity, shares it, or mentions it. The conduct in small advantages predicts the conduct in large ones, and the criterion can be observed by the man in a relationship of some months' duration.

**3. The treatment of people who can do nothing for him.** The staff, the subordinate, the relative with no resources. This is the criterion most frequently identified by men who have been betrayed, in retrospect, and it is the one that is most easily observed from the beginning of a friendship.

**4. The handling of a small confidence.** Whether the man keeps an ordinary piece of information that was given to him without consequence. The test is available early, it is inexpensive, and its result is informative. The grading principle of Section 26.5 — extend the reliance in proportion to the record — requires exactly this kind of test before a substantial reliance.

**5. Whether the terms can be discussed.** A man who can discuss the terms of an arrangement — the repayment, the contribution, the boundaries, the expectations — without discomfort is a man with whom material arrangements are possible. A man who becomes uncomfortable at the discussion of terms will not be reliable in the arrangement, and the discomfort is frequently the signal that the man treats the friendship as a licence.

**6. Behaviour in a difficulty of the man's own.** The criterion that carries the greatest weight and that is tested least often, because it is the one that requires the friend to have an opportunity. Its observation is therefore frequently a consequence of the crisis rather than the preparation for it, which is the reason that Section 26.5's Rule Three — more than one confidant — is the practical protection.

### The Construction

Four measures for building the arrangements, and the specific form matters.

**Reinstate one relationship from before the man's obligations consumed his time.** Chapter 4's method: two men contacted, one arrangement proposed, a fixed form. Men report that the reinstatement is frequently easier than expected, because the lapse was mutual and because men are generally pleased to be contacted.

**Add one group.** A group of men with an agreed rule of honesty: a discussion group, a professional association, a group of fathers, a men's group meeting for a defined purpose. The group's function is to supply the membership and disclosure functions at once, and it removes the concentration that Section 26.1 identified.

**Test the terms in one relationship.** Choose one friendship and state a term explicitly: the arrangement for the money, the boundary concerning the household, the requirement of confidence. The exercise is uncomfortable and its result is informative, and men generally report that the response was better than anticipated.

**Establish one arrangement that will hold after a crisis.** The practical test of any friendship is whether it contains an arrangement that would operate in a difficulty. Men should identify, for each of their principal friendships, what the friend would actually do if the man were hospitalised for three months, and whether the answer has ever been discussed. Where the answer is unexamined in every relationship, the vulnerability described in Section 26.2's third form remains unaddressed.
