## 11.3 Delayed Achievement and Disappointment

Delayed achievement is the condition of the man who is working towards a goal that has not yet arrived, and who has been working towards it for longer than he expected. It is the ordinary condition of a large proportion of men in mid-life, and it is the source of a specific and under-recognised distress.

### The Distinctive Character of Delayed Achievement

The experience has four features that distinguish it from failure.

**There is nothing to mourn.** A man who has failed at a definable task can respond to it: he can analyse, adjust, and set a new objective. A man whose achievement has been delayed has not failed — he is still working, still moving, still within his own account of the plan — and therefore has no event to process. What he has is a chronic discrepancy between where he is and where he expected to be.

**The timetable is usually unspoken.** Most men cannot say precisely what they expected at thirty, at forty, and at fifty, because the expectation was never formulated. It was absorbed from the comparisons of the environment described in Section 11.1. A man therefore experiences disappointment at missing a deadline he never wrote down.

**The evidence is ambiguous.** Some of the markers of success are present and some are not: a man may have a stable income and no property; educated children and no reserve; a respected position and no retirement provision. The ambiguity prevents any clean conclusion, so the man remains permanently uncertain whether he is behind or on schedule, and the uncertainty itself becomes exhausting.

**The comparison is continuous.** Because the reference group is visible and mobile, a man encounters evidence of other men's achievement at weddings, at funerals, on social media, in the family house, and in the market. Each encounter reactivates the timetable.

### The Psychological Consequences

Four consequences follow, and they commonly co-occur.

**A chronic sense of being behind.** Not an acute distress but a standing background condition, frequently described as a pressure that is always present and never articulated.

**Compensatory activity.** Working longer, taking on additional engagements, and pursuing accelerated plans. This is the most common response and it is frequently counterproductive: the additional activity increases risk, reduces the quality of decisions, and erodes health and relationships.

**Loss of pleasure in the present.** Where all satisfaction is deferred to the arrival of the goal, the intervening years are experienced as a waiting room. Men in this position frequently describe a decade of their lives as having been spent entirely in preparation.

**Reluctance to celebrate.** Because celebration would imply that the position is acceptable, and the position is not yet acceptable.

### Temporary Delay and Structural Delay

The practical response depends on a distinction that men frequently do not make.

**Temporary delay** is a delay produced by a defined and time-limited circumstance: a further qualification being completed, a market that is in a cyclical downturn, a business in its growth phase, a debt being cleared, a care obligation that will end. The appropriate response is to continue, with a defined review point at which the situation will be reassessed if the delay has not resolved.

**Structural delay** is a delay produced by conditions that will not change on the current path: a ceiling in a profession, a market that has permanently changed, an employment structure that no longer exists, a plan that depends on capital the man cannot obtain, an obligation that has been redefined as permanent. The appropriate response is a change of plan.

The diagnostic question is simple, and men frequently find it uncomfortable to answer: **if nothing changes in my circumstances, and I continue on this path for five more years, will the position be different?** If the honest answer is no, the situation is structural, and continuing is not persistence; it is a strategy of avoidance. Chapter 24 addresses the transition that follows from accepting such a conclusion, and Chapter 38 addresses the rebuilding.

## 11.4 Unemployment, Underemployment, and Lost Opportunities

Three specific conditions produce the most severe pressure on men's self-regard, and they require separate treatment because their effects differ.

### Unemployment

Unemployment damages men's mental health more severely than it damages women's, on average, in most countries for which data are available, and the effect is strongest in men who were the principal earner of a household. The mechanisms are documented: loss of income; loss of the structure and social contact that employment supplies; loss of the role through which the man's standing in his household and community is constructed; and the pressure of time, which produces rumination rather than rest.

The specific features of unemployment that men report, and that families should expect, are the following.

- **A collapse of structure.** Sleep and waking times drift; days become undifferentiated; the absence of an imposed schedule is experienced as disorientation rather than freedom.
- **Social withdrawal.** Avoidance of gatherings at which the question *what are you doing now?* will be asked, and, frequently, avoidance of former colleagues and of the street on which the former workplace stands.
- **Anger and irritability.** The strain is discharged at home, which is the most damaging feature of the whole condition, and which frequently produces a deterioration in the relationship that is the man's principal support.
- **The job-search decay.** Effort applied to the search declines over months, not through laziness but through the accumulated effect of rejection and the erosion of the belief that the effort will succeed.
- **Health deterioration.** Reduced activity, increased alcohol use, poor diet, and the deferral of medical care. The elevated mortality risk associated with unemployment, which is well documented in the research literature, operates substantially through these routes.

What helps, in the accounts men give, is four things: structure deliberately imposed on the week; the preservation of one or more identities not connected to employment; the separation of the job search into timetabled activity rather than a continuous background condition; and, crucially, at least one person with whom the position can be discussed honestly rather than performed. Chapter 38 addresses the sequence in which these should be established after a job loss.

### Underemployment

Underemployment is the condition of working in a position below one's qualifications, experience, or capacity: a graduate working in informal trade, a professional in a role well below his level, a man with a trade who can obtain only casual labour. It is frequently more corrosive to self-regard than unemployment, for three reasons.

**The conditions of the role are a daily reminder.** Unemployment is a condition; underemployment is a condition experienced inside a workplace, in the form of unsuitable tasks, reduced autonomy, inadequate pay, and the observed position of less-qualified colleagues.

**It attracts comment.** Men in this position are frequently discussed by relatives and friends, and the discussion includes the assessment that they have not done well, that they have wasted their education, that they should have done something else.

**It appears optional.** Because the man is employed, his difficulties are frequently not recognised as difficulties. He is not offered the support that an unemployed man would receive, and he is expected to be grateful.

The practical approach to underemployment has three components. First, the work should be recognised as a legitimate achievement rather than a status: it feeds a household, and the fact that it is below his capacity does not make it a failure. Second, a plan for movement should be made explicit — a qualification, a skill, a network, a transition route — with a timetable, because the combination of a household's immediate needs and the demands of the present role tends to eliminate all planning capacity. Third, the domain of identity should be diversified deliberately, since the present role will not supply it.

### Lost Opportunities

The third condition is the most private of the three, and it is frequently disclosed only in counselling or late in life: the sense that a specific opportunity was lost, and that the loss determined everything that followed.

The opportunities men name are specific and recurring. The scholarship or admission not taken up. The job not accepted because the family needed him at home. The business not started because the capital was used for a relative's obligation. The move to another city not made. The partnership declined because of a disagreement that now appears trivial. The training not undertaken.

The psychology of lost opportunity has three features. It is comparative: the loss is measured against an imagined alternative life, which is always more successful than any actual one would have been. It is frequently attached to a specific person: a parent, a spouse, or a superior whose requirement caused the opportunity to be declined. And it is often accompanied by a conviction that the opportunity was the only one, a belief that is rarely accurate and that is maintained by the fact that the alternative path has never been tested.

Three responses are useful. First, the opportunity should be recorded accurately, including the constraints under which the decision was made, since decisions made under constraint are not the same as mistakes. Second, the imagined alternative should be examined, because it is a constructed version of a life that would have contained its own difficulties. Third, and most usefully, the loss should be converted into an assessment of what remains available: opportunities of a different kind, at a different life stage, which the man has been excluding from consideration because he has been measuring them against the one that was lost. Chapter 28 develops this work in full.
