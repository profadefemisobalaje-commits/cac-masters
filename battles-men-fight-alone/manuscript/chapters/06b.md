## 6.2 Irritability, Withdrawal, and Emotional Numbness

The three symptoms named in this section are, in practice, the three most common ways in which depression presents in men. Each deserves a careful description, because each is systematically misread.

### Irritability

Irritability is the most frequently reported symptom in studies of depression in men, and it is the one most likely to be attributed to the man's personality rather than to his condition.

Its features are distinctive. The threshold for frustration falls sharply, so that minor events — a child's interruption, a misplaced item, a slow queue, a request repeated for the third time — produce a reaction disproportionate to the event. The reaction is frequently brief and intense, and it is followed, in many men, by a private sense of shame that is itself uncomfortable and that raises the likelihood of a further reaction. The irritability is rarely directed at a single person and is frequently worst at home, where the consequences are lowest, which produces a characteristic and distressing pattern: a man who is civil at work and harsh at home, and who cannot understand why.

The mechanisms by which depression produces irritability are well understood. Depleted energy reduces the capacity to regulate emotion; disturbed sleep reduces it further; and the experience of feeling flat and impaired produces frustration that has no legitimate outlet. In addition, as Chapter 3 explained, anger is frequently the only emotional channel available to a man, and therefore the one through which a large number of other states are expressed.

For the reader, two practical implications follow. The first is that irritability in a man who was previously even-tempered should be treated as a symptom and investigated, not as a moral failing. The second is that the same is true of a man's irritability that has persisted for years: chronic low tolerance is a common presentation of untreated depression, and it responds to treatment.

### Withdrawal

Withdrawal in depression takes two forms, and their relation to work is the diagnostic clue.

In the first form, the man withdraws from everything, including work, and the withdrawal is visible. He is late, absent, or mentally elsewhere. He declines invitations and stops attending his associations and religious gatherings.

In the second form — considerably more common in men — the man withdraws from everything **except** work. He continues to attend, and he frequently increases his hours, because work is the one domain in which he retains a sense of competence and in which the structure of the day provides relief from unstructured time. He withdraws from friends, from family activities, from football, from the community association, from the church or mosque, and from his children, and he substitutes additional work for all of it. His family reads this as dedication. His employer reads it as commitment. It is neither.

The withdrawal is self-reinforcing, and Figure 6.1 sets out the cycle. Depression produces withdrawal; withdrawal removes the activity, contact, light, exercise, and purpose that sustain mood; the loss of these things deepens the depression; and a deeper depression produces more withdrawal.

### Emotional Numbness

The third presentation, and the one that men find most disturbing when they recognise it, is emotional flatness. Its features are specific.

The man does not feel sad, or he feels sad only occasionally and briefly. What he feels is nothing. Music that used to move him does not. His children's achievements produce no reaction that he can identify, and this is frequently the point at which he concludes that something is wrong, because he remembers what it used to feel like. He can identify events that ought to produce feeling and observes his own absence from them. Sexual interest declines. He may cry rarely or not at all, and he may find that he is unable to cry on occasions when he wants to.

Men describe this state with difficulty, and the descriptions they produce are valuable because they are so consistent: *I am behind glass. I am watching my life from a distance. Everything is in grey. Nothing matters, but not in a distressing way; it simply does not matter.*

Two clinical points about numbness deserve emphasis. First, it is a recognised symptom of depression, not a personality change and not a sign of coldness. Second, it is frequently accompanied by the absence of the emotional signals that would otherwise prompt help-seeking, which means that men in this state are less likely to seek help than men who are visibly distressed. The condition removes both the feeling and the motive to address the loss of feeling.

Beyond the three principal presentations, the following are frequently present in men and are worth recording.

## 6.3 The Dangers of Untreated Depression

The consequences of untreated depression are serious and, in a substantial number of cases, irreversible. The reader who is inclined to wait — for a better time, for the pressure to lift, for the symptoms to resolve — should read this section carefully, because waiting is not a neutral strategy. It has a documented cost.

### Death

The most serious consequence is death from suicide. Depression is the condition most strongly associated with suicide, and male suicide rates exceed female rates in almost every country with reliable data. The pattern is particularly stark in men of working age, in men who have experienced separation or divorce, in men who have lost employment, and in men who are socially isolated. Chapter 34 addresses suicidal thinking directly, including what to do, and Appendix Q lists sources of immediate support. The essential point here is that untreated severe depression can end in death, that the risk is higher in men, and that this is the principal reason the chapter insists on professional assessment rather than self-management for anyone whose symptoms are significant.

### Deterioration of the Condition

Depression tends to worsen without treatment, and it also tends to recur. Each episode increases the probability of a further episode, and episodes that are treated early and properly are associated with better long-term outcomes. The practical version of this finding is simple: treatment today is easier than treatment in a year.

### Physical Health

Untreated depression is associated with poorer outcomes across a wide range of physical conditions, including cardiovascular disease and diabetes, partly through direct physiological mechanisms and partly through its effects on behaviour: reduced exercise, poor diet, disordered sleep, increased alcohol use, and non-adherence to treatment for other conditions. Men frequently present the physical consequences rather than the depressive condition, and both require attention.

### Family Consequences

Depression in a man affects his household. Partners report loneliness, exhaustion, and a sense of living with somebody who is present but unavailable. Children of a depressed father show, on average, higher rates of emotional and behavioural difficulties, and the mechanism is well documented: reduced engagement, increased irritability, inconsistent discipline, and impaired communication. These effects are not a reason for guilt in the man concerned — they are a reason to treat the condition, and they improve substantially when the depression is treated.

### Occupational and Financial Consequences

Loss of function at work is common before the condition is severe: reduced concentration, missed deadlines, errors, and declining relationships with colleagues. Men frequently respond by working longer hours, which temporarily disguises the loss and permanently increases the risk. In a substantial number of cases, the depression ends a career through resignation, dismissal, or the collapse of a business, none of which need have happened had the condition been treated a year earlier.

### Alcohol and Substance Dependence

Self-medication is common, and it is dangerous in two directions. Alcohol deepens depression in the medium term while appearing to relieve it in the short term; and dependence developed during an episode does not resolve when the episode resolves. A man who drinks heavily to manage low mood is at risk of acquiring two conditions where he began with one. Appendix M deals with substance use, and Chapter 23 examines it in detail.
