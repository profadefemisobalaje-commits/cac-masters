## 2.4 Responsibility Without Emotional Support

The most consequential feature of the male script is not its demands but its silence about support. A man is given an account of what he must do and no account of what he may need, and the absence is maintained by the man himself, by his household, and by the institutions he deals with.

### The Asymmetry

The asymmetry is visible in every setting where men and women are observed together.

**In families.** A man's difficulty is frequently met with an assessment of its practical consequences; a woman's difficulty is met with attention to the difficulty itself. The research on social support consistently finds that women receive more emotional support than men, that they report more people to whom they can turn in difficulty, and that the difference persists across countries and socioeconomic groups.

**In friendships.** As Chapter 4 establishes, men's friendships are frequently built around shared activity rather than shared disclosure, and the disclosure channel is therefore frequently absent even in long-standing relationships.

**In marriage.** A man's principal source of intimacy is frequently his wife, and the arrangement is therefore a single point of failure. Where the marriage contains a difficulty, or the wife's own capacity is exhausted, the man has no second source.

**In the workplace.** A man's difficulty is a risk to his position. The disclosure of a personal problem to an employer is frequently managed with a practical accommodation and an unspoken reassessment, and men's assessment of the risk is accurate on this point.

**In health care.** Men consult less frequently, present later, and describe symptoms in reduced form. The consequences are documented across conditions: later diagnosis, more advanced disease at presentation, and higher mortality for conditions that are treatable when detected early.

### Why Support Is Not Offered to Men

Four mechanisms, each of which operates independently of anybody's intentions.

**The signals are absent.** Support is offered in response to the signals of distress — tears, distress in the voice, the statement of need, the visible disorganisation of a person's life. Men's distress signals are frequently the opposite: increased work, increased irritability, longer hours, less speech. A household looking for distress will frequently find the opposite of its signs, which is why a wife will say, accurately, that she had no idea.

**The man declines it.** The offer of support is refused, in a manner that ends the matter. The refusal is frequently of the form *I am managing*, *it is not a problem*, or the practical deflection of the conversation, and it is registered by the person offering as a conclusion. Most support to men is not withheld; it is declined, and the decline is honoured.

**The supporting person is exhausted.** The most under-discussed mechanism. In many households, the wife has been supporting children, parents, siblings, the household's administration, and her own difficulties, and she has no residual capacity for a further load. Her withdrawal is not indifference; it is depletion, and the man's assessment of his marriage as unsupportive is frequently a description of a household in which nobody has capacity for anybody.

**The culture has no script for male support.** There is an available script for supporting a woman: attention, presence, practical assistance, listening. There is no comparable script for supporting a man, and the available alternative is a set of approaches that men find ineffective: instruction, motivation, comparison with other men, the suggestion that the man should be grateful for what he has, and the recommendation that he should be stronger. The absence of a script is a failure of culture rather than of the individual household.

### The Consequences

**Delayed recognition of serious conditions.** Depression, hypertension, diabetes, cardiac symptoms, and cancer are all detected later in men, and the delays are frequently measured in years.

**The conversion of difficulty into behaviour.** Where support is unavailable and disclosure is prohibited, distress is expressed in behaviour: alcohol, risk-taking, work, spending, and anger. Each is treated by the environment as the problem, and the underlying condition is therefore neither identified nor treated.

**Poor decisions made in isolation.** A man without a confidant or a reviewer decides alone, on impaired judgement, in conditions of chronic strain. The consequences are visible in men's financial disasters, business failures, and legal entanglements, each of which is frequently traceable to a decision made without any conversation.

**The single point of failure in marriage.** Where the wife is the sole confidant, the death or illness of the wife removes the man's entire support system at once. The research on widowers' mortality is unambiguous on this point, and Chapter 25 addresses the long-term consequence, which is frequently a decade or more of isolation.

**The transmission to the next generation.** A son who observes a father carrying responsibility without support learns that this is what manhood consists of. The learning is by observation and it is extremely effective. Where a father has one confidant, one friend, or one professional relationship that his children know about, the transmission is different, and this is one of the few interventions in this book whose effect is measurable in the next generation's arrangements.

## 2.5 When Manhood Becomes a Lifelong Examination

The final pressure is the one men find hardest to name: the sense of being permanently under assessment.

The examination has a specific structure. It has **criteria**, which are the six requirements in Section 2.1. It has **examiners**, who are everybody, and whose comments are the events from which the man infers his standing. It has **no timetable**, since it begins in childhood and ends at death, and its periods — the years in which a man is expected to have married, built, and established — are enforced by comment rather than by rule. It has **no certificate**, which is to say that success never produces a conclusion, only a temporary position. And it has **no appeal**, since there is no forum in which the assessment can be challenged.

### The Three Features That Make It Damaging

**Postponement of relief.** A man under examination does not permit himself the ordinary reliefs that other people allow themselves, because relief would be evidence in the assessment. Holidays are deferred, rest is deferred, medical care is deferred, and enjoyment is deferred until the conclusion — which never arrives. Men in this pattern frequently arrive at sixty with a list of things they intended to do when they had finished.

**Comparisons under constant review.** The examination is conducted comparatively. The reference group — school colleagues, relatives, neighbours, professional contemporaries — is monitored continuously, and the monitoring is facilitated by every gathering, every ceremony, and every device.

**The transition of the examination into the household.** The most damaging feature, and the one that affects other people. A man under permanent assessment begins to conduct his household as an examination hall: the children's results become evidence, the wife's manner and appearance become evidence, the household's expenditure becomes evidence. The arrangement is understandable, and it is extremely damaging to the people inside it. Children in these households frequently describe the experience of being assessed on their father's behalf, and Chapter 17 addresses the consequences directly.

### What the Examination Does in Mid-Life and Later

Four consequences are consistently reported.

**The collapse of the arrangement at retirement.** Where a man's identity has been supplied by the examination, retirement removes the examiner's instruments: the position, the income, the daily evidence. The result is frequently an acute crisis of purpose, described in Chapter 24, which arrives within months of the final working day.

**The retrospective audit.** At some point between fifty and sixty-five, most men in this pattern conduct an audit of their position against the criteria. Where the audit finds the arrangement satisfactory, the man is frequently left with a puzzle: he has done what was required and does not feel what he expected to feel. Where the audit finds the arrangement unsatisfactory, the man's difficulty is compounded by the time remaining, which is frequently insufficient for the remediation he has in mind.

**The collapse of the body's cooperation.** The examination requires a body that performs. Where the body begins to fail — blood pressure, joints, vision, the prostate, the heart — the man encounters the examination's most difficult proportion, which is that its criteria cannot be met by effort alone.

**The withdrawal of the examiners.** As a man ages, the people who conducted the assessment — the father, the uncles, the colleagues, the community's senior figures — die. Men frequently report, in their sixties, that they no longer know for whom they are performing. This is an important and frequently liberating discovery, and it comes later than it should: the examiners were mortal all along, which means the examination was always largely internal, and the man is therefore able to conclude it himself.
