# The Private Battles of Identity and Self-Worth {.part num="VI" img="/images/part6.jpg" summary="The conflicts between who a man is and who he is expected to become"}

# The Man in the Mirror {.chapter num="21" short="The Man in the Mirror"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the image a man presents to the world, identify how it is constructed and maintained, and assess its cost in his own life.
2. Analyse the gap between the presented image and the man's private account of himself, and explain why the gap widens over time.
3. Explain the structure of private shame and the fear of exposure, including the specific behaviours that shame produces in men.
4. Describe the obstacles that prevent men from accepting themselves, and distinguish acceptance from resignation and from lowering standards.
5. Apply a structured method for facing the truth about himself without self-destruction, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Construct a specific and dated plan for becoming the man he intends to be, rather than the man the image requires.
:::

## Introduction

A man stands in front of a mirror at the end of a working day. There is nothing unusual in the scene: he is tired, he is unshaven, he is dressed in the clothes he wore to the office. But look at what he is doing. He is not examining his appearance. He is conducting an assessment, and the assessment is familiar to him.

It runs as follows. He notes that he is fifty-one, that he is carrying weight around the middle, that the shirt he is wearing was bought when he earned less and cared more. He notes that the man who looks back at him does not correspond to the account he gives to others: the man who is managing, who knows what he is doing, whose household is in order, whose judgement is sound. He notes one specific item — a decision made badly, an obligation unmet, a thing done that nobody knows about, a failure that he has not disclosed. And he concludes the assessment in the usual manner, which is not with a plan but with a sentence: *this is not who I was supposed to be.*

This chapter is about the man in the mirror: the private assessment that men conduct, the gap between the account they present and the account they hold, the shame that lives in that gap, and the question of what can be done about it.

Its premise is that the gap is normal and its size is not. Every adult presents a version of themselves; the difference between a functioning life and an intolerable one is not the existence of the gap but whether the man can acknowledge it, whether anything in his life permits him to be seen as he actually is, and whether the gap is closing or widening. For a great many men, the gap widens for thirty years, and the widening is not detected until the assessment at the mirror produces something more serious than discomfort.

The chapter proceeds in six sections. Sections 21.1 and 21.2 examine the image and the gap. Section 21.3 examines shame and the fear of exposure, which is the mechanism that prevents the gap from being closed. Section 21.4 examines the obstacles to self-acceptance. Sections 21.5 and 21.6 set out the work: facing the truth without destroying oneself, and constructing a plan for becoming the man one intends to be.

## 21.1 The Image a Man Presents to the World

The image a man presents is not a lie and it is not a mask in the ordinary sense of a disguise. It is a selection, made continuously and mostly without deliberation, of the material that can be safely shown. It is assembled from four components.

### The Four Components

**1. The record of accomplishments.** The position held, the property acquired, the children educated, the contributions made, the crisis managed. This material is factual, it is legitimately the man's, and it constitutes the greater part of the image.

**2. The competence in the present.** The appearance of knowing what to do: the confident answer, the decision delivered without visible hesitation, the handling of a matter without appearing to find it difficult. This component is performed continuously and its performance is frequently confused by others, and sometimes by the man, with the underlying state.

**3. The absence of need.** The image contains no requirements. The man who presents it does not ask for help, does not appear uncertain, does not require reassurance, and does not disclose what he is carrying. The absence is not produced by deceit; it is produced by the prohibition described in Chapter 2, which operates even where a man is willing to speak.

**4. The management of appearance.** The visible markers: the clothing, the vehicle, the household's presentation, the children's institutions, the contributions made in public. This component is the most easily mistaken for vanity, and it is more often a form of accuracy as the man understands it: the markers are the language in which his position is legible to his community, and their maintenance is frequently a requirement of the position rather than a preference of the man.

### How the Image Is Maintained

The maintenance consumes a substantial quantity of a man's daily effort, and the effort is invisible because it is continuous.

**By selective disclosure.** The man reports the items that fit and omits the items that do not. The operation is performed in every conversation: at work, at family gatherings, at community functions, and, in the more advanced cases, at home.

**By the management of information about the household.** The children's difficulties, the marriage's conditions, the financial position, the state of the man's health. Each is disclosed in the form that the image can absorb.

**By the avoidance of situations in which the gap would be visible.** Men in this pattern decline invitations to occasions where the household's position would be examined, avoid the company of those who know an earlier version of them, and reduce contact with the relatives who are aware of the material that the image excludes.

**By the replenishment of the image.** The maintenance of an image requires material. Where the position does not move, the image weakens, and the man responds by acquiring: the vehicle, the obligation performed conspicuously, the contribution made larger than the budget permits. Chapter 9's account of the maintained standard in financial terms is the same mechanism applied to the whole of a man's presentation.

**By the conversion of the image into a standard.** The most consequential form of maintenance, and the one that produces this chapter's subject. A man who presents the image for long enough begins to measure himself against it, and the measurement is conducted without mercy, because the image was constructed for public consumption and cannot be satisfied in private.

### What the Image Costs

Four costs, all of them documented in men's accounts.

**The exhaustion of maintenance.** The continuous effort of presenting a version of the self requires energy that is not available for anything else, and men in this pattern describe a permanent low-grade fatigue that is distinct from overwork: the fatigue of being always legible, always composed, and always available.

**The exclusion of support.** The image contains no need, and a man who presents no need receives no support. The mechanism is described in Chapter 2.4 and its consequences are described throughout Part I.

**The escalation of the requirement.** The image is comparative and public, as Chapter 11 established, and its standard rises as the reference group does. A man who obtained his position through the performance of the image will find that the performance's requirement increases without his consent.

**The impossibility of intimacy within it.** A person can only be known to the extent that they are visible. A man whose presentation is a selection cannot be known by anybody who has only the presentation to work with, and the closest relationships are therefore conducted in a permanent partial visibility that both parties experience as a limitation they cannot name.
