# Rebuilding After Everything Falls Apart {.chapter num="38" short="Rebuilding After Everything Falls Apart"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe what a total collapse consists of, distinguish it from a partial loss, and identify the specific features that make it difficult to address.
2. Conduct the four tasks of the first period — safety, information, the triage of obligations, and one ally — and identify the actions that constitute each.
3. Recognise the seven errors that men make in the first period and the consequences of each, and identify which he is at present inclined toward.
4. Conduct a full reckoning: the accounting of assets, liabilities, obligations, capacities, and relationships, together with the identification of what remains intact.
5. Apply the staged reconstruction, in the order supplied, and state what belongs to each stage and what must not be attempted until the earlier stages are complete.
6. Conduct the reconstruction with his household, using the disclosure and planning forms of this book, and recognise the household's own losses as a separate matter requiring its own arrangements.
:::

## Introduction

A man's difficulties in the earlier parts of this book were partial: a position lost, a business failing, a marriage in difficulty, a debt concealed, a material carried and unshared. This chapter concerns the position in which the losses arrive together and the man's entire structure is affected at once: the business has failed and the debts are not payable, the employment has ended and the household's income has stopped, the marriage has ended or is in the process of ending, the property is gone or at risk, the standing in the community has been affected, the health has been affected, and the relationships through which the man would ordinarily be supported are the relationships that the collapse has reached.

The chapter is written on five commitments.

The first is that the position is survivable and that the material's central proposition applies to it in its strongest form. Men rebuild after total collapse, and the accounts of how they do it are consistent; the chapter supplies them as a sequence rather than as encouragement, and its order is drawn from the practice material on financial collapse, insolvency, bereavement, redundancy, and family crisis.

The second is that the first period has its own requirements, and that the failure to meet them is what converts a collapse from a period of years into a decade. The four tasks of Section 38.2 are small, they are specific, and they can be conducted by a man in the state the collapse produces — which is the reason they are prescribed in that form.

The third is that the errors of the first period are predictable, that men make them for comprehensible reasons, and that the errors are expensive. Section 38.3 sets them out because the reader's own inclination, at the point at which the collapse occurs, will be toward one or more of them, and because the material's function is to place the inclination in front of him before he acts on it.

The fourth is that the reconstruction is staged, and that the staging is not a matter of temperament but of arithmetic. A man rebuilding after a collapse whose income is not yet restored will not settle his obligations; a man whose obligations are unaddressed will not rebuild his standing; a man whose standing is not rebuilt will not rebuild his largest opportunities. The order is mechanical and it can be stated, which is what Section 38.5 does.

The fifth is that the household is the party through which the reconstruction is conducted and not the party to be protected from it. The material in Chapters 34 and 35 applies in full: the household's arrangements depend on the man's position, the household will construct an account in the absence of information, and the household's own losses require their own arrangements.
