## 7.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Kept the Standard

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family counselling, small-business advisory work, and financial counselling.

**Segun is fifty-four and has run a printing business for twenty-two years.** He employs nine people. He built a house in a peri-urban area, educated four children — two at university, one completed, one at secondary school — and has been the person who pays for the family's medical costs on both sides, including a brother-in-law's dialysis and his mother's regular medication.

For eleven years, the business expanded steadily. In the last three years, three things changed: paper and ink costs rose sharply, two large customers moved their work to a competing firm offering lower prices, and a bank facility taken during the expansion came up for renewal on less favourable terms.

Segun handled these developments in ways that were individually reasonable and cumulatively disastrous. He maintained the household budget unchanged, because reducing it would have required an explanation. He continued his contributions to the family obligations, because defaulting would have been noticed. He financed the shortfall with two informal loans and by delaying payment to two suppliers. He told nobody: not his wife, not his accountant, not his brother.

Over twenty-eight months, the business's debt grew while its capacity shrank. Segun's sleep deteriorated; he began to wake at four and to spend the hour before dawn on calculations. His irritability increased, and he became particularly sharp with his second son, whose university fees were the largest single item in the household budget. He began to avoid the two suppliers he owed.

The crisis arrived when a supplier refused to deliver without cash, which halted production for four days, which produced a missed delivery to a customer, which produced the loss of that customer.

His wife learned the position in the worst possible way: a supplier's representative came to the house on a Saturday and asked to speak to her, in front of two of the children, about a debt that was then four months old.

What followed is instructive in three respects.

**The first month was worse than the crisis.** The disclosure produced anger, fear, and, from Segun's brother, a set of judgments about his competence that were considerably harsher than anything the situation required. Segun described this period as the low point of his adult life, and men should be prepared for it: the truth does not necessarily produce comfort, and it frequently produces several weeks of difficulty before it produces relief.

**The position was manageable once it was a position rather than a secret.** With a financial counsellor, the family established four facts within a fortnight: total debt, monthly obligations, realistic income, and the number of months for which the situation could be sustained if expenditure were reduced. The number was three. The expenditure reduction was substantial, and it included the deletion of eleven monthly commitments that Segun had been maintaining for social reasons and had never examined. Two of the three largest — the vehicle maintenance on a second car and a contribution to a social club — were removed entirely.

**The recovery was slower and more durable than the collapse.** The house was not lost. One of the two university students moved to a degree programme with lower costs, with the family's agreement. One of the informal loans was restructured at a lower rate through a cooperative. Over three years, the business stabilised at a size about sixty per cent of its previous peak, with fewer fixed costs and better margins.

Segun's own summary, given in a review conversation two years later, was as follows: *The money problem took three years to fix. The secrecy almost took everything. I lost the customer, the trust of my brother, and my son's confidence for a period, and none of those were caused by the money. They were caused by my determination that nobody would find out that I was not managing.*
:::

The case illustrates the whole of this chapter, and four elements deserve emphasis.

**The concealment was not for personal advantage.** Every expenditure Segun maintained was for the benefit of others: fees, medical costs, family contributions, and the household standard. He concealed the position because disclosure would have required him to disappoint people, which he experienced as a failure rather than a decision. This is one of the most common patterns in male financial difficulty, and it is the pattern in which concealment does most damage.

**The identity was fused with the record.** Segun's sense of himself was constructed from eleven years of expansion. To disclose the difficulty would have been to replace that identity with another one, publicly, and his actions were organised to prevent this from happening. Chapter 24 deals with this transition directly.

**The costs that could not be recovered were relational.** The debt was restructured, the business survived, the house was kept, and the education continued with adaptation. What did not recover quickly were the brother's regard, the wife's trust, and the son's sense of his father — all damaged not by the financial failure but by the twenty-eight months of concealment.

**The reduction of the standard was survivable.** The largest fear — that reducing the household standard would destroy his standing and his family's regard — was tested, and it did not occur. Eleven commitments were deleted; two were reinstated later; the community did not collapse. Most men who complete this exercise report the same outcome, and it is worth stating plainly because the fear is the principal obstacle: the disclosure of a financial position rarely produces the social death that men anticipate, and it frequently produces assistance, advice, and time that were not otherwise available.

## 7.8 Practical Exercise: The Worth and Performance Audit

This exercise has three parts and takes about two hours. It is best done in a single sitting, in writing, and it is frequently more useful when the results are discussed with one other person.

### Part One: The Hidden Equations

Complete the following sentences with what you actually believe, not what you think you should believe.

- A man is only as good as ______________________.
- If I cannot provide ______________________, I will have failed as a man.
- My family's respect for me depends on ______________________.
- If people knew ______________________, they would think less of me.
- I would rather ______________________ than be seen as having failed.
- The last time I felt genuinely satisfied with something I achieved was ______________________.

Each of these sentences contains an implicit equation between what the man does and what he is worth. Write the equation out explicitly for each. For example: *My family's respect depends on my paying the fees* becomes *worth = paying the fees*.

### Part Two: The Cost of the Equation

For each equation, answer three questions in writing.

1. **What has this equation cost me?** Consider sleep, health, honesty, the quality of relationships, decisions taken badly, opportunities not taken, and years spent in a state of preparation for exposure.
2. **What has it produced that is genuinely valuable?** Most of these equations began as something honourable: a commitment to family, a professional standard, a real responsibility. Identify what is worth keeping.
3. **How would a man act differently if his worth did not depend on results?** Be concrete. What would he say, refuse, buy, sell, confess, rest from, or start?

### Part Three: The Revision

Write a statement of no more than 150 words, in the first person, describing the relationship you intend to hold between your performance and your worth. It must contain four elements.

- **An acceptance of responsibility**, including the specific responsibilities you intend to keep.
- **A statement that your worth does not depend on the outcome of those responsibilities.**
- **A specified action** that follows from the separation: reduce the standard in one area; tell one person the true position of the finances; delegate work you normally keep; take rest without justifying it; report a failure accurately and early.
- **A test** by which you will know whether the separation is being maintained: for example, being able to describe a setback without using the word *I* about it.

### Part Four: The Failure Disclosure Rehearsal

For the failure you are currently concealing, or the one you most fear, complete the following account. It is the document referenced in Stage Three of Section 7.5, and it is designed to be written and used.

- **The facts.** What has happened, with dates and figures.
- **The position.** Assets, liabilities, obligations, and the number of months the situation can be sustained unchanged.
- **My part.** The decisions that were mine, stated plainly and without embellishment.
- **The plan.** Two specific actions, with dates.
- **The request.** What I am asking of the person I am telling: to listen, to help me think, to assist practically, or to hold me to the plan.
- **The next update.** The date on which I will report progress.
