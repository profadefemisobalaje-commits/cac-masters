## 37.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I am building something. When it is established, I will rest."**

The condition is not going to be satisfied, and the case material in this chapter and in Chapter 24 is consistent on the point: the position's requirements expand to the capacity available, and the man's construction is generally complete by the time he is considering the question. The available measure is therefore the specification rather than the condition, and the case material's finding is that the men who adopted it while building did not lose the building; they lost the eleven per cent of income that came from the patients who required a doctor at two in the morning.

**"My household needs what I earn. Rest reduces the earnings."**

The statement is examined in Section 37.5's first part, and the accounting is generally decisive. The specific finding for men in this position: the earnings lost to the man's reduced capacity, the medical costs, the days not worked, the crises produced by the absence of margins, and the household's own losses — the arrangements not made, the opportunities not taken, the man's absence at the years that will not return — are generally larger than the earnings at issue, and they are paid by the household in a currency that does not appear in the accounts.

**"I rest. I watch television every evening."**

The screen's use is examined against the definition: the effect on the man's capacity. The material's finding, which men report consistently when they examine it: the screen frequently supplies the cessation of work without supplying any of the six categories, and the man's state after two hours of it is frequently the state he began with, with the addition of the night's sleep reduced. The substitute is not a failure and it is not rest; the chapter's position is that it should be identified as what it is, and that one category of Section 37.2 should be added to the week in place of some of it.

**"My father rested even less and he lived to eighty-five."**

The observation is entirely possible and it is not evidence about the reader. The material on individual variation in the effects of sustained work is the same as in any other field: some men sustain arrangements that damage others, and the reader's own position is established by his own presentations in Part One of the exercise and not by comparison. The specific finding from the case material is that the men who offer the observation are generally the men with three or four of the presentations of Section 37.3.

**"I cannot take the leave. The position cannot be left."**

Then the position is a structure with a single point of failure, which is the material of Chapter 33 and which is a risk that the man's employer or organisation owns whether or not it has acknowledged it. The practical measures are the ones in Section 37.6: the rota, the additional capacity, the specified hours, and the arrangement for the telephone. Where the man genuinely cannot leave and the position's capacity cannot be constructed, the matter should be treated as information about the position rather than about the man, and the available response is the reduction of the position's scope — which is a decision, and which is generally available and generally deferred.

**"I feel guilty, and I still do not rest. What now?"**

Then the exercise should be conducted in the order of Section 37.5's final part: the accounting first rather than the argument. The man who cannot accept that rest is legitimate for its own sake can generally examine what the absence of it has cost, and can adopt the arrangement for his household's sake. The finding is that the position, once adopted, becomes the man's own, and that the guilt reduces with the arrangement rather than before it: the guilt is not the obstacle that must be removed before the arrangement is constructed, and the arrangement is frequently what removes the guilt.

::: summary
**Chapter 37 in brief.** Rest is difficult for men for four reasons: the man's account of himself is composed substantially of what he does and provides, so that stopping is experienced as a pause in his standing rather than in his activity; the rule that a man is worth what he produces, with a short accounting period, so that rest registers as unaccounted time; the man's model of what his household requires, which is generally more demanding than anything the household has stated; and the environment's reinforcement, since continuous work is rewarded and its absence is recorded in the organisation's informal account before any formal one. The four sources produce two prohibitions: on stopping, and on being restored.

Rest is the conduct that restores the man's capacity. It is not the absence of work, and it is not sleep, and it is defined by its effect rather than its form. Six categories of recovery are identified — physical, mental, sensory, social, creative, and purposeful — and four distinctions apply: rest is not a reward, is not uniform, is mostly small, and is mostly unimpressive in appearance.

The absence of rest produces a documented decline that the person affected notices last: performance and judgement degrade while the man's own assessment degrades more slowly; recovery requires a period that cannot be reduced to a shorter substitute; sleep restriction has measurable costs across vigilance, mood, judgement, and health; and sustained work without recovery is associated with the presentations described throughout this book. Those presentations develop in stages: the reduction of the man's margins, the loss of his restoration, the body and the mind, cynicism and the loss of meaning, and the collapse or the emergency — while the household pays in reduced arrangements, in the man's conduct toward its members, in its management of his state, and in the transfer of his condition to the people who are available.

The guilt of resting is produced by a rule, available in five forms — worth, security, desert, comparison, and inheritance — and it operates by applying a short accounting period to a proposition about the man's standing. Seven statements are supplied with their counter-arguments, and the decisive reframing is that the position's requirements are met by the man's judgement, availability, and conduct, none of which is served by a man whose capacity is depleting: the measure is a component of his obligations rather than an indulgence. The arrangement has six components — the sleep specification, the daily measure, the weekly specification including the telephone, the annual specification, the boundary, and the stated arrangement with the household — and the first measure of progress is the existence of the arrangement rather than the man's state on the day it is constructed.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the recovery audit. What is the last full day you did not work, what is your average sleep, and which of the six categories are absent?
2. Which of the five stages of Section 37.3 is your position? Which presentation has your household noticed, and what has it done about it?
3. Complete the four questions put to a member of your household. What did the answers contain that you did not expect?
4. Write the rule in your own terms. What is the accounting period you apply to yourself, and what evidence would you require from another man before accepting it about him?
5. Which of the seven statements is strongest in your case, and which part of the counter-argument in Section 37.5 do you accept?
6. Write the weekly specification in full, including the telephone's arrangement, and name the person who will hold the copy.
7. What did your father's working pattern cost him, what did it produce, and what do you owe him? Write the two sentences separately.

## Further Reading

- **On sleep and performance:** the research literature on sleep restriction and its effects on vigilance, mood, judgement, and health, including the findings on the accumulation of effects across successive nights and the evidence on the difference between measured and self-assessed performance.
- **On recovery from work and on occupational burnout:** the literature on recovery from work, including the findings on the categories of recovery, on the value of short frequent episodes as against extended breaks, and on the associations between sustained work without recovery and the outcomes described in this chapter.
- **On overwork and health:** the research on long working hours and health outcomes, including the findings on cardiovascular outcomes, on mental health, and on the relationship between hours and productivity.
- **On leisure and wellbeing:** the research on leisure and on the forms of activity associated with restoration, including the findings on physical activity, on engaging pursuits, and on the social and creative forms.
- **Practical resources:** general practice and occupational health services for sleep, blood pressure, and the presentations of Section 37.3; employee assistance programmes; leave and flexible-working arrangements; and the material in Appendices C, N, and P.
