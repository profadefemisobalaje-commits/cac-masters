## 5.7 Illustrative Case: The Executive Who Kept a Notebook

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns commonly reported in clinical and occupational health settings.

**Anthony is thirty-nine, a finance manager in a manufacturing company, married, with two children under ten.** He is regarded as the most reliable person in his department. He arrives early, leaves late, keeps a meticulous record of every transaction and conversation, and has never missed a deadline.

Six years ago, Anthony made an error that cost his employer a modest sum — approximately the cost of a month of his own salary — in a tax filing. He discovered it himself, reported it immediately, and the matter was corrected without comment. His supervisor at the time told him not to worry about it.

Anthony has not stopped worrying about it for six years.

The concern did not remain attached to the original event. Instead, it migrated. He began to check his work repeatedly, then to check other people's work, then to keep a personal record of every decision he made, in a notebook kept in his bag, so that he could demonstrate, if required, that he had acted correctly. Over three years the notebook became a daily practice. By the fifth year he was photographing documents on his telephone so that a record would exist outside the company's systems.

In parallel, three other things developed. He began to sleep poorly, waking at about four o'clock with a distinct sensation that something had been overlooked. He became irritated by interruptions, particularly by colleagues asking him questions in front of others. And he developed a set of physical symptoms — a band of tension across the upper back and shoulders, occasional stomach pain, and a persistent dry mouth — for which two physicians found no cause and for which he was eventually prescribed muscle relaxants.

He never told anyone about the notebook. His wife knew that he worked long hours; his supervisor considered him exceptionally thorough. He did not consider himself an anxious man, and when a physician asked him, during a routine consultation, whether he was under stress, he answered that work was busy but that he was managing. He was telling the truth as he understood it.

The change came about through a conversation with a colleague who had himself been treated for an anxiety disorder and who, having noticed the notebook, asked a direct question. The question was not *Are you all right?* It was: *Anthony, why do you keep that book?* He answered, for the first time, that if he did not, something would go wrong again. Eight months later, after treatment consisting of cognitive behavioural therapy and a short course of medication, he described the situation in a sentence that is worth quoting because it captures the whole chapter: *The mistake cost the company a month's salary. The fear of it has cost me six years of my life, and until the day I explained it to somebody, I did not know that was the bill.*
:::

The case contains five features that are typical, and each corresponds to a section of this chapter.

**The distress was invisible and had no obvious cause.** The precipitating event was minor, resolved, and known to two people, one of whom had forgotten it.

**The presentation was behavioural and physical rather than emotional.** Checking, recording, tension, and insomnia. At no point did Anthony describe himself as anxious, and he answered the standard screening question negatively.

**Compensatory behaviour preserved the appearance of excellence.** The notebook, which was a symptom, was read by his employer as a virtue. This is one of the mechanisms by which anxiety in capable men survives undetected: the symptoms are frequently indistinguishable from, or even identical to, exceptional conscientiousness.

**The cost was measured in years, not in events.** The original incident cost a month's salary; the anxiety cost six years of Anthony's attention, and it also cost his sleep, his comfort with colleagues, and a substantial amount of his family life.

**The door was opened by a direct question from a peer.** Not by a physician's screening question, which was answered automatically, but by a specific question about a specific behaviour, asked by somebody who had been in a similar position. This is consistent with the evidence on male help-seeking and is why Chapters 26 and 27, and Appendix J, treat the presence of at least one informed peer as a public-health matter rather than a matter of preference.

## 5.8 Practical Exercise: Writing the Account You Cannot Say

This exercise produces a short written account of your current internal state, of the kind a physician, counsellor, or psychologist can work with. Complete it in a single sitting if possible, and write rather than type if you can, because handwriting slows the process enough to allow accuracy.

### Part One: The Timeline

Draw a horizontal line and mark the last three years. On the line, write every event that changed your state of mind, with its month where you can remember it: a loss, a job change, a financial event, an illness, a conflict, a disappointment, a diagnosis, a death, a move, a separation. Add anything from earlier that still produces a reaction when you think about it, with an approximate year.

The purpose of this part is to answer the clinician's first question — *When did this begin?* — which men frequently cannot answer, and which is often supplied by the timeline within ten minutes.

Complete the following table as honestly as you can. Where the answer is *no*, write no. Where the answer is *yes*, add how long it has been happening.

### Part Three: The Consequences

Write, in no more than six lines, what this has cost you in the last twelve months. Use specific, observable statements rather than general ones: days missed from work, arguments that recur, an invitation declined, a task not completed, a relationship that has changed, a decision postponed, a trip not taken, a period not remembered.

### Part Four: The One Sentence

Draft a single sentence, of no more than twenty-five words, that summarises your situation for a reader who does not know you. It should describe what is happening rather than why. Two examples of the form:

> *Since my father died in March I have not been able to sleep properly, and I have become irritable with my children in a way that is not like me.*

> *For about two years I have had a persistent sense that everything I have built could collapse, and I now check things repeatedly and cannot rest.*

### Part Five: The Next Contact

Identify, from the list in Section 5.5, the single most accessible route for you: a general physician, a hospital clinic, a counsellor, an employer scheme, a telephone line, or an online service. Write down the day and time you will make contact, and what you will say in the first thirty seconds. A serviceable script is:

> *I would like to make an appointment to talk about something that has been affecting me. I have not been sleeping well and I have not been myself for some months. I am not sure what the problem is, and I would like to have it looked at.*

Keep the written account with you when you make the contact. If the words will not come, the document can be handed over instead, and this is a perfectly acceptable method that practitioners see frequently.
