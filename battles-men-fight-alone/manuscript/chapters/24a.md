# When a Man Loses Himself {.chapter num="24" short="When a Man Loses Himself"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the process by which a man's identity becomes attached to his roles, and explain why the attachment is invisible until the roles change.
2. Identify the specific events in which a man's roles disappear, and describe the loss that follows each.
3. Recognise the features of the period in which a man does not know who he is, and distinguish it from depression, grief, and ordinary transition.
4. Analyse the search for meaning outside achievement, and assess the resources available to him.
5. Apply a structured method for reconstructing a self after a rupture, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Construct a purpose for the second half of life that is not reducible to occupation, income, or standing.
:::

## Introduction

There is a specific kind of collapse that occurs in men who have done nothing wrong. A man works for thirty-two years, provides for his household, holds a position, is known in his community, and retires on a Friday. On the following Monday he has no function, no schedule, no audience, and no answer to the question of what he is. Within a few months his wife observes that he has become silent, that he follows her around the house, that he has begun to drink in the afternoon, and that he is angry about matters that did not previously concern him. His children observe that he telephones them with no subject. His physician records a rise in blood pressure and a loss of weight. And the man himself, if he is asked how he is, will say that he is adjusting, because he has no vocabulary for what has occurred and because the event that occurred — retirement — is not understood by anybody as a loss.

This chapter addresses that collapse and its many equivalents. Its subject is the loss of the self, and its thesis is specific: men's identities are frequently constructed almost entirely from their roles, and a role is not a self. A man may occupy, simultaneously, the roles of provider, husband, father, professional, community officer, and family representative; each role supplies structure, purpose, standing, and a definition of what to do next; and the ordinary experience of a man's adult life is of these roles reinforcing one another. When they are removed — by retirement, redundancy, the children's departure, separation, bereavement, illness, or a business failure — there is nothing underneath, and the collapse is experienced not as the loss of a job or a relationship but as the loss of the person.

Two clarifications are necessary before the analysis proceeds.

The first is that this is not a failure of imagination in the men concerned. A man who has spent thirty years working sixteen hours a day and supporting eleven people has not had the time to build a self that exists independently of those obligations, and his community has not supplied him with any account of what a man is apart from his function. The collapse is a structural product of the arrangement described throughout Part II of this book, and its treatment is therefore the reconstruction of something that was never built, not the recovery of something that was lost.

The second is that the collapse is frequently misattributed. A man in this condition presents with symptoms — insomnia, irritability, loss of appetite, withdrawal, loss of interest, a persistent sense of emptiness — and he is assessed as depressed, which he frequently is, and treating the depression alone leaves the condition intact, because the depression was the surface of a structural absence. The chapters on treatment in Part X apply, and this chapter supplies the material that the treatment will require.

## 24.1 The Loss of Identity in Mid-Life

Identity, in the sense used in this chapter, is the answer to three questions: what am I, what am I for, and who knows me? A man's answers to the three questions are assembled from his roles, and the assembly is invisible to him because it is never examined while the roles continue.

### How Identity Is Assembled

A man's identity is built from five materials, in the following order of the weight most men give them.

**Occupation.** The work the man does, and the position within it. Its contribution to identity is enormous and frequently underestimated by men who are in work. It supplies structure, purpose, competence, an audience, a social position, and the answer to the question *what do you do?* which is the first question asked of a man at any gathering.

**Provision.** The man's capacity to support his household and his extended family. It supplies the tangible evidence of his usefulness and the reason for his effort, and it is the material of Parts III and V of this book.

**Relationships in the roles.** Father, husband, son, brother, uncle, head of a family. Each supplies a definition of what is required and a position in the family's structure.

**Standing.** The man's position in his community, his association, his profession: the respect, the position held, the recognition, the capacity to be called upon.

**Interests, capacities, and relationships outside the roles.** This material is the smallest for most men in the pattern this chapter addresses, and it is the material on which the reconstruction depends.

### The Characteristic Structure

Four features of the assembly are worth stating because they determine the collapse.

**The materials are all function-based.** Occupation, provision, and the role-relationships are all definitions of what a man does rather than of what he is. Chapter 18's account of the husband's role and Chapter 17's account of the father's role are examples of the same structure.

**The materials are mutually dependent.** A man's occupation supplies the provision, and the provision is what makes him a valid father and husband in his own and his family's account. The materials therefore fail together, and a partial failure — a loss of income, for example — is experienced as the failure of the whole.

**The materials produce a busy life.** The arrangement described in Chapters 10 and 12 supplies structure to every hour of a man's day, and the structure prevents the questions from arising. This is the mechanism by which the examination is postponed: a man who is occupied cannot ask what he is.

**There is no material outside the roles.** For most men in this pattern, there is no activity, relationship, or interest that is genuinely the man's own and not attached to a function. The absence is invisible until the roles end, at which point the man discovers that he has no arrangement for the hours and no account of himself.

### The Point of the Chapter

The purpose of this analysis is not to recommend that men detach themselves from their roles, which is neither possible nor desirable, and not to suggest that a role-based identity is a defect. A man who is a good father because his identity requires it is performing a good that would otherwise not be performed. The purpose is to establish the practical point: **a man should hold one material of identity that does not depend on the roles continuing.** One activity, one relationship, one capacity, one contribution, one thing he is that is not a function. The chapters that follow describe the construction, and the case in Section 24.7 describes what happens without it.
