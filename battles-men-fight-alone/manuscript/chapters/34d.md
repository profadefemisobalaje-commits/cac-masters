## 34.5 The Emergency: The Safety Plan

This section concerns the situation in which the material has reached the point of risk to the man's own life, and it is written in the form used in clinical practice rather than in the form of the rest of the book. The reader who recognises his own position in Group One of Section 34.4 should treat this section as the chapter's first instruction and should act on it before continuing.

### The First Instruction

**If you are thinking about ending your own life, or if you have formed the view that the people around you would be better off without you, the matter requires a professional assessment today, and it should not be managed privately.**

Three routes, and any of them is sufficient.

**The crisis line or the emergency number.** Every state operates one or more, and the specific numbers for the reader's country are in Appendix Q of this book. A crisis line is staffed by people trained for exactly this conversation, it is confidential, it can be used by a man who does not want to explain himself to anybody he knows, and it can arrange the next step.

**The emergency department of a general hospital.** Attendance, at any hour, with the words the man is able to use: *I have been thinking about ending my life.* The sentence is sufficient. No further explanation is required at that point, and the practitioner's next step is the assessment.

**A general practitioner or a mental health practitioner, urgently.** Where the man has an existing relationship, this is the fastest route to the assessment. Where the man cannot obtain an appointment promptly and the risk is present, the emergency route should be used instead and the appointment should not be waited for.

### The Obstacles and Their Answers

Four obstacles stop men in this category from acting, and each has an answer that is drawn from the material of the clinical practice on men and risk.

**"I do not want to be a burden, and I do not want to be seen like this."** The concern is real and the specific finding is worth stating: the practitioners who receive men in this position do not regard them as burdens, and the professional route exists precisely because the material may need to be held by somebody who is not the man's household. The confidentiality obligations of the professional arrangement are the mechanism.

**"Nothing will change."** This belief is characteristic of the condition and not of the situation, and it is the symptom that most reliably distinguishes a treatable state from a settled assessment. A man whose perception has been altered by a condition cannot evaluate his own position from inside it; the evaluation must be conducted externally, which is the reason the assessment rather than private deliberation is required.

**"I have a position to protect."** Then the professional route is the one that protects it. The confidentiality of medical and psychological consultation is a legal and professional obligation rather than a favour, and the men in the case material who hold substantial positions are generally able to obtain an assessment without it becoming known.

**"I am needed, and admitting this means I am failing my household."** The household requires the man alive and functioning rather than the man's concealment. This is not a rhetorical claim; the bereavement material on families after a man's death is unambiguous, and the chapters on men's families in this book supply its content.

### The Safety Plan

The following plan is the form used in clinical practice, and a man in or near the risk category should write it out and keep it where he can find it — in his wallet, on his telephone, or in a document he will see. It is written for the man and it is also written to be handed to somebody who is trying to help him.

**Step 1: The warning signs.** What the man's own pattern is when the risk is rising. Examples from the practice material: the early waking; the flatness on a particular day; the withdrawal from a person; the belief that the household would be better off; the desire to drive a particular route fast; the feeling of being trapped; the period after a specific loss; the weekend; the month of a specific anniversary. The list is specific to the man, and it should be honest.

**Step 2: The internal measures.** The things the man can do on his own that reliably reduce the state, even partially, and that he has used before. The practice material includes: physical exertion to the point of exhaustion; a specific route walked; cold water on the face and hands; the reduction of the immediate setting; sleep; eating; the company of a specific person; a task requiring concentration; the postponement of any decision; the reduction of the means of harm in his own environment.

**Step 3: The people.** The names and numbers of the people the man will contact. At least two, and the plan should state the sentence he will use: *I am not in a good state and I need to be with somebody.* Where the man has nobody, the plan names the professional and the crisis line, and the man should treat the absence of a person as the reason to use the second.

**Step 4: The places and the settings.** The settings that must be avoided when the risk is rising: the location the man has considered, the routes, the vehicle, the alcohol, the firearm or the medication, the person, the argument. The plan's requirement is specific and it is the same requirement as the one on means safety described in Step 5.

**Step 5: The means.** The plan states what will be done about the means of harm: the medication returned to the clinic or handed to somebody; the firearm stored elsewhere; the keys, the alcohol, and the vehicle managed; the specific location avoided; the arrangement established with a person who will hold the items. This step is the one with the strongest evidence in suicide prevention, and it is the step men most frequently omit.

**Step 6: The professionals.** The name, the arrangement, and the number: the general practitioner, the practitioner the man is seeing, the crisis line, the emergency department. The plan states that the professional route is used when Steps 2 and 3 have not reduced the state, and that it is used immediately when the risk is rising.

**Step 7: The reasons.** Written in the man's own hand and in his own terms, and specific: the people, the obligations, the things that would be lost, the account of what his absence would do. The step draws directly on the material of this book: the man's duties to his household in Part V, the people who depend on him in Part IX, the conduct he has discharged, and the life he has built as Chapter 28.6 counts it. The step's function is that it is the man's own statement, written in the state in which he was able to reason, and it is available to him in the state in which he is not.

### The Plan for Somebody Else

Where the reader is concerned about another man, the plan is used as follows.

**Ask directly.** The evidence is consistent on this point and it is worth stating in full: asking a man whether he is thinking about ending his life does not increase the risk, and the direct question is the single most useful act available to a person who is concerned. The question should be plain: *Are you thinking about ending your life?* The man's answer is frequently the first disclosure he has made.

**Do not leave him alone with the material.** Stay with him; help him make the first contact; where the risk is immediate, do not leave him to travel alone and do not leave him with the means of harm.

**Help him make the call rather than advising him to make one.** The mechanism is the arrangement rather than the advice, as Chapter 27.5 established: the man who cannot construct the first step will frequently take it if somebody is present while he does.

**Do not keep the confidence.** Where the man has told you he intends to harm himself, the professional obligation of a friend is that the arrangement is contacted. The instruction should be stated plainly: the confidence is not a confidence where the man's life is at risk.
