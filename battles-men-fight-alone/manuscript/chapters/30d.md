## 30.5 When the Loss Cannot Be Spoken Of

The chapter's most difficult category is the loss that cannot be acknowledged, because the acknowledgement would cost the man something that he cannot afford to lose. The following five forms are reported consistently, and each is presented here for the purpose of recognition rather than for the purpose of judgement.

### The Five Forms

**1. The death whose manner the community treats as a judgement.** The death by suicide, by overdose, by an illness that carries stigma, by violence in circumstances that reflect badly on the family, or by an event that the community reads as the consequence of the household's conduct. The man's own position is compounded: he has lost somebody and he is required, simultaneously, to manage the community's interpretation of the death, which consumes the attention that the mourning would require. Men in this position describe the household's management of the facts as the first priority in the period after the death, and the grief is deferred indefinitely.

**2. The relationship that the man's household does not know existed.** The loss of a person with whom the man's connection cannot be disclosed: a relationship from before his marriage, a child for whom he was never acknowledged, a companion in a matter that would damage him, a person whose connection to the man would damage the man's family. The loss is real and the mourning is impossible in every arrangement available to him, which produces the position described in Section 29.2 in its most complete form: the material is held privately, protected from correction, and carried without permission.

**3. The loss connected to the man's own conduct.** The person whose death followed a matter in which the man was involved: the man killed in a collision in which the man was driving, the death during an arrangement the man proposed, the death of somebody the man introduced to a risk. This form combines the material of this chapter with the material of Chapter 29, and the two must be worked together: the mourning concerns the person, and the remorse concerns the man's own conduct, and neither is discharged by the other.

**4. The loss the man is not permitted to mourn.** In some circumstances the man's mourning itself would be an admission: the father who is not permitted to display grief for a child the family regards as having disgraced it; the man whose grief for a former partner would be read by his household, his children, or his present wife as an act of disloyalty; the man whose loss concerns a person his own community regards as an opponent or an offender. Men in this position frequently describe their own house as a place in which the material cannot be present.

**5. The loss of a person with whom the man's history is a secret.** A category described by men in treatment for addiction, in prison settings, and in the material of veterans' services: the loss of a companion from a period of the man's life that is unknown to his household, so that the mourning would require a disclosure that the man has never made. The person is real, the loss is real, and the absence of any recipient is total, because the only people who knew the relationship are the people in the concealed period, with whom the man has no contact.

### What the Man Requires in This Category

Four requirements, and they should be stated as requirements rather than as suggestions, because the category has no self-managing form.

**1. One recipient whose obligation is confidentiality.** The requirement is structural: the man requires at least one recipient who is not part of the audience that the material must be protected from. A professional arrangement — a counsellor, a general practitioner, a psychologist, an employee assistance service — satisfies the requirement exactly, since the confidentiality is governed by an obligation and the practitioner's judgement of the material is not the judgement of the community. Where the material concerns the man's own conduct as well, the arrangement is a clinical one, for the reasons given in Chapter 29.6.

**2. A place of the man's own.** A location, an hour, a drive, a room, a time on a stated day: somewhere the material can be engaged in forms that are not the household's. The requirement is not decorative. Men in this category consistently report that the presence of the material is continuous and that it requires a place rather than an occasion, and the construction of a private place is frequently the element that makes the material bearable.

**3. The material conducted with reference to the person and not to the secret.** The most important of the four, and the one that distinguishes the work of this chapter from the work of Chapter 22. Where the material concerns a relationship the man cannot disclose, the work must retain the person as its subject: what the person was, what the man owed him, what he means in the man's account of himself. The material conducted with reference to the secret becomes a second concealment, and men in this position describe a specific and painful result: the person's memory becomes an inventory of the man's circumstances rather than a memory of the person.

**4. A decision about the secret that is separated from the decision about the loss.** The two questions must be answered separately: whether the matter will ever be disclosed to the household, and what the man will do about the loss now. Men in this category frequently conflate the two and conclude that the loss cannot be addressed until the disclosure is settled, which postpones the work indefinitely. The chapter's position is that the two decisions are separate, that the second can proceed without the first, and that a decision taken in either direction on the first question is legitimate provided it has been taken deliberately rather than by default.

## 30.6 The Arrangements That Give Grief a Place

This section consolidates the practical work: the arrangements that supply, for a grief without an occasion, what the occasion would have supplied.

### The Six Arrangements

**1. The date.** A date in the man's calendar associated with the person: the anniversary of the death, the person's birthday, the date of a shared event. Its function is to gather the material into a place in the year rather than allowing it to be distributed across the whole of it. The arrangement is the one the chapter recommends most strongly, and men who adopt it report a specific benefit: the material arrives on the date, is attended to, and does not arrive continuously.

**2. The place.** The grave, the town, the house, the workshop, the spot. Where the place does not exist, the substitute — a photograph in a particular position, an object on a shelf, a route driven annually, a tree, a page in a record. The place supplies the anchor that the physical arrangements of a funeral would have supplied.

**3. The account.** The written record of the person and of the loss: what he was, what the man owes him, what the man's own position is, and what will be maintained. The document is the man's own and it need not be seen by anybody unless he chooses to show it.

**4. The recipient.** One person with whom the loss can be discussed, on the man's own terms. Where a person who knew the deceased is available, that person is preferable, for the reason given in Section 30.4: the shared account and the acknowledgement of a loss that is also somebody else's. Where no such person exists, the arrangement is professional, and the men who have used this route report that the specific benefit of the professional arrangement is that the man's own account of the loss can be stated without the requirement to protect the listener's feelings.

**5. The conduct in the person's name.** The obligation or the practice the man maintains in relation to the loss: the support of the deceased's household, the training of a young man as the deceased had trained the man, the annual contribution, the maintenance of the grave, the completion of the deceased's matter, the correspondence with the deceased's children. Chapter 29.6's material on redirection applies, and its value here is that it moves the material into conduct rather than leaving it in the man's private state.

**6. The household's arrangement.** The final and most frequently omitted element: the man's own household is entitled to know that he is carrying a loss, in the form that the loss permits. The disclosure does not require the content; it may consist of the fact. *The man who died was my friend for thirty years and I am not all right about it* is a sentence that men report as changing the arrangements in the household immediately, because it supplies the household with the explanation for the change in the man's temper, availability, and quietness that they had been attributing to something else.

### What These Arrangements Require of the Man

Two things only: that the arrangements have dates and that they are his. The first requirement comes from the material on transition in Chapter 24: a matter acquires a place when it has a time and a location. The second is the requirement that the arrangements not be constructed from a template — the place, the form, and the conduct must correspond to the man and to the person, and an arrangement that has been adopted from a general prescription will not be maintained.

### Two Clarifications

**This is not a programme for closure.** The arrangements do not end the grief and they are not intended to. Their function is that the material has a place, that it is connected to the person, and that it is not distributed into the man's temper, his household, his work, his body, or his drinking.

**The arrangements are conducted in the man's own form.** A man who cannot speak of the loss at the graveside can stand there. A man who cannot describe the person to a listener can write the page and leave it in the file. A man who cannot attend a support arrangement can maintain the deceased's obligation, and the conduct is a form of mourning. The chapter's position is stated plainly: the work is the man's engagement with the material in whatever form he can sustain, and the forms supplied in this chapter are not a prescription but a list from which the man selects what he will actually do.
