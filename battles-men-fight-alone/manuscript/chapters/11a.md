# The Pressure to Become Successful {.chapter num="11" short="The Pressure to Become Successful"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the social definition of a successful man, identify its components, and explain how that definition is transmitted and enforced.
2. Analyse the relationship between career ambition and personal identity, including the point at which ambition begins to substitute for identity.
3. Describe the experience of delayed achievement and its psychological consequences, and distinguish between delay that is temporary and delay that requires a change of plan.
4. Recognise the specific effects of unemployment, underemployment, and lost opportunity on men, and identify the support that is appropriate in each case.
5. Assess the cost of constant comparison in his own working life, and apply the practical measures that reduce it.
6. Construct an individual definition of success, expressed in specific and verifiable terms, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

There is a conversation that takes place in almost every extended family, at almost every gathering, and it follows a predictable script. A young man is asked what he is doing now. He answers with his employment, his business, or his studies. The elder responds with a comparison: a cousin who has been called to the bar, a neighbour's son who has a job with an oil company, a nephew who has built a house in the town. The comparison is intended as encouragement, and it is received as a measurement.

Twenty-five years later, the same man is fifty, and the conversation has changed format but not function. He is now the one being described to others, and the description consists of his position, his property, his children's institutions, and his contribution at the last ceremony. He has, by the standards of the conversation, done reasonably well. He is also aware, with a precision that he does not discuss, that he was overtaken by two men he started with, that he did not become what he was expected to become, and that some of the ambitions he held at twenty-five are now permanently out of reach.

This chapter is about the pressure to become successful, and it proceeds from a distinction that will organise everything else. There are two questions in this territory, and they are frequently confused. The first is: **am I making progress towards something I actually value?** The second is: **am I ahead of the people I am being compared with?** The first is a question about a life. The second is a question about a ranking. Most men, if asked, would say that the first matters more. Most men, under pressure, spend their working lives answering the second.

## 11.1 Society's Definition of a Successful Man

The definition of success varies in detail between communities, and its structure is remarkably consistent. Five components do most of the work.

**Income and its visible signs.** A man's income is not usually known, so it is inferred from what can be seen: housing, vehicle, clothing, the school attended by his children, the size of his contributions, and the ease with which he responds to requests for assistance.

**Occupation and title.** The nature and status of a man's work, together with any formal position he occupies. In many communities the distinction between salaried employment, self-employment, and informal work carries substantial evaluative weight, independently of the income that each produces.

**Family formation and its success.** Marriage, children, and the visible wellbeing of the household. A man whose marriage has failed, or whose adult children are not prospering, is frequently assessed as having been unsuccessful regardless of his professional record.

**Property.** Land, housing, and, frequently, a house in the home area. Property is the most durable and most public of the markers, and its acquisition is frequently the standard by which a working life is judged.

**Standing in the community.** Positions held, respect shown, the ability to be called upon, and the recognition of the family as one that has produced someone.

Each component is legitimate as a value. Housing, family, useful work, and community standing are goods in themselves. The difficulty is produced by three features of the aggregate definition, and they are worth examining carefully, because each of them causes specific damage.

### Feature One: The Definition Is Comparative

Success in this framework is not defined by a standard but by a position. A man is successful if he is ahead of a reference group, and the reference group is not fixed. This produces the consequences set out in Chapter 7: satisfaction is temporary, the standard rises with achievement, and the conclusion is never reached. It also produces a specific distortion in the assessment of one's own life: a man may have made genuine progress in the terms that matter to him — income sufficient for his household, children educated, health intact, marriage stable — and still assess himself as unsuccessful because the comparison is unfavourable.

### Feature Two: The Definition Is Public

Men are assessed by others, and the assessment is conducted in public and recorded in visible form. The consequence is that a man's private evaluation of his own life is constantly contradicted, or confirmed, by other people's commentary: the remark about the car, the question about the house, the observation that a contemporary has been promoted, the unsolicited advice about what he should consider doing next.

The public quality of the assessment has an important implication. A man cannot simply decide to adopt a private definition of success without cost, because the public definition continues to operate on him through the people around him. The practical work, described in Section 11.6, therefore involves both a private standard and a practical strategy for handling the public one.

### Feature Three: The Timetable Is Borrowed from a Different Economy

The third feature is the most damaging and the least often discussed. The definition of a successful man was established in economies with predictable career ladders, stable employment, and rising real incomes, and it assumed a life in which a man would be established by his late thirties and secure by his fifties. It is now applied to men living in economies with high youth unemployment, unstable earnings, informal-sector work, currency instability, and career paths that are frequently interrupted rather than progressive.

The consequence is a systematic setting of expectations that many men cannot meet, through no failure of their own. A man of thirty-five who is working hard, supporting his household, and making genuine progress is measured against a timetable calibrated to a different economic world, and he is instructed — by the comparison, by his family, and, most severely, by himself — that he is behind.

::: keypoint
The pressure to succeed combines five legitimate values — income, work, family, property, and standing — with three destructive properties: a comparative standard, a public assessment, and a timetable borrowed from an economy that no longer exists. The values can be kept. The three properties must be replaced.
:::

## 11.2 Career Ambition and Personal Identity

Ambition is not a disorder. The desire to build something, to advance, to be of use, and to achieve a standard is honourable, and it is one of the sources of the considerable achievements of the men this book is written for. The difficulty arises when ambition ceases to be an activity and becomes an identity, and this happens so gradually that most men cannot identify the point of transition.

### The Three Relationships of a Man to His Work

It is useful to distinguish three positions, because the interventions differ.

**Work as a means.** The man works in order to fund a life. His identity is elsewhere: family, faith, community, craft, or interests. He performs well, frequently out of professional obligation, and he leaves the work at the end of the day. This position is increasingly uncommon among men in professional employment, and it remains legitimate and durable.

**Work as a vocation.** The man is genuinely engaged by the work itself: he finds it interesting, he develops, he takes pride in quality, and he is willing to invest heavily in it. His identity is substantially invested in the work, and this is a stable arrangement as long as the work remains available and as long as the man retains some identity outside it.

**Work as an identity.** The man's entire sense of himself is contained in his professional role and its position. This position is unstable in three specific ways: it fails when the work is lost; it produces chronic insecurity even when the work succeeds, because the identity requires continuous confirmation; and it produces the erosion described in Chapter 24, in which a man who loses his role loses his sense of who he is.

The transition from the second position to the third is rarely deliberate. It occurs through the ordinary accumulation of years in which the man's competence, recognition, and usefulness are supplied almost exclusively by his work, and in which the other sources of identity — friendships, interests, community participation, and family roles — have been allowed to become secondary or have been abandoned for lack of time.

### The Substitution of Achievement for Identity

A specific mechanism is worth naming, because it is common among men in mid-life. A man who has lost an important external source of identity — a marriage, a father, a community role, a faith practice, a friendship group — frequently substitutes professional achievement for what he has lost, without recognising that this is what he is doing. The substitution is effective in the short term, because achievement supplies structure and self-regard. Its costs are three.

**It requires continuous output.** Where identity is supplied by achievement, the cessation of achievement is an identity emergency rather than a period of rest. Men in this position cannot take leave without discomfort, and retirement is frequently experienced not as relief but as bereavement.

**It is fragile to market conditions.** Work-based identity is vulnerable to events entirely outside the man's control: a company restructuring, an economic downturn, an industry change, an illness.

**It removes the other sources.** The time invested in achievement is taken from the relationships and activities that would supply identity in other circumstances, which means a man who has substituted achievement for identity has also destroyed his alternatives.

### The Ambition That Cannot Be Satisfied

A further pattern is worth describing because it is so frequently reported by successful men: the ambition that expands at the rate of achievement, so that no level of attainment produces satisfaction. Its features are recognisable. Each goal, once achieved, is immediately replaced by a larger one. Rest feels like falling behind. Competitors are monitored continuously. Achievements are enjoyed for a period measured in days, if at all.

The pattern has a source that is worth understanding: the ambition is not calibrated to a life but to a position. Because the position can always be improved, and because the reference group can always be enlarged, the ambition has no terminus, and it delivers a sustainable supply of dissatisfaction with a variable supply of accomplishment. Chapter 7 set out the machinery. In this chapter, the important point is that the pattern is frequently mistaken for dedication, and that the men who hold it are frequently respected for the very arrangement that is making them miserable.
