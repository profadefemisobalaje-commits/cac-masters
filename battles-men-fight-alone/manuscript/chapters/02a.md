# The Burden of Being a Man {.chapter num="2" short="The Burden of Being a Man"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the expectations that a man's society places on him, and identify the specific ways in which those expectations are communicated to boys and maintained in adult life.
2. Analyse the three duties of adult manhood — to provide, to protect, and to perform — and assess the personal cost of carrying all three without relief.
3. Explain the fear of being considered weak, distinguish it from the fear of being weak, and describe the behaviour it produces in work, marriage, and friendship.
4. Identify the structural reason that men receive less emotional support than women and describe the consequences for health, relationships, and decision-making.
5. Recognise the pattern in which manhood becomes a lifelong examination with no examiner and no conclusion, and describe its effects in mid-life and later life.
6. Apply a revised understanding of strength, and use the exercise at the end of this chapter to audit the script he is carrying.
:::

## Introduction

Every society distributes certain expectations by sex, and every society therefore hands each man a script before he can read. The script is rarely written down, and it is remarkably consistent in its outline across countries, religions, and economic systems, which is what makes it worth treating as a subject in its own right rather than as a local custom.

In the case of men, the script has five clauses. A man must be able to provide. A man must be able to protect. A man must be able to perform — to succeed at whatever he undertakes, and to be seen to succeed. A man must endure, which is to say that he must absorb difficulty without visible strain. And a man must control his feelings, particularly the feelings that would make him appear dependent: fear, sadness, and the need for comfort.

The script has real value, and it is important not to caricature it. It produced the men who built the roads, staffed the hospitals at night, carried households through currency collapses, and stayed at their posts when leaving would have been easier. Many of the qualities it prescribes — reliability, courage, endurance, the willingness to be responsible for other people — are genuine virtues, and this book does not propose that men should shed them.

What this chapter examines is what the script costs the man who keeps it. Five costs are consistent enough across the research and the clinical accounts to be treated as established. The first is that the script requires performance without accompaniment: it specifies what a man must do and says nothing about what a man may need. The second is that its clauses are more demanding than the resources available to most men in most economies, which means that a large proportion of men are permanently in deficit against a standard they did not choose. The third is that the script defines rest, disclosure, and dependency as failures, which removes the three mechanisms by which human beings recover from prolonged strain. The fourth is that it is enforced by other men as much as by women, so that the nearest source of relief is also the nearest source of censure. And the fifth, which is developed in Chapter 4, is that performance of the script reliably succeeds at its visible objective while reliably failing at the one a man needs most, which is to be known.

## 2.1 Society's Expectations of Masculinity

The expectations can be summarised as six requirements, and it is worth setting them out individually because each of them produces a distinct pressure.

**Provide.** A man is expected to be the principal source of income for a household, to meet the requirements of dependants, and to be the person who resolves a financial emergency. This clause is the most heavily loaded in most of the countries where this book will be read, and it is the one that produces the material of Chapters 9 to 12.

**Protect.** A man is expected to be the one who manages danger: to intervene in a conflict, to accompany a relative through a hostile situation, to defend the household's position in a dispute, to handle the encounter with officials, employers, or creditors. Protection is frequently expected of men who are not physically imposing and who have no capacity to provide it, and the expectation is applied to the physical and the social domain at once.

**Perform.** A man's competence, output, and advancement are understood as evidence of what he is. Performance is not merely a work requirement; it is an assessment of the man, applied to professional duties, sexual capacity, fatherhood, and the management of his household.

**Endure.** A man is expected to absorb pain, exhaustion, and disappointment without displaying the strain. The allowance made for a man's suffering is visibly smaller than the allowance made for a woman's, and it is frequently smaller for the sons in a family than for the daughters.

**Decide.** A man is expected to be the one who knows, who determines, and who takes responsibility for the outcome. Where the decision belongs formally to somebody else — a wife who manages the household finances, a mother whose judgement governs the family, an employer — the expectation is still attached to the man, and his position is therefore both responsible and powerless.

**Control feeling.** The prohibition is specific rather than general. It applies to sadness, fear, longing, tenderness, shame, and the need for reassurance. It does not apply to anger, which is the one emotion men are permitted, and which consequently becomes the channel through which every other emotion is expressed, as Chapters 3 and 15 describe.

::: keypoint
The script does not ask a man to have no feelings. It asks him to have no feelings that require anything from another person. Anger, being the one emotion that requires nothing, is therefore the only one the script permits — and it is the reason that a distressed man is so frequently described as an angry one.
:::

### How the Script Is Transmitted

The script reaches men through five channels, and it is worth naming them because men who can identify the channels can interrupt them.

**Direct instruction in childhood.** Sentences such as *boys do not cry*, *be a man*, *stop behaving like a girl*, *nobody will help you if you are soft*, and *do not let anybody see you afraid*. The instruction is frequently affectionate in its intent and it is memorable in its effect; men asked to recall the first time they were told not to cry can usually produce the sentence and the year.

**Models.** The men available to the boy: a father who worked at two jobs and never mentioned difficulty, an uncle who spoke only about money and achievement, a teacher who rewarded silence. The modelling is more powerful than the instruction, because it demonstrates what is actually required.

**Correction in adolescence.** The specific moments in which a boy is treated as a man and learns what manhood requires of him: the first time he is sent to negotiate, the first time he is told to handle a family matter, the first time he is told the truth about the household's finances and sworn to silence. The transition is frequently abrupt, and men describe it with precision.

**Enforcement in adult life.** The script is maintained by other men, by women, and by institutions. The mechanisms are comment, comparison, exclusion, and the withdrawal of respect: the remark about a man who has been left by his wife, the joke about the man who attends to his children, the assumption that a man who discusses his difficulties is unreliable. Research on conformity indicates that this kind of informal enforcement is more effective than formal instruction, and the evidence from men's accounts supports that assessment.

**Economic conditions.** The fifth channel is the most powerful and the least discussed. Where a household depends on one income, the clause that requires provision is enforced not by anybody's opinion but by the household's requirements. This is why the script cannot be undone by a man's private decision alone: a man who has decided not to carry the burden of provision will still have to explain the decision to a household that depends on it.

### The Mismatch Between the Script and Contemporary Conditions

The script was assembled in conditions that no longer describe the economic lives of most men. Three mismatches are consequential.

The script assumes stable employment and progressive income across a working life. Most men now experience interrupted employment, irregular income, and a labour market in which the work available does not match the qualifications held.

The script assumes that a man's household is his principal economic obligation. In most of the countries where this book will be read, a man supports a household and an extended family whose requirements are not capped by his income and whose claims are enforced by custom rather than negotiated.

The script assumes that a man's worth is visible and publicly assessable, in the form of a house, a position, and the wellbeing of his children. Where the economy prevents a substantial proportion of men from reaching those markers, the script produces a class of men who are, by its own criteria, unsuccessful, and who are so in public and in permanent form.

The consequence of the mismatch is not that men abandon the script. It is that they carry it with more strain and less support than it was calibrated for, and that the failure they experience in meeting it is interpreted by themselves and by their communities as personal inadequacy rather than as a change in conditions. Chapter 11 addresses the consequences directly, and this chapter's remaining sections address the internal machinery.
