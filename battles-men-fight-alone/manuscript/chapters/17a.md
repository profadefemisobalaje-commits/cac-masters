# The Father Who Suffers in Silence {.chapter num="17" short="The Father Who Suffers in Silence"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the weight of paternal responsibility as men actually experience it, and distinguish it from the popular account of fatherhood as authority and provision.
2. Analyse the silent sacrifices that fathers make, including the specific mechanisms by which those sacrifices become invisible to the household and to the man himself.
3. Explain the fear of failing as a father, its origins, and the way it distorts the decisions a man makes about work, presence, and discipline.
4. Identify the processes by which fathers lose the capacity to show emotion, and describe the effects on children of a father who is present but emotionally unavailable.
5. Apply a structured approach to fatherhood that preserves authority and provision while restoring presence, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Design the legacy a father intends to leave, in terms of knowledge, relationship, and example rather than property alone.
:::

## Introduction

A man stands at the door of his house at eleven o'clock at night, takes off his shoes, and enters quietly so as not to wake the children. He has been away for fourteen hours. In the refrigerator there is a plate that his wife left for him. On the table there is a school notice that requires a signature and a payment. In the bedroom the children are asleep, and the youngest one has left a drawing on the bed with the words *for Daddy* written in it.

He eats standing up. He signs the notice. He stands in the doorway of the children's room for a moment, and he does not go in, for the reason that entering would wake them and the morning will be difficult. At half past six the following morning he leaves for work before they rise. The cycle will repeat for approximately two hundred and forty days of the year.

Ask this man whether he is suffering, and he will tell you no. Ask him what he is sacrificing, and he will tell you that he is not sacrificing anything — he is working. Ask him what his children will remember about him in thirty years, and he will pause, because the question has never been put to him and because he has never permitted himself to consider the answer, since considering it would be an admission that his presence matters and that he does not have it to give.

This chapter is about the father who suffers in silence: the man whose paternal responsibility is the heaviest part of his load, who discharges it as fully as he is able, and who pays for it in a currency he does not count and rarely discusses. The chapter does not argue that fathers should work less, which many cannot; it argues that a man should know the cost of the arrangement he has chosen or accepted, because an arrangement whose costs are known can be adjusted, and an arrangement whose costs are unknown will run for twenty years and then present its bill in the form of children who are polite strangers.

## 17.1 The Weight of Being a Father

Fatherhood is discussed in public in two registers, and neither of them describes what fathers experience.

The first register is **authority**. The father is described as the head of the household, the one whose word is final, the disciplinarian, the decision-maker. Where men are criticised, it is frequently in these terms: that they are absent, authoritarian, or uninvolved.

The second register is **provision**. The father is described as the breadwinner, assessed by his capacity to fund the household, and evaluated by the visible markers of that funding.

What is missing from both registers is the **responsibility as experienced from the inside**: the continuous awareness of dependence, the sense of being the person on whom consequences land, and the specific fear that has no name in either register, which is the fear of what will happen to the children if the man fails.

### The Five Components of the Weight

**1. The consciousness of dependence.** A man with children carries, permanently, the knowledge that other people's outcomes depend on him. This is not a thought he has occasionally; it is a background condition that shapes every decision. Its practical effect is that risk becomes almost impossible for him: the business opportunity that would require two years of reduced income, the move that would require the family to relocate, the career change that would require further study — each carries an assessment not of the man's own prospects but of the children's, and the assessment operates continuously.

**2. The impossibility of the standard.** The standard a father carries is not derived from his circumstances; it is derived from his expectations and from the comparison described in Chapter 11. The result, consistently reported by men, is the sense that they are providing substantially and are nevertheless short of what is required.

**3. The absence of an equivalent return.** A father's work for his children is frequently conducted in an arrangement that supplies him with no feedback. A man can assess his performance at work by output and assessment; at home, the feedback is delivered years later, in the form of who his children have become, and there is no interim appraisal. The absence of feedback is one of the reasons that fathers drift: nothing tells them that a change is required until the change is expensive.

**4. The expectation of being the household's shield.** A father is the person who absorbs bad news and communicates it in reduced form: the financial difficulty that becomes a scheduling adjustment, the illness that becomes a caution, the job loss that becomes a period of consulting work. The shielding is frequently well-intentioned and it is frequently misjudged, since it produces the household's ignorance of its own position, as Chapter 9 establishes.

**5. The fear for the children's world.** The fifth component is the most difficult and the least discussed: the father's fear for the world his children will inhabit. Employment conditions, economic instability, and the cost of education figure in it, but its content is broader: the father's knowledge of the world's difficulty and his inability to transmit that knowledge usefully. Men describe this fear with precision: *I know what it takes to survive out there and I cannot teach it to them and I cannot protect them from it.*

### The Structure of the Load

The load is distributed across four relationships at once, and this is the feature that distinguishes a father's position from most others.

| Relationship | The father's obligation | The characteristic strain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children | Provision, protection, guidance, example | Presence — the obligation with the least available time |
| Partner | Support, custody of the relationship's intimacy, joint management | Attention — the first obligation to be deferred under pressure |
| His own parents | Respect, care in illness, financial support | Duty — enforced by custom and by his own standards |
| Extended family and community | Contributions, mediation, representation, reputation | Standing — visible and continuously assessed |

Table: The four fronts on which a father's obligation is distributed, and the strain characteristic of each.

The strains are not additive, they are competing. The hours that satisfy the first reduce the second; the money that satisfies the fourth consumes the time that the first requires; the deference that the third requires consumes the decisions that the second needs to be shared. This competition — not the volume of the burden — is what fathers describe when they say that they are stretched: not that they have too much to do, but that each requirement is met at the cost of another.

### What Men Do Not Say

Three things about the weight are not said by men, and it is worth naming them explicitly, because each is common and each is treatable.

**That the children are the source of the pressure.** Men describe their children as the reason for their effort and rarely as a burden. Both statements are true, and the second is frequently unavailable, because the admission seems disloyal. In clinical accounts, the pressure associated with the paternal role is one of the most frequently concealed of all the strains men carry.

**That the marriage has been deprioritised deliberately.** Where a father has chosen work over attention to his wife, with the justification that the work serves the household, the admission is rarely made to anybody, and the consequences arrive as described in Chapter 15.

**That the man does not know his children.** Men frequently discover, in the children's adolescence, that they cannot describe their children's interior lives: what they worry about, whom they are close to, what they want, what they fear. The discovery is usually painful and it is usually late.
