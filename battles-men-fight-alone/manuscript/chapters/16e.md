## 16.8 Practical Exercise: The Injury Audit and Recovery Plan

This exercise takes about ninety minutes and is completed in writing. It is intended for a man carrying a rejection or a betrayal of some consequence, whether recent or of long standing.

### Part One: The Classified Record

List, briefly, the significant rejections and betrayals you have experienced and not resolved. For each, record four items.

| The event | How long ago | Domain | Resolved, carried, or active? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | |

Domains: family, peers or community, work or profession, children, marriage, institutional. Mark each as resolved — meaning concluded, understood, and no longer active in your thinking — carried, or active.

### Part Two: The Injury Breakdown

For the single most significant unresolved injury, answer the four questions corresponding to the four injuries in Section 16.3.

1. **Material:** what was lost, in specific and measurable terms?
2. **Relational:** which relationships were lost or altered, and how many people were involved in the knowledge?
3. **Model of reality:** what did this event teach you about how you assess people, and has that assessment changed permanently?
4. **Self-account:** what question about yourself does this injury continue to raise?

### Part Three: Phase Assessment

Mark your current phase, with evidence rather than by impression.

- **Acute:** the events are recent; sleep, work, or functioning are disturbed; the material is reviewed involuntarily and frequently.
- **Reconstruction:** the events are understood but not concluded; the material can be discussed without breakdown; the account is still being assembled.
- **Restoration:** the position is clear; the practical steps have been taken or are being taken; the material occupies a defined place rather than the whole.

Where you are in the acute phase, suspend the remainder of the exercise and implement the six measures in Phase One. The rest is done later.

### Part Four: The Written Account

Write the account of the injury in three sections and no more than 1,200 words.

- **What happened.** Factual, in sequence, without interpretation.
- **What I did and what I now think of it.** Including conduct you regard as unwise.
- **What is unresolved.** Facts not established, questions without answers, matters requiring a decision.

### Part Five: The Facts and the Narrative

Two columns.

| What I know to be true | What I have constructed |
| --- | --- |
| | |

Then answer: what proportion of my current distress rests on the left hand column, and what proportion on the right?

### Part Six: The Recovery Plan

Write specific items with dates in each of the following.

- **Material:** what can be recovered, and by what means? Where legal advice is required, the date by which it will be obtained.
- **Boundary:** with whom, of what kind, stated in one sentence.
- **Presence:** which participation have I withdrawn from, and when will I resume it?
- **Disclosure:** the small number of people who will be told the position, and what they will be told.
- **Verdict:** the conclusion about myself that the injury produced, and the accurate replacement for it.

### Part Seven: The Forgiveness Decision

Answer three questions.

1. Am I willing to stop conducting the internal prosecution — the daily review, the rehearsed arguments, the imagined confrontations? If not, what condition would have to be met first?
2. Does the decision to release the account require me to resume the relationship? (The correct answer is that it does not.)
3. What will I do with the time and attention currently occupied by this matter?

## 16.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I was accused of something I did not do, and people believe it. Should I defend myself publicly?"**

State the position once, factually, to the people who matter, and then continue to be present. A campaign of correction sustains the matter in circulation and is read as defensiveness; silence accompanied by withdrawal is read as confirmation, as Segun's case demonstrates. Where the allegation is formal — made to an institution, a professional body, or a legal authority — the answer belongs to that body, in writing, and should not be circulated more widely.

**"How long am I supposed to keep greeting somebody who betrayed me?"**

The boundary and the greeting are separate matters. Ordinary courtesy can be maintained indefinitely in most community and family settings, at low cost, while cooperation and disclosure are withheld. The practical test is whether the contact is costing you something: where it is, reduce it; where it is a sentence exchanged in the street, it is usually cheaper to sustain than to avoid.

**"My own brother did this. How do I continue in the family?"**

Three positions are available and each is legitimate: a revised relationship with boundaries and no dependence; ordinary contact without cooperation on the matter in which he failed; and estrangement from that individual while remaining within the family, conducted without explanation to others beyond a single factual statement. What is not workable is a concealed estrangement in which the man continues to appear, contributes, and carries a grievance that nobody knows about — the arrangement described in Section 15.5 as a ledger.

**"Is it weakness to admit that this still affects me?"**

No, and the reverse is more accurate. The men who continue to be affected and never acknowledge it are the men who organise decades of their conduct around the injury without recognising it. The acknowledgement is the condition of the recovery, and its audience does not have to be large.

**"I cannot forgive him. Does that mean I will never recover?"**

Forgiveness in the sense used in this chapter is the decision to stop carrying the account, and it is not a prerequisite of every recovery; some men recover fully while maintaining a permanent unwillingness to release the matter, particularly where the conduct was severe and unacknowledged. What is required for recovery is not forgiveness but the completion of the practical steps and the removal of the injury from the centre of the man's account of himself. Forgiveness, where it occurs, is generally later and easier than men expect once those steps have been taken.

**"Should I tell my children what happened?"**

At a level appropriate to their age and involvement, and without bitterness. Where the children have observed the effects — the withdrawal, the tension, the absence — an explanation reduces their anxiety and prevents them from supplying a worse one. What should not be transmitted is the general lesson that trust is a mistake; the accurate lesson is the one in Section 16.6, that trust is extended in stages, verified, and adjusted according to the evidence.

::: summary
**Chapter 16 in brief.** Rejection is a decision that the man is not to be included; betrayal is a violation of trust by a person inside it. Both occur in every domain of a man's life — family, peers, work, children, community — and their effects are less discussed and longer carried than the effects of the romantic forms examined in Chapter 13.

Rejection damages men through six mechanisms: social pain, which is neurologically real and not metaphorical; the simultaneous reduction of belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaning that the ostracism research consistently finds; self-blame and the search for a personal defect; the development of rejection sensitivity, expressed either as accommodation or as anticipatory withdrawal; the two opposing responses of withdrawal and aggression, each producing what the other is designed to prevent; and rumination, which feels like problem-solving and re-inflicts the injury each time it occurs.

Betrayal has four elements — the act, the concealment, the participation of others, and the moment of discovery — and produces four distinct injuries: material, relational, to the man's model of reality, and to his account of himself. Its two destructive responses are hardening, which permanently reduces the capacity for closeness, and exposure, which organises the man's life around the injury.

The pain of being misjudged has its own structure: an allegation that circulates without a hearing, framed so that it cannot be answered, producing shame and then withdrawal, where the withdrawal is read as confirmation. The method that works is presence sustained at whatever level of discomfort, together with a small number of factual corrections delivered once to the people who matter.

Recovery proceeds in three phases. Phase One protects: physical stability, a small number of confidants, no irreversible decisions, reduced exposure, arrangements for the moments of maximum intensity, and professional or legal help where indicated. Phase Two reconstructs: the written account, the separation of facts from constructed narrative, an honest assessment of the man's own judgement, the conversion of a general loss of trust into a graduated practice, and the containment of rumination within a daily appointment. Phase Three restores: recovery of what is recoverable, deliberate restoration of standing through presence, contribution, and time, the addressing of humiliation, the decision about forgiveness understood as an internal release rather than a reconciliation, and the decision about the relationship.

Trust is not a single quantity and not a decision; it is rebuilt in graduated steps within the specific domain where it was damaged, with verification made visible rather than covert, results recorded as data, and extension or limitation decided on the evidence. A man's confidence in his own judgement is restored by separating the assessment from the outcome, recording the signals that were genuinely available, and introducing one reviewing relationship for significant arrangements.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. From the forms of rejection in Section 16.1, which have you experienced — and which have you never named as rejections at all?
2. Which of the six mechanisms in Section 16.2 operates most strongly in you? If it is rejection sensitivity, is your pattern accommodation or anticipatory withdrawal?
3. Apply the four injuries from Section 16.3 to the most significant betrayal in your life. Which has been the most damaging, and which have you never addressed?
4. If you have ever been misjudged or falsely accused, did you withdraw or stay? What did your response produce in the months that followed?
5. Complete Part Five of the exercise. What proportion of your distress rests on established fact, and what proportion on what you have constructed?
6. Which phase — acute, reconstruction, or restoration — describes your position, and what evidence supports the assessment?
7. What boundary have you been unwilling to set, and what is it costing you to leave it unset?

## Further Reading

- **On ostracism and social exclusion:** the experimental literature on ostracism, including the research using the ball-tossing paradigm and its replications across cultures, and the consistent finding that exclusion reduces belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence within minutes.
- **On the neuroscience of social pain:** the research on the overlap between the neural systems involved in physical pain and in social rejection, together with the accompanying debates about the interpretation of those findings.
- **On rejection sensitivity:** the literature on rejection sensitivity as a personality disposition, including its development, its association with depression and relationship difficulty, and the evidence on its modification.
- **On betrayal and trust:** research on trust violation and repair, including the findings on the relative difficulty of repairing competence-based and integrity-based violations and the conditions under which trust can be reconstructed.
- **On rumination:** the substantial literature on rumination as a maintaining factor in depression and distress, together with the evidence on the specific interventions that reduce it, including structured writing, behavioural activation, and the scheduling of worry.
- **Practical resources:** legal advice obtained early where the injury has a legal dimension; mediation services, which are frequently more effective than litigation for family and community disputes where a continuing relationship is required; and counselling, which is indicated where the injury has produced a depressive or anxiety disorder rather than a maintained grievance.
