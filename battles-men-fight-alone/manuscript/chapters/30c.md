## 30.3 What Grief Does Behind the Silence

A grief with no audience does not remain still. It presents in forms that are recognisable, and the following nine are the ones most consistently reported by men and by the practitioners who see them.

### The Nine Presentations

**1. Irritability and the collapse of tolerance.** The most common presentation in men. The man who has always been steady becomes short with his household, intolerant of ordinary friction, and disproportionately severe about small matters. The household experiences a change in the man's temper and attributes it to work, age, or ill health, and the man himself generally attributes it to the same things.

**2. The expansion of work.** The man's hours extend, the man takes on additional work, the man occupies the evenings. The mechanism is legitimate in the man's account — *there is a lot to do* — and its function is the reduction of the unstructured time in which the material arrives. Men in this presentation are frequently described as coping well.

**3. The drinking, and the other numbing.** The alcohol, the gambling, the sexual conduct, the screens, the substances. A distinct and well-documented relationship exists between bereavement and substance use in men, and the mechanism is the one described in Chapters 22 and 23: the behaviour supplies the function of relief and removes the requirement to construct any other arrangement. The presentation is important because it is frequently the first thing the practitioner sees and the last thing the man connects to the loss.

**4. The risk-taking.** The driving, the investment, the business decision, the drink-driving, the confrontation, the affair. A specific association between bereavement and risk behaviour in men is described in the literature, and its mechanism includes both the numbing described above and a direct quality that men describe in their own accounts: *I did not care what happened to me for about a year.*

**5. The somatic presentation.** The chest, the head, the stomach, the back, the sleep. Men present to practitioners with physical complaints and the loss is not mentioned, and the finding from primary care is consistent: a substantial proportion of the presentations that follow a bereavement in men are physical. The material's own channels — sleep, appetite, energy, tension, and the immune response described in Chapter 27.3 — are the body's.

**6. The flatness.** The absence of interest, the absence of pleasure, the absence of appetite for the things the man ordinarily enjoys. Distinguished from depression by its association with the loss and by its usual course, and difficult for the man to interpret because its symptom is the absence of something rather than the presence of pain.

**7. The anniversary and the date.** The material that arrives annually, or at a particular month, or at the time of a particular event, frequently with an intensity that surprises the man and permits no explanation. Men describe the specific association precisely: a period of low mood that recurs at the same time each year, whose connection to a loss is not drawn until it is pointed out.

**8. The delayed arrival.** The material that arrives months or years later, generally triggered by an unrelated event: a smell, a piece of music, a road, an item of clothing, a stranger who resembles the deceased, a second loss. Men describe the arrival with particular force and with a specific disbelief: *it happened four years later, and I had thought I was past it.* The finding from the literature is that delay is common, that the man's competence at the time of the loss is frequently the reason for it, and that the arrival, when it comes, is not evidence of failure.

**9. The grief that is discharged into the household.** The material that is expressed as conduct toward the people available: the man's severity with a child, his sustained coldness toward his wife, his obsessive management of a matter that resembles the loss. Section 29.7's material on the transfer of the debt applies, and the effect is that people who had no part in the loss receive its cost.

### The Four Signs That the Material Is Unattended

These signs are not symptoms of illness but indicators that the material has no place, and each is useful to the man in identifying whether the work of Section 30.4 is required.

**1. The subject cannot be mentioned.** The man's name, the place, the matter, or the person cannot be raised in the man's presence without a change in the room. Where a man has to manage a subject, the material has no place.

**2. The material has not been described to anybody.** Where the account of the loss has never been stated in full to one person, the man's version has never been revised, and the version carried in private is generally more severe than the account that would survive delivery.

**3. The loss has no date and no place in the man's calendar.** No anniversary, no visit, no object, no ritual. Where a loss of significance has no mark in the man's year, the material is not placed but stored.

**4. The man's conduct toward a specific person or in a specific setting is different in a way he cannot explain.** The avoidance of a village, a family, a colleague's wife, a particular month, a particular conversation. Section 29.3's fifth component applies: the material has a management, and the management is the evidence of the material.

## 30.4 The Unattended Funeral

This section addresses the specific and common situation in which the man was not present at the loss and the ordinary mechanisms of mourning were therefore impossible.

### The Forms of Absence

**The funeral that was not attended.** Distance, cost, work, estrangement, a visa, a conflict with the family, an illness, a miscommunication, or a decision made in the disorder of the period. The specific and durable nature of the material is described consistently by men: not *he died* but *I did not go*. The pattern's mechanism is that the man's attendance would have supplied the four functions from the introduction, and its absence left the loss unmarked in his own conduct.

**The funeral that occurred before the man knew.** The death in another country or another state, the family that did not inform him, the burial conducted within the required period, the notification that arrived after the fact. The man's position is that a completed event has to be mourned from a distance and after the fact, and the ordinary sequence of the arrangements — the visits, the condolences, the presence — cannot be reconstructed.

**The death that was never confirmed.** The missing relative, the person lost in a disaster, the man whose body was never found. The grief has no event and no conclusion, and men who carry it describe a state that the literature correctly identifies as ambiguous loss: the material cannot be closed because the fact to which it would attach has never been established. Section 30.5's material on the household's arrangements applies at its strongest here: the family may spend decades in a position that no occasion can resolve.

**The death in custody, in detention, or in a place where the body was not released.** A category with a specific difficulty: the mourning is inseparable from a dispute about the circumstances, and the family's attention is substantially occupied by the question of the cause, which delays the mourning and sometimes prevents it.

**The restrictions that prevented attendance.** The periods in which gatherings, travel, and funerals were restricted, and the men whose parents or siblings died during them. Men in this category describe a form of material that they identify individually and that the surrounding arrangements did not recognise at the time.

### The Reconstruction of the Mechanism

Where the ordinary mechanisms did not operate, they must be constructed, and the following five elements constitute the form.

**1. The place.** A location that the man associates with the deceased: the grave, the family house, a place they went, a place the man chooses. The element's function is the physical anchor that the funeral would have supplied, and its specific value for the instrumental grieve is that it is a place at which to be rather than a conversation to be held.

**2. The visit.** A specified period at the place, conducted with a defined purpose: to say what the man would have said, to speak to the person, to perform a task the person would have valued, to be present. Men report that a defined visit, done properly once or repeated annually, is a real arrangement and that it works.

**3. The account.** The loss, the man's own position, and what the man would have said, in writing. Where the man is unwilling to write in the form of feeling, the account can take the form of a record: what the person was, what the man owes him, what the man intends to do in relation to the loss. The document's form matters less than that the account is made.

**4. The witness or the assembly.** Where the man can supply a person who knew the deceased, a conversation with that person is the closest available mechanism to the absent funeral: the shared account, the shared memory, the exchange of what each knew, and the acknowledgement of the loss by somebody for whom the loss is also real. Where such a person is unavailable, a men's group or a professional arrangement supplies the function partially.

**5. The obligation discharged.** The matter that the man would have performed had he been present: the support to the household, the maintenance of the grave, the assistance to the children, the completion of a matter the deceased had begun, the payment of a sum. This element is the one to which men respond most strongly, and its function is the discharge of the position that the absence created.

### The Position Where Nothing Can Be Reconstructed

Where none of the five elements is available — the man does not know where the grave is, the family will not receive him, the person's history cannot be established, the circumstances remain disputed — the work reduces to the internal elements and the chapter states them plainly: the account, the stated position on what the man owes, the maintenance of the person's memory in the man's own conduct, and the dated reckoning. Men carrying these cases report what the material of Chapter 29 reports: the loss is present and it is not expressed in the man's household, his work, or his body, and it has a place in the man's own arrangements.
