# Fighting Demons Nobody Can See {.chapter num="5" short="Fighting Demons Nobody Can See"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the range of internal struggles men experience, and explain why these struggles are systematically invisible to others.
2. Recognise anxiety as it actually presents in men — frequently as physical tension, irritability, and control-seeking rather than as named worry.
3. Describe the nature of persistent worry and intrusive thoughts, distinguish them from normal concern, and explain the mechanism by which they intensify.
4. Explain the operation of shame, guilt, and unresolved memory, and distinguish between them in practical terms.
5. Identify the signs that distress has reached a level requiring professional assessment, and describe what such an assessment involves.
6. Use structured methods to find language for invisible pain, and prepare an account of his own state that can be given to a professional, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.

## Introduction

A man in his early forties described his situation to a physician in a single sentence that took him four years to construct: *Nothing has happened to me, and I am not well.* He had a job, a wife, two children, and no identifiable crisis. He had also, for three years, been unable to be still. He woke with a sensation in his chest that he described as *a readiness for something*. He checked the locks twice. He lay awake running through scenarios in which his employer discovered a mistake he had made two years earlier. He had developed a habit of checking his wife's mood before speaking, and a habit of leaving rooms when more than four people were in them. He did not describe any of this as anxiety, because the word in his language and in his mind belonged to people who were visibly paralysed by fear, and he was not paralysed. He was functioning, and functioning extremely well, at a cost that he could not compute and had never reported to anybody.

His sentence — *Nothing has happened to me, and I am not well* — is a useful starting point for this chapter, because it identifies the peculiar condition of the man whose battle is real and whose evidence is absent. There is no wound, no diagnosis, no bereavement, no bankruptcy. There is an internal conflict that consumes enormous resources, produces measurable symptoms, degrades sleep and relationships, and is entirely invisible to everybody who knows him.

The chapter deals with four such internal battles: the general phenomenon of invisible psychological conflict; anxiety as men actually experience it; persistent worry and intrusive thoughts; and the pair of states that produce the most private suffering of all — shame and guilt, together with the unresolved memories that accompany them. It closes with the practical question of when to seek professional help, and how to describe what is happening in a way that a clinician can use.

## 5.1 Internal Struggles and Psychological Distress

The expression *internal struggle* is not a clinical term. It is a description of a common human experience: conflict conducted entirely within a person, with no external manifestation and, frequently, no external cause. Before examining the specific forms it takes in men, it is worth setting out why these struggles are so well hidden — and the answer is not simply that men refuse to talk.

### The Four Concealments

**Distress is concealed by competence.** Competence is a visible, verifiable, daily output. A man who continues to work, provide, and function generates continuous evidence that he is managing, and this evidence is not false. The difficulty is that competence of this kind can coexist with an internal state that is deteriorating rapidly, and outsiders — reasonably — take the verifiable evidence as the whole picture.

**Distress is concealed by the absence of a vocabulary.** As Chapter 3 established, many men can describe a situation accurately and a feeling only vaguely. An internal struggle that cannot be described produces no report, and an unreported struggle produces no help.

**Distress is concealed by the masculine presentation of symptoms.** Anxiety in men frequently appears as irritability, restlessness, controlling behaviour, physical tension, disturbed sleep, and increased alcohol use rather than as fear or worry. Even a man who does report *something* to a physician may report only the physical elements, and a clinician who asks the standard question — *Do you feel anxious or depressed?* — may receive a negative answer from a man whose anxiety is expressed entirely through his jaw, his temper, and his sleeping pattern.

**Distress is concealed by shame.** Of the four, this is the most powerful and the least discussed. A man who is struggling internally frequently experiences the struggle itself as evidence of inadequacy. To describe it is to confess it. The shame is not merely about the content of the difficulty; it is about the fact *of* having difficulty. Chapter 22 deals with this mechanism at length, and the section on shame below examines its operation in the specific case of hidden psychological distress.

### The Range of Internal Conflicts

The internal battles men describe fall into a small number of recognisable families. They are worth listing, because a man who reads the list will usually recognise two or three items and thereby begin the process of naming his own situation.

| Family of conflict | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| **Conflict between self and expectation** | "I am not the man this role requires." The sense of being perpetually short of a standard. |
| **Conflict between actions and values** | "I have done things I would not defend." Guilt, moral injury, and the sense of a double life. |
| **Conflict between desire and duty** | "I want a different life and I cannot have it." Ambition, frustration, and resentment that cannot be expressed. |
| **Conflict about worth** | "If they knew the truth about me, they would revise their opinion." The theme of Chapters 7 and 8. |
| **Conflict over the past** | "There is a matter I have never resolved." Unfinished grief, unforgiven conduct, unacknowledged harm. |
| **Conflict about the future** | "One day this will collapse." Chronic anticipation of a disaster that has not occurred. |
| **Conflict about identity** | "I do not know who I am without this role." The subject of Chapter 24. |
| **Conflict about being needed** | "They need me and I have nothing left." The exhaustion described in Chapters 10 and 33. |

Table: The eight families of internal conflict. Most men recognise between two and four in their own lives; the number is not a measure of health or illness.

What distinguishes a difficulty from a disorder is not the presence of conflict — conflict is normal and, in many cases, productive — but three features: **intensity**, **duration**, and **impairment**. A man is not suffering from a clinical condition because he has an internal conflict. He may be suffering from one because the conflict dominates his attention for months, produces clear physical and psychological symptoms, and interferes with sleep, work, or relationships. This distinction is important, and it is the reason that this chapter, unlike those that follow, is organised around the recognition and description of an internal state rather than its treatment.

### The Cost of Unrecognised Internal Conflict

The costs of an unrecognised internal struggle are similar to those of concealment, but with one important addition. Concealment of a known difficulty is expensive. Concealment of an *unrecognised* difficulty is more expensive still, because the man is not merely failing to report the problem; he is failing to have an accurate model of what is happening to him, and therefore attributing the symptoms to causes that cannot be addressed.

The attributions are recognisable and they are costly.

- The chest tightness is attributed to a heart problem, which produces an unnecessary cardiac investigation and no improvement.
- The irritability is attributed to the family, which produces conflict with the people who are most likely to help.
- The insomnia is attributed to age, which produces acceptance of a treatable symptom.
- The loss of interest in everything is attributed to the pressure of work, which produces more work.
- The alcohol consumption is attributed to stress, which produces more stress.

In each case, the actual situation is not addressed, and an unaddressed situation becomes more difficult over time. This is one of the clearest practical arguments for reading a book of this kind: what is being purchased is not comfort but an accurate description, and an accurate description is the precondition of competent action.

::: keypoint
An unrecognised internal struggle is not merely hidden from others. It is misdescribed to the man himself, and it is this misdescription — not the concealment — that produces the wasted years.
:::

## 5.2 Anxiety Hidden Beneath Confidence

Anxiety disorders are among the most common psychological conditions in the world, and among the most treatable. Men are affected in large numbers, and men are considerably less likely than women to be identified, diagnosed, and treated. One principal reason for this is that anxiety in men frequently does not look like anxiety.

### The Standard Picture, and the Male Picture

The standard description of anxiety — a sense of nervousness, dread, or fear, frequently with an escalation in specific situations, and often accompanied by an awareness that the fear is disproportionate — is accurate for many people. The male presentation overlaps with it and differs from it in emphasis. The differences are practical, not philosophical: they determine whether a man is identified and whether he accepts the identification.

| Element | Common presentation | Frequent presentation in men |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Named feeling | Worry, fear, dread, "nervous" | Nothing named; "pressure," "stress," "work frustration" |
| Visible behaviour | Avoidance, visible nervousness, seeking reassurance | Control-seeking, overwork, irritability, risk-taking, excessive activity |
| Somatic emphasis | Palpitations, sweating, trembling, breathlessness | Headaches, back and chest tension, stomach problems, fatigue |
| The subject of the thoughts | Anticipated failure, social judgement, health | Loss of income, failure of a business, letting family down, discovery of a fault |
| Response to the state | Seeking help relatively early | Increasing effort, drinking, exercise, or work |
| Presentation to a clinician | Direct report of anxious mood | Physical complaint, sleep problem, "I cannot cope with this workload" |

Table: Anxiety as it is commonly described and as it frequently presents in men. Adapted for this edition from the literature on sex differences in symptom presentation.

The table has an immediate implication for the reader. If you have not thought of yourself as an anxious man, the absence of the label tells you nothing. The relevant questions are behavioural and physical: Do I check things repeatedly? Do I need to be in control of arrangements that do not require me? Do I sleep badly in a specific pattern, waking early with a sense of dread? Is there a matter about which I think continuously and cannot resolve? Do I avoid particular conversations, places, or people with reasons that sound plausible? Have I increased drinking, smoking, or activity in the last year? If the answers point in the same direction, the label is a technicality.

### The Specific Anxiety of Men with Responsibilities

There is a form of anxiety that attaches specifically to responsibility, and it deserves separate description because it is common in the men this book is written for and is rarely described as anxiety.

Its features are as follows. The man is not afraid of a specific event; he is afraid of a general collapse. He experiences a continual sense of things being held together by his own effort, with the understanding that if he stops, the structure fails. The fear is not irrational in his circumstances — the structure often *is* held together by him — which is what makes the state so persistent and so resistant to reassurance. He is anxious because his position is genuinely precarious, and his anxiety is the correct emotional response to a real condition.

Two consequences follow. First, the man frequently rejects psychological treatment on the grounds that his anxiety is objective: *I am not imagining the problem; the problem is real.* In many cases he is right about the problem, and the treatment is still appropriate, because the objective load is producing symptoms that require management, and because a man whose resources are depleted by anxiety is less able to solve the objective problem. Second, the appropriate response is frequently structural rather than psychological: reducing genuine overload, redistributing responsibility, renegotiating a debt, obtaining professional financial advice, or bringing another person into a decision. This is the argument developed at length in Parts Three and Nine.

### Recognising It in Yourself

Five markers indicate that what a man is experiencing may be anxiety of clinical relevance rather than ordinary pressure. They are set out here as a screening aid, not a diagnosis; Appendix B provides a fuller structured tool, and no self-assessment can substitute for professional assessment.

1. **Persistence.** The state has been present for most days for more than six months, or it has intensified steadily.
2. **Physical signature.** There are regular physical symptoms — tension, headaches, gastric symptoms, palpitations, disturbed sleep — that clinical examination has not explained.
3. **Control behaviour.** There is an increasing need to control details, to be informed, to prevent surprises, or to check matters repeatedly.
4. **Interference.** Sleep, work performance, sexual function, appetite, or relationships are affected.
5. **Compensatory use.** Alcohol, sedatives, tobacco, food, gambling, or work itself is being used to regulate the state.
