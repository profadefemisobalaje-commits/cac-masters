## 13.3 Rejection and Damaged Self-Worth

Rejection in the romantic domain has a distinctive effect on self-regard, and the effect is frequently more damaging than the loss itself. The purpose of this section is to describe the mechanism, because understanding it is what allows a man to reduce it.

### The Idea of a Verdict

Rejection is experienced by men as a verdict rather than an event. Where a man is left, refused, ignored, or replaced, the experience is read as an assessment of him: of his worth, attractiveness, competence as a partner, and adequacy as a man. The reading is supported by the terms in which rejection is frequently delivered and discussed. A man is not told that a relationship has not worked; he is frequently told, by implication or explicitly, that he is insufficient in some specific respect — earning, presence, ambition, attractiveness, religious observance, family obligations, or emotional availability.

The results of this reading are specific and are visible in the accounts men give.

**The search for the defect.** A man in this position conducts an examination of himself, frequently for months, to identify the particular failing that produced the rejection. The examination is conducted with the standard described in Chapter 8: successes discounted, and any identified deficiency treated as conclusive evidence.

**A generalisation from one relationship to all.** The specific rejection becomes an assessment of the man's capacity for relationship, and it produces a belief that recurs in the accounts of many men after a serious rejection: *this happens to me.* Where the belief is established, later relationships are approached with an expectation of the same outcome, and the expectation produces behaviour — testing, withdrawal, defensiveness, or an anxious accommodation — which contributes to its own fulfilment.

**The loss of an assumed partnership.** Where a man has built his identity around being part of a couple, or around being a husband and father, the rejection removes the role as well as the relationship, which is the dynamic described in Chapters 11 and 24.

**Shame rather than grief.** This is the most important effect and the least recognised. Men frequently do not identify the state following a rejection as grief, because grief is what one feels for something that has been lost, and shame is what one feels for having been found insufficient. The two produce different behaviours: grief seeks comfort, while shame seeks concealment. A man who experiences the rejection as shame will therefore do the opposite of what would help him, withdrawing from precisely the people who might provide comfort.

### What Reduces the Damage

Five measures reduce the damage to self-worth, and they follow from the mechanism described above.

**Separate the decision from the verdict.** A partner's decision to end a relationship is a decision about a relationship and about a person's own life, made on the basis of a very large number of factors that include her interests, circumstances, history, family, and future plans. It is not an assessment, and it is not an assessment of the other person's worth. Writing out the factors that were genuinely relevant to the decision, as an observer would list them, is the practical form of this measure.

**Establish the actual reasons rather than the imagined ones.** Where possible, men benefit from obtaining the actual reasons rather than reconstructing them. This requires a single structured conversation, ideally without recrimination, in which the man listens and does not argue. Men who complete such a conversation report with remarkable consistency that the reasons were more specific, more ordinary, and less damning than the ones they had constructed for themselves.

**Reduce the exposure during the acute period.** Contact, monitoring of social media, and enquiries through mutual acquaintances prolong the acute phase. A deliberate period of contact reduction — not necessarily permanent, particularly where children require cooperation — is the single most effective measure in the first two months.

**Reconstruct the whole relationship rather than the ending.** Men in the acute phase idealise what was lost: the relationship is remembered as it was in its best months. A written account of the relationship as it actually was, including its difficulties, is distressing and useful. Men frequently discover, on completing it, that they are grieving less for the relationship than for the version of it that they have constructed since it ended.

**Address the underlying beliefs directly.** Where the reading is *I am not enough*, the corrective is not reassurance but specificity, in the manner described in Chapter 8: which capacity, in what respect, and according to whom? The general claim does not survive the examination, and the specific claims can be addressed.

## 13.4 Betrayal and Broken Trust

Betrayal is a distinct experience from rejection, and it requires separate treatment because its effects differ. Rejection involves a decision about the relationship; betrayal involves a violation of the terms on which the relationship was conducted. The distinction matters because the injuries are different: rejection damages self-regard, while betrayal damages the man's model of other people.

### The Forms of Betrayal

The forms that men report, in order of frequency, are infidelity; the concealment of a significant matter such as a debt, a previous relationship, a child, or an illness; the diversion of shared resources to another person or to a private purpose; an alliance with a third party against the man — including an alliance with his own relatives; a public humiliation delivered by a partner in company; and abandonment at a moment of the man's greatest need, such as an illness, a financial collapse, or a bereavement.

Not every betrayal ends the relationship. Some are survived, and the work of repair is covered in Chapters 15 and 16.

### The Effects

The effects that men describe are consistent and they include the following.

**A general collapse of trust.** The most consequential effect is not the loss of trust in the specific person; it is the acquisition of a belief that applies to people in general. Men who have been betrayed report a durable change in their assumption about others: an expectation of hidden agendas, a need to verify, and a reduced willingness to be dependent on anybody. This is a serious injury, and it affects the man's future relationships, his work, and his willingness to accept help, as Chapter 35 describes.

**Humiliation.** Betrayal that becomes known — infidelity, in particular — produces a public element that men describe as worse than the private injury: the knowledge that others knew, that they discussed it, that they pitied him, and, frequently, that some of them participated in concealing it.

**A reinterpretation of the past.** The betrayed man reviews the entire relationship in the light of the betrayal. Periods of happiness are reconstructed as periods of deception, and this retrospective rewrite is one of the features that distinguishes betrayal from other losses: it damages the memory of the relationship as well as the relationship.

**Rage, and the fear of it.** The anger produced by betrayal is frequently intense, and men report being frightened by it. This fear is well founded: the risk of harmful behaviour towards the partner, the third party, or himself is elevated in this period, and the appropriate response is to arrange safeguards rather than to rely on self-control alone.

**The temptation of revenge.** The most common thoughts men report: exposure, retaliation in kind, financial revenge, and legal action pursued for its punitive rather than its practical value.

### The Early Period: What to Do and What Not to Do

The first weeks after a betrayal are a period of high risk, and the following guidance is framed accordingly.

**What not to do.** Do not make decisions that cannot be undone: resign, relocate, dispose of assets, enter a new relationship, or confront the third party in circumstances where any of these could be forced. Do not consume alcohol in the amounts that the situation will suggest. Do not conduct the dispute in public, on social media, or through the children, in any circumstances. And do not assume that the intensity of the initial reaction is a reliable guide to the eventual decision, because it is not: most men who leave after a betrayal do so after weeks, and most of those who stay do so after consideration rather than in the first week.

**What to do.** First, physical and practical stability: sleep, food, activity, and an arrangement for the immediate household. Second, one confidant: a person who is not a member of the extended family, if possible, so that the man has somebody in whom he can speak freely. Third, professional help: for legal matters where relevant, and for the emotional consequences, which are frequently best addressed by a counsellor rather than by friends. Fourth, a restriction on the number of people who know, in the first weeks, because an announcement cannot be withdrawn and because the man's own judgement is temporarily impaired. Fifth, and importantly, an explicit plan for the period of maximum anger: how the man will leave any situation in which he feels dangerous to himself or others, whom he will telephone, and what he will not do.

### Trust After Betrayal

A question men ask, and one that deserves a direct answer, is whether trust can be restored. Three answers apply, depending on the situation.

**Where the relationship continues and both parties are committed to repair,** trust can be rebuilt, and the process is specific: full disclosure of what occurred, an acceptance of accountability by the person who betrayed, a period in which verification is permitted without objection, the involvement of a counsellor, and time measured in months rather than weeks. Chapter 15 provides the detail. Men should note that this process requires the man to remain in a state of discomfort for a long period, which is the price of continuing, and that it is not a failure to decide that the price is too high.

**Where the relationship ends,** trust can still be restored, but the object of the restoration is different: it is trust in the man's own judgement and in the general reliability of others. This is achieved through ordinary experience — the accumulation of small, verified interactions with people who prove reliable — and the process is slower than men expect. It is also frequently assisted by the presence of at least one relationship in which trust has been maintained throughout.

**Where the betrayal was by a family member or a trusted friend,** the same principles apply with an additional element: the continuity of the family or community makes avoidance impossible, and boundaries rather than estrangement are frequently the practical answer. Chapter 16 and Chapter 26 address this in detail.
