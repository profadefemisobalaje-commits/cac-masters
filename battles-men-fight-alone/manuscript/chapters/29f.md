## 29.8 Practical Exercise: The Regret Inventory and the Plan

This exercise takes about three hours, over three sittings, and it is conducted entirely in writing. The material it concerns is private; the documents should be kept where the man keeps his private records, and the exercise does not require that any of them be shown to anybody except where a step of the plan specifies it.

### Part One: The Inventory (Sitting One)

List the regrets you carry. The list should include the items you have never described to anybody, which is the purpose of the exercise. Five columns.

| The matter | Date | Who was affected | What I did or did not do | Does anybody know? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | |

Then, for the item you carry most heavily, apply the components of Section 29.3.

- **The event, described in no more than five sentences.** (Keep the description short; the length of the man's account is generally an indication that the event and the rumination have been confused.)
- **The counterfactual.** What I believe would have happened otherwise. Then, under it: **the evidence for that belief** — what do I actually know, and what am I constructing?
- **The verdict.** The conclusion I have drawn about myself.
- **The debt.** What I believe is owed, and to whom.
- **The management.** What I do to keep the matter out of view: the place, the person, the subject, the hour, the drinking, the busyness, the silence.

### Part Two: The Classification and the Mode (Sitting Two)

**Classification.** For each item on the inventory, assign the class from Section 29.1.

| Item | Class One (error) | Class Two (wrong) | Class Three (tragedy) | My reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | |

For each Class One item, record the facts as they stood at the time, without the benefit of the outcome, and state whether the decision was competent on the information available. For each Class Three item, divide the outcome into three parts: what I did, what was done by others, and what was circumstance. The three parts are written separately and in different columns.

**Mode.** For each item, apply the five markers of Section 29.3 and record which are present: the material is replayed without alteration; no conduct has been altered by it; the disclosure is refused; ordinary goods are treated as illegitimate; and the material escalates with mood, drink, or anniversary.

Then answer: **which of my items are operating in the punitive mode?** The marker that decides the matter is the second: if no conduct of yours has been altered by the material, it is functioning as a sentence and not as a correction.

### Part Three: The Plan (Sitting Three)

**Section A: Repairable matters (Class Two).** For each, complete the three parts.

| The person | The acknowledgement (what I did, without explanation) | The restitution (the sum, the act, the schedule) | What will be different (specific and checkable) | The date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | |

Apply the four rules of the acknowledgement before proceeding: no explanation, no defence, no request, no distribution of responsibility. Apply the test at the end of Section 29.5: if I could not tell anybody that I had done this, would I still do it?

**Section B: Irreparable matters.** For each, complete the five elements.

1. **The account.** Written once, completely, with the event, my conduct, the harm, and an accurate assessment of what I controlled. (Where useful, use the three columns from Part Two.)
2. **The witness.** The person I will tell, and the date. (Where the material is severe, record the professional arrangement.)
3. **The stated position on self-punishment.** In my own words: what has my suffering achieved; who has paid for it; what is the relationship between my suffering and the harm I caused.
4. **The redirection into conduct.** The harm I caused, and the conduct of the same kind that I will perform without requiring it to be observed.
5. **The dated reckoning.** The date on which I will review it, and what the review will consist of: the conduct of the past year, whether the commitments held, and whether any part of the matter has become available for repair.

**Section C: The transfers.** For each person in my household with respect to whom my attention is disproportionate or my requirements are unusually firm, state the obligation I am carrying and the person to whom it actually belongs. Then write the correction: what the household member actually requires of me, stated in the forms of Parts III and V of this book.

## 29.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Everyone carries something. Why should I dismantle a burden that keeps me careful?"**

Because the chapter proposes no dismantling of the material; it proposes the removal of the punitive mode, which does not keep the man careful. The corrective mode is what keeps a man careful, and it operates with the material present, examined, and available. The punitive mode supplies nothing: by the second marker in Section 29.3, it has altered no conduct, and by the third element in Section 29.6, its cost is charged to people who had no part in the original matter.

**"The person I wronged is dead. Is it dishonest to be relieved by writing a page nobody will read?"**

The page is a step and not the work. The work of an irreparable matter consists of the accurate account, the witness, the stated position on self-punishment, the redirection into conduct, and the dated reckoning; the page is the first element. The relief men report after these steps is not a claim that the matter is settled but the ending of the arrangement in which the man's private suffering stands in the place of reparation.

**"I will not tell anybody. What then?"**

Then the witness does not occur, and the material remains held by one person. The chapter's position is that this is a legitimate choice with a known cost, and the cost is the correction described in Section 29.2's first mechanism: the man's account remains the only account, unconverted, and unexamined by anybody. Where the refusal is based on the fear of judgement, the professional arrangement is the appropriate route, since the recipient's obligation of confidentiality removes the exposure and the recipient is trained for the material. Where the refusal remains, the remaining four elements are still available and are still worth conducting.

**"My regret concerns something done to me, not by me."**

Then the material belongs, initially, to Chapter 13 and to Chapter 16. It is worth noting, however, that a substantial proportion of the items men list under this heading contain a second element, which is a regret at the man's own conduct in the matter: that he did not resist, that he did not report, that he did not leave, that he did not tell. Where such an element is present, the material should be divided: the harm done to the man is addressed in its own chapter, and the regret concerning his own conduct is addressed here, with the Class Three treatment if the man's responsibility was real and partial.

**"How long does the work take?"**

The written work takes three sittings. The repair of a Class Two matter takes as long as the restitution takes, and in the case of a material sum this is frequently a period measured in years. For an irreparable matter, men report a change in the material's operation within two to six months of conducting the five elements, and they report the dated reckoning continuing indefinitely. The chapter's position should be recorded accurately: the material is not intended to close, and the work is not offered as a means of obtaining peace.

**"Is this not simply a way of letting myself off? "**

Only if the work is conducted without the restitution, without the acknowledgement, and without the redirection. The chapter's arrangement is deliberately constructed so that the lenient path is unavailable: the man is required to state what he did to the person he wronged, to make good the material part of it, to write down what his suffering has and has not achieved, and to perform conduct of the same kind as the harm for the remainder of his life. Every element of the arrangement imposes cost. What is refused is the additional and uneconomical cost of an indefinite private sentence that benefits nobody, and the reader should test the distinction by asking which of the two arrangements the person he wronged would actually require.

::: summary
**Chapter 29 in brief.** Regret must be distinguished from remorse. Regret concerns an outcome the man wishes had been different; remorse concerns conduct he judges to have been wrong and carries an obligation. Men's private regrets cluster in nine categories: the decision that failed somebody, the failure to act, the words said, the words not said, the person not attended to, the money, the failure to protect, the compromise, and the particular act never described. Each item is an error (a competent decision with an adverse outcome), a wrong (conduct that was wrong at the time, with an obligation attached), or a tragedy (real but partial responsibility).

Private regrets are heavier than disclosed ones for five structural reasons: there is no correction available, the material is protected from the disclosure that would revise it, the man has no permission to be affected, the standard he has violated is unknown to anybody else and therefore uncalibrated, and the material is triggered without warning and without explanation. Regret has five components — the event, the counterfactual, the verdict, the debt, and the management — and it operates in two modes. In the corrective mode it is examined once and supplies a standard the man acts upon. In the punitive mode it is replayed without alteration, alters no conduct, is held privately, renders the man's ordinary goods illegitimate, and escalates with mood, drink, or anniversary. The marker that decides which mode is operating is whether the material has altered any conduct. The functions of regret are correction, prevention, the calibration of the man's standard, and the grounds of his treatment of others; its abuses are rumination, self-punishment, compensation in the wrong direction, the denial of the man's present standing, and avoidance.

Repairable matters are conducted in three parts: the acknowledgement, stated without explanation, defence, request, or distribution of responsibility; the restitution, where a material element exists, with a full accounting and a performance schedule; and the statement of what will be different, specific and checkable. The injury is not required to forgive and the relationship is not required to continue. Irreparable matters are conducted by five elements: the account stated once in writing, the witness, the stated position on self-punishment, the redirection into conduct of the same kind as the harm, and the dated reckoning. The obligation of a regret may not be transferred to somebody who was not involved, and the practice of doing so — the over-managed child, the enforced obligation, the rigidity of procedure — charges the man's debt to people who had no part in the original matter. The work does not promise peace. It promises that the material will be carried accurately, will be converted into conduct, and will no longer be charged to the man's household.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the inventory. How many items on your list has anybody ever been told about, and what does that number tell you about the maintenance described in Section 29.3?
2. For your heaviest item, write the counterfactual and then, separately, the evidence for it. What do you actually know, and what have you been constructing?
3. Apply the five markers of the punitive mode. Which apply, and what has the material produced in conduct over the last ten years?
4. Which of the five abuses in Section 29.4 are present in your material: rumination, self-punishment, compensation in the wrong direction, the denial of your present standing, or avoidance of a specific place or person?
5. For each Class Two item, write the acknowledgement without explanation, defence, or request. What is the restitution, and what is the first date on the schedule?
6. For each irreparable item, write the elements. What is the specific conduct of the same kind as the harm, and how will you perform it without requiring it to be observed?
7. Where has an obligation been transferred to somebody in your household — a child who is monitored, a plan that is refused, a rigidity that has no explanation? Write the obligation's actual object and the correction the household member actually requires.

## Further Reading

- **On regret:** the psychological literature on regret, including the distinction between regret and remorse, the research on the most frequently reported regrets across the lifespan, and the findings on the difference between regret that motivates action and regret that produces rumination.
- **On counterfactual thinking:** the research on counterfactual reasoning, including the findings on the ease with which alternatives are constructed, the influence of the ease of construction on the certainty that the alternative was available, and the conditions under which counterfactual thought improves rather than impairs subsequent performance.
- **On rumination and self-criticism:** the literature on rumination and on self-criticism, including the evidence on rumination as a maintaining factor in depression and anxiety, and the findings on the difference between reflection and rumination.
- **On apology and repair:** the research on apology and on restorative practice, including the findings on the components of an effective apology, the role of restitution and of committed future conduct, and the evidence on when apology produces repair and when it fails.
- **On moral injury:** the literature on moral injury, including work on the experience of having transgressed one's own values, the distinction between moral injury and fear-based traumatic responses, and the treatment approaches that address the experience of transgression directly.
- **Practical resources:** counselling and psychological services for the conduct of the witness element and for material of this kind; mediation and restorative services where a repair involves other people; services for credit and debt where the regret is material; men's groups in which an account of this kind can be received; and the material in Appendices K, L, and R.
