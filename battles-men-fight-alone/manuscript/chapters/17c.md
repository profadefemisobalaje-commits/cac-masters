## 17.4 When a Father Cannot Show Emotion

This section addresses the pattern that has the longest consequences and the least deliberation: the father who cannot show feeling, who loves his children entirely, and who is experienced by them as distant, severe, or unknowable.

The mechanism has been described in Chapters 2 and 3: the suppression of emotion is learnt in childhood, maintained by the script, and experienced by the man as ordinary self-control. In the paternal context, the suppression acquires a justification, because a father who conceals his fear or his grief believes he is protecting his children from carrying it.

### The Effects on Children

The research and clinical literature on paternal emotional availability identifies six consistent effects. They are stated here as risks rather than as certainties, because the outcome depends on the whole family system and because many men who are emotionally unavailable as fathers are nevertheless deeply loved, respected, and eventually understood by their children.

**Uncertainty about the father's regard.** A child does not experience a father's love as a fact; the child infers it. Where the inference must be drawn from provision alone, children frequently conclude that they are the subject of an obligation rather than an affection. Children of emotionally unavailable fathers frequently describe, in adulthood, a persistent uncertainty about whether their father liked them, which is distinct from and more corrosive than certainty that he did not.

**The reading of silence as disapproval.** A child confronted with a father's silence supplies an explanation, and the explanation available to a child is about the child. The result is a pattern described by many adults: a lifelong attempt to secure a father's approval which is never registered as either given or withheld.

**The learning of the same repertoire.** Children learn the emotional practice of their household. Sons of silent fathers have a measurably higher likelihood of being silent fathers themselves, and daughters of silent fathers frequently select partners whose arrangement reproduces the one they grew up in. The transmission is the strongest argument in this chapter for a father's own work: it is the only effect that extends beyond the man's own life with certainty.

**Difficulty with authority and with conflict.** Where a father's displeasure was unreadable, children frequently develop either a heightened vigilance for others' displeasure or a neglect of it. Both patterns are visible in adult life: the first in excessive accommodation, the second in the insensitivity that produces avoidable conflict at work and at home.

**The difficulty of receiving affection.** A child who has not practised receiving a father's affection becomes an adult who is uncomfortable receiving affection, which produces difficulty in marriage. The pattern is described in Chapter 15 as one of the mechanisms producing a marriage of logistics rather than intimacy.

**The consolidation in the father's old age.** The most painful of the six. Where the relationship has been conducted in silence for forty years, the father's old age frequently produces a sudden, urgent desire for closeness that the children cannot meet, because the arrangement does not contain the means. Men describe the experience in the same terms: *my father wanted to talk to me when he was seventy and I did not know how, and neither did he.*

### What Changes

Five measures change the pattern, and their order matters because the first is the condition of the others.

**1. Say one true sentence about the man's own state, in front of the children.** Not a lecture about his feelings, and not an account of his difficulties. A single sentence, delivered in an ordinary moment, and repeated at intervals: *I was worried about money this month.* *I was tired today and I was short with you, and I am sorry.* The purpose of the measure is not to disclose but to demonstrate that words for such things exist in the household.

**2. Explain the man's own silences where they have been noticed.** Where a child has experienced the father as unavailable — during a specific period, or throughout — the explanation is worthwhile. *When you were small I was working nights for three years and I know I was not there and I am sorry for it.* The statement does not need to be received with forgiveness; the purpose is the removal of the child's self-directed explanation.

**3. Establish a recurring, specific activity with each child.** Not general time, which does not occur: an activity, weekly or fortnightly, with each child individually, in which the father and child are together without a task. The activity should be chosen to suit the child rather than the father, which is the point of it, and it should be held for a defined period before its value is judged.

**4. Receive what the child offers.** Children offer contact in the form of their own interests, their own accounts, and their own requests. A father who listens to a description of a child's interest for fifteen minutes, without correcting, improving, or redirecting, produces more effect than a substantial conversation about the child's future. This is the most under-valued measure in the whole of fatherhood, and it is the one most frequently omitted by men who are otherwise attentive parents.

**5. Say the direct thing.** A sentence, once, and periodically repeated, in the man's own words: *I love you and I am proud of you.* Men frequently resist this sentence on the grounds that their conduct has demonstrated it, which is true and is not a substitute. Children of fathers who never said it describe the absence regardless of the conduct, and the sentence is worth saying even at the age of sixty-five, when it frequently produces more effect on the father than on the child.

## 17.5 The Father Who Is Present but Unseen

A distinct pattern deserves separate treatment because it is frequently missed: the father who is present in the household, works locally, attends to the children's logistics, and remains entirely unseen.

### The Features

**He is a function rather than a person.** He drives, pays, repairs, approves, and attends. He is consulted about the practical matters and not about anything else, and he is aware that this is the case.

**His interior life is unknown to his household.** He has never described a fear, a disappointment, or an ambition to his wife in years, and he has never described one to his children at all. The material of Chapter 14 applies in full.

**His authority is procedural.** He is obeyed in the matters he manages and ignored in the matters he does not, and he has no standing in the household's emotional domain, which is managed by somebody else.

**His relationship with the children is conducted through the mother.** Requests, information, and serious matters reach him via his wife, and he learns what his children think from being told, rather than from being told by them.

**He is frightened of a conversation.** This is the defining feature. The man would like to be known and is unable to initiate the process; the longer the arrangement continues, the more expensive the initiation becomes, since a man of fifty who has never spoken to his children about himself cannot begin without the beginning being remarkable.

### The Consequences

The consequences distinguish this pattern from the absent father's, and they are worth stating because they are frequently underestimated.

**The children lose the man's history.** What he did, what it cost, what he believes, what he regrets, who he was before he was a father. This material is the inheritance that children actually use, and its absence means that the children begin their adult lives with no knowledge of the man who raised them.

**The man is left with no reciprocal relationship.** In old age, the pattern produces a father who is looked after and not known. The provision continues in reverse: the children provide the visits, the medical arrangements, the financial assistance, and the relationship.

**The opportunity of the children's adulthood is lost.** Adulthood is the stage at which a father and a child can meet as two adults, which requires that the father be a person rather than a function. Men who establish this relationship describe it as the most valuable of the whole of fatherhood, and it requires the presence of the father's own interior life, which has to have been made available at some point.

### Breaking the Pattern

The practical steps are those in Section 17.4, with two additions specific to this pattern.

**Begin with the children individually and with something the man has made.** A meal, a journey, a repair, a task. The activity supplies the occasion, and the occasion supplies the permission to speak. Men find that conversation is easiest while doing something with their hands and while not facing the other person, which is a practical observation and not a deficiency.

**Answer questions honestly when they are asked.** Children ask about their father's past: what he was like, whether he was afraid, what he wanted to do. These questions are the openings the pattern most needs, and the answers that are honest, specific, and slightly unflattering are the ones that produce the relationship. Men should note that a child who asks such a question is not seeking information; the child is seeking the father.
