## 21.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not simply dishonest to present a version of myself that is not the whole truth?"**

Selection is not deception. Every person is known in part, and the ordinary management of what is disclosed is what makes social life possible. The distinction this chapter draws is between selection and concealment: selection is the omission of material that does not need to be discussed, and concealment is the omission of material whose discussion is necessary for the man to be known by the persons who require it. The test is simple: would the person most concerned be harmed by discovering that the material had been known to me for years and withheld from them?

**"What if the disclosure damages my position?"**

Sometimes it does, and the risk should be assessed rather than assumed. The distinctions worth making: disclosure of the material that concerns a man's competence in his work carries real occupational risk; disclosure of the material that concerns his history, his household, and his states carries considerably less, and it is the material that does the most damage when concealed. The structured conversation in Chapter 35 provides a method for assessing the audience, the content, and the timing.

**"I have carried this for decades. What is the point of disclosing it now?"**

Two answers, one of which is decisive. The first is that concealed material continues to operate: it produces the avoidance, the anger, and the maintenance cost described in Section 21.1, and those effects do not reduce with time. The second is that the disclosure's effect on the man frequently exceeds its effect on anybody else, as Section 21.7's first item illustrates: the material is frequently lighter than it has been made to feel, and the man is the party who has been carrying the weight rather than the party who has been threatened by it.

**"How do I know whether an item belongs in the private account or is simply my personality?"**

By the test of concealment: an item that the man would alter his conduct to avoid disclosing is in the account; a matter that he simply does not discuss, and would discuss without difficulty if it arose, is not. The second test is the test of weight: material in the account is weight-bearing, and the man will find that it occupies his attention disproportionately to its actual significance.

**"My wife says I present a version of myself to her as well. Is that normal?"**

It is the most common form, and it produces the arrangements described in Chapter 14. The relevant question is not whether the presentation exists but whether anything in the marriage permits the man to be known outside it. Where the answer is no, the practices in Chapters 14 and 18 and the graduated disclosure above are the route, and the first disclosure is the hardest.

**"I am not a bad man. Why do I feel like a fraud?"**

Because the estimate of oneself described in Category 6 of Section 21.2 is rarely derived from conduct; it is derived from the comparison between the private account and the presented image, and it will therefore be unfavourable regardless of the man's actual conduct. The remedy is the counter-record in Section 21.5 and the inventory itself, whose most consistent effect is that the record is considerably better than the impression.

::: summary
**Chapter 21 in brief.** The image a man presents is a selection rather than a lie, assembled from four components: the record of accomplishments, the competence in the present, the absence of need, and the management of appearance. It is maintained by selective disclosure, by managing information about the household, by avoiding situations in which the gap would be visible, by acquiring the material to replenish it, and — most consequentially — by converting the image into a standard against which the man measures himself. Its costs are the exhaustion of maintenance, the exclusion of support, the escalation of the requirement, and the impossibility of intimacy within it.

The gap between the image and the man's private account contains failures not disclosed, conduct regretted, states he is in, facts about his household, doubts about his competence, and his own estimate of himself. It widens through time, the accumulation of unspoken items, the increase of responsibility, the absence of any corrective relationship, and the comparison with a standard that the image invented. It appears in specific forms in the successful man, the man of ordinary means, and the man who has carried out of duty something that has damaged him, and it produces the progressive reduction of what the man permits to be known.

Shame is distinct from guilt: guilt concerns an act and is addressed by repair, while shame concerns the self and produces concealment. Its features in men are the conviction of being different, the belief in exposure as catastrophe, the performance of the correct version, anger at the prospect of exposure, contempt for those who are seen, and the impossibility of receiving. It is reduced by one mechanism: graduated exposure to a safe person, with the outcome recorded, because the mechanism is evidence rather than catharsis.

Self-acceptance is not resignation, not the lowering of standards, and not a feeling. Six obstacles stand in its way: the over-developed standard, the comparison mechanism, the rejection of what the man actually is, the confusion of acceptance with giving up, the absence of any experience of being known, and shame. It requires an accurate inventory, a distinction between the man's conduct and what arrived, a distinction between open, amendable, and concluded matters, and one accurate account given to one person.

Facing the truth without self-destruction requires four protections: the examination is conducted in writing rather than in thought; the account is classified before it is assessed; the examination is bounded and time-limited; and it concludes with specific items rather than a verdict. The three practices that follow are the re-reading at intervals, the counter-record of genuine capacities and contributions, and the disclosure of one item within six months.

Becoming the man one intends requires a self-authored specification in five parts — what I will not do, the capacities I intend to hold, the relationships I intend to have, the work and provision with its ceiling and boundary, and the record I intend to leave — converted into a man by three practices: the daily sentence, the monthly review, and the annual account. The specification should be known by at least one person, and it will conflict with somebody's expectation, which is evidence that it is a specification rather than a description.

## Reflection Questions

1. From the four components of the presented image in Section 21.1, which do you maintain at the highest cost? What does the maintenance consist of, in hours and in money?
2. Complete Stage One, Sitting A. How many rows are concluded but carried as open? How many concern your conduct and how many concern what arrived?
3. What is your Category 6 estimate of yourself — the conclusion at the bottom of the assessment — and when was it formed? Is it derived from your conduct or from the distance between your account and your image?
4. Which of the six features of shame in Section 21.3 do you recognise: the conviction of being different, the belief in exposure as catastrophe, the performance, the anger at the prospect of exposure, the contempt for those who are seen, or the impossibility of receiving?
5. Select the item for the graduated disclosure and write it down. What is the predicted consequence, in your own words, and what evidence supports the prediction?
6. Write Part One of the specification — what you will not do. Read it and answer: how many items on the list have you done in the last twelve months?
7. Who will be told about the specification, and by what date? What will you do if their response is indifference?

## Further Reading

- **On shame and its distinction from guilt:** the psychological literature on shame and guilt as distinct affects, including the extensive research associating shame-proneness with concealment, withdrawal, and poorer mental health outcomes, and the evidence that guilt, by contrast, tends to motivate repair.
- **On self-concealment:** the literature on the concealment of personal information, including the consistent finding that concealment is associated with increased distress, reduced social support, and greater physiological stress responses.
- **On self-compassion:** the research on self-compassion and its correlates, including the findings on its association with lower anxiety and depression and with behaviour change rather than with complacency.
- **On identity and the self-concept:** the literature on identity development in adulthood, including the work on possible selves and on the role of self-authored standards in motivation and wellbeing.
- **On impression management:** the sociological and psychological research on self-presentation, including the findings on the costs of continuous impression management and the conditions under which it becomes damaging.
- **Practical resources:** counselling and psychotherapy, particularly approaches addressing shame and self-criticism; structured programmes on identity and purpose for men in mid-life; and the material in Appendices I and O, together with Chapters 8, 11, and 35 of this book.
