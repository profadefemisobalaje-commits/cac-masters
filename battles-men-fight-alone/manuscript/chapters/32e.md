## 32.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Could Not Ask the Doctor

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in primary care, in vocational counselling, and in men's group practice.

**Ahmed is sixty-three, a retired long-distance driver, married to Ramatu for thirty-six years, with four adult children and eleven grandchildren.**

Ahmed's working life was spent on a route between two cities and he was, by the account of his employer and his colleagues, the most reliable driver in the fleet. He had no formal education beyond the final year of secondary school, he had acquired his competence on the road and in the workshop, and his standing among the drivers rested on it entirely. He was the man the younger drivers brought their questions to: the road, the vehicle, the checkpoints, the passengers, the weather, the rules.

Ahmed's eldest son, Suleiman, had attended a university and worked as a bank officer. The relationship between the two is the substance of the case.

The matter began when Ahmed was fifty-eight, with a deterioration in his driving vision at night. He described the sequence as follows.

**The concealment.** Ahmed's night vision had begun to reduce, and he described the decision exactly: *I stopped taking the long route in the second half of the year and I told the office that I was preferring the shorter route. I could see the road. It was the signs at a distance that I was not reading as quickly. I did not tell my wife and I did not tell the doctor, because there is one thing a driver cannot be and it is a man who cannot see.*

**The near-incident.** In the following year, an incident on the road in which the vehicle's nearside front wheel passed over the shoulder of the road and a passenger complained. No damage occurred. Ahmed's account: *I blamed the road and I blamed a boy on a motorcycle and I knew it was my eyes.*

**The composition of a false account to a professional.** In the second year, Ahmed attended a clinic for a general examination arranged by his employer, and he described answering the vision questions inaccurately: *The nurse covered one eye and pointed to the letters. I know the letters because I have been reading them for years. I read the top line and I told her the second line and I could not see the fourth line plainly, and I said I could. I do not know why. She was a young girl and she was writing what I was saying.*

**The discovery by his son.** In the third year, Suleiman drove with his father on a family journey and observed the manner in which Ahmed drove at dusk: the following distance, the use of the lights, and the speed. His account, given in the family consultation, was: *I sat beside him for three hours and I knew. He was not seeing the road markings properly and he was compensating, and he was driving as though nothing was wrong, and I have never been more frightened in my life.* Suleiman raised it with his father the following week and Ahmed's response was, in his own words: *I told him it was nothing and that I had been driving before he was born, and I told him that the day I cannot drive is the day I die, and I stopped speaking to him for about two months.*

**The escalation.** In the fourth year, Ahmed was involved in a collision at a junction at dusk, with damage to a pedestrian's bicycle and no injury to the person, and the matter was reported to the police and to the employer. The employer's medical assessment found a substantial loss of visual acuity, and Ahmed was removed from driving duty permanently.

The work that followed occupied about fourteen months and consisted of five elements.

**The professional arrangement.** Ahmed was referred by the employer's occupational health service for an assessment that included both the vision and his psychological position. He described the first two appointments as resistance and the third as the point of change: *The doctor asked me what I would have done if one of my own drivers had driven with eyes like mine. I said I would have taken the vehicle from him the same day. He said nothing after that. He did not have to say anything. I sat there and I understood that I had been a man who could not be told a thing.*

**The correction of the record.** Ahmed requested, and the practitioner arranged, a written account to the household: the period of the concealment, the false answer to the nurse, the incident, and his own position. The disclosure was made to his wife and to Suleiman, jointly. His account of the conversation: *I told them everything, and the worst part was not the eyes. The worst part was Suleiman. He said that for four years he had been thinking that his father did not trust him, and that I had stopped speaking to him when he raised the thing that was destroying me. He said that he had been carrying the road every time I left the house.*

**The reclassification of his standing.** The element that changed the case, and it was conducted with the practitioner and subsequently with the drivers' association. Ahmed's account: *I have been the man who knows the road for thirty years. The doctor asked me what I would be if I could not see, and I could not answer him. Then he asked me whether the young men had ever asked me about the road or about my eyes. I said the road. He said, then you have another thirty years.*

**The arrangement.** Ahmed became, over the following year, the association's instructor: the man who taught the young drivers the road, the routines, the vehicle, the loading, the timing, the checkpoints, and the discipline of the route. His eyes were adequate for the instruction and were not adequate for the driving, and the association's arrangement was constructed explicitly on that basis. He also acquired, in the second year, a pair of glasses for distance work, which he described as having postponed for four years *because of a girl with a chart.*

**The statement.** The final element occurred in the men's group that Ahmed joined in the eighth month of the work. He described the position in these terms: *I have been asked questions my whole life and I have never once said I do not know. Not to my wife, not to my children, not to the doctor, not about my own eyes. And the thing I discovered is that I did not fail at driving. I failed at the one question that came to me and told the truth. I have told this group the sentences I could not say: I could not see the road signs, I could not follow the accounts, and I could not tell my son what was happening. I have said them all now.*

Ahmed's summary, three years after the collision, was: *The doctor asked me what I would do to one of my own drivers and I could not answer him. I lost the route. I did not lose the thing the route was for. If I had said one sentence — I cannot see the signs at night — I would have given up the road two years earlier and I would still have been the man my son asks about the vehicle. I paid four years and a collision for one sentence I could not say.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The requirement was personal rather than institutional, and the man's standing rested entirely on it.** Ahmed had no credential, no qualification, and no document; his authority existed only because the fleet regarded him as the man who knew. This is the first form of Section 32.3 and the case is included because it is the form in which the requirement operates most completely and in which the correction is the most difficult.

**The bluff was delivered to a professional who recorded it.** The false answer to the vision test is the first cost of Section 32.2 in its purest form, and the case is included because the specific mechanism should be understood: the man's answer ended the enquiry, the professional recorded the answer, and the arrangement then proceeded on the basis of a false fact. The nurse's assessment was accurate, the record was accurate, and the man's capacity was concealed by his own response.

**The household's protective behaviour was already in operation.** Suleiman's four-year silence, and his own account of carrying the road every time his father left the house. This is the fourth cost of Section 32.2, and the case records it because the household's behaviour was produced by an accurate reading of the man: the son had raised the matter once and had received the response he had received.

**The correction was prompted by a question about a third party.** *What would you have done if one of your own drivers had driven with eyes like mine?* The practitioner's question is the practical form of the third question in Section 32.6 and it is included because the technique is available to the reader directly: the matter is put in a frame in which the man's own standard is applied to somebody else, which bypasses the requirement completely.

**The standing was transferred to the domain of conduct rather than knowledge.** The instructor's position: the association no longer needed the man's eyes and it still required his history, his competence, his teaching, and his standard. This is the first measure of Section 32.3, and the man's own account records the mechanism precisely: *He asked me whether the young men had ever asked me about the road or about my eyes. I said the road.*
