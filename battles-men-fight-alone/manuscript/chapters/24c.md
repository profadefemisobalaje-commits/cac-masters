## 24.3 The Empty Season: When a Man Does Not Know Who He Is

This section describes the condition itself, and it is written so that a man in it can recognise what is occurring, because the condition's principal feature is that it presents without a name.

### The Features

**1. The absence of purpose in the day.** The man wakes without an obligation and experiences the absence not as freedom but as a weight. The hours are not merely empty; they are undifferentiated, and men describe a specific effect: the day passes without any memory of it.

**2. A muted or absent pleasure.** The capacity for enjoyment is not merely reduced; it is frequently absent, and the man is unable to identify anything he would like to do. This feature is shared with depression, and its presence requires the assessment described in Section 24.4.

**3. A specific form of loneliness.** Not the absence of company — the man is frequently surrounded by his household — but the absence of a position. The condition is frequently described in exactly these terms: *I am in the house and nobody needs me to do anything, and I do not know what to do with myself.*

**4. Irritability about small matters.** The response is calibrated to the material described in Chapter 15: an account of what the man has done, held privately, and a household that appears not to recognise it.

**5. An increase in the behaviours that manage states.** Alcohol, screen use, gambling, spending, sleep. Chapter 23's material applies, and the empty season is one of the most common periods in which a behavioural pattern is established.

**6. Withdrawal from the relationships that remain.** The most consequential feature. The man declines invitations, reduces contacts, and avoids the gatherings at which the question of what he is doing will be asked.

**7. A persistent sense of having been left behind or overtaken.** Comparative, and supplied by the environment: the colleagues who have progressed, the contemporaries who have been appointed, the former subordinates who are now senior. Chapter 11's material applies in the specific form produced by a loss of position.

**8. A diffuse anger with no object.** Described under illness above, and present in most forms of the condition: the man is angry and cannot identify the party at fault, because the loss was produced by time, by the economy, by a decision he made, or by his own body.

### The Distinctions That Matter

Four distinctions determine the treatment, and each requires a specific assessment.

**From depression.** Depression is a condition with a defined set of criteria, it is treatable, and it requires assessment. The distinction's practical importance is that depression must be treated before the reconstruction work will hold; Chapter 6 and Appendix B provide the criteria, and where they are met, assessment precedes the material in Sections 24.5 and 24.6. The two conditions co-occur frequently, and the presence of the second does not exclude the first.

**From grief.** Where the loss is a bereavement or a separation, the material of Chapter 13 applies, and the reconstruction work proceeds after the acute period rather than during it. Where the loss is a role rather than a person, the grief is frequently unrecognised, and one of the practical interventions is to name it as a loss and permit the grief: the retirement, the lost position, the departed household.

**From ordinary transition.** Every change of role requires a period of adjustment, and the period's normal length is a matter of months. The indicator that the condition is more than an adjustment is the absence of any reconstruction after twelve months, together with the presence of withdrawal, increased alcohol use, and the loss of the capacity for enjoyment.

**From a physical condition.** The symptoms of the empty season overlap substantially with those of hypothyroidism, anaemia, sleep apnoea, testosterone deficiency, and the effects of medication. A man over forty-five presenting with loss of energy, loss of interest, and disturbed sleep should obtain a medical assessment, and the assessment should be conducted before the material in this chapter is worked on. This is not a formality; a substantial proportion of men presenting with the condition described here have a treatable physical component.

### The Two Errors Men Make in This Period

**The first error is the attempt to recover the lost role.** The man applies for the same kind of position, returns to the same working pattern, or attempts to restore the role in a similar form. The attempt is not unreasonable and it frequently succeeds in part. Where it fails repeatedly, the man is left in a worse position, because each attempt has been conducted against the reconstruction, and because the failure supplies further evidence for the assessment he is making of himself.

**The second error is the attempt to fill the time.** Travel, projects, the assumption of obligations, the management of relatives' affairs, the acquisition of qualifications with no purpose. The activity is real and it does not address the condition, because the condition is not the absence of activity; it is the absence of an account of what the man is. Men describe the outcome precisely: *I was busy for two years and I was exactly where I started.*

The productive direction is neither the recovery of the role nor the filling of the time, and is set out in the sections that follow.

## 24.4 The Search for Meaning Outside Achievement

The material in this section addresses what the reconstruction requires, and it is deliberately practical, because the subject of meaning attracts writing that is either abstract or sentimental and neither is useful to a man in the empty season.

### What Is Actually Required

The reconstruction requires that the man possess three things that do not depend on his roles continuing. Each is a specific item rather than a disposition.

**1. One activity that is his own.** An activity performed for its own sake, of no value to anybody else, in which the man's competence or interest is a matter of his own assessment. Its function is to supply a structure that the man has chosen, and to provide the daily evidence that he is a person with preferences rather than a function. The nature of the activity is not important. What matters is that it is not attached to a role.

**2. One relationship in which he is known and not needed.** The material of Chapters 4, 10, and 18. Its function is to supply the answer to the third question — who knows me — in a form that does not depend on the roles.

**3. One contribution that is not an obligation.** A contribution the man elects: teaching, mentoring, a practical skill given to a group, a role in an organisation that is chosen rather than inherited or assumed. Its function is to supply purpose in a form the man controls, and the specific property that distinguishes it from an obligation is that the man may conclude it.

### Why Achievement Cannot Supply It

The essential distinction of this chapter is between achievement and meaning, and it is worth stating precisely because it explains why successful men are as susceptible to the condition as unsuccessful ones.

**Achievement is comparative; meaning is not.** A man's achievement is assessed against a standard or a position, and it therefore requires a reference group and a continuing supply. Meaning requires no comparison, which is why a man who teaches a single person to read has obtained something that a man who has exceeded his targets has not.

**Achievement terminates; meaning does not.** Each achievement concludes, and its conclusion returns the man to the position he occupied before it. The arrangement results in the permanent requirement to achieve again, described in Chapters 7 and 11. Meaning is sustained by the activity itself rather than by its conclusion.

**Achievement is external; meaning is internal.** The reward for achievement is supplied by other people, and its supply is therefore not within the man's control. The reward for meaning — that the activity is worth doing, that the contribution is useful, that the relationship is real — is available regardless of anybody's assessment.

**Achievement requires capacity; meaning does not.** The final distinction, and the one that matters most after fifty-five: a man's capacity to achieve reduces with age, illness, and the economy's conditions, and a life composed of achievement is therefore a diminishing resource. Meaning is available to a man in a wheelchair and to a man whose business has failed.

### The Available Materials

For the man who has built nothing outside his roles, the materials available are specific and they are more numerous than he expects. The following list is drawn from the accounts of men who completed the reconstruction, and each entry is a category rather than a suggestion.

- **Skill transmission.** What the man knows that others need: a trade, a professional competence, a practical skill, an administrative ability, language, driving, accounting, negotiation.
- **Organisations that require competent people.** Community associations, cooperative societies, professional bodies, schools, sports clubs, health services, disaster responses. Men with administrative competence are in demand in all of them, and the roles are frequently available on the man's own terms.
- **Interests that were abandoned.** The instrument, the sport, the reading, the making of things, the collecting. The material of Section 19.5 applies.
- **The relationships that were neglected.** Former colleagues, school friends, siblings, relatives with whom contact was reduced for lack of time.
- **A practice.** Something performed regularly, of no consequence, which establishes the shape of the day: walking, exercise, the study of a subject, the maintenance of something.
- **The household's arrangements.** The transfer of functions described in Chapter 18 and the assumption of specific household work. This is the material most frequently overlooked and the most immediately available, and it is also the material most likely to produce a conflict, because the household has arranged itself without the man's participation and the man's assumption of functions disrupts it.
