## 26.7 Illustrative Case: The Two Men and the Contract

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in business practice, mediation, and counselling.

**Segun and Bayo are cousins and business partners, aged forty-nine and forty-seven, who operated a building-materials supply business for eleven years in a state capital.**

The business began in Segun's house in 2009, with Segun contributing the initial capital from a gratuity and Bayo contributing the relationships, being the son-in-law of a man who had traded in materials for thirty years. Their arrangement was verbal: Segun held the capital, managed the accounts, and controlled the bank mandate; Bayo managed the customers, the suppliers, and the relationships; the profits were shared equally; and neither drew a salary.

The business grew from a single shop to three shops and a yard, and by 2016 it was supplying three construction companies under annual arrangements. Its turnover at that point was approximately fourteen times the initial capital.

The events that produced the case occupied about two years and consisted of a sequence of four matters.

**The first matter** was the introduction of Bayo's younger brother, Tunde, as a staff member in 2014: a salaried position managing the yard, with no ownership. Segun agreed to the appointment without a discussion of its implications.

**The second matter** was a contract with a construction company introduced by Bayo in 2016, requiring the business to extend credit of a sum equal to about half the business's capital for a period of four months. Segun agreed, on Bayo's assurance of the customer's reliability, and the necessary funds were obtained by Segun's personal guarantee at a bank.

**The third matter** was the customer's default, at the end of 2016, on approximately two-thirds of the credit extended. Segun's personal guarantee was called upon in the first quarter of 2017, and he met it from the sale of a plot of land.

**The fourth matter** was the discovery, in the second quarter of 2017, that Bayo had, over the previous two years, established a parallel supply arrangement in his own name with two of the business's customers, using the business's supplier relationships, at a volume of approximately a fifth of the business's turnover. The arrangement had accommodated the credit extension of 2016 by directing the business's cash to the parallel supply at the point when the customer's payments were delayed.

Segun's account of the discovery has three parts that are typical of the cases in this section.

First, he did not confront Bayo directly. He reduced his involvement over the following four months, attended the business less frequently, and ceased to raise the matters that required discussion. In his description: *I did not want the family to know, and I did not have it written anywhere, so I would have been the one accusing my cousin of something I could not prove.*

Second, he conducted the matter privately, drawing up his own records of the business, the bank payments, and the customer's default, and, at the same time, constructing a larger private account: the introduction of Tunde, which he now regarded as the beginning of the diversion; the nineteen years of relationship; the land that had been sold; and his own conclusion that he had been *the one who supplied the money and the premises while Bayo did the business*.

Third, the family became involved, and not by Segun's decision. In the third quarter of 2017, Bayo's parallel arrangement became known within the family through a supplier, and Segun's position was characterised in the family's discussion as one of two partners in a failed arrangement rather than as the party who had lost his land to Bayo's contract.

The intervention, which occurred through a mediation service after Segun's wife insisted on seeking professional advice, consisted of four elements.

The mediator required, as a first step, the production of the business's records: the bank statements, the contracts, the customer accounts, and the correspondence. The records established the four matters set out above with precision, and Segun's own account of the effect is instructive: *The day we put the papers on the table, I stopped being a man with a grievance. There were eleven pages and the papers said what had happened.*

The second element was the disclosure to the family, conducted jointly by Segun and Bayo before the family's council, with the records presented. The established facts were: the parallel arrangement, the diversion of cash, the personal guarantee that Segun had met from the sale of his plot, and the written valuation of Bayo's parallel turnover. The council's finding, which the mediator had anticipated, was not a moral one: the parallel arrangement had used the business's supplier relationships, and the value was quantifiable.

The third element was the settlement, which consisted of: the transfer of Bayo's interest in the two new shops to Segun; the cessation of the parallel arrangement and the transfer of the two customers to the business; the repayment by Bayo of a sum equivalent to two-thirds of the land's value, in instalments over twenty-four months; the confirmation of the salaries and roles of Tunde; and the writing of a partnership agreement containing the terms that had never existed, including the treatment of related-party transactions, the requirement of joint authorisation above a defined value, the accounts procedure, and the exit provisions.

The fourth element concerned the family rather than the business, and it was the element that Segun identified as the most difficult. The family's council required, as part of the settlement, a stated position: that the dispute was concluded, that neither party would circulate it further, that the children of both households would not be involved, and that both men would attend the family's occasions. Segun described his acceptance of that element as the most difficult decision of his life and described its effect, three years later, as the reason his children still knew their cousins.

Segun's summary, three years after the settlement, was: *I lost a friend and I did not lose a cousin, and I am not sure which of those sentences I mean. The lesson I took was not that men cannot be trusted. It was that I had run a business for eleven years on an agreement that existed only in my own head, and that I had called that friendship. The papers would have protected both of us.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The agreement existed only in the men's understanding, and each man's understanding differed.** Eleven years of business on a verbal arrangement with no terms concerning related-party transactions, joint authorisation, accounts, or exit. This is the failure of arrangements described in Section 26.5's Rule Two, and the case is included because the form is extremely common: the materially successful partnership conducted without terms, whose dissolution is attributed to the other party's character rather than to the absence of an agreement.

**The man accommodated rather than raising the matter, and the accommodation accumulated.** The appointment of Tunde, the credit extension accepted on assurance, the delayed payments, and the reduced contributions were each accepted in the name of the relationship. The material of Chapters 15 and 20 applies: the undisclosed account was the substance of what he eventually lost, and the man's own conduct contributed to the arrangement that made the loss possible.

**The records converted the matter from an accusation into a case.** This is the finding of Sections 16.7 and 26.3 reproduced: eleven pages of statement and contract, after which the man's position was established and no longer dependent on his account of it. The men who settle family and partnership disputes most effectively are overwhelmingly those who obtain the documents.

**The family's council produced a conclusion that a court would not have produced.** Bayo was found to have used the business's relationships and was required to compensate; Segun received the shops and the customers; the partnership was provided with written terms; and the family was required to conclude the matter and to protect the next generation. The case is included as an illustration of the power of a competent family or community body in matters where the parties must continue to live in relationship, which courts handle poorly and which this kind of body handles well.

**The outcome was a settlement that preserved both the material position and the family structure, and the man's grief was not thereby resolved.** Segun's final statement is deliberately included: the arrangement was concluded, the family held, and the friendship was lost. Both outcomes were present at once, and the case is included because the expectation that a settlement produces a resolution of the emotional material is inaccurate and produces a further disappointment.
