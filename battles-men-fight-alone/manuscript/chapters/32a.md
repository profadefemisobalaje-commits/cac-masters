# The Man Who Must Always Have Answers {.chapter num="32" short="The Man Who Must Always Have Answers"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the requirement to have answers, identify its sources, and explain why it is imposed on men more heavily than on others.
2. Explain the four costs of the requirement, including its effects on the man's learning, his judgement, and the people who depend on him.
3. Identify the knowledge trap: the position in which a man's standing rests on his being the one who knows, and the specific difficulty of asking for help from inside it.
4. Distinguish the four kinds of question a man is asked, and assess which of them he actually has the authority and information to answer.
5. Apply the protocol for saying *I do not know* in a form that preserves the position and improves the outcome.
6. Use questioning as a competence rather than as a defect, and apply the exercise at the end of the chapter to identify where the requirement is currently costing him and his organisation.
:::

## Introduction

There is a sentence that men in positions of responsibility learn early and generally without noticing: *You are the one who is supposed to know.*

It arrives with the position, with fatherhood, with the role of the eldest, with the trade, with the household's finances, with the title, with the uniform, and with the age at which a man becomes the person others consult. From that point onward, a specific requirement attaches to him: whatever the matter is — the money, the decision, the illness, the marriage, the child, the document, the machine, the discipline, the direction — he is expected to have an answer, and his own uncertainty is not among the responses the role permits.

This chapter concerns that requirement: its cost to the man, its cost to the people who depend on him, and its correction.

The requirement is not imaginary and it is not imposed by hostile people. It is a functional requirement of a position: a group that cannot obtain decisions does not function, and men are frequently the people in the group who have been assigned the deciding. The chapter's position is not that men should cease to be competent, cease to decide, or abandon the authority their positions require. It is that the requirement, in its absolute form, degrades four things: the man's learning, his judgement, the accuracy of the information available to him, and the confidence of the people around him — and that all four improve when the requirement is corrected into a specific and workable practice.

The chapter's approach has four commitments.

The first is that the difficulty is produced by the requirement rather than by the man's vanity. Men who cannot say *I do not know* are frequently not arrogant; they are operating the arrangement they were given, and they have accurate information about its terms. A man who says *I do not know* in a setting that requires answers from him frequently observes a change in how he is regarded, and the observation is generally correct. The problem requires a method rather than a resolution to be more humble.

The second is that the requirement's cost is borne principally by other people. The man who bluffs an answer to a technical question puts his organisation at risk; the man who will not ask the doctor a question affects his household's decisions; the man who cannot say *I was wrong about this* keeps a failing arrangement running; the man who must answer every question from his children supplies them with information he does not have. The chapter's material on judgement in Chapter 27.3 applies with an additional feature: the isolated man's decisions affect people who had no part in his isolation.

The third is that the correction is technical and can be stated in a form the man can use. Sections 32.5 and 32.6 supply the protocol and the practice: the three-part reply to a question the man cannot answer, and the use of questions as a competence rather than as a confession of ignorance. The protocol is deliberate and it is used by professionals in every field where the man's credibility and the outcome both matter.

The fourth is that the choice is not between knowing everything and knowing nothing. The absolute requirement and its opposite — the man who refers every matter upward, who will not commit, who says *I do not know* as a means of avoiding responsibility — are both failures, and the material is written to establish the position between them: a man who knows what he knows, says so plainly, says plainly what he does not know, and then does the work of finding out.
