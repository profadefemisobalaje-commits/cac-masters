## 7.5 Recovering from Disappointment

Disappointment is the ordinary, recurring experience of adult life: the promotion not received, the tender lost, the examination failed, the child who chose a different path, the marriage that did not produce what was promised, the plan that failed. Most disappointments are absorbed without much difficulty. The ones that require a method are those that are **serious** — involving substantial loss — and **public** — involving an account that must be given to others.

This section sets out a method for those cases. It has five stages, and they are sequenced deliberately.

### Stage One: Stop the Bleeding

Before any assessment or repair, the man must stop the activity that is increasing the loss. This usually means one or more of the following.

- **Stop additional commitments.** Do not take on a new loan, a new contract with heavy penalties, or a new obligation in order to conceal the current situation.
- **Stop concealment-driven expenditure.** The vehicle, the ceremony, the contribution, and the standard of living being maintained solely to prevent others from concluding that something has happened: these must be examined, because they are the mechanism by which a difficulty becomes a catastrophe.
- **Protect the basic functions.** Sleep, food, exercise, and medical treatment. A man recovering from a serious failure with four hours of sleep and no exercise will make worse decisions than he is capable of.

This stage is frequently the hardest, because it involves the admission that the situation is real. It is also the stage at which professional assistance is most valuable — an accountant, a lawyer, a physician, a counsellor, or all four.

### Stage Two: Establish the Actual Position

The second stage involves removing interpretation from the situation and establishing the facts. This is done on paper, and it has four components.

**Assets and liabilities.** What is owned, what is owed, what the interest costs, and what is secured against what.

**Income and obligations.** Actual income, and the sum of obligations to household, dependants, and creditors.

**Time.** How long the position can be sustained in its present form, given the income and the obligations. A precise figure, in months, is the single most useful number in the whole exercise. Men report that producing it is unpleasant and clarifying in equal measure.

**Options.** Written out, however unattractive: renegotiate, restructure, sell an asset, reduce household expenditure, take on a partner, take employment, declare a position, borrow from a named source at a named rate, or do nothing. The list should include the options that the man has been refusing to consider.

### Stage Three: Report to One Person

The third stage is disclosure to a single person: a spouse, a trusted relative, a mentor, a professional adviser, or a counsellor. The purpose of the first disclosure is not to obtain permission, forgiveness, or rescue. It is to end the condition of being the only person who knows.

Three practical notes improve the outcome.

**Prepare.** Write the account of the position — the facts from Stage Two — before the conversation. Men who attempt to improvise generally omit essential information and produce a worse conversation than they intended.

**Announce the format.** *I need to tell you something difficult. It concerns money. I have written down the position. I am not asking you to fix it. I am asking you to hear it and help me think.* This format prevents the two most common failures of such conversations: that the listener panics and demands immediate action, and that the listener receives the information as a personal betrayal and responds with blame before the facts are complete.

**Say what you want.** Men frequently disclose a serious problem and then resent the help they receive, because they did not specify what they wanted. Assistance, listening, practical involvement, and time to think are four different requests, and the listener cannot guess which is being made.

### Stage Four: Act on the Two Largest Items

Recovery is rarely achieved by addressing everything. It is achieved by addressing the two items that most affect the position: usually the largest debt or the largest cost, and the largest opportunity foregone. A plan with fifteen items will not be executed. A plan with two specific actions and a date will.

### Stage Five: Set a Review Date

The final stage is the creation of an end. Write down the date — one month, three months, six months ahead — on which the position will be reassessed. The review serves three functions: it prevents the situation from continuing indefinitely as a state of emergency; it produces evidence of progress (or of the need for a different approach); and it allows the man to stop carrying the whole burden in every hour of every day, because the next examination has a date.

### The Account Given to Others

Most serious failures require an account to be given to people beyond the first confidant: children, relatives, colleagues, employees, or the community. Men find this the most painful element of the whole process, and four principles make it manageable.

**Give the facts you know, and say what you do not know.** *The contract has been lost. I do not yet know what the effect will be on next term's fees. I will tell you by the twentieth of the month.* Uncertainty is more tolerable than vagueness or concealment.

**Do not apologise for the event, but own your decisions.** *The decision was mine, and it was wrong* is different from *I am a failure*. The first is a statement of accountability that a family can work with. The second transfers a burden of shame that serves no one.

**Give the plan, not the anxiety.** Families can handle difficulty; they handle it far less well when the head of the household communicates panic. A plan — even an incomplete one — is the single most reassuring thing a man can provide.

**Keep updating.** Concealment resumes through silence after the initial disclosure. A brief, factual update at the intervals promised is what maintains trust.

## 7.6 Separating Personal Worth from Performance

The final section addresses the underlying task of the chapter. Everything above is a method for managing a situation. This section concerns the arrangement that produced the situation's severity.

### What Fusion Looks Like

The fusion of worth and performance operates as a system of accounting in which a man's value is calculated from his results. Its features are recognisable.

- Good results produce relief rather than satisfaction.
- Bad results produce shame rather than disappointment.
- Rest feels like decline.
- Failure cannot be described without the word *I*.
- The achievements of others are experienced as a verdict on oneself.
- A period without results produces a sense of having no value at all.

The system is exhausting, and its most serious feature is what it does at the point of failure. A man whose worth is separate from his performance can lose a business, a job, or a contract and still act: he can speak to his wife, negotiate with creditors, take employment, and rebuild. A man whose worth is fused with his performance must protect his identity while managing the crisis, and the protection of identity and the management of the crisis frequently require opposite actions — which is why fused men so often conceal, over-trade, and collapse.

### How the Separation Is Built

The separation is not achieved by a change of belief. It is built by four practices, repeated over months.

**Change the language.** The single most effective practice is to describe what happened without a verdict about the self. *The tender was lost. Two competitors were cheaper. I made an error in the pricing.* This is not positive thinking; it is precision. Chapter 22 and Appendix H provide the method for identifying and rewriting the automatic verdicts that accompany setbacks.

**Establish a domain of worth that does not depend on results.** Family relationships, physical capability, skill, humour, craft, community contribution, religious practice, friendship — any domain in which a man can identify value that is not measured by outcomes. Men who possess such a domain survive setbacks better, in the experience of most counsellors, than men who do not, and its construction is entirely within the man's control.

**Keep an evidence file.** A written record of what has been done well, what was contributed, and what others have said, kept for the periods when memory supplies only failures. This is not vanity; it is a corrective for the well-documented tendency of the human memory to weight negative information more heavily.

**Accept praise.** This sounds trivial and is not. Men who receive a compliment and immediately discount it — *anyone could have done it, it was luck, he is only being polite* — are actively maintaining the fusion. Accepting the compliment and saying thank you is a small, repeatable exercise that changes the accounting.

### A Note for Men in the Middle of a Serious Setback

If the reader is currently inside the situation described in the first paragraph of this chapter, three sentences are offered directly.

**The situation is recoverable, even if the schedule is not what you planned.** Men recover from financial disasters at fifty, at sixty, and after seventy, and the majority of those who do so had no expectation of it in the month the loss occurred.

**Concealment is more expensive than the failure.** The financial loss can be reconstructed. The trust lost through months of concealment frequently cannot, and this is the finding that men who have been through the process report most consistently.

**The first conversation is the whole game.** Everything in this chapter — the facts on paper, the report to one person, the plan, the review date — follows from a single decision to stop being the only person who knows. Chapter 35 describes how to make that decision operational, Appendix D provides the financial worksheet, and Appendix E provides the conversation scripts.
