## 32.5 Saying "I Do Not Know" Without Losing the Room

The correction of the requirement is a protocol, and the protocol has three parts. Its function is not the man's relief; it is the accuracy of the information on which the group, the household, and the man himself will act.

### The Three-Part Reply

**Part One: what you know.** The man states accurately and without amplification what he knows about the matter: the facts, the previous results, the comparable case, the part of the question that is within his domain. The part's function is to establish that the man is competent and engaged, and it is the part that the other two depend upon.

**Part Two: what you do not know.** The man states the boundary clearly and without apology: *I do not know that.* The sentence should be delivered in the same register as Part One — without a change in tone, without an explanation, without a compensating display of competence in an adjacent matter. The register is the whole of the technique: an apology or a nervous explanation signals that something has occurred, whereas a boundary stated plainly signals that the man has supplied an accurate account.

**Part Three: what happens next.** The man states who will know, how the matter will be established, and when the answer will arrive: *X knows that; I will ask him this morning. I will have the answer by tomorrow and I will bring it to you.* The part is what preserves the man's position completely, because the position's requirement is not the information itself. It is the assurance that the matter is in hand.

### The Protocol in Practice

The protocol's four applications, with the form of the reply.

**In a meeting, to a technical question outside the man's domain.** *What I know is that the previous contract was priced at X and that the cost has moved. What I do not know is what the current rate for the component is — that is the procurement officer's assessment. I will have it before the next meeting and it will be in the paper.*

**In the household, to a question about a matter the man cannot resolve.** *I do not know how the fees will be paid in the second term. What I know is that we can cover the first and that I have applied to the cooperative and I will know their position by the end of the month. If they decline, these are the three options and we will decide them together.*

**In the man's own domain, on a matter that requires information he lacks.** *I am going to decide this by Friday, and I cannot decide it until I know what the supplier's position is. I will not decide it on an estimate, because the last estimate cost us four months.*

**To a question the man has been asked for years and has answered incorrectly.** *I have been telling you something about that which I now know is wrong. Here is the correction.* The form is the one in Section 32.3's fourth measure, and it is the single most effective act available to a man whose standing depends on his knowledge.

### The Five Obstacles to the Protocol

**1. The fear that the question will be read as the man's failure.** Answered by the protocol itself: the third part supplies the assurance that the position requires. The men who operate the protocol report the finding consistently: the group's confidence in the man is not reduced and is frequently increased, since the man has demonstrated that his answers can be relied upon.
**2. The man's own history of never having said it.** Men describe the first occasion as the most difficult and describe subsequent occasions as ordinary. The pattern is worth stating: the first *I do not know* in a setting where the man has always known is an event; the twentieth is a habit.
**3. The setting in which the man has been making claims.** Where the man has, for years, been the source of answers in a domain he does not command, the correction requires an act of exposure. The act should be conducted deliberately, once, with the correction stated plainly, and the men who have done it report that the exposure is considerably smaller than the dread of it and considerably shorter.
**4. The status competition of men's settings.** Where the question is part of an exchange in which the man is competing for standing, the protocol's use requires the man to decline the exchange rather than to lose it. The available form is the one that ends the exchange without a concession: *I do not know that one. What I know is X.*
**5. The absence of an alternative in the man's own domain.** Where the man is the only person in his setting with any relevant competence — the sole trader, the lone professional in a small town, the last surviving practitioner of a trade — the protocol's third part has no referent. The correction is then the arrangement rather than the protocol: a peer elsewhere, a professional association, a technical service, an arrangement for a second opinion. Section 27.5's material on the construction of arrangements applies, and the finding is the same: the man's position requires the arrangement, and the arrangement is constructible.

### The Signal the Protocol Sends

The material on this point is consistent in professional practice, and the chapter states it because it corrects the man's central assumption.

The protocol does not read as weakness in a competent man. It reads as accuracy, and accuracy is the attribute that the people around the man actually need from him. The settings in which *I do not know* is read as weakness are the settings in which nothing was known and nobody established anything: the informal competition of men's talk, the exchange among strangers, the environment in which the man's standing is nothing but his display. In the settings that matter to the man's household, his organisation, and his professional standing, the reverse operates: the man whose answers are bounded is trusted in a way that the man whose answers are universal is not.

## 32.6 Questions as a Competence

The correction's second half, and the part that converts the arrangement from a concession into a skill.

### The Five Questions

The following five questions are the ones with the strongest record in leadership practice and in professional settings, and each is a question a man in authority can ask without any reduction in his position because the question demonstrates that he is assessing rather than being assessed.

**1. What am I missing?** Addressed to a named person who sees the matter from another position. It is the highest-yield question available to a man in authority: the information asymmetry of Section 31.1 means that the man is systematically uninformed, and this question is the instrument that retrieves what the arrangement withholds.

**2. Who disagrees, and what is their case?** Asked before a decision rather than after it. The question converts disagreement from a threat into an assigned function and, in the practice's own terms, it is the measure that most reliably prevents the decision that nobody had the standing to oppose.

**3. What would have to be true for this to fail?** The question of the leader who is assessing a plan he has decided to pursue, and its function is to place the failure analysis in the room before the plan rather than after.

**4. What is the part of this that nobody has raised?** The question for the matter with an unusual absence of objection, and it is the most useful single instrument available against the group's tacit decisions.

**5. What have I done in the last month that I should know about?** Asked of a small number of people who have indicated that they will answer. The question is uncomfortable, it is the most consequential of the five, and the men who have practised it report that it is the measure that has kept their conduct calibrated.

### The Practice

**Frequency and setting.** The five questions monthly, in a fixed setting: a meeting with two or three people, a supervision arrangement, a walk, a call. The practice requires regularity rather than intensity.

**The conditions.** Two, and they are the conditions of the Chapter 31.5 third arrangement: the question must be asked of people who can answer honestly, and the man must not defend himself against the answer. The man's first response to an unfavourable answer should be an acknowledgement and a follow-up question, and the practice's whole value depends on it: a man who asks *what am I missing* and then argues with the reply has taught his organisation that the question is a formality.

**The record.** The answers, written, with dates. The record supplies the man with the calibration described in Section 31.1, and it supplies him, at the end of a year, with an account of his own conduct that he would not have obtained by any other means.

### The Difference Between Asking and Abdicating

Two clarifications, because the material can be misread.

**The man who asks has not ceased to decide.** The questions are the input to a decision that remains the man's. The distinction is between a decision made with the group's information and a decision delegated to the group, and the members of a group are generally able to distinguish the two accurately.

**The man who asks has not become uncertain.** The questions operate on the man's account of the matter and not on his confidence in himself. The men with the strongest record in this practice describe their confidence as unaltered: what changed was the accuracy of the information and the number of matters that arrived before they became problems.
