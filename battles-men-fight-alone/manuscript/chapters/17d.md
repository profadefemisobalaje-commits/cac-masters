## 17.6 Building a Father's Legacy of Honesty

The final substantive section addresses what a father leaves, and it proceeds from a finding that men find uncomfortable and then, frequently, useful: the material inheritance is not the part of a father that lasts, and the part that lasts is assembled from ordinary conduct over decades rather than from a plan.

### The Three Inheritances

**The material inheritance.** Property, assets, education funded, position in the community. Its value is real and its transmission is unreliable: it can be divided, lost, disputed, or consumed. The research on inherited wealth indicates that a substantial proportion of it does not persist beyond the second generation, and the disputes it generates frequently damage the family relations it was intended to support, as Chapter 19 discusses.

**The competence inheritance.** What the man has taught: practical skills, judgement, the ability to handle institutions, financial literacy, the management of a household, the conduct of a negotiation. This inheritance is more durable than the first and it is transmitted by involvement rather than by instruction. A father who takes his son to the market, the bank, the construction site, and the mechanic's yard transmits an inheritance whether or not he names it as such.

**The relational inheritance.** The pattern the man has established for how people in a family treat one another: whether difficulties are discussed, whether affection is expressed, whether silence is the household's method, whether a man may be weak in front of those who depend on him. This is the inheritance most likely to survive, and it is the one least likely to be chosen deliberately. Chapter 17's central proposition is that a father should choose it.

### The Practical Construction

Five measures build the three inheritances, and the first is the one that most fathers omit.

**1. Transmit the history rather than the summary.** A father should tell his children what actually happened: the jobs, the failures, the money that was lost, the decision that was made badly, the period of difficulty, the people who helped. Children who know the actual history inherit a usable map; children who know only the summary — *I worked hard and I educated my children* — inherit a standard against which they will measure themselves unfavourably and inaccurately.

**2. Teach the competence explicitly.** The skills a father holds are frequently so ordinary to him that he does not transmit them. The practical test: what do my children not know how to do that I could teach them in six sessions? Budgeting, negotiation, vehicle maintenance, the management of a rental property, cooking, the interpretation of a payslip, the conduct of a job interview, the handling of a family dispute, first aid, swimming. Six sessions per competence, one competence per year, is a transmission of substantial value.

**3. Write the things that are not said in speech.** A letter, a document, or a small notebook: what the father believes, what he regrets, what he hopes for each child, what he would do differently, and what he wants them to know about him. The document has a specific property that speech does not: it survives, it can be returned to, and it can be read after the man is no longer available to be asked. Men who write such a document frequently report that the writing changed their conduct as well, because it required them to know what they actually thought.

**4. Establish a recurring obligation of contact.** A fixed arrangement — a monthly call with each child, an annual journey, a standing meeting — that continues after the children have left the household and continues when the man has nothing practical to discuss. The arrangement matters because the alternative, contact that occurs when there is a reason, produces a relationship that consists of reasons.

**5. Say what the children need to hear while it can still be said.** Three statements, each of which children report as significant when they have been made and as absent when they have not: that the father is proud of them; that a specific failure does not change his regard; and that he has been affected by them in ways he can describe. The third is the most powerful and the least spoken: a father who can tell a child what that child has given him has supplied something that no provision can supply.

### Correcting the Record

A specific measure is available to a father who recognises that his conduct in a particular period was damaging: the correction. It consists of four elements, and it is best delivered in the form set out here because its structure prevents it from becoming either a defence or a confession.

1. **The conduct, stated factually.** *For three years I was working two jobs and I was not present, and when I was present I was short with you.*
2. **The impact, acknowledged.** *I know that you learned to manage without me, and that you had to be careful about what you said to me.*
3. **The reason, given without excuse.** *I did it because I believed the household would fail otherwise. That does not change what it was like for you.*
4. **What the man will do now.** *I cannot recover the years. What I can do is be available now and not make excuses about it.*

The measure is not a request for forgiveness and it should not be made as one. Its purpose is the removal of the child's self-directed explanation for the father's conduct, which is the specific and frequently unrecognised injury of the father who was present but absent.

## 17.7 Illustrative Case: The Two Notebooks

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family practice, counselling, and the research literature on fatherhood.

**Bashir is sixty-one, a retired nurse who worked for thirty-four years in a general hospital in Kano, married to Hadiza for thirty-five years, with five children aged between twenty-four and thirty-four.**

Bashir's account of his own fatherhood, given in the context of a family difficulty concerning his second son, was unusually clear and unusually bleak. He said: *I provided. I was present in the house. I do not think any of them knows me as a person, and I know that because of something that happened at my own father's burial.*

The events that produced the case were as follows.

Bashir's eldest daughter, Aisha, was preparing a family information document — a health history that her own physician had requested — and she asked her father, over the telephone, a series of questions about his parents, his own medical history, and his work. The conversation lasted forty minutes, and Bashir, in his description, found himself unable to answer most of the questions: he did not know his mother's age at her death, he did not know the diagnosis that had killed his father, and he could not recall the years in which he had held two of his own former positions.

The exchange had a further consequence. His daughter asked, in passing, why he had never taken the nurse-training position in Jos that he had mentioned once, and Bashir explained, for the first time to any of his children, that he had declined it in the year of his mother's illness because the family required his presence and because the relocation would have been unaffordable for his father. He had never mentioned the matter to his wife, who was present during the call, and the disclosure required a conversation afterwards in which she asked why he had not spoken of it.

Over the following six months, Bashir did three things, all of which are ordinary and all of which changed the family's arrangement.

First, he began to make notes. He bought two small notebooks. The first held factual material: the family's medical history, the dates, the events, the names, the years of employment, the property transactions, the debts that had been cleared. The second held what he described as the material he had never said: what each of the five children had been like as small children, the specific features of each one he had noticed, what he had feared for each of them, what he thought they should know about him, and, per child, one incident in which his own conduct had been poor and what had produced it. The second notebook took him about eleven months to complete.

Second, he conducted the corrections. He spoke to each of the five children individually, in the form set out in Section 17.6: the conduct, the impact, the reason, and the present arrangement. For three of the five children, the material was minor. For the fourth child — his second son, whose difficulty with his own marriage had prompted Bashir to seek help — the conversation concerned the four years in which Bashir had worked night shift, during which the boy, aged between nine and thirteen, had been managed by his mother and had come to believe that his father preferred his siblings. For the fifth child, the correction concerned a decision Bashir had made about her education, in which he had directed her to nursing against her stated preference for accountancy. She had complied and had become a successful nurse. She described the conversation with her father, twenty-five years later, as the moment at which something she had carried since the age of eighteen was finally released.

Third, and according to Bashir the most useful element, he established a monthly arrangement with each of the five: not a call in which there was news, but a standing call on a fixed day, in which the subject was whatever either of them wished to discuss. Three of his children have maintained the arrangement for four years; two keep it irregularly.

Bashir's summary, at the end of the case, was: *I gave them everything I had and I gave them nothing that was mine. I was the wage, the discipline, and the door that was always open and always empty of a person. The notebooks were the first thing I ever made for them that was actually me.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The disclosure was triggered by an ordinary practical question.** The health history, requested by a physician, produced the material. This is a recurring pattern in men's disclosures: the opening is frequently practical, and the questioner frequently does not know what has been opened.

**The abandoned opportunity was unknown to the man's wife of thirty-five years.** The declined position in Jos had not been disclosed to anybody, and its disclosure required a subsequent conversation. This is the sacrificial invisibility of Section 17.2 in its purest form: a sacrifice made in a specific year for the man's family of origin, carried for thirty-five years, and never registered by the household it benefited.

**The notebook method imposed a structure that men find workable.** Factual material and interior material in separate books; per-child entries; one conduct item per child. The structure matters, because the principal obstacle to this work is not unwillingness but the absence of a form in which a man can conduct it.

**The corrections were specific and small in content and large in effect.** For three of the five children the content was minor; for the daughter directed into nursing it concerned a decision made twenty-five years earlier. The measure's value is not the severity of the conduct; it is the removal of the child's own explanation for it.

**The change was in the arrangement, not in the man's character.** Bashir did not become a man who discusses his feelings in general. He established five recurring calls and completed two notebooks. The evidence from family practice on this point is consistent, and it is encouraging for men who believe that the change required is a change of personality: what is required is a structure, and structures can be adopted at any age.
