## 27.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I have a wife and children. Surely that is not nobody to call."**

The household is a genuine arrangement and it is one of the most important in any man's life. The question the chapter addresses is different, and it concerns the specific function described in Section 27.3: an arrangement outside the household in which the material can be delivered. The reasons are practical and they are not a criticism of the marriage. A man's wife is an interested party to most of the material — the money, the household's decisions, the marriage itself — and she cannot supply the second perspective, since she has her own position. A man's children are frequently the subject matter, which places the material outside their range. And the household can be lost, by bereavement, by separation, or by a period in which the marriage is in difficulty, and the arrangements that were carried by it are then discovered to be absent. Men in these circumstances report the finding described in Section 27.1's second form, and the recommendation is not that the wife is insufficient but that one arrangement outside the household is required.

**"I do not want to burden my friend. He has enough of his own."**

The concern is legitimate and it is addressed by the reciprocal form in Stage Three of Section 27.5: an arrangement in which the man is a supplier as well as a recipient. Men's arrangements hold when both parties bring something to them, and the specific finding from men who have built such arrangements is that the reciprocal form, once established, is stable for years. The concern also rests on a prediction about the friend's response which is worth testing with a small delivery rather than assuming.

**"What if I make the call and it is awkward?"**

It will, occasionally, be awkward, and awkwardness is not an indication of failure. The first contact after eleven years, as the case in Section 27.6 illustrates, frequently opens with a practical matter and lasts forty minutes. Where a contact is poorly received, the outcome is information about that particular person. The standard for evaluating the attempt is not the quality of the conversation but whether the arrangement now exists and whether a second contact is possible.

**"Is there any point in this if the situation is not going to change?"**

The situation frequently does not change, and the standard is not resolution. The documented effect of the arrangement is on the man's capacity to carry the situation, on his judgement, and on his physical and mental health, as Section 27.3 sets out. The man whose circumstances cannot be altered is exactly the man who most requires the capacity, which is the finding on which Part IV's material rests.

**"I have tried therapy and it did not help."**

The professional route's variability should be acknowledged: the outcome depends on the match, the practitioner's approach, and the material. Where an arrangement has failed, the relevant distinctions are whether the practitioner was appropriate to the material, whether the arrangement was of sufficient length, and whether the man was able to deliver the material or only the presenting complaint. The professional route is an entry point, not a single appointment, and the men who report substantial benefit generally describe a first several months in which little occurred.

**"I am the man other men call. What am I supposed to do?"**

The position described as Form Five in Section 27.1, and it is the most common among men in positions of responsibility. The requirement does not change: one arrangement outside the man's responsibility, with a defined purpose, and preferably a peer relationship or a professional one. Men in this position frequently describe the greatest difficulty in the world, since they have no experience of being a recipient and their entire professional life is organised around supplying. The finding from practice is that the professional arrangement, where the purpose is defined and the confidentiality is absolute, is the route that such men will actually use.

::: summary
**Chapter 27 in brief.** Isolation among men takes six forms: the man who was never asked, whose childhood supplied no experience of being attended to; the man whose arrangements were removed by bereavement, divorce, migration, retirement, or loss; the man with arrangements who cannot ask; the man whose isolation is arranged by the structure of his other obligations; the man who is surrounded by people who depend on him and has no recipient for his own material; and the man who chose it, whose arrangement is not a defect where it can hold under load.

The positions are produced by seven processes: the narrowing produced by responsibility, migration and relocation, status change, grief and loss, the household process in which the man's arrangements were carried by his wife, the concealment process in which the man's avoidance dissolves the arrangements themselves, and the conclusion process in which the man ceases to extend arrangements after being betrayed. Alcohol, overwork, and substitute arrangements accelerate every one of them.

The consequences are documented. Isolated men face substantially elevated mortality risk, with physiological and behavioural mechanisms identified; elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and suicide; and a specific and under-recognised damage to judgement, whose practical forms in men's lives are the large irreversible commitment made without a second opinion, the concealment continued beyond the point at which disclosure was affordable, the adjustment made in private and never revisited, and the conclusion formed without challenge.

Eight barriers stop men from reaching out: there is nothing to say; it will be used against me; it will cost me at work; I will be a burden; he has his own problems; it will change how my household sees me; I do not know who to call; and it will not help. Four of them are variations on a single proposition — that the material, once delivered, becomes a fact in the world with consequences — and the graded method addresses them all, since the arrangements are built by degrees, each delivery small enough to be survived.

A network is built in six stages. Stage One requires the identification of one person, on the criteria of availability, non-dependence, position outside the material's audience, and a record of reliability, generated by asking who has ever asked a question that was not about the man's position or usefulness. Stage Two requires contact without the material. Stage Three establishes a reciprocal arrangement, in which the recurring arrangement is the most durable form. Stage Four delivers a first small item of material, whose purpose is to establish that the channel will hold. Stage Five is the professional route, which is the entry point for a man who will not talk and which requires nothing of the man that he cannot supply today. Stage Six is the group, which supplies what no single relationship can: membership, more than one man, and the correction of the man's own account.
:::

## Further Reading

- **On social isolation and mortality:** the research literature on social connection, social isolation, and mortality, including the systematic reviews and meta-analyses that established the association and estimated its magnitude.
- **On isolation and men:** the literature on men's social networks, including the findings on the smaller size of men's networks, men's reliance on a spouse for their social connection, and the elevated risks faced by separated and widowed men.
- **On loneliness and health:** the research on loneliness as distinct from objective isolation, including the physiological and psychological findings, and the material on the specific effects of loneliness in men.
- **On men's help-seeking:** the literature on men's reluctance to seek help, including the findings on the specific barriers men report, the role of professional identity and self-reliance, and the interventions that have been shown to increase help-seeking among men.
- **On decision-making in isolation:** the research on group and dyadic decision-making, including the findings on the effects of the absence of a second perspective, the value of thinking a matter through aloud, and the phenomenon of the statement that changes in the speaker's hearing when it is delivered to another person.
- **Practical resources:** general practice and primary care, where the routine assessment questions supply an entry point; employee assistance programmes and occupational health services; men's groups operating with agreed rules of confidentiality; peer support services for men in specific occupational groups; and the material in Appendices J and Q.
