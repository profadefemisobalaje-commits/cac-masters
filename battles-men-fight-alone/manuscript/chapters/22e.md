## 22.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not simply a matter of discipline? Other men manage."**

Other men are managing considerably less well than the appearance suggests, and the appearance is the reason the belief persists. The specific finding that men report on entering a group or a professional arrangement for the first time is that the material they believed was unique to them is held by most of the men present. Discipline is a component of the work and it is not the whole of it, as Section 22.3 establishes, and the men who change are those who add the arrangement to the intention.

**"My weakness is anger. Is that not just my personality?"**

Temperament is real and the behaviour at a given moment is not the temperament itself. The distinction is testable, and the test is in Part One: is the behaviour concentrated in specific situations and states? Where the answer is yes, the behaviour is a response pattern rather than a disposition, and response patterns are changeable. Where the behaviour occurs continuously and across every setting, the assessment is different, and a professional opinion is worth obtaining.

**"I have tried many times. Why would this be different?"**

Because the method differs at the four points identified in Section 22.3: the cue is altered rather than resisted, the function is supplied rather than removed, the behaviour is measured rather than prohibited, and the isolation is ended. If the previous attempts consisted of decisions made after an incident and held by resolve, then none of these four elements was present, and the comparison is not between the same method applied twice.

**"Can I do this without telling my wife?"**

For material of the kind in Chapter 23, the disclosure question is set out there and depends on the severity. For an ordinary weakness, the answer depends on the effect on the household. Where the behaviour affects the household — the anger, the expenditure, the drinking, the absences — the concealment is a second problem, and the household's participation in the arrangement is frequently decisive. Where the behaviour is private and affects nobody else, the disclosure is a matter of the man's judgment and the accountability arrangement can be conducted with a professional or a peer.

**"What if the person I tell uses it against me?"**

Then the material is information about that person, and it should be recorded rather than regretted. Two practices reduce the risk: choosing the person on the criteria in Section 22.6, and disclosing in the specific form rather than as a general confession about the man's character.

**"How long does this take?"**

The observable pattern is that the environment and the substitute produce a measurable change within two to six weeks; that the measurement and the accountability arrangement are required indefinitely; and that the psychological work — the belief, the situation, the shame — takes between six months and two years, in the cases in which it concludes at all. Men should plan on the arrangement's being permanent and the work's being finite, which is the reverse of the usual expectation.

::: summary
**Chapter 22 in brief.** A personal weakness is a repeated behaviour that the man believes is against his own interest, that he has attempted to change and has not changed, and for which he bears some responsibility. Four categories are habitually merged under the term and require different treatment: character weaknesses, which are supported by the man's own judgement in the moment; habits, which are the most changeable because their mechanism is known; conditions, which require treatment; and circumstances with behavioural results, where the arrangement rather than the behaviour is the subject.

The weaknesses men most commonly hold against themselves are anger, alcohol, sexual conduct, gambling, lying and concealment, procrastination, pride and contempt, and the behaviours that are symptoms of an untreated condition. Three principles govern the naming: name one weakness rather than a catalogue, name the behaviour rather than the character, and address the smallest version that is the problem. The word *weakness* carries its weight for four reasons: it is read as evidence about the person, it contradicts the script's requirement of control, its cost frequently falls on other people, and it is old — which produces the conclusion that it is permanent.

Private shame has six costs: the distortion of the man's account of himself; the loss of the relationship's truth; the energy of concealment; the escalation of the behaviour, since the behaviour manages the distress the behaviour produces; the prevention of help; and the transmission of the pattern to the household.

Willpower fails at four points: it operates against the cue rather than on it; it removes the behaviour without supplying what the behaviour provided; it draws on a capacity that has been spent by the end of a working day; and it is stated in the form that produces the greatest shame. The replacement methods are the alteration of the cue in advance, the substitute that fits the function, measurement rather than prohibition, and accountability that ends the isolation.

Understanding one's own patterns requires a trigger record kept at the time of occurrence, which reliably identifies the timetable, the states, the company and place, and the function the behaviour serves; a function test that asks what the behaviour actually produces and what would produce the same outcome without its cost; and a history that locates the period in which the weakness began, which is frequently the situation that requires attention.

The change programme proceeds in five stages: the environment, the substitute, the behavioural method of measurement and defined review, the psychological work on the belief, the situation, and the shame, and the handling of failure through analysis rather than sentencing, rapid resumption, a change to one element of the arrangement, and a report to the accountable person. Accountability requires the conversion of secrecy into confidentiality: one person at minimum, selected for their independence, their record of discretion, their willingness to state an assessment, and their availability over time; a defined interval of reporting; and four prohibitions concerning unsuitable confidants, the use of disclosure as absolution, the conversion of the arrangement into a general assessment, and the abandonment of the arrangement at the point of improvement.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Name the single weakness you will address. What have your previous attempts consisted of, and which of the four mechanisms in Section 22.3 were absent from them?
2. What does the weakness do for you — stated in one sentence beginning *I do this in order to*? Does it actually produce that outcome?
3. When did the behaviour begin, and what was happening in your life at that time? What has never been addressed from that period?
4. From the six costs of shame in Section 22.2, which are you paying now — the distorted account of yourself, the loss of the relationship's truth, the energy of concealment, the escalation, the prevention of help, or the transmission to your household?
5. On the trigger record, what is the timetable of your behaviour? What state precedes it most often, and which single environmental change would interrupt the sequence?
6. Who is your accountable person, and does the person meet the four criteria in Section 22.6? Who is the person you should not tell, and why?
7. Write your failure plan: the situation in which a recurrence is most likely, what you will do instead, and within what period you will resume the plan.

## Further Reading

- **On habit and behaviour change:** the research literature on habit formation and change, including the findings on cue-based automaticity, the role of context and friction, and the evidence on the relative weakness of intention as a predictor of behaviour.
- **On self-regulation:** the literature on self-control as a limited and context-dependent capacity, including the findings on ego depletion and the substantial evidence that environmental change outperforms resistance.
- **On anger and its management:** the research on anger and aggression in men, including the findings on the physiological and cognitive mechanisms of anger episodes, and the evidence on structured anger-management programmes.
- **On procrastination:** the psychological literature on procrastination, including the consistent finding that it is associated with aversive task appraisal and emotional avoidance rather than with time management, and the evidence on the interventions that reduce it.
- **On accountability and disclosure:** the research on the effects of disclosure on distress, including the findings on expressive writing, and the literature on accountability and goal attainment.
- **Practical resources:** counselling for specific behaviours; structured anger-management and habit-change programmes; men's groups with an agreed rule of honesty; and the material in Chapters 21, 23, and 35 of this book.
