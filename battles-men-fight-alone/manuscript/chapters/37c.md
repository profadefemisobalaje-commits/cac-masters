## 37.3 What the Absence of Rest Produces

The material on sustained work without recovery is substantial, and the following findings are the ones the reader can apply to his own position.

### The Research Findings

**1. Performance declines, and the decline is not noticed by the person affected.** The research on sustained work without recovery, in settings where the consequences are measurable, reports a consistent finding: performance, vigilance, and judgement degrade over extended periods of continuous work, and the affected person's assessment of their own performance degrades more slowly than the performance itself. The finding is important because the man's evidence for the adequacy of his capacity is his own assessment, which is the measure most affected.

**2. Recovery has a threshold.** The recovery of performance and of physiological state requires a period that is not reducible to a shorter substitute: many of the effects of sustained work are corrected by sleep and by the categories of Section 37.2, and they are not corrected by a brief break, a weekend, or the man's intention. The finding is consistent with men's own reports: the holiday that produced no restoration, the weekend that left them in the same state, the Sunday evening in which nothing had changed.

**3. Sleep is a specific intervention.** The research on sleep restriction and performance is among the most consistent in this field: the restriction of sleep degrades vigilance, mood, judgement, emotional regulation, and physical health; the effects accumulate over successive nights; and the affected person's rating of their own performance is substantially better than their measured performance. For the reader, the finding means that a chronic reduction in sleep is not a lifestyle preference; it is a condition with a measurable cost, and it should be treated as a matter for the general practitioner where it has persisted.

**4. Sustained work without recovery is associated with the presentations described throughout this book.** The research on burnout and on prolonged occupational strain reports associations with depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, cardiovascular outcomes, alcohol use, and the degradation of relationships. The presentations in Section 37.4 are the ones reported in this book's own case material in Chapters 30, 31, and 34, and the finding is that they are frequently attributed to the position, the age, or the man's temperament rather than to the absence of recovery.

### The Presentations in the Man

The following are reported consistently by men in the case material and by the practitioners who see them, and they are arranged in the order in which they develop.

**Stage one: the reduction of the man's margins.** The man's patience, tolerance, availability, and interest reduce, and the reduction is generally attributed to the workload's temporary intensity. The specific sign is the man's conduct in the minor matters: the traffic, the noise, the staff, the household's ordinary friction.

**Stage two: the loss of the man's restoration.** The categories of Section 37.2 cease to be available or cease to work: the sleep reduces in quality; the exercise stops; the interests are set down; the social arrangements are cancelled with reasons; the quiet is replaced by the screen. The man's account is that he has no time, which is accurate, and the account's consequence is that the measures that would restore his capacity are the ones removed to make room for the work that requires it.

**Stage three: the presentation in the body and the mind.** The sleep, the blood pressure, the chest, the stomach, the headaches, the weight, the alcohol; the flatness, the anxiety, the reduced concentration, the exhaustion that sleep does not correct. The presentation that most frequently brings the man into contact with a service, and the one that is most frequently treated as a separate matter from the work that produced it.

**Stage four: the cynicism and the loss of meaning.** The man's account of the work, the people, and the organisation becomes contemptuous; the position's purpose is no longer available to him; and the work continues. This is the presentation described in Chapter 31.4 as the core of the burnout pattern, and its feature is that the man's performance is generally maintained.

**Stage five: the collapse or the emergency.** The illness, the medical event, the collapse at the desk, the household's crisis, the accident, the conclusion of Chapter 34.3's first category. The stage is frequently the point at which the man first rests, and the finding men report is the one that gives the chapter its title: *the first proper rest I had in nine years was three weeks in the hospital.*

### The Presentation in the Household

The cost that the chapter treats as decisive, because it is the one the man does not count.

**The household's reduced arrangements.** The occasions not attended, the conversations not held, the arrangements conducted by the spouse without the man, the children's events missed, the contributions reduced. The household's account is generally more accurate than the man's: the members of a household observe the man's state without the man's explanation of it, and their arrangements adapt to what they see.

**The man's conduct toward the household.** The specific and most frequently reported consequence: the man with no margins is short with the people who are available, and the people who are available are the people who cannot impose a consequence. Men in the case material describe the discovery of this arrangement late: *I was the most patient man in the office and I was shouting at my own children about their shoes.*

**The household's management of the man.** The children's selections, the spouse's timing, the occasions organised around the man's state, the subject not raised. This is the arrangement described in Chapters 18 and 31.6, and it is the chapter's principal argument for the accounting: the household's arrangements are constructed around the absence of the man's restoration, and its members pay for the work in a currency that does not appear in the organisation's accounts.

**The transfer of the man's condition to the household.** The material of Chapters 29.7 and 30.3: the man's state discharged into the people available, and the household's members carrying the consequences of a condition whose cause is elsewhere and which they cannot alter.

## 37.4 The Guilt of Resting

The section takes the guilt seriously and analyses it, because the analysis is what produces the counter-argument.

### The Rule

The guilt of resting is produced by a proposition, and the proposition is generally available to the man in the following forms.

**The worth form.** *I am worth what I produce, and a day of rest is a day of zero.*

**The security form.** *My position exists because I am producing. If I stop producing, the position is at risk, and everything that depends on the position is at risk with it.*

**The desert form.** *I have not earned it.* The form's mechanism is the arrangement in which rest is the reward for a completed quantity of work, and its feature is that the quantity is never completed, because the position's requirements expand to fill the available capacity.

**The comparison form.** *Other men are working.* The form is supplied by the environment's evidence: the colleague's hours, the competitor's activity, the man who is always available, the men who are succeeding.

**The inheritance form.** *My father did not rest.* The form is delivered in the man's own history and is generally accompanied by an accurate description of the father's conduct: the man in the case material who cannot rest is frequently the son of a man who did not, and the rule is transmitted without its costs ever having been stated.

### The Mechanism

The rule operates as follows.

The man's environment rewards output and penalises its absence, as Section 37.1 established. The man generalises the arrangement into a proposition about himself. The proposition is applied continuously, and the accounting period is short, generally the day. Rest therefore appears as an unaccounted period in which the man's standing is not being maintained, and the man experiences the expected consequence of standing being reduced. The guilt is not a feeling about rest; it is the sensation of the rule's operation.

The mechanism explains three of the chapter's most frequently reported findings: the guilt is frequently strongest in the first hours of rest and reduces over a longer period, because the rule's application is periodic; the guilt is frequently relieved by productive activity during a rest period, which is the arrangement in which men perform restoration's activities as tasks; and the guilt is generally experienced as evidence that the man's rest is wrong, which is the rule's operation interpreted as information.

### The Seven Statements Men Make

The guilt is generally presented in one of seven statements, and each requires the counter-argument stated in Section 37.5.

1. *I cannot afford to rest.*
2. *If I stop, everything I have built will be at risk.*
3. *There is nobody else to do it.*
4. *I will rest when the work is finished.*
5. *This is what the position requires.*
6. *My father worked every day of his life and did not complain.*
7. *The work is the only thing I am good at.*

The statements are not irrational. Each is accurate in its own terms, and the counter-argument in the following section is drawn from the evidence rather than from an assertion to the contrary.
