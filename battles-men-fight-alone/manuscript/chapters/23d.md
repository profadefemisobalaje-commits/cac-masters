## 23.6 Relapse, and the Long Work

The subject of relapse is treated separately because the popular account of addiction misrepresents it and because the misrepresentation causes men to abandon the work after a single episode.

### Relapse Is Expected

Relapse is a common feature of recovery from dependence, in the same manner that the adjustment of treatment is a common feature of the management of diabetes and hypertension. The evidence on treatment outcomes indicates that a substantial proportion of people who achieve recovery do so after one or more episodes of return to use, and that the factors associated with a good long-term outcome include the speed of return to treatment rather than the absence of any return.

The practical consequence is a rule that men should establish in advance: **a return to use is information about the plan, not a verdict on the man.** The alternative understanding, which is the common one, is that the episode demonstrates that the condition is untreatable and the man is beyond help, and that understanding produces the pattern in which a single episode ends a two-year recovery.

### The Danger Points

The research on relapse identifies a set of situations in which risk is elevated, and knowing them in advance is the principal preventive measure.

- **Negative emotional states:** the argument, the financial loss, the humiliation, the bereavement.
- **Positive emotional states:** the celebration, the promotion, the wedding, the business success.
- **Interpersonal conflict:** a specific quarrel, a family dispute, a marital crisis.
- **Social pressure:** the specific person, the occasion, the environment associated with use.
- **Physical states:** pain, exhaustion, hunger, illness, the effect of medication.
- **The early period after a significant period of abstinence**, including at the point where the man's confidence is highest.

Men should write the specific danger points for their own condition, and a plan for each: what will be done, whom will be called, and what will be avoided.

### The Handling of an Episode

Five steps, and their order matters.

**Stop the episode at its shortest.** The most consequential variable. The difference between one evening and a three-month return is almost always attributable to the interpretation the man places on the first evening, not to the severity of the condition.

**Tell the person who knows.** The disclosure to the practitioner, the group, or the one confidant should occur within days, and it is a feature of the recovery rather than a failure of it.

**Review the plan, not the man.** What was the situation, what was the trigger, and what will be different in the arrangements? The review should be conducted in the form the man uses elsewhere in this book: specific items with dates.

**Do not make it the central fact about the recovery.** The danger of a review conducted in the register of shame is that the episode acquires a position in the man's account that it does not occupy in the plan.

**Return to the treatment arrangement without delay.** Where the treatment had concluded, it should be resumed; where it continues, the frequency should be increased for a period.

### The Long Work

Recovery in the durable sense is not an event but an arrangement that is maintained. Four elements are consistently present in the accounts of men who have maintained recovery for years.

**A permanent disclosure to at least one person.** Not a public announcement, which is a matter of the man's judgment, but the presence of at least one person who knows the condition and who remains in contact. Its function is to prevent the isolation that maintains the condition.

**A continuing practice.** A group, a meeting, a professional relationship at reduced frequency, a routine of record-keeping. Its function is maintenance, and its duration is indefinite.

**A revised arrangement of life.** The environment, the company, the hours, the work, and, in a significant proportion of cases, the household's arrangements. Recovery that leaves the arrangement which produced the use untouched has a lower prospect of durability, and the changes in Chapters 12 and 18 are frequently part of the same work.

**A purpose that is not the recovery itself.** A specific observation from the accounts of men in long-term recovery: the men who maintain it are those for whom something has taken the place the substance or the behaviour occupied — a relationship, a work, a contribution, an activity. A recovery defined only by the absence of the behaviour is fragile, because absence is not a life.

## 23.7 When It Is Someone Else

This section addresses the reader who is not the person with the condition: the man whose son, brother, wife, colleague, or friend is dependent, or who occupies a position in which he must manage the situation. It sets out what helps and, more importantly, what does not.

### What Does Not Help

**The repeated request.** Asking a dependent man to stop, repeatedly and in the same form, produces no change and consumes the relationship's remaining capacity. The evidence on the effectiveness of unaided requests is not encouraging, and the requests frequently postpone treatment by reinforcing the man's belief in his own capacity to manage.

**The rescue that removes the consequences.** Paying the debts, covering the absences, repairing the damage, and concealing the effects from the household and the employer. This is the most common action taken by families, and it is the one most consistently associated with the continuation of the condition. Its removal — the decision that the man will bear the consequences of his use — is the action that most frequently produces a decision to seek treatment.

**The moral lecture.** The effect is described in Section 23.2: shame increases, and shame maintains the behaviour.

**The threat that is not carried out.** An ultimatum stated and not implemented teaches the man that the threats are not to be taken seriously, which removes the one instrument available to the household.

**The secrecy maintained on the man's behalf.** Where a father conceals his son's condition, or a wife conceals her husband's, the condition's progression is assisted by the very person most concerned about it.

**The declaration that the person must want help.** The claim that treatment is impossible until the person is ready is not supported by the evidence. Coerced or strongly pressured entry into treatment, whether by an employer, a court, or a family, produces outcomes broadly comparable to voluntary entry, and the practical implication is that pressure applied with a specific and available treatment option is worthwhile.

### What Helps

**The assessment.** The most useful single action available to a family member is obtaining a professional assessment and ensuring the man attends it. The offer should be specific: the appointment made, the date given, the journey arranged, and the reduction of every practical obstacle.

**The described consequence.** The household states what will change if the situation continues, in specific and implementable terms, and implements it. Not a threat about an unmanageable outcome; a stated arrangement. Examples: the money will be separated; the household will not carry the debts; the children will be removed from the situation; the man will leave the house until the situation is addressed.

**The boundary.** The arrangements in Chapter 10's method, applied to the condition: what the household will and will not do, stated in advance and held. The purpose is not punishment and not leverage; it is the household's survival, which is required for the household to be in a position to support a recovery.

**The offer that is specific and repeated.** The offer of help should be specific — the assessment, the clinic, the journey, the cost, the practitioner's name — and it should be repeated at intervals, in the same form, without a lecture. Repeated specific offers are among the more effective interventions available to families, and their effect is frequently delayed by months.

**The household's own support.** The family members of a dependent man need support of their own: a counsellor, a group for families, a professional relationship. The pattern of the family's own health deteriorating while it manages the man's condition is one of the most consistent findings in the literature on families and addiction.

**The safety plan, where it is required.** Where the household contains the possibility of violence, threats, or the endangerment of children, the safety material in Appendix G takes precedence over everything in this section, and it should be implemented before any other work is attempted. The incidence and severity of domestic violence are both substantially elevated in the presence of alcohol, and the household's safety is not negotiable in the interests of the man's treatment.
