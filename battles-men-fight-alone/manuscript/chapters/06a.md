## Introduction

The most common sentence spoken by a man who is depressed, and the one least likely to be understood as a description of his condition, is: *I am tired.*

He is tired when he wakes. He is tired at eleven in the morning, when the work that used to interest him requires an effort he cannot explain. He is tired in the evening, and he does not sleep well. He attributes the tiredness to age, to the volume of work, to the state of the economy, or to a deficiency of iron, and he is frequently right about one of these things, which is why the explanation holds. What he does not say, because he has not identified it and would not know how to say it, is that he has also stopped looking forward to anything, that he feels nothing when his son shows him a school report, that he has become unreasonably angry about small matters, and that a private, unspoken flatness has settled over his life so gradually that he is no longer certain whether anything has changed at all.

Depression in men is common, serious, and — under the right conditions — treatable. It is also frequently missed, by the man himself, by his family, and, in a substantial number of cases, by the health professionals he sees. The purpose of this chapter is to describe what depression looks like when the person experiencing it is a man, to explain why it is missed, to set out what happens when it is left untreated, and to describe what helps.

## 6.1 Understanding Depression in Men

Depression is not sadness. Sadness is a normal emotional state, proportionate to circumstances, and it passes. Depression is a condition with a specific set of symptoms, a duration, and an effect on the capacity to function. Its recognised features include persistent low mood, loss of interest and pleasure, fatigue, disturbed sleep and appetite, difficulty concentrating, a sense of worthlessness or inappropriate guilt, and, in severe cases, thoughts of death or self-harm.

Three points about the condition are important before the male presentation is described.

**It is a real condition, not a weakness or a failure of discipline.** The evidence for this is found in the effectiveness of treatment, the consistency of symptoms across cultures and social classes, and the substantial body of research on the biological, psychological, and social factors that contribute to it. Men who believe that their condition is a personal deficiency frequently delay treatment by years, and the delay is often the most damaging element of the whole episode.

**It varies in severity, and severity determines treatment.** Mild depression may respond to structured psychological support, activity planning, and social re-engagement. Moderate and severe depression usually requires a specific treatment — talking therapy, medication, or both in combination — and severe depression with risk of self-harm requires urgent specialist care. A single approach will not fit every case, and the reader should be cautious of any claim that it will.

**It is frequently accompanied by other conditions.** Anxiety, alcohol or substance use, chronic pain, and physical illness commonly occur alongside depression, and each can conceal the other. A man who presents with heavy drinking may be drinking to manage a depression that is not diagnosed; a man who presents with depression may also need treatment for an anxiety disorder. A competent assessment looks for more than one condition, which is one reason that professional assessment is worth more than self-assessment.

### The Scale of the Problem

Depression affects a very large number of people worldwide, and it is a leading cause of disability. Men constitute a substantial proportion of cases, and the recorded figures almost certainly understate the true number, because men are less likely to report symptoms, less likely to consult, and less likely to be diagnosed. The most serious consequence of this under-recognition is a well-documented pattern: men die by suicide at substantially higher rates than women in almost every country for which reliable data exist, despite lower recorded rates of diagnosed depression. The most plausible explanation, supported by a large body of work, is not that men suffer less but that they present later and in less recognisable forms, and that their distress becomes lethal before it becomes visible.

This is the reason the chapter opens with a warning rather than a definition: the material below is not academic. It concerns a condition that kills men who consider themselves strong, and it kills them quietly, in the last stages of a process that was visible for years to anybody who knew how to look.

### Why It Is Missed

Depression in men is missed for four reasons, and each of them operates in ordinary life rather than in exceptional circumstances.

**The symptoms are attributed to character.** Irritability is read as a bad temper. Withdrawal is read as pride or fatigue. Lateness, forgetfulness, and unfinished tasks are read as decline in commitment. Short-temperedness with children is read as harshness. Each of these attributions is available, plausible, and wrong.

**Men present physical symptoms.** A man who is depressed frequently reports headaches, back pain, digestive problems, or chronic tiredness, and a physical investigation follows. This is appropriate — physical causes must be excluded — but the emotional dimension is frequently not raised by the patient and not enquired into by the clinician.

**Men answer screening questions in the negative.** The standard questions concern sadness, crying, and hopelessness. A man who is angry, flat, and drinking more than before will answer those questions accurately and unhelpfully, and the condition will not be identified. This is why the following section sets out the male presentation in detail: it provides the reader with the questions that are more likely to be informative.

**Men conceal the condition deliberately.** A man who suspects the truth may spend considerable effort concealing it — presenting as busy, keeping up appearances, avoiding medical contact, and refusing to reduce his load. Concealment of depression is one of the most self-defeating activities a human being can undertake, because it removes the social support that protects against deterioration, and it is extremely common among men.
