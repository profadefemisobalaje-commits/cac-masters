# Loving Someone Who Does Not Understand Your Pain {.chapter num="14" short="Loving Someone Who Does Not Understand Your Pain"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the situation of the man whose partner does not understand or acknowledge the difficulties he carries, and identify the specific and typical forms that this takes.
2. Explain why disclosure frequently produces an inadequate response, without assuming that the partner's response reflects indifference.
3. Identify the features of the relationship that develop when a man concludes that his partner cannot understand him, and assess whether those features have begun to appear in his own marriage.
4. Apply a structured approach to being understood: what to disclose, when, in what form, and what to do with an inadequate response.
5. Recognise the limits of a relationship's capacity, and distinguish between a partner who is unwilling to understand and a partner who is unable to understand.
6. Decide, using a structured assessment, what to do where understanding cannot be obtained, including the options of external support, renegotiation of the relationship, and, where necessary, ending it.
:::

## Introduction

Consider two statements that men make about their marriages, and note how differently they are heard.

The first: *my wife does not understand what I go through.* This statement is heard as a complaint about insufficient appreciation, and it is frequently answered with the observation that the wife also has difficulties, that the man should communicate better, or that he is asking for sympathy he has not earned by disclosing anything.

The second, which is the substance of the first: *I have told the person closest to me what I am carrying, more than once, and the response I received made it clear that the matter would not be discussed again. Since then I have stopped telling her anything that matters. I live with somebody who is my partner in the household and a stranger to my interior life, and I intend to continue doing so for the rest of my life.*

This chapter addresses the second situation, and its tone is deliberately careful, because the subject is one on which both partners are frequently misjudged. The chapter does not assume that the wife is cold, indifferent, or incapable. It does not assume that the man has communicated adequately, because a substantial proportion of men who say they have disclosed have done so once, indirectly, at a bad moment, and in three sentences. It does assume that this is a genuine and common condition, that it hurts, and that the options are more numerous than men generally believe.

The chapter also proceeds from a finding that should be stated at the outset, because it structures everything that follows. In the great majority of the marriages this chapter describes, the wife's difficulty is not a deficiency of love. It is a combination of four things: she has been given insufficient information; the information she was given was delivered in a form that could not be received, such as anger or silence; her own resources are exhausted; and she, too, has concluded that her own difficulties are not understood. The last of these is the most consequential, and it is the one that men in this position most frequently fail to consider.

### What This Chapter Is Not Saying

Two clarifications are necessary before the analysis proceeds, because the subject invites two errors in opposite directions.

**The first error is to treat the man's complaint as a demand for sympathy.** In the accounts given by men, the request is rarely for comfort. It is, in the great majority of cases, for one person in the world to know the actual position. Men in this situation do not generally want to be excused from their obligations; they want to stop maintaining the impression, at home, that their obligations are being carried without cost. The distinction is important because a request for knowledge can be met, while a request for relief from responsibility frequently cannot.

**The second error is to treat the wife's inadequate response as the cause.** The cause is a sequence of exchanges, and the sequence includes the man's part: the form in which he disclosed, the moment he selected, the fact that he has usually disclosed once rather than consistently, and the fact that he has generally shown no interest in what his wife is carrying. Therefore the entire analysis in this chapter proceeds on the assumption that the arrangement has two authors, that both of them are acting reasonably on the information available to them, and that the most effective interventions operate on the exchange rather than on either person's character.

To these two clarifications, a third should be added concerning the scope of the chapter. What follows is written for men in marriages and long partnerships that are not abusive, in which both parties are basically willing, and in which the difficulty is a matter of unspoken material rather than a matter of safety. Where a relationship involves violence, coercion, or a threat to the man's safety or the safety of children, the framework in this chapter does not apply and the guidance in Appendix G should be followed instead.

## 14.1 When Your Partner Does Not Understand

The experience described in this section has recurring features. Men who recognise four or more of them are likely to be in the situation this chapter is written about.

### The Ten Features

**The first attempts at disclosure were badly received.** The man recalls the specific occasion, sometimes years later: what he said, what she said in reply, and what he concluded. The replies he remembers are specific, and they are almost never cruel. They are: *you are the man, this is what you are supposed to do*; *everybody has problems*; *what do you want me to do about it?*; *if I begin to worry about your problems, who will carry mine?*; *I have my own burdens*; or the response of turning the subject to her own difficulties.

**The subject has not been raised since.** Because the first attempt produced a conclusion, and the man does not attempt a second disclosure of the same type with the same person. This is the central mechanism of the whole condition: silent men are frequently silent because they have an accurate memory of what happened the last time they spoke.

**A specific repertoire of behaviour has developed to avoid the subject.** The man works later. He answers questions about his state with *I am fine.* He attends to the household's practical matters in a formal and effective manner. He conducts any substantial conversation with his wife about matters of interest but not about himself. Some men describe sleeping in separate rooms, not out of hostility but for the absence of conversation.

**Anger is the message when the subject is unavoidable.** The anger is produced by the pressure, not by the wife, and it is received as though it were directed at her, which is why the pattern is so damaging to a marriage: the only signal that reaches the partner is the signal that most damages her.

**Loneliness is present inside the marriage.** This is the defining feature and the most difficult to describe. The man is not alone; he has a partner, a household, and frequently children. He is also aware that if he were to receive bad news, the first person he would tell is not the person to whom he is married.

**An accommodation is reached and maintained.** The couple arranges itself in a settled equilibrium: the man manages his difficulties externally or privately, and the wife manages the household and her own difficulties with her own network. The arrangement works, and its costs are that neither partner has a confidant, and that the relationship contains partnership without intimacy.

**A separate source of support is developed, or is not.** Some men develop one: a close friend, a sibling, a professional, a group. Others do not, which greatly increases the risk described in Chapters 4, 5, and 6.

**There is a survivor's interpretation of the wife.** Men in this position hold a settled view: that she does not care, that she has never been interested in his interior life, that she married a position rather than a person. Any examination of this interpretation requires the material in Section 14.2, because it is frequently inaccurate and it is difficult to test without a conversation that the man has decided is not worth attempting.

**Comparisons occur.** With a colleague's marriage, with a brother's, with an imagined ideal. Comparisons of this kind are corrosive and they are almost always based on public information.

**The alternative is considered.** Men in this position consider, at least occasionally, the possibility of another relationship in which they would be understood. Where the consideration remains a thought, it is usually a symptom of the underlying deprivation; where it becomes a plan, Chapter 16 applies.

### Why the First Attempt Failed

It is worth examining the first failed disclosure in detail, because it is the pivot on which the entire condition turns, and because men generally interpret it as evidence about the wife's character rather than as evidence about the exchange.

Consider four recurring forms of the first failed disclosure.

**The sudden disclosure.** A man who has carried a difficulty for a long time releases it, at night, in a compressed and urgent form, frequently with anger or tears. The partner receives an undigested mass of material, with no warning, at the end of her own long day, in a form that suggests crisis. Her response will be oriented to containing the crisis rather than understanding the man's experience. Men frequently report being told to calm down, or having their account corrected on points of fact, or getting a practical instruction. None of this is a refusal to understand; it is a response to an emergency.

**The instrumental disclosure.** The man mentions his difficulty as an explanation for a decision or a refusal: he cannot attend the event, he cannot take the additional obligation, he is not able to discuss the matter now. In this form, the difficulty is presented as an obstacle, and it is answered as an obstacle, with negotiation about the obstacle. The man experiences the negotiation as a refusal to engage with what lies behind it.

**The disclosure hedged into insignificance.** The man includes a caveat that cancels the disclosure: *it is nothing, I am managing, I only mentioned it.* The partner takes the caveat at its word, which is a rational response to the information received. The man records that she was not interested.

**The disclosure with a demand.** The man connects the disclosure to an expectation: that she should adjust her behaviour, acknowledge his difficulty, or accept a specific change. The partner hears the demand and responds to it. The man hears the response as the dismissal of the difficulty.

The pattern across all four is the same: the exchange concerned a message, and it is read afterwards as evidence of a character. The correction is not to excuse the wife's response — some responses are genuinely inadequate — but to establish what was communicated, in what form, at what time, and what a different form might produce.

## 14.2 Why Disclosure Is Hard — For Both of You

This section shifts the focus, deliberately, and examines the wife's position. The shift is not a concession; it is a practical necessity, because a man who holds an accurate model of his partner's situation has a materially better chance of obtaining the understanding he wants.

### The Wife's Position

Five features are regularly present.

**Her own load.** In the households described in this book, the wife frequently carries substantial and continuous responsibilities: household management, children, employment, extended-family obligations, and, in many cases, financial management of a household whose income is unstable. Her capacity to receive additional emotional material at the end of a day is genuinely limited, and this is a matter of capacity rather than love.

**Her own undisclosed difficulties.** Chapter 22 addresses the recognised fact that women's emotional load is frequently carried invisibly and unevenly. Many wives in these households are themselves carrying material they have never fully disclosed, and some of them have made their own attempt at disclosure that received an inadequate response. Where both partners have concluded invisibly that the other is unavailable, the marriage contains two people who have each decided, separately, not to speak.

**The cultural instruction she has received.** Many women have been taught, explicitly and from childhood, that a man's difficulties are his to carry, that a wife's role is to manage the household and not to question her husband's inner state, that a man who complains has been weakened by something, and that a woman who listens to a husband's despair will be required to carry it. These instructions are not the wife's invention and they are frequently held alongside genuine affection and concern.

**The fear of what comes next.** Where a man discloses that he is struggling, the wife's first concern is frequently practical and well-founded: what happens if he stops working, or becomes ill, or leaves. Her response may therefore be oriented to stability: reassurance, minimisation, and a request to continue. To the man this reads as refusal; to the wife it is management of an unmanageable risk.

**Her own history of the man's disclosures.** Many wives have been in the position of receiving disclosures that were later used against them — mentioned in arguments, denied, or followed by a decision the wife had not agreed to. A woman whose experience of her husband's disclosures includes subsequent injury will, entirely rationally, respond to the next one with caution.

### The Collision

Put the two positions together and the outcome is predictable, and it is worth presenting as a sequence because it explains how a marriage of two willing people arrives at twenty years of silence.

1. The man carries a difficulty, having no vocabulary and no expectation of help.
2. His capacity is reduced, and he becomes irritable, absent, or unavailable, without explanation.
3. The wife observes the change and cannot identify its cause. She infers a cause, frequently the wrong one: work, another woman, dissatisfaction with the household, or contempt for her.
4. The difficulty eventually becomes visible, or is disclosed in one of the four failed forms.
5. The wife responds to what she can see, which is an emergency, a demand, or a change in behaviour.
6. Each partner concludes something about the other: he concludes that she does not want to know; she concludes that he does not trust her, or that he has become somebody she does not know.
7. Each acts on the conclusion in a way that confirms the other's. He stops disclosing; she stops asking, and, in many cases, does more of the household's practical work as compensation, which means she now has less capacity to ask.
8. The equilibrium is established, and it is maintained for years, with both parties believing it was the other's choice.

The eighth step is what makes this chapter necessary. Neither partner has decided to live this way; both have made a series of rational responses to information that was incomplete.
