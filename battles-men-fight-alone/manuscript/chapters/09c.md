## 9.5 Asking for Assistance Without Losing Dignity

The moment at which a man must ask for help — from a relative, a friend, an employer, a bank, a cooperative, or a spouse — is the point at which the whole of this chapter converges. Men identify it as one of the most difficult acts of their lives, and the difficulty is not irrational: the act requires the abandonment of the self-presentation that has organised their position in the family and the community.

The purpose of this section is to make the act practicable. It has five parts.

### Deciding Whether to Ask

Not every shortfall requires assistance. Before asking, three questions establish whether assistance is genuinely required.

- **Is the difficulty temporary and bridgeable, or structural?** A bridgeable gap — a payment due before a receivable arrives, a medical cost in a bad month — can be addressed with a loan or a deferral. A structural gap, in which income does not cover obligations month after month, cannot be solved by assistance and requires a restructuring. Asking for the former when the latter is required produces a debt that makes the position worse.
- **Is the assistance the best available option?** Compare the cost of the assistance with the alternatives: renegotiating with the creditor, selling a productive asset, taking a lower-cost facility, or reducing the household standard. Assistance from a relative frequently carries a cost to the relationship that is higher than the cost of a commercial alternative.
- **What exactly is needed?** A precise figure and a purpose. Requests of the form *I need help with money* are difficult to grant and are frequently refused. *I need 240,000 naira before the fifteenth of March to prevent a judgment; I will repay it in six instalments of 40,000 beginning in April* is grantable.

### Deciding Whom to Ask

The choice of person matters more than the wording. Four criteria identify the right person.

**Capacity.** The person can actually help — many requests are made of relatives who have no capacity, which produces refusal and damage for no purpose.

**Discretion.** The person will not discuss the matter with others. This should be assessed before the request rather than discovered afterwards, and men are frequently wrong on this point; the safe test is whether the person has kept confidences before.

**The nature of the relationship.** A person with whom the man has an established pattern of reciprocity is a better prospect than one with whom the relationship is entirely one-directional in either direction.

**The absence of leverage.** Where possible, the person should not be a business associate, a competitor, a person in the man's chain of authority, or a relative whose assistance would establish an ongoing claim over the man's decisions.

### Making the Request

Six elements make a request credible, and their absence is the principal reason that requests are refused.

1. **The introduction.** *I am going to ask you for something, and I want to explain the position first.* Announcing the format prevents the two common failures: that the listener panics, or that the request appears as an ambush.
2. **The position.** The facts: what has happened, what is owed, what the resources are, and how the shortfall arose. Brief and without self-justification.
3. **The amount.** Specific, with the purpose stated.
4. **The plan.** How it will be repaid, from what income, and over what period, with dates.
5. **What you are doing.** The steps being taken independently: expenditure reduction, additional work, disposal of an asset, professional advice obtained. This is the element that most strongly determines the answer, because it establishes that the man is not transferring the problem.
6. **The concession.** What happens if the arrangement cannot be met — a stated fallback, such as a transfer of the asset securing the debt, a schedule renegotiation, or the involvement of a witness. Stating the fallback in advance is the strongest available evidence of good faith.

### Offering Something in Return

Assistance is more readily given, and the relationship is better preserved, when the request includes a reciprocal element. The reciprocal element need not be financial. Men have successfully offered: an agreed schedule with a written record and a witness; a defined service in return, within their competence; the transfer of an asset until the debt is cleared; the involvement of a third party to hold both parties to the arrangement; or instruction and mentorship of the lender's relation.

The offer of a written record, with a witness, is worth particular emphasis. It converts an informal request, which is emotionally expensive for both parties, into an arrangement with terms, and it removes the possibility of later disputes about what was agreed. Men report that producing a written schedule at the moment of request changed the character of the conversation entirely.

### Refusal, and What to Do With It

Refusals are common and should be expected. A refusal is not necessarily a verdict on the man: capacity, prior commitments, past experience, and the opinion of the lender's own spouse all influence the answer.

Two rules apply. **Do not argue.** A request that has been refused is closed, and pressing it damages the relationship while producing nothing. **Do not withdraw.** The most common consequence of a refusal is that the man stops speaking to the person altogether, which converts a financial matter into the loss of a relationship. The appropriate response is ordinary: thank the person for hearing it, and continue the relationship as before.

### The Last Resort: Public Awareness

For a minority of cases, the position becomes known without disclosure: a judgment, a repossession, a supplier's representative at the house, a child sent home from school. Where this occurs, three principles reduce the damage.

- **Get ahead of the narrative.** A brief, factual statement to the family and, where necessary, to the relevant community body, is considerably better than a rumour. *This is what happened, this is the position, this is the plan* — in that order.
- **Do not answer every question.** Men frequently respond to exposure by explaining themselves at length to everyone who asks, which produces further exposure. A single, consistent, three-sentence account is sufficient, and repetition of it ends the matter more quickly than disclosure.
- **Protect the children absolutely.** Whatever the position between adults, children should not be told the details, used as messengers, or left to hear the situation from others. Chapter 17 addresses this; the relevant point here is that the household's difficulty can be managed without the children carrying the arithmetic.

## 9.6 Rebuilding Financial Stability

The final section deals with recovery, and it is deliberately practical. It applies whether the man is at the beginning of a difficulty or several years into a concealed one.

### Stage One: The Position Paper

Produce a written statement of the position in five parts: income; obligations with amounts, rates, and dates; assets with realistic market values; the monthly arithmetic, showing whether the month closes and by how much it does not; and the number of months the position can be sustained unchanged. This document serves three purposes: it is the basis of any advice or assistance, it removes the uncertainty that drives the anxiety, and it is the document from which the plan is built.

### Stage Two: The Reduction

Set out the reductions, in three categories: **immediate** (expenditure that can stop this month without explanation — subscriptions, services, discretionary transport, the second vehicle where applicable); **negotiated** (obligations that can be renegotiated — rent, fees, credit terms, obligations to relatives, the standard of household expenditure); and **structural** (changes to the pattern of work, business, or living arrangements, which take longer and have larger effects).

Chapter 10 treats the negotiation with family members in detail, and Chapter 12 treats the reduction of work itself.

### Stage Three: The Creditor Plan

For each creditor: the amount, the rate, the proposed repayment, and the date of first communication. Prioritise by cost, protect secured assets where possible, and communicate before default rather than after. Where the position is severe, professional advice should be obtained before any communication, because an incorrect proposal can establish a legal position that is worse than the silence it replaced.

### Stage Four: The Rebuilding

Recovery is not only the reduction of obligations. It requires a rebuilding of capacity: income, skills, assets, and credit standing. Five elements recur in the accounts men give of successful recovery.

**Restore the productive base first.** Before any other objective, protect and rebuild the asset or activity that produces income.

**Reduce the cost base permanently.** Men who recover from serious financial difficulty almost always describe a permanent change in their standard of living that they came to prefer: fewer obligations, less display, and a lower level of recurring commitment.

**Build a buffer.** Once the immediate position is stable, establish a reserve sufficient for one to three months of obligations. This is the single measure that prevents the next difficulty from becoming a crisis, and men who have experienced a serious financial failure describe it as the change they most wished they had made earlier.

**Restore credit standing deliberately.** Where the household needs future access to credit, the process is deliberate and takes years: no missed payments, no new obligations taken on without necessity, and the systematic closure of accounts as they are redeemed.

**Change the reference group.** One of the more surprising findings in the accounts men give is the importance of reducing exposure to the comparison culture that produced the overspending. Men who recover frequently describe a change in the company they keep: fewer events attended, fewer contributions made, and a narrower circle in which a lower standard of living attracts no comment.

### A Final Word on the Household

Two notes complete this chapter. First, the rebuilding should be undertaken **with the household informed**, even where the disclosure comes late and painfully. The alternative — recovery conducted in further concealment — is the pattern that produces the second collapse. Second, the household should be given a role. Men frequently attempt recovery as a solitary administrative project, which reproduces the very pattern that caused the difficulty. Families recover better when they are told what is happening, given one or two specific tasks (reduced expenditure in a named category, delayed purchase of a named item, an agreement about requests for assistance), and informed of progress at intervals.
