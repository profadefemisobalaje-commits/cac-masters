## 32.1 Where the Requirement Comes From

The requirement has six sources, and they reinforce one another. Identifying the ones operating in the reader's case is the first practical step, because the correction differs by source.

### Source One: The Position

A position of authority requires decisions, and decisions are answers. The group's functioning depends on a person who will bring a matter to a conclusion, and the person assigned that function is the man and, in the case of a group of any size, the handful of people who occupy the positions. The requirement follows from the function and it is not a matter of culture: a group in which everybody defers the decision indefinitely will not function, which is why the man who has been given the function is under a genuine obligation to supply answers in the domain of his authority.

### Source Two: The Cultural Instruction About Men

The instruction is delivered in specific forms: that a man should not ask for directions; that a man should know how to do the thing; that a man who asks is not the man for the position; that a husband should be the one who knows the way; that a father should have the answer for his children. The instruction is documented in research on men's help-seeking, where it appears among the principal barriers: the belief that seeking help is a sign of inadequacy, the fear of being seen not to know, and the anticipation of a loss of standing.

The instruction's effect is not the elimination of the man's questions but their suppression. Men ask them elsewhere, anonymously, privately, of a search engine, of a stranger, of a practitioner who has an obligation of confidence. The information obtained privately is frequently accurate and it is not shared with the people who needed it, which is the practical cost.

### Source Three: The Household

A man's own household imposes the requirement in a specific form, and its instrument is the man's assumption about what his household needs from him. Men report, with high consistency, the belief that their spouse and children require certainty from them: that a father who says *I do not know how we will manage this* is abandoning his household, that a husband who admits fear is transferring the fear, and that the man's function is to hold the household's picture of the future steady.

The belief is partly accurate and it is generally applied too broadly. The material in Parts III and V establishes the distinction: the household requires the man's competence in the domains in which he has power, and it requires his honesty in the domains in which he does not. A household informed of the actual position makes better arrangements than a household holding an account the man has constructed, which is the finding of Chapters 18 and 20 in a different register.

### Source Four: The Previous Generation

The requirement is frequently inherited. The father who never explained, who never admitted ignorance, who never discussed a matter he did not control, who answered a question with a fact rather than with an account. The son of such a man frequently reproduces the form, because it is what he knows, and he reproduces it with the same consequences: the household's information is organised around what the man can supply.

### Source Five: The Man's Own Account of Himself

The requirement is, in many men, an identity arrangement rather than a role requirement: the man's account of himself includes being the one who knows, and the arrangement is carried because the alternative is a change in the account. The material of Chapter 24 applies. The specific markers: the man who is unable to say in a meeting that he does not know; the man who is unable to ask a practitioner to explain; the man who is uncomfortable when his household manages a matter without him; and the man who experiences the disclosure of an error as a loss of standing rather than as information.

### Source Six: The Rewards

The requirement is reinforced by its outcomes. Men who supply answers promptly are promoted, chosen, deferred to, and trusted, and the reinforcement is real. The difficulty is that the reinforcement attaches to the answer rather than to its accuracy, which means that in a domain where the man's knowledge has an edge, the arrangement is rewarded, and in the domain where it does not, the arrangement is still rewarded. The pattern is documented in the research on overconfidence in expert settings: the man's rapid answer is read as competence by people who cannot assess it.

::: keypoint
The requirement to have answers has six sources: the position's functional need for decisions; the cultural instruction that a man should know; the household's requirement as the man understands it; the inherited form from a father who never said *I do not know*; the man's own account of himself; and the rewards that attend a prompt answer irrespective of its accuracy. The suppression of the man's questions does not remove them; it relocates them to settings with an obligation of confidence and removes the information from the people who required it.
:::

## 32.2 What the Requirement Costs

The four costs are documented and they fall on four different parties, which is why the requirement is frequently described as a strength by the man's environment.

### Cost One: The Bluff

The direct cost, and the most consequential. The man who has the answer requirement and lacks the information supplies an answer, and the answer is composed of the material available to him. Its forms.

**The confidently wrong answer.** The man gives a specified figure, a route, a period, a name, a mechanism, or a legal position, and the information is not correct. Men in every sector describe having done it: the operating rule given to a young operator, the tax position stated to a client, the consequence explained to a patient's family, the timing given to a household, the diagnosis offered to a subordinate, the measurement stated on a building site.

**The answer that is concealed by its delivery.** The man's tone, certainty, and brevity carry the answer, and the hearer therefore cannot assess it. The mechanism is documented: the man's status in the setting determines the credence given to his statement, which is the reason the confident answer is the most dangerous form of the error.

**The answer that ends the enquiry.** The most costly form, and the one that is the least visible: the man's answer prevents the question from being asked of somebody who knows. Several men or a whole group then proceed on the basis of a statement that was supplied in order to close a matter rather than to inform it.

### Cost Two: The Arrested Learning

The requirement prevents the man's own development, and the mechanism is structural.

A man who cannot say *I do not know* cannot ask a question in public, so the matters he does not understand are not addressed in the settings in which they arise. He learns them, where he learns them, privately, after the event, and the learning is frequently too late to be of use. Over years, the arrangement produces a specific profile in men: wide practical competence in the domains in which the man has experience, and a substantial gap in the domains in which he does not, which persists for decades though it would have closed within months if the questions had been asked.

The pattern is documented in the research on men's help-seeking and it is described by men with a specific regret: *I spent eleven years pretending to understand the accounts before I asked somebody to explain the difference between two of the lines.*

### Cost Three: The Distorted Information

The requirement alters what arrives to the man, and the alteration is produced by his own conduct.

A man who must always have answers must also always be correct, and the second requirement is what changes the information. Subordinates learn what happens to a bearer of bad news; colleagues learn which matters can be raised; households learn which difficulties can be described. The organisation's information is thereby shaped by the man's intolerance, and the man is the last person to learn of the distortion because nobody informs him of it — the same asymmetry described in Chapter 31.1.

The specific consequence, and the reason this cost is the most expensive for the man's organisation: the man's position is at its most vulnerable in the domain of his own errors. Nobody raises the matter with him, the matter develops, and the correction arrives late or not at all.

### Cost Four: The Withdrawal of the People Around Him

The most consequential cost for the man's household and his closest relationships.

A man who cannot be uncertain is a man whose household cannot deliver uncertainty to him, and the material is therefore redirected. The spouse manages alone and says nothing; the children select their disclosures; the subordinate makes the decision without telling him; the friend does not bring his own trouble. The pattern produces the arrangement described in Chapters 17, 18, and 27: a man surrounded by people who no longer bring him anything, and who believes that nothing is being carried by anybody.

The specific and most frequently reported version in men's accounts is the man's late discovery of a matter his household had been managing for years: the debt, the illness, the child's difficulty, the marriage's condition, the relative's conduct. In each case the household's purpose was to protect him from the requirement, and the protection was accurate: the requirement was genuine, and the alternative the household saw was the man's distress, his anger, or a burden added to his load.

The four costs belong to the man and to the parties around him: the bluff falls on the people who act on his answer; the arrested learning falls on the man; the distorted information falls on the man's judgement and on the organisation; and the withdrawal falls on his household. This distribution is the strongest available argument for the correction, and it is the reason the chapter's material is not framed as a matter of the man's personal growth.
