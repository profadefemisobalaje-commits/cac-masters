## 2.6 Redefining Strength Beyond Endurance

The constructive work of this chapter is a revision of the concept of strength. The revision is not an abandonment of the traditional virtues; it is a correction of the list, because the list in current use is incomplete in a specific and damaging way.

### What the Current Definition Leaves Out

The current definition of strength measures a man by what he can carry alone. Its logic, stated plainly, is: the more a man can absorb without assistance or complaint, the stronger he is. Two consequences follow from that logic, and both are observable.

First, the definition rewards concealment, since a difficulty that nobody knows about is a difficulty carried alone. Second, the definition penalises the capacities that actually sustain a man over a working life: the capacity to identify what is happening to him, the capacity to obtain help, the capacity to maintain relationships in which he is known, and the capacity to keep himself functioning over decades rather than months.

A man on the current definition is therefore strong in an emergency and fragile over a lifetime. The pattern described in Chapter 12 — the man who cannot afford to break down — is produced by the current definition applied consistently.

### The Revised Definition

Strength, properly defined, is the **capacity to sustain responsibility**. The phrase is chosen because it is measurable and because it preserves everything of value in the traditional account.

A man who sustains responsibility over a working life requires six capacities, and the current definition recognises only the first two.

| Capacity | What it means | Is it recognised by the current script? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Endurance | Absorbing difficulty and continuing | Yes |
| Competence | Performing required tasks to an adequate standard | Yes |
| Articulacy | Describing his own state accurately when necessary | No |
| Help-seeking | Obtaining assistance before a difficulty becomes a crisis | No |
| Relational maintenance | Keeping relationships in which he is known | No |
| Self-maintenance | Protecting sleep, health, and recovery over decades | No |

Table: The six capacities that sustain responsibility, and the two recognised by the script in current use.

Each of the four unrecognised capacities has a documented effect.

**Articulacy** is the precondition of treatment, of negotiation, and of the diagnostic assessment of a problem. A man who can describe his condition accurately obtains help twice as fast as one who cannot, because the first conversation with a physician, an employer, or a lawyer is resolved by the description he supplies.

**Help-seeking** is the mechanism by which problems are solved at their smallest size. The man who asks the mechanic before the engine fails, the physician before the symptom becomes a condition, and the accountant before the debt becomes a liability pays a fraction of what the man who handles everything alone will pay.

**Relational maintenance** is the mechanism by which a man remains known, and it is the subject of Chapter 4. Its practical value is that relationships are the means by which information, assistance, and perspective reach a man who does not believe he needs them.

**Self-maintenance** is the mechanism by which a man is still functioning at sixty-five. Its components are ordinary and its neglect is the most common single cause of the health and career outcomes described in Part IX of this book.

### Adopting the Revised Definition

Four practical steps, in the sequence that men report as workable.

**Step One: re-describe three behaviours.** Take three things you currently describe in the language of failure — asking for help with a matter, taking leave, declining an obligation, attending a physician, leaving early to be with your children — and re-describe them accurately: as maintenance of the capacity to work, as the management of a risk, as the protection of the household's long-term position. The re-description is not a deception; it is an accurate account of the same act.

**Step Two: add one capacity per quarter.** Not all four. The order that men find easiest is articulacy first — which begins with the one-sentence daily writing in Chapter 3 — then self-maintenance, then help-seeking, then relational maintenance. Each capacity takes a quarter to establish as a practice.

**Step Three: demonstrate it in front of the people for whom it matters.** A son who sees his father ask a colleague for help, attend a clinic, or decline an obligation on grounds that he states openly has learned that these acts are compatible with being a man. This step has the largest effect of any measure in this chapter, because it interrupts the transmission described in Section 2.4.

**Step Four: hold the definition in conversation.** Where a man is told that he is being weak — by a relative, a colleague, a friend, or his own interior voice — the response is not a defence but a statement: *I am keeping myself able to carry what I am carrying.* The sentence is accurate, it is short, and it is remarkably effective, because it is difficult to argue against and because it re-states the position in the terms of strength rather than of need.

### What the Revision Does Not Mean

It does not mean that endurance is unnecessary; it means that endurance is not sufficient. It does not mean that a man should disclose everything to everybody; it means that he should have an arrangement in which a defined number of people know his actual position. It does not mean the abandonment of responsibility; the entire purpose of the revised definition is the capacity to sustain responsibility over the whole of a working life rather than to break under it at fifty-one. And it does not mean the absence of standards, which Chapter 8 addresses explicitly: the standard remains, and what changes is the man's relationship to his own failures against it.

## 2.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Never Off Duty

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in occupational medicine, family practice, and the research literature on male role strain.

**Ikenna is fifty-two, a civil engineer with a state ministry in the south-east, married with four children, and was until eighteen months ago the person his entire extended family called first.**

His situation at the age of fifty had six components, none of which any single person in his life was aware of in full.

He was the ministry's most senior engineer on the road programme, working between sixty and seventy-five hours weekly in the working season. He was responsible for the household's finances, which had been under strain for three years since his wife's tailoring business lost an institutional contract. He was married to Ngozi, whose mother had lived with them for four years since a stroke, requiring daily care that Ngozi provided and that he managed financially. He supported three relatives' school fees, in an arrangement established when his income was higher and never revised. He held the position of projects coordinator in his community's development union, an office that required attendance, contribution, and the management of disputes. And he had been, for two years, the person whom two cousins, an uncle, and a neighbour consulted on all major decisions, including two property matters that had been in professional mediation for years.

His own position was not disclosed to anybody. His colleagues knew about the hours. His wife knew about the household's finances. His cousins knew about the property matters. Nobody knew about all six, and nobody had been told that Ikenna had, three years earlier, begun experiencing chest tightness on exertion, and that he had twice woken at night with his heart beating heavily, and that he had decided that this was stress and that it would pass.

The events of the following year are worth setting out in the sequence they occurred, because the sequence illustrates the mechanism of this chapter.

In March, he was passed over for the ministry's road programme directorship. The position went to a colleague four years his junior. He was given no explanation and did not request one, on the grounds that requesting one would look like complaint.

In June, he held a meeting at which a property matter involving his uncle and a cousin's family was resolved in a manner that pleased neither. He was blamed by both, and spoke to neither for four months.

In August, his wife's business lost its second contract, and he did not tell her that the household's reserves were exhausted, since he had intended for the reserve to be a private matter and had been using it since April.

In November, he attended a family gathering at which a younger relative, meaning to be flattering, observed that he was "the one managing everything" and asked what the family would do without him. Ikenna's response was a joke, and his recollection of the evening is that he was unable to sleep at all afterwards.

In February, he collapsed at a site meeting. The episode was brief, was attributed to the heat, and required two days in hospital, where an ECG was performed, a diagnosis of hypertension made, and a referral to a cardiology clinic given. He did not attend the referral.

The turn came not through the collapse but through a physician's question. In April, Ikenna attended a different hospital for an unrelated matter — a minor eye condition — and the physician, whose examination included a blood pressure reading, asked him how long it had been since his last proper assessment. When Ikenna said that he had been referred in February and had not attended, the physician asked a further question: *What are you carrying at the moment that made you decide not to go?*

Ikenna's account of the following year is specific. He attended the cardiology clinic, was treated, and was given restrictions on the site work, which he initially disregarded and later observed after his physician arranged a joint conversation with him and his wife — the first occasion on which Ngozi heard any of the medical history. He wrote, at the physician's suggestion, a list of every obligation he was carrying, and it took two pages. He then, over about eight months, took four steps: he resigned the community union position; he renegotiated the school fees with one relative and reduced the arrangement for the other two with a defined date; he told the uncle and the cousin that he would no longer act as mediator in the property matter; and he told Ngozi the position of the household's finances in full.

His summary, two years later, was: *Nobody ever asked me to do all six. I was carrying the union because my father had, the fees because I had started them, and the property matters because I was the one who could be trusted. If you had asked me in February which of them I would drop, I would have said none. In fact I have dropped three and nobody has died. What has died is the idea that I am the only one who can do anything.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The six obligations were each legitimate and none was agreed as a package.** Every element was honourable: the road programme, the finances, the mother-in-law's care, the school fees, the union office, the mediations. The damage arose from the combination, which no single person had ever seen and which Ikenna himself had never totalled.

**The symptoms were recognised and dismissed.** Chest tightness on exertion and night-time episodes occurred for three years before the collapse, and the dismissal was not ignorance; Ikenna knew what the symptoms might indicate. The obstruction was the script: attending a clinic would have required an admission, and the admission would have had to be made in front of his household.

**The promotion was a rejection that could not be discussed.** The passing over was a substantial blow, and no conversation about it was possible in any of Ikenna's six domains: not at work, where it would look like complaint; not at home, where the household was under financial strain; not in the family, where his standing was at issue. The absence of a channel is the material of Section 2.4.

**The trigger was a question, not a crisis.** The physician asked what he was carrying that made him decide not to attend. The question is the intervention, and its form is worth noting: it presumed the existence of a load and asked only about its contents. Men respond to that form of question with disclosure far more frequently than to a question about how they are feeling.

**The reduction of obligations did not produce the disaster that the script predicted.** Four obligations removed, with no collapse of the family, the household, or the man's standing. The prediction that the household would fail without his universal function was tested and found to be false, and Ikenna's own statement — that what died was the idea that he was the only one who could do anything — is the practical content of the revised definition in Section 2.6.
