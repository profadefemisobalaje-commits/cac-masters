### Part Two: The Safety Plan (Thirty Minutes, Written and Kept)

Write the plan in your own hand, or type it and keep it where you will see it. It should be one page and it should be specific. The seven steps:

1. **My warning signs.** What my pattern is when the risk is rising. (Be specific: the hour, the belief, the impulse, the place, the date, the person.)
2. **My internal measures.** What has worked before, and for how long.
3. **My people.** Two names and numbers, and the sentence I will use: *I am not in a good state and I need to be with somebody.*
4. **My places to avoid.** The location, the route, the setting, the argument, the person.
5. **My means.** What will be done about the medication, the firearm, the keys, the alcohol, the vehicle, the location — and who will hold each.
6. **My professionals.** The general practitioner, the practitioner, the crisis line, the emergency department, with numbers.
7. **My reasons.** Written in your own terms and specific: the people, the obligations, the account of what your absence would do. This step should be written when you are able to reason, and kept for the times when you are not.

Then record the date on which you will give a copy — to your spouse, a friend, a practitioner — and do it. A plan that nobody else holds is a plan that cannot be used.

## 34.8 Common Questions Men Ask

**"My silence protects my household. Why would I remove that protection?"**

Because in a large proportion of cases the protection has stopped operating and the household is being affected without the information it requires. The test is Section 34.1's first criterion: state what the silence protects, and state whether the protection is still in force. Where the household has noticed the man's temper, withdrawal, drinking, or absence and has constructed an account of it, the silence is no longer protecting anything and the household is making its arrangements on an incorrect basis.

**"I cannot tell my wife. She will not survive this."**

The assessment of another person's capacity is frequently inaccurate, and the case material on this judgement is consistent: households that are believed to be unable to hold a position are frequently the parties who address it most competently once they have the information. The professional route exists where the man's assessment is correct, and the household's entitlement to the fact — what is being carried and what it changes — is separate from the household's receipt of the content.

**"If I disclose at work, I will lose the position."**

The risk is frequently real, and the handling differs by category. Where the material is a regulated matter — a professional registration, a security clearance, a driving licence, a position with a specific health requirement — the professional advice should be obtained before the disclosure, and the routes are frequently narrower than the man assumes. Where the material is not regulated, the disclosure to the employer is generally not required, and the disclosure to the household and to a professional arrangement should be conducted independently of it.

**"I have been carrying it for thirty years. Is there any point?"**

The evidence and the case material are consistent: men conduct their first disclosure of material of this age and report the change in its operation. The specific finding concerns the debt's interest rather than its principal: the matter itself does not change with the delay, and what has accumulated is the arrangement around it — the account held in private, the consequences carried by the household, the man's own management. The professional arrangement is generally required for material of this duration, since a thirty-year silence is not ordinarily opened in a single conversation with a household.

**"I told one person and nothing changed."**

Then the arrangement was not completed. The disclosure performs one function, which is that the material is known; the remaining functions require the arrangements of Chapter 27.5 — the professional relationship, the group, the household's arrangements, and the specific repairs where a repair is available. The disclosure is the first step and it is not the work.

**"What if the person I told uses it against me?"**

The outcome should be recorded as information about that person, in the form of Chapter 26.5, and the next arrangement should be constructed with a recipient whose confidentiality is an obligation rather than a favour. The professional route is the general answer to this concern, and the case material is unambiguous on its value: the man whose material is held by a practitioner under an obligation of confidence has the arrangement that does not depend on anybody's goodwill.

**"I am worried about a man I know. What should I do?"**

Read Section 34.5's material for somebody else and follow it: ask directly; do not leave him alone with the material; help him make the contact rather than advising him to make one; and do not keep the confidence where his life is at risk. Then read Chapter 26's material and remain present over the following months, since the arrangement's continuation is the part that most frequently fails.

::: summary
**Chapter 34 in brief.** Silence has a range in which it functions, and past the edge of that range it produces the outcomes it was intended to prevent. Three criteria establish the edge: the silence has stopped performing its function, so that the protection it was supposed to supply is no longer in force; the material is operating somewhere other than in a decided arrangement, and the man can identify where; and the maintenance of the silence has become a substantial activity in the man's life, so that the material is carrying him rather than the reverse.

Unspoken material operates through three documented mechanisms. Sustained suppression does not reduce the physiological response; it removes its expression, and the body continues to carry the load. The maintenance of a concealment consumes attention continuously, producing a measurable reduction in the man's working memory, performance, and patience. And the material alters the man's conduct through avoidance, compensating behaviour, reduced ambition, severity at home, and the four forms of damaged judgement. The cost of addressing the material rises with delay, in three specific ways: the material grows, the account becomes more difficult to deliver because it now includes the reason for the silence, and the audience's established account of the period must be corrected.

Five silences carry a specific risk: the silence about a risk to the man's own life, which is the most urgent and requires a professional assessment rather than private deliberation, since the belief that nothing can change is a symptom of the condition rather than a fact about the man's circumstances; the silence about an addiction; the silence about violence, in both directions; the silence about a medical condition; and the silence about a financial collapse.

A checklist of indicators is supplied for the man himself, for his household, for his organisation, and for the practitioner, and any indicator from the first group warrants immediate action. For that group, the chapter supplies the routes — the crisis line, the emergency department, the urgent practitioner — the answers to the four obstacles that stop men from using them, the seven-step safety plan, and the guidance for somebody who is concerned about another man: ask directly, do not leave him alone with the material, help him make the contact, and do not keep the confidence where his life is at risk.

For the ordinary case, the disclosure is an arrangement rather than an act, and it is conducted through four decisions — its purpose, its recipient and the recipient's capacity, the smallest true version, and what the man requires — with the six obstacles identified and handled, and with a statement in four parts: the material in one or two sentences, the reason for the silence stated without defence, the consequences the recipient has already observed and attributed elsewhere, and what happens next. Where the man cannot deliver the content, the professional arrangement is the required step and the household's entitlement is to the fact.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Apply the three criteria of Section 34.1. Which of them is satisfied in your case, and what is the evidence?
2. Record where your material is currently operating: the body, the sleep, the temper, the drinking, the risk, the attention, or the avoided settings. What has it produced in the last two years?
3. Which of the five dangerous silences applies to you? What is the date on which it began, and what is its cost so far in money, health, and relationships?
4. Complete the safety plan. Write Step 7 now, in your own terms, and record the person who will hold a copy.
5. Write the four parts of the disclosure statement for your material. Which part is the one you cannot yet say, and what does that indicate about the recipient or the content?
6. Complete the four decisions. What is the smallest true version of the material, and what is the one sentence in which you will state it?
7. Where a household member has constructed an account of your conduct — the arrears, the banking delay, the work pressure — what is their account, and what would change in it if they were told the material?

## Further Reading

- **On emotional suppression:** the research on expressive suppression and on the effects of withholding the expression of feeling, including the findings on physiological load, on the persistence of the response, and on the social and cognitive consequences of habitual suppression.
- **On concealment and cognitive load:** the literature on secrecy and concealment, including the findings on the effects of holding concealed material on attention and working memory, and the evidence that disclosure reduces the load.
- **On men's suicide risk:** the epidemiological and clinical literature on suicide in men, including the findings on methods, on the lower rate of help-seeking and of reported depression among men, on the role of isolation and loss, and on the interventions that are associated with reduced risk.
- **On means safety and safety planning:** the evidence base for safety planning and for means restriction in suicide prevention, including the findings on which measures are associated with reduced risk and the recommended content of a safety plan.
- **On disclosure and stigma:** the research on disclosure of stigmatised conditions, including the findings on the benefits and the risks of disclosure, the factors that determine the outcome, and the evidence on the outcomes of disclosure in the workplace.
- **Practical resources:** crisis lines and emergency services; general practice and mental health services; domestic violence, child protection, and sexual assault services; addiction and gambling treatment services; debt counselling and insolvency advice; and the material in Appendices Q, M, D, and R.
