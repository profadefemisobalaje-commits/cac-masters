## 3.8 Practical Exercise: Building the Vocabulary

This exercise is cumulative. It takes fifteen minutes a day for twenty-one days, and it is the single most commonly recommended starting point for men who have decided to work on emotional life. Nothing in it requires another person, and no part of it requires that anybody else be told.

### The Daily Practice

Each day, complete four lines in a notebook or a note on your phone. Keep the notebook private and keep it in a place that nobody else uses.

**Line 1 — Sensation.** *In my body I notice ______________________.* (jaw, throat, chest, stomach, hands, shoulders, breathing, restlessness, heaviness, numbness)

**Line 2 — Intensity.** *On a scale of one to ten, it is ______.*

**Line 3 — Situation.** *It appeared or changed when ______________________.* (an event, a conversation, an hour of the night, a person's name, a particular room)

**Line 4 — One honest sentence.** *What I would say if I could say one true thing is ______________________.*

An example, taken from the practice of a man of forty-nine who works as a logistics supervisor:

> *In my body I notice that my jaw is tight and my chest feels compressed.*
> *On a scale of one to ten, it is six.*
> *It appeared when my wife said that her sister would be staying for a month.*
> *What I would say if I could say one true thing is that I am frightened about money and I do not want another person in the house.*

The four lines take about three minutes. The remaining twelve minutes are the part that matters: **read the previous weeks' entries.** Patterns become visible within a fortnight. Most men discover that two or three situations account for the majority of the intensity, and that these situations have very little to do with one another on the surface. That is the identification of the actual load.

### The Weekly Review

Once a week — the same day each week — write a short paragraph in response to four questions.

1. **What recurred?** Which situations produced the highest readings?
2. **What did I do with it?** Did I speak, write, work, drink, sleep, or ignore it?
3. **What did it cost me?** Sleep, patience, time, money, or peace?
4. **What is the smallest true statement I could make about the largest item?**

### Working With the Results

After twenty-one days, select the three highest-intensity items and do the following, in order.

**Rank them by weight, not by drama.** The item that appears most often is not always the heaviest. The item that consumes attention, disturbs sleep, and shapes decisions is the heaviest, even if its subject appears modest.

**Choose one for the first conversation.** Not all three. One. Chapter 35 explains how to ask for help, Appendix E provides scripts for the conversation itself, and Appendix O covers the case in which the conversation must include a boundary.

**Write the first sentence before you say it.** Men report that the first sentence is the hardest and that having it written makes an approach possible. The most useful formula is short and factual: *I have something I want to talk about, and I would like to do it properly, so let us set aside an hour.*

### A Note on Stopping

If at any stage this exercise produces distress that is not manageable — persistent insomnia, waking with dread, intrusive memories, a rise in drinking, or any thought of self-harm — stop and consult a professional. Appendix B sets out the recognised signs that require prompt consultation, and Appendix Q explains how to make contact. Stopping is not failure. It is the correct response to information.

## 3.9 A Word to Partners, Children, and Friends

This chapter is written for men, but a short section addressed to those close to them will be useful, not least because men frequently hand the book to somebody else to explain themselves.

**Do not require tears as evidence.** The absence of crying is not evidence of the absence of feeling, and asking a man why he is not weeping is unlikely to help him weep. What helps is presence without demands, and time without an agenda.

**Ask specific questions rather than general ones.** *How are you?* is answered automatically. *You have been quiet since your brother's funeral — what has that been like?* cannot be answered automatically, because it contains an observation and an invitation.

**Expect the first version to be incomplete.** A man who has not described his interior life for two decades will not describe it accurately the first time, and often minimises it. The purpose of the first conversation is not to obtain the whole truth. It is to establish that the subject can be raised at all.

**Do not take charge of his feelings.** A helpful response to disclosure is acknowledgement: *I am glad you told me. That is a lot to carry.* An unhelpful response, offered with the best intentions, is instruction: *You should have told me earlier; you must see a doctor at once; I will book it for you.* Instruction is the most common form of help offered to men and the least likely to be accepted.

**Do not mistake composure for absence of feeling.** The most frequent error made by those close to men is to assume that a calm surface indicates a settled interior. Men who have suppressed feeling for decades are frequently at their most distressed when they are at their most orderly, which is why the occasions on which a family is most shocked by a man's collapse are so often the occasions he had appeared to be bearing best. The signals to attend to are changes rather than states: the man who has stopped making a particular joke, who no longer plays football on Sundays, who has become unusually busy, or who has taken to sitting in the car after arriving home.

**Watch for the substitute signals.** Withdrawal from associations, reduced tolerance for noise, a fall in appetite, sleeping badly, sitting alone in a vehicle or workshop: these are the male forms of grief, and reading them is more useful than waiting for language that may not come for weeks.
