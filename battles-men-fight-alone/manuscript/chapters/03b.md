## 3.3 The Difference Between Self-Control and Emotional Avoidance

The distinction in this section is, in practical terms, the most useful in the chapter. Men routinely defend their emotional habits by invoking self-control, and the defence is sometimes valid. The problem is that self-control and avoidance look similar from the outside and are entirely different from the inside, and the difference determines whether a man will eventually cope or eventually collapse.

### The Distinguishing Features

Self-control is the capacity to feel what you feel and choose what you do with it. It is a competence, it is learned, and it is a genuine strength. Avoidance is the attempt to prevent the feeling from registering in the first place. It is not a competence; it is a strategy for not knowing, and it produces a specific set of downstream effects.

![Self-control and emotional avoidance, distinguished. The two look similar from outside a man; they are entirely different inside him, and they produce opposite long-run outcomes.](/figures/fig-03-01.png){width=94%}

Figure 3.3 sets out the two side by side. Five differences do the work.

**Awareness.** Self-control requires awareness: a man knows he is angry, frightened, or grieving. Avoidance requires the opposite. If you do not know what you felt at your father's funeral, self-control was not at work.

**Choice about expression.** Under self-control, the delay in expression is a decision with a date attached: I will not raise this now; I will bring it up on Saturday. Under avoidance, there is no plan, because the emotion has been ruled out of the agenda altogether. If the conversation you deferred two years ago has still not happened and you have no intention of holding it, that is not restraint.

**Effect on the body.** Self-control involves noticing the body's signals and deciding what to do about them. Avoidance replaces the signal with something else — tension, busyness, alcohol, activity — and the replacement is usually invisible to the man.

**Discharge.** Self-control requires that the emotion be processed at some point: spoken about, written about, wept over, prayed over, or otherwise completed. Avoidance does not complete anything; it defers, and deferral accumulates.

**Others' experience of you.** People who live with a man exercising self-control experience honesty: *I am angry about this, and I will not discuss it tonight.* People who live with a man in avoidance experience absence: a person who is present, pleasant, and nowhere.

### A Working Scale

It is more useful to think of a scale than a binary.

| Position on the scale | Description | Typical indicator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skilled self-control | Feels fully, names it accurately, chooses expression deliberately, processes afterwards | Can say what he is feeling and when he will deal with it |
| Developing self-control | Feels it, names it imperfectly, eventually deals with it | Needs prompting; often speaks after a delay |
| Partial avoidance | Feels it, suppresses expression, does not process it | Becomes tense, sleeps poorly, forgets conversations |
| Substantial avoidance | Does not reliably know what he feels; substitutes tiredness, busyness, or anger | "I'm not angry." "I'm just tired." |
| Global avoidance | Cannot report emotional states at all; experiences only physical or situational facts | "There is nothing wrong. Work is difficult. That is all." |

Table: The self-control scale. Most men will locate themselves in a different position for different areas of life — skilled in one domain, globally avoidant in another.

The value of the scale is that it gives the reader a way to assess himself without accusation. A man who is at the second or third position has not failed; he has simply never been taught the skill, and he can learn it at almost any age.

### The Argument Against the Common Defence

Men frequently defend avoidance with three arguments, and each deserves a direct answer.

**"Someone has to stay functional."** True, and it is exactly why self-control is more valuable than avoidance. A man in avoidance is functional until he is not, and the timing of the break is outside his control. A man who exercises self-control remains functional deliberately and knows when he needs recovery.

**"It doesn't help to talk about it."** Sometimes true in the short term. But notice what the argument concedes: that talking is the only alternative to suppression. A man may discharge emotion through writing, physical work, exercise, structured reflection, or a single conversation with one person. The choice is not between silence and a confession; it is between processing and storing.

**"There is nobody I could talk to."** Also frequently true, and it is a statement about the man's social position rather than about his emotional policy. Appendices J and O, and Chapters 27 and 35, address it directly.

### The Cost of Not Being Read

There is one more consequence of suppression that deserves attention, because it operates through other people and is therefore rarely attributed to its cause.

Human beings read one another continuously and largely unconsciously. A colleague notices a change in the pace of a man's speech; a wife notices that he has stopped making a particular joke; a friend notices that he has begun to answer quickly, in short sentences, as though closing a door. These readings are the basis of ordinary human support, and they operate on signals that suppression removes. A man who is holding a difficult state entirely inside himself produces no readable signal, or produces a misleading one: concentration, tiredness, preoccupation with work.

The consequence is a specific and painful experience which many men report without connecting it to the subject of this chapter: the experience of going through the worst period of one's life while everyone around behaves as though nothing is happening. The man concludes from this that nobody cares, that his situation is invisible, or that the people around him are indifferent. In most cases the more accurate account is that the information was never supplied.

It follows that the two most practical acts available to a man in difficulty are also the two that feel most costly: to produce a signal, and to name it. A signal may take any number of forms. It can be a sentence: *I have had a difficult month and I do not want to talk about it yet, but I did not want you to think everything is normal.* It can be a change of arrangement: declining the meeting, taking the leave, or asking the brother to handle the family matter this time. It can be a direct request: *I would like you to check on me once a week.*

None of these acts requires the man to disclose the content of his difficulty. All of them require him to allow the fact of it to be known. That is a smaller step than it appears, and it is frequently the step that converts a private crisis into a shared problem with an available solution. A problem that is shared is not necessarily easier, but it is no longer invisible, and invisibility is the feature that makes the male pattern of distress so dangerous.

## 3.4 Crying Without Shame

No chapter on this subject can avoid the central image. Crying is the most heavily regulated expression of emotion in men, and the regulation produces a durable inability in a large number of adult men — with measurable consequences.

### What Crying Does

Tears of emotional release are a physiological event with observable properties: they differ in composition from the tears produced by irritants such as onion vapour, and researchers have linked emotional crying to recovery in the minutes and hours afterwards. Beyond the physiology, the psychological functions are well described: crying signals to others that the person requires support; it provides a discharge that reduces the intensity of a state; and, in human development, it is the mechanism through which an infant first acquires comfort from a caregiver. The capacity to cry, and to be comforted after crying, is one of the earliest forms of what later becomes the capacity to be supported by other people.

A man who has lost the ability to cry has not simply lost an emotion. He has lost a route to being comforted, and a route to completing a state that is otherwise completed by no other mechanism.

### The Social Calculation

The reason men do not cry is usually not physiological but social, and it can be stated precisely. Adult men report a consistent set of expected costs: loss of respect, loss of authority, uncertainty of others' reactions, and the fear that showing emotion will be read as a permanent change in how they are regarded. In private, many also report a specific fear that is rarely articulated: that if they begin to cry, they will not stop. This fear is worth naming, because it is inaccurate in an important way. Crying that is properly expressed follows a course: it rises, it lasts for a period measured in minutes, and it subsides into a calm that is noticeably different from the state before. The fear of the flood is the fear of accumulated material, and it is usually evidence that something has been stored for a very long time.

### Crying and Men: A Practical Position

This book takes the following position, and it is worth stating plainly because readers will disagree with parts of it.

Crying is not a requirement of healthy masculinity, and no man should be told that he must weep in order to be well. Many emotionally healthy men cry rarely, and some never do. Crying is one channel among several — and there is nothing wrong with a man whose emotional processing happens in speech, writing, work, prayer, or exercise.

But the situation of the man who *cannot* cry, even when he wants to, even when the loss is enormous, even when he is alone, is different. An inability to cry that persists across decades is not a preference. It is a restriction, and it usually indicates that a great deal has been stored. When such a man eventually does cry — and the occasion is frequently something small: a song, a kindness from a stranger, a child's question — it is not unusual for the experience to be described later with words like *the floor moved*.

For the man wishing to restore the capacity, three practical notes apply. First, it usually returns gradually, and in private. Second, the most reliable conditions are physical tiredness, darkness, music, memories of the person concerned, and above all solitude without a deadline. Third, and most importantly, tears commonly arrive when a man is speaking the truth to someone he trusts, and many men experience the first episode in a counselling room, at a graveside with a single companion, or in a conversation with a brother at two o'clock in the morning. What such men report afterwards is consistent and worth recording here, because it contradicts the fear that keeps the prohibition in place: they do not report humiliation. They report relief, and, frequently, surprise at having been able to do it at all.

::: keypoint
The question is not whether a man cries. It is whether he can, when the occasion requires it, in whatever private or trusted setting he chooses — and whether he would despise himself afterwards if he did.
:::

## 3.5 Unprocessed Grief and Emotional Exhaustion

Suppression of emotion over years produces two distinct adult conditions that are frequently confused with one another, and that require different responses. It is worth separating them carefully, because the chapter deals with both and the reader will recognise one more strongly than the other.

### Unprocessed Grief

Unprocessed grief is a specific and well-recognised phenomenon: a loss that has not been emotionally assimilated. The signature of unprocessed grief is delay. The man functioned at the time of the loss, organised what needed organising, supported those around him, and then continued. The emotional response did not arrive. It may arrive years later, triggered by an anniversary, a photograph, an unexpected encounter, or a second loss that combines with the first.

Men with unprocessed grief report a characteristic cluster. They may be unable to think about the person for long. They may avoid the grave, the family house, or a particular piece of music. They may be unable to speak the person's name in ordinary conversation. They may have dreams of the person, sometimes recurrent and vividly real. They may feel a persistent and inexplicable flatness that they attribute to age. They may notice that grief in other people — at funerals, on the news — affects them more than it should, or less than it should.

Unprocessed grief is not weakness or disrespect. In many men it is the direct consequence of having been assigned the role of the strong one at the moment of loss, often by a family that needed him to be strong. Chapter 30 deals with it in full; this chapter simply identifies it as one of the two conditions produced by long suppression.

### Emotional Exhaustion

Emotional exhaustion is a different condition. It is the state produced by prolonged emotional labour without recovery, and it is characterised by depletion rather than by a specific unassimilated loss. The man is not grieving something in particular. He is simply empty.

| Feature | Unprocessed grief | Emotional exhaustion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | A specific loss that was not assimilated | Prolonged emotional labour without recovery |
| Experience | Waves, dreams, avoidance of reminders, numbness | Constant flatness, low tolerance, no reserve |
| Triggers | Anniversaries, places, songs, second losses | Additional demands of any kind, however small |
| Typical presentation | Withdrawal from the subject of the loss | Irritability, indifference, difficulty starting anything |
| Response | Structured grief work; sometimes professional help | Reduction of load, restoration of sleep, boundaries, support |

Table: Distinguishing the two conditions. Readers commonly have both, and the treatment order matters: reduce the load first, then work on the grief.

The practical significance of this table is that a man who is emotionally exhausted should not begin by opening his largest wound. The first step is to reduce the load and restore the basic functions — sleep, food, structure, and at least one supportive relationship — and only then to undertake the work of grief. Appendix C is a thirty-day plan designed for precisely this order of operations.

A further distinction is worth recording, because it determines what kind of help is required. Emotional exhaustion is frequently accompanied by a specific and damaging belief: that the man has become a different person — colder, harder, less affectionate than he used to be. He has not. He has become a depleted person, and depletion produces exactly the symptoms he has noticed: low tolerance, low warmth, low initiative, and a reduced capacity to be interested in anybody. Restoration of this kind is not primarily emotional. It is physical and structural: sleep, food, activity, the removal of at least one continuing obligation, and the reintroduction of one thing that used to give pleasure. Men who begin their recovery with an attempt to feel more affectionate, without restoring sleep and reducing load, frequently conclude that they have become irreversibly cold, and they abandon the attempt. Men who restore the foundations first commonly find that the warmth returns as the depletion lifts, and that the fear of having changed permanently was itself a symptom of the exhaustion.
