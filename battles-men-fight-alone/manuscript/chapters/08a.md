# The War Against Self-Doubt {.chapter num="8" short="The War Against Self-Doubt"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the experience of chronic inadequacy that persists despite objective achievement, and explain why it is so common among capable men.
2. Explain impostor feelings — the persistent expectation of exposure — and distinguish them from accurate self-assessment and from ordinary humility.
3. Trace the contribution of childhood criticism, comparison, and family expectation to adult self-doubt, and describe how such material is carried into professional life.
4. Identify the patterns of negative internal dialogue, recognise whose voice is speaking, and apply methods for examining and revising those statements.
5. Use the methods set out in the chapter and in Appendix H to build a realistic confidence that is grounded in evidence rather than in reassurance.
6. Work towards a healthier self-concept that neither inflates nor diminishes the man who holds it.

## Introduction

There is a particular and uncomfortable kind of experience that many capable men recognise immediately when it is described, and almost never mention otherwise. It is the experience of being good at what you do and not believing it.

The man in question has held a responsible position for years. He has delivered results that other people could not have delivered. He has been promoted, referred to, praised, and depended upon. His professional judgement is sought. And privately, in the intervals of the day — in the car, in the shower, at the moment before sleep — he carries a set of beliefs about himself that contradict every piece of external evidence: that he has been fortunate, that the work was easier than it looked, that he is about to be asked a question he cannot answer, that the whole edifice is held up by a series of small improvisations, and that at some point somebody will notice.

This is the war against self-doubt, and it is fought privately, without allies, by men who frequently appear to be the most composed people in the room. It is not the same as modesty, which is a virtue, or as accurate self-assessment, which is a competence. It is a systematic error in the estimation of oneself, and it has consequences: it produces overwork, avoidance, refusal of opportunity, difficulty in resting, a lifelong inability to enjoy achievement, and, in a proportion of cases, a professional collapse produced not by incapacity but by fear.

This chapter describes the condition, traces where it comes from, and sets out what is known to help.

## 8.1 Feeling Inadequate Despite Achievement

The first task is to establish the nature of the pattern, because the men who experience it usually believe that they are the only ones, and that their private verdict about themselves is accurate while everybody else has been deceived.

### The Structure of the Experience

Four features define the presentation.

**A persistent gap between achievement and self-estimate.** The man's record and his opinion of himself are not merely different; they are systematically opposed. This is not a matter of mood or of a bad week. It is a standing arrangement, present in good periods and bad.

**Discounting of evidence.** Achievements are attributed to factors other than ability: favourable circumstances, the difficulty being lower than it appeared, the support of others, the absence of real competition, or luck. The attributions are not absurd — every achievement does involve circumstances and help — but they are applied asymmetrically. When something goes wrong, the cause is internal (*my fault, my limitation*); when something goes right, the cause is external (*favourable circumstances*). This asymmetry is one of the best-documented patterns in the psychology of achievement, and it is the engine of the whole condition.

**Anticipation of exposure.** The man expects that his actual capacity will be discovered. The discovery is imagined in specific forms: the question he cannot answer, the task he cannot perform, the mistake that will be found, the challenger who will displace him. The anticipation is continuous and produces a low-level physical vigilance described in Chapter 5.

**A prohibition on enjoyment.** Success does not yield satisfaction. It yields relief, followed immediately by the next requirement. Men in this pattern frequently report that they cannot recall a period of genuine professional contentment, and the inability is not a memory problem; there has been no period to remember.

### Why Capable Men Are Particularly Affected

One of the paradoxes of the subject is that self-doubt is at least as common among the highly capable as among those of modest attainment, and in some settings it is more common. Four reasons explain this.

**The standard rises with the position.** A man operating at a higher level is surrounded by more capable people, engages with more difficult problems, and confronts the boundary of his competence more frequently than a man in a simpler role. He therefore encounters evidence of his own limits daily, while the evidence of his own competence is old and familiar and has been discounted long ago.

**Errors become more consequential and more visible.** At a senior level, mistakes are larger, they affect more people, and they are more likely to be discussed. The experience of making consequential errors is experienced as confirmation of inadequacy rather than as the ordinary condition of responsibility.

**Feedback becomes rarer and less candid.** Junior staff receive correction, coaching, and grading. Senior men receive deference, agreement, and silence. A man who no longer receives honest feedback loses his calibration, and in the absence of information the mind supplies its own estimates, which are rarely generous.

**Responsibility outruns authority and knowledge.** Most senior positions require a person to take decisions of a kind for which nobody has been fully prepared, with incomplete information, under time pressure, in areas beyond his expertise, and to be accountable for consequences that he cannot fully control. This is not a perception; it is the actual structure of senior work. It produces a genuine and permanent uncertainty that a man misinterprets as evidence that he is a fraud.

### The Distinction from Accurate Self-Assessment

It is essential to separate this pattern from a more realistic attitude, which is not a disorder and should not be discouraged.

Accurate self-assessment includes knowing what one does well, knowing what one does badly, recognising the limits of one's knowledge, and being able to say so. A competent professional standard is full of statements of the form *I do not know, and I will find out.* Such a man is not suffering from self-doubt. He is displaying the intellectual humility described in Chapter 32.

The distinguishing marks of the problematic condition are the asymmetry (errors attributed to the self, successes to circumstance), the permanence (the verdict does not change with evidence), and the anticipated exposure (the belief that discovery is coming).

Figure 8.1 sets out the four layers in which men ordinarily describe themselves. The difficulties produced by self-doubt arise from the collapse of the outer layers into the innermost one: a man who cannot distinguish his role from his identity, or his reputation from his worth, will experience every professional setback as a personal verdict.

## 8.2 Impostor Feelings and Insecurity

The pattern described above is commonly called the impostor experience: the persistent sense of being less capable than one's position implies, accompanied by the fear of being found out. The description is useful because it has been widely studied, and three findings of that research matter practically.

**It is very common, including among high achievers.** Studies across professions — medicine, academia, engineering, business, the arts — consistently find that a large proportion of successful people report the experience, and that it is not correlated with actual incompetence. A man in this position should take a specific and factual reassurance from this literature: the experience is evidence about the human mind and about particular social environments, not about his ability.

**It does not improve with achievement.** The pattern usually intensifies with seniority, for the reasons set out in Section 8.1: more visible errors, rarer honest feedback, wider responsibility, and a reference group of more capable people.

**It is associated with overwork and with avoidance of opportunity.** Two costly responses are common. Men in the pattern work excessive hours, checking, preparing, and rehearsing, because thoroughness is the only available defence. And they decline or fail to pursue opportunities that would expose them to assessment — a promotion with a probationary period, a public presentation, a competitive examination, a business expansion — because the risk of exposure surpasses the value of the advancement. The second behaviour is the more damaging, and it is the one that most frequently produces the outcome the man fears, in the form of a career that plateaus for reasons that are internal rather than external.

### Insecurity and the Male Presentation

The word *insecurity* is used loosely in ordinary speech and deserves precision. Insecurity is not the presence of uncertainty about oneself; it is the presence of uncertainty combined with the absence of any capacity to tolerate it. The secure man does not know whether he will succeed; he can proceed anyway. The insecure man does not know whether he will succeed, and he cannot proceed without resolving the uncertainty — through reassurance, through control, through preparation, or through the avoidance of the situation entirely.

The male presentation of insecurity is recognisable and frequently misunderstood by those around the man.

| Behaviour | What it looks like to others | What it frequently is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overwork and long hours | Dedication | A defence against being found out |
| Refusal to delegate | High standards | Fear that others' work will expose the standard |
| Reluctance to speak in meetings | Reserve or authority | Fear of a question that cannot be answered |
| Defensiveness about correction | Pride | Exposure of the material the man most fears |
| Control of details | Thoroughness | Anxiety management |
| Discomfort with praise | Modesty | Disbelief, and the discomfort of a claim that cannot be accepted |
| Avoidance of assessment | Confidence in existing achievements | Fear of an unmanageable verdict |
| Aggressiveness about status | Ambition | A permanent attempt to establish a position that never feels secure |

Table: The male presentation of insecurity. Each item is a behaviour that is usually read as evidence of strength.

The table has a practical purpose: it tells the reader that the behaviours he may have cultivated as defences are legible to careful observers. Colleagues at senior levels frequently perceive the pattern, even when they cannot name it.

### What Does Not Help

Three common pieces of advice are ineffective, and a man should be warned about them.

**Reassurance.** Being told that one is capable produces relief that lasts hours. The belief is not corrected by testimony from others, because the man discounts the testimony in the way described in Section 8.1: *she says that because she does not know the details.*

**Correction by achievement.** The pattern does not respond to further success. Each new success is discounted, and the standard is revised upward.

**Instruction to lower one's standards.** Men in this pattern frequently hear that they should be less demanding of themselves. The advice misconceives the difficulty: the standard is not the problem, and the man's adherence to a high standard is one of the things that makes him effective. What requires correction is the accounting — the attribution of successes to luck and failures to the self.

### What Does Help

What helps is a change in the method by which a man judges himself. Four practices are developed in Section 8.5 and in Appendix H: keeping an evidence file, changing the standard of evidence, separating the task from the self, and graded exposure to being seen. Before those methods are described, it is necessary to look at where the pattern came from, because the source determines the most effective intervention.
