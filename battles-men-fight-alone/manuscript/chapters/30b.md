## 30.1 The Grief Men Are Not Given

The distinction in this chapter is between a loss that is acknowledged and a loss that is not. The following nine categories cover the great majority of the material men report, and they share the feature that no occasion is provided.

### The Nine Categories

**1. The death of the man's own friend.** A man's friend dies and the man's household provides him with a period measured in days. The wife of the deceased is a widow and is recognised as such; the man's own position is invisible, and it is not recognised by anybody, including, frequently, the man. The loss is frequently discovered late and it is frequently severe, for the reason given in Chapter 26.1: the friend occupied a large share of a small network.

**2. The death of the man's colleague or work companion.** The person with whom the man spent more waking hours than with his household. The employer supplies a day of leave and a condolence; the man's mornings are rearranged; the loss is treated as a staffing matter. The specific difficulty is that the relationship was frequently intimate in its content — the private conversations of a working life — and it was not of a kind that supplies a mourning position.

**3. The bereavement of a marriage that ended.** The former wife, the former husband of a relative, the children's other parent. Where the marriage ended in conflict, the man's position at the death is ambiguous, and he may be unwelcome at the funeral; where it ended amicably, he is present and has no recognised standing. The grief is compounded by the man's own history with the person and by the presence of children whose loss is recognised while his is not.

**4. The loss of a child before birth or in infancy.** A category with a distinct structure. The loss occurs early in a pregnancy; the man's household and community do not know about the pregnancy, or know and offer no occasion; the mother is attended to because she bore the physical event; the man's own loss is not provided with a position at all. Men describe the specific finding that the material arrives years later and that it has never been spoken of, including between the parents.

**5. The death of a mentor.** The person who taught the man the trade, the profession, the craft, or the standard. The loss of a mentor is a loss of a position and a part of the man's history: the man who knew the beginnings, the person to whom the man's early failures and early hopes were known, and, frequently, the person who supplied the standing described in Chapter 27.3.

**6. The loss of a companion from the man's cohort.** The classmate, the training group, the unit, the team, the group of men with whom the man came up. Each death removes one of the people who held the same history, which is what makes this class progressively heavier: at the tenth death, the man is the last holder of a particular period of his own life.

**7. The loss that occurred remotely.** The parent who died in another country, the sibling who died during a period of estrangement, the family member whose funeral the man could not afford to attend, the relative whose burial had already occurred when the man was informed. The absence of the man's own attendance is frequently a second loss and, in men's accounts, it is frequently the more durable of the two.

**8. The person who disappeared, or whose death was never confirmed.** A child, a sibling, a friend who left and was not heard of; a body never recovered; a death in a place where the man cannot obtain information. The loss has no event, which removes every mechanism by which mourning is conducted, and the men who carry it describe a specific and indefinite occupancy that may extend for decades.

**9. The death of the man's animal, home, land, or country.** The category that the culture most readily dismisses. The dog that the man kept for a decade and with whom he spent more hours than with most people; the house in which the man's entire adult life occurred and whose loss was a taking or a forced sale; the land that was divided or confiscated; the country that was left. Each of these is a loss to which the man had attached a substantial proportion of his daily life, and the absence of any recognition reinforces the man's own conclusion that the material is not legitimate.

### The Four Features They Share

**The loss is real.** The absence of an occasion is not evidence about the loss. The categories in this chapter are the ones with the strongest documented associations with prolonged grief, because the absence of acknowledgement is the single most consistently identified factor in the persistence of grief.

**The man is expected to continue immediately.** Work provides a few days, the household provides a period of irregularity, and the community provides a condolence. Then a specific requirement is imposed: the man's competence is required, which the man himself accepts. There is no assignment of a mourning period, and a man who attempted to claim one would be imposing upon his household's arrangements, which is the transaction men generally refuse.

**The loss is frequently not the man's to display.** Where the man has children, or a position, or a household that has just lost somebody, his own grief is subordinate to theirs. This is a real responsibility and it is coherent: he is the one who carries the household through the event. Its cost is that he is the person to whom nobody attends, at the moment of his own loss, which is the form of male isolation described in Chapter 27.3 in its most acute version.

**The material is unshared between the man and his own household.** The most consequential of the four features. The man's own household, at the loss, has its own grief and its own arrangements, and the man's specific loss — the friend, the colleague, the child before birth, the mentor — is not shared by the people with whom he lives. It therefore has no place in the household's account of the period, and the man carries it while conducting the household's own mourning. The pattern is common and it is invisible: the man who has organised the funeral of his father with complete competence and who is carrying, privately, the death of a friend who died in the same month.

::: keypoint
Men carry losses for which no occasion is provided: the friend, the colleague, the bereavement of a marriage that ended, the child lost before birth, the mentor, the companion from the man's cohort, the loss that occurred remotely, the person whose death was never confirmed, and the animal, home, land, or country. The absence of acknowledgement is the factor most strongly associated with prolonged grief, and the man's own competence is generally required by his household at the moment of his own loss, which removes the only period in which the loss could have been attended to.

## 30.2 How Men Grieve, and Why It Looks Like Nothing

The cultural account of grief is dominated by one pattern: the expression of feeling, tears, the sharing of the material, and a progressive return to ordinary life. Men's grieving frequently does not look like this, and the difference has been studied. This section sets out what the research describes, because the assessment of a man's own process depends on it.

### The Two Styles

The grief literature distinguishes, in broad terms, between two patterns, and reports that both are common and that neither is a deficiency.

**The intuitive style.** Grief is experienced and expressed as feeling. The mourner's attention is on the internal state, the material is shared with others, and the process runs substantially through expression and through the company of other mourners. This is the pattern the culture describes and that most people assume is universal.

**The instrumental style.** Grief is experienced and managed substantially through thinking and doing. The mourner's attention is on the practical, the mastery of tasks, the arrangement of affairs, and the thinking through of the loss, and the expression of feeling is limited and frequently private. The pattern is more common in men, in the men who report it the process is real and the loss is felt, and the observable signs of mourning are frequently absent.

The research reports that the two styles are not exclusive, that many people combine them, and that the widespread belief in a single correct pattern has two consequences: it produces the expectation that a man who is not visibly mourning is not affected, and it produces the man's own conclusion that something is wrong with him when he does not experience the material in the prescribed form.

### The Five Legitimate Forms of a Man's Mourning

**1. Activity as mourning.** The man's process runs through the practical: the arrangements, the estate, the household, the support of the family, the completion of what the deceased required. Men describe this accurately: *I did not sit down and cry. I did the things that had to be done, and I was thinking about him the whole time.* The activity is not an avoidance of grief; in the instrumental pattern, it is the medium of it.

**2. Solitary thinking as mourning.** The man's engagement with the material occurs privately and in thought: on a long drive, in a period of work that requires no conversation, in the early hours, at a place associated with the deceased. This is a real engagement with the loss and its absence of witnesses is what makes it invisible to the household, which then concludes that the man is unaffected.

**3. Physical forms.** The work, the walk, the drive, the manual labour, the training. Men report, with strong consistency, that physical activity is where the material moves: the period in which the loss is engaged coincides with exertion, and the necessity is real. This form has a documented basis and it is more effective in men than the verbal forms that are generally prescribed.

**4. The private conversation with the deceased.** The man who speaks to the person, in the car, at the graveside, over a photograph, on a particular date. Men report this form frequently and generally with embarrassment, and it is worth recording that the practice is generally treated in the grief literature as a normal and functional engagement with a continuing bond rather than as a failure of acceptance.

**5. The maintenance of the bond.** Men describe maintaining the connection deliberately: the object retained, the anniversary observed, the photograph in the workroom, the ritual of the visit, the habit of the person continued. The grief literature describes this as a continuing bond and notes that its maintenance is common and is not associated with worse outcomes; the cultural instruction that a man should *let go* is, in the research, not well supported.

### The Two Errors of Interpretation

**The man's error.** The man reads his own non-expression as evidence that he is not affected, or as evidence that he is deficient in feeling. The error is made at the beginning of the process and it determines the rest of it: a man who concludes that nothing is occurring does not construct any of the arrangements described in Section 30.4, and the material then operates through the forms described in the following section.

**The household's error.** The household reads the man's competence as a statement. The finding from the family practice literature is consistent: the man's composed conduct at the funeral is read by the household as evidence that the loss has not affected him, that his attention is available, and that he is the appropriate person to manage the others. The result is the arrangement described at the end of Section 30.1: the man conducts the household's mourning and has no arrangement for his own.

### The One Requirement

Both styles require a place in which the material is engaged. The difference between the intuitive and the instrumental style is the form of that engagement, and not its existence. The instrumental mourner's requirement is frequently not a conversation about the loss but the availability of a setting in which to think and to do: the drive with nobody present, the visit, the work performed for the deceased, the object handled, the account written. The chapter's practical work therefore concerns the setting rather than the disclosure, and this is the reason that men who reject the idea of talking about a loss frequently conduct the work of this chapter successfully.
