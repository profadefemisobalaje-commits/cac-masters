## 29.1 The Regrets Men Carry

The material in this chapter is stable across men's accounts, and it clusters. Nine categories cover the great majority of what men report when they are finally able to describe it.

### The Nine Categories

**1. The decision that failed somebody.** The man made a choice that had a consequence for another person: he declined the guarantee, refused the loan, opposed the marriage, advised against the treatment, chose one child's schooling over another's, decided not to move, decided not to return. This category is the most common, and its characteristic feature is that the man's decision may have been defensible in itself and is nonetheless connected to a harm that he now sees in the light of everything that followed.

**2. The failure to act.** The refusal or omission at a moment that required action: the man did not intervene, did not speak, did not attend, did not sign, did not report, did not go back. This category carries the heaviest weight in men's accounts and it is frequently the smallest act on the list: a telephone call not made, a statement not put, a person not stood up for, a matter left to somebody else. Men describe a specific and durable form: *the moment lasted thirty seconds and I have carried it for twenty-five years.*

**3. The words said.** The statement made in anger, contempt, exhaustion, or drink to a wife, a child, a parent, a subordinate, a friend. The material is generally known in part, since the circumstances are frequently known, and the actual content is not: the specific words, the setting, and what the man's face did when he said them.

**4. The words not said.** The acknowledgement never made, the apology never delivered, the appreciation never expressed, the last conversation that did not occur. Its weight is generally discovered late, and this category is the one most frequently reported in the years following a bereavement.

**5. The person not attended to.** The parent who was cared for competently and not known; the wife who was provided for and not regarded; the child whose years passed in the man's preoccupation; the friend whose deterioration was noticed and not addressed. This category is the substance of Chapters 17 and 18 and it is present in the material of the chapter with a specific addition: what the man regrets is not the absence of provision but the absence of attention at a time when attention was available.

**6. The money.** The debt not repaid, the man who was held to an obligation that the debtor could have softened, the partner who was not properly compensated, the family member whose share was not defended, the person who was treated unfairly in an arrangement the man controlled. This category is repairable considerably more often than men believe, which is the reason Section 29.5 treats it at length.

**7. The failure to protect.** The specific and severe category: the man did not protect somebody to whom he owed protection. A child from the conduct of another adult; a sibling from a violent parent; a wife from his own household's treatment of her; a younger colleague from an injustice that the man's position could have prevented; a subordinate from a supervisor; a family from a debt that his own conduct incurred. Men carry this material the longest of any category, and it is the one for which the material in Section 29.6 is most frequently required.

**8. The compromise.** The man's own conduct in a system that required something of him: the false declaration to support a family, the payment to a policeman, the accounts that were prepared falsely, the colleague whose contribution was taken, the examination that was not honestly passed, the work that was signed off when the standard was not met. The category is frequently accompanied by the man's own rationalisation, and the rationalisation is generally accurate as far as necessity goes and does not resolve the material.

**9. The particular act never described to anybody.** The category the chapter exists for. An act the man has never disclosed in any form: an episode of dishonesty, an act of cowardice, a betrayal of somebody who trusted him, an abandonment, an involvement in somebody's harm, a cruelty in youth, an act he regards as being completely out of character and which therefore cannot be reconciled with the account he holds of himself. Its purpose is not the induction of shame; it is the accurate recognition that this class of material is the one that produces the condition described in Sections 29.2 and 29.3, and that the work of the chapter must be capable of handling it.

### The Distinction That Organises the List

Once the material has been listed, each item falls into one of three classes, and the class determines the work.

**Class One: the error.** The decision that turned out badly and that was not wrong in itself. The man made a competent judgement on the information available and the outcome was adverse. Class One items require a specific form of work and it is the opposite of what the man generally applies to them: the establishment of the facts as they stood at the time, without the benefit of the outcome, which is the hindsight correction described in Chapter 16.5. Nothing is owed, and the material is to be closed by accurate assessment rather than by repair.

**Class Two: the wrong.** The conduct that was wrong when it was done, on the information available at the time. The man knew what he was doing, or could have known, and did it. Class Two items carry an obligation, and the obligation is dischargeable to the extent that repair is possible.

**Class Three: the tragedy.** The situation in which the man's conduct was defensible, the outcome was severe, and the man's responsibility is real but partial: the decision distributed among circumstances, other people's conduct, and the man's own contribution. Class Three is the most common and the most difficult, because it requires the man to hold a real degree of responsibility without either denying it — which is what the culture's response supplies — or inflating it into full authorship of an outcome he did not control.

::: keypoint
Men's private regrets cluster in nine categories, and their treatment is determined not by how heavily the man carries them but by their class: the error, in which the decision was competent and the outcome was bad; the wrong, in which the conduct was wrong at the time and an obligation is owed; and the tragedy, in which the man's responsibility is real and partial. Class One is closed by accurate assessment, Class Two by repair, and Class Three by the difficult work of holding real but partial responsibility.
:::

## 29.2 Why the Regrets Nobody Knows About Are the Heaviest

The private class of regret operates differently from the disclosed class, and the difference is not a matter of severity. Men report that a disclosed regret of a large event is carried more easily than an undisclosed regret of a small one, and the reasons are structural.

### The Five Mechanisms

**1. There is no correction available.** The chapter has returned to this mechanism repeatedly because it is the mechanism of the whole book. A disclosed regret is corrected by the version of events that somebody else holds, and the correction frequently alters the material: what the man remembers as contempt is recalled by his wife as exhaustion; what he remembers as a decision made in thirty seconds was in fact made over months. An undisclosed regret receives no correction, so the man's account of it is the only account, and it has been constructed, over years, in private, from the least favourable available materials.

**2. The material is protected from the disclosure that would alter it.** The reason the material is held privately is generally that its disclosure would cost the man something, and the cost is frequently accurate: the disclosure of the act, the words, or the omission would change how a specific person sees him. The material is therefore secure in its place, which means it is never revised.

**3. The material is carried without the ordinary permission to be affected.** A disclosed difficulty is met by others with acknowledgement, and the acknowledgement supplies the man's permission to be troubled. The private material receives none, so the man holds it in a form in which he cannot raise it, cannot indicate that he is occupied, and cannot receive company — while the material operates continuously. The pattern is the one described in Chapter 27.3: the man is surrounded by people and has no recipient for the item that most occupies his attention.

**4. The private standard is not known to anybody.** This mechanism is subtle and important. The man holds a standard of conduct which he has violated, and the standard is his own. His wife, his family, and his community do not know the standard was in force, so they cannot assess the size of the violation. The consequence is that the material has no external scale at all: the man measures it against his own account, which is at once the most demanding scale available and the least calibrated, since nobody has ever said that the matter was smaller than he thinks.

**5. The material is available to be triggered without warning and without explanation.** The man encounters the situation, the person, the place, or the circumstance of the original event, and the material returns. Because nobody knows, nobody can make sense of the man's response, and the man must manage it in public without the ordinary arrangements that a known difficulty supplies. Men describe this pattern precisely: the sudden flatness at an occasion, the withdrawal from a conversation, the unnecessary harshness in an unrelated matter.

### The Reason Men Keep Them

Four reasons, and each is accurate in its terms.

**The first is the cost of disclosure.** The material concerns something the man did, and its disclosure exposes him to judgement rather than to sympathy. There is no available construction of the disclosure as a misfortune.

**The second is the harm to the people who now know.** A man who discloses a wrong committed against a dead parent, a departed wife, or a person who is now absent creates a new burden for the living.

**The third is the position it would place others in.** A disclosure frequently implicates other people: the relative who encouraged the man, the friend who participated, the family that benefited. The material is frequently held in order to protect those people.

**The fourth is the man's determination to carry it.** A distinct and important reason, and one that men state openly when they are finally able to describe the position: the man has decided that the matter should not be set down. *I deserve to carry it* is the sentence, and it is occasionally correct in its reasoning and almost always damaging in its consequence, and Section 29.6 addresses it directly.
