## 8.7 Illustrative Case: The Two Years Before the Obvious Question

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in occupational psychology, professional mentoring, and clinical practice.

**Dr Femi is forty-eight, a medical specialist in a state teaching hospital with seventeen years of experience.** He trained abroad, holds a fellowship, has published research, and teaches residents. He is regarded by colleagues as meticulous and by his juniors as unapproachable.

For nine years he has carried a private conviction. He believes that his appointment to the hospital was a mistake; that his research output has been thin; that his clinical judgment is adequate rather than good; and that at some point a case will expose him completely. He has never mentioned this to anybody, including his wife and including the colleague with whom he trained.

The conviction has organised his working life in four ways.

First, he prepares to an extreme degree. Before a complex case, he reviews the literature for several hours, and he rehearse his reasoning aloud, alone, so that he will not hesitate if questioned.

Second, he does not pursue opportunities. Twice he declined invitations to take up a regional advisory role, and on a third occasion he did not apply for a position he was qualified for. In each case he gave a practical reason relating to family commitments, and each reason was at least partly true, which is how the pattern survived.

Third, he does not delegate work that touches his reputation, particularly documentation, which means that he works about sixty hours a week and has not taken an annual leave of more than a week in five years.

Fourth, he is remote with junior colleagues. He avoids teaching at the bedside where he might be asked a question he cannot answer, and he has acquired a reputation for formality which he does not recognise as fear.

Two events changed the arrangement. The first was an error: a misreading of an imaging study, discovered by a resident, which led to a delay in treatment of two days. The patient recovered fully. The error was reported, discussed, and closed within a week. Femi described the following fortnight as the worst of his professional life, and it was during this period that he first considered leaving medicine entirely.

The second was a conversation, at the hospital's departmental review, with a senior colleague who mentioned, in the course of a general discussion about training, that he had himself felt like a fraud for the first fifteen years of his career and had assumed that everybody in the room knew something he did not.

Femi said, in the only disclosure he made, that he had felt that way for nine years. The colleague's response — an ordinary and immediate *of course, we all do, and the ones who do not are the dangerous ones* — was, in Femi's account, the single most useful clinical intervention he had ever received.

He subsequently made three changes, and he described them as follows: he began a file recording what he had actually done each week, which supplied evidence that contradicted his estimate; he accepted a teaching commitment at the bedside, having decided that being asked a question he could not answer once a week was a smaller cost than the years he had spent avoiding it; and he saw a psychologist for eight sessions, during which he examined where the standard had come from and what it had cost him.
:::

Five features of this case are worth isolating, and each corresponds to a section of the chapter.

**The pattern was invisible and was read as virtue.** Meticulous preparation, long hours, high standards, and refusal to delegate are read by institutions as excellence. In Femi's case, and in the experience of many men, the very behaviours that were destroying his quality of life were being rewarded.

**The cost was measured in opportunities declined, not decisions failed.** The most consequential losses were invisible: the advisory roles, the promotion, the five years without proper leave, and the teaching he did not do. Chapter 7 identified this pattern as the most expensive feature of the fear of judgement, and this case demonstrates it.

**The error was survivable, including the exposure.** The feared event occurred: a genuine mistake, found out by somebody junior, discussed by others. The outcome was a fortnight of distress and a full recovery. This is the experiential evidence that men in this pattern lack, and it is the reason that graded exposure is the central method of Section 8.5.

**The disclosure was made to a peer, in an ordinary conversation.** Not to a counsellor, and not to his wife, at least not first. The most effective intervention in the case was an unremarkable observation by an older colleague who had himself been through the pattern. This is one of the reasons that mentoring relationships and honest conversation among men in the same profession are treated in this book as substantive health measures rather than as pleasant extras.

**The origin was examined and the practice was changed.** Femi's work had two components, and both mattered: an inquiry into where the standard came from, and a set of concrete practices — the evidence file, the deliberate exposure, the weekly questions — that changed the accounting. The second component produced the results.

## 8.8 Practical Exercise: The Critic's File

This exercise has four parts and is designed to be worked at over a period of four weeks. It is written for a man with a demanding schedule, and each part takes less than an hour.

### Part One: The Critic's Sentences (Week One)

Over the next seven days, carry a small notebook or use a note application, and record every automatic statement about yourself that appears in response to work, family, or social situations. Record it verbatim, without correcting or evaluating it. Aim for between fifteen and thirty statements.

At the end of the week, review the list, and group the statements under the six headings from Section 8.4: all-or-nothing judgements, magnification of the negative, minimisation of the positive, mind-reading, catastrophising, and labelling.

Then answer, in writing:

- Which form appears most frequently?
- Whose voice does the most frequent statement sound like?
- How old is that sentence, and when did it first speak?

### Part Two: The Evidence File (Weeks Two and Three)

For two weeks, complete the following five-line entry once a week, on the same day.

1. **What I did** (describe the work, without evaluation).
2. **What resulted** (as an observer would state it).
3. **What was actually mine** (decisions, judgements, skills).
4. **What others said** (direct quotations where possible).
5. **What I found difficult** (an honest, specific entry).

The purpose is to produce evidence that the critic has not accounted for.

### Part Three: The Fair Observer Test (Week Four)

Select the three most consequential situations from the previous month in which your self-estimate was negative. For each, complete the following.

| Question | Your answer |
| --- | --- |
| What happened, in facts? | |
| What did I conclude about myself? | |
| What did I attribute the outcome to? | |
| What would a fair observer with the same information conclude? | |
| What is the accurate statement? | |

Then write the accurate statement in the first person, in no more than fifteen words, and keep it where you will see it during the following week.

### Part Four: The Exposure Plan

Choose four actions, arranged from easiest to hardest, from the list in Section 8.5, and write them into your calendar with dates. For each, record:

- **The action.**
- **The date.**
- **What I predict will happen.**
- **What actually happened.**

The final column is the substance of the exercise. Men who complete it frequently find, on reviewing the predictions against the outcomes, that the gap between them is the largest single source of their self-doubt — and the gap is measurable, which means it can be reduced.

### Two Voices Worth Distinguishing

Work with the internal critic is easier when two things that men habitually merge are separated: the **critic** and the **standard**.

The critic is the voice described throughout this chapter: global, personal, permanent, and concerned with the verdict on the man. Its sentences begin with *you are* and they apply to everything.

The standard is the man's own assessment of what good work, responsible fatherhood, or a properly conducted life requires. Its statements are specific, they are concerned with the work rather than the person, and they are directed at improvement. Its sentences begin with *this is not right yet* or *that should be done differently*, and they are the reason the man's work improves at all.

The two voices frequently use identical material. Both, for example, may object to a report that was submitted late. The critic concludes that the man is unserious and will never amount to anything and that the incident confirms everything. The standard concludes that the deadline was missed, that the cause was the man's habit of leaving the final drafting to the last week, and that the practice should change. The registered tone may be similar; the consequences are entirely different. One produces paralysis, concealment, and the deferral of the next task; the other produces a change in procedure.

Three practical implications follow. First, when the critic speaks, the man should ask the diagnostic question introduced in Section 8.4: is this sentence about the work or about me? Where it is about him, the sentence is not information and should be treated as an event rather than a finding. Second, the standard should be preserved deliberately, because men who attempt to silence the whole internal commentary frequently lose their standards along with the critic, and become less effective and less careful as a result. Third, the standard should be written down, since a written standard is specific, revisable, and operational, while an internalised standard is indistinguishable from the critic's general complaints. The exercise in Section 8.8 is designed to produce exactly this separation, and the men who complete it commonly report that the written standard is the most durable of its outputs.

### A Note on Professional Support

Where the pattern is accompanied by persistent low mood, by anxiety that interferes with sleep or work, by a history of childhood criticism that was severe or violent, or by any thought of self-harm, the methods above should be undertaken alongside professional support rather than instead of it. Chapter 35 describes how to obtain it, and Appendix H provides the extended version of the cognitive methods used in this exercise.
