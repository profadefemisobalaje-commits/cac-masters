## 27.2 How a Man Ends Up with Nobody to Call

The positions described in Section 27.1 are generally not chosen. They are produced by processes, and the processes are describable, which matters because a man who can identify the process operating in his life can interrupt it.

### The Seven Processes

**1. The narrowing produced by responsibility.** The ordinary and most widespread process. A man's obligations expand: the job, the marriage, the children, the extended family, the community, the additional income, the second position. Each additional obligation consumes the time available for relationships, and the arrangements that are consumed first are those with no claimant. A man's friendships have no claimant; they will not send a reminder, incur a charge, or appear at the door. They are therefore the first item to be dropped and the last to be restored, and the process operates silently for two decades.

**2. The migration and relocation process.** Each move removes the arrangements that were in place and requires them to be rebuilt. Men frequently do not rebuild, for the reason given in the previous process and for a second reason: the arrangements at the destination require an introduction that the man does not have and a period of availability that his obligations do not allow. Men who have moved three times in a career frequently have substantial relationships in three cities and no functioning arrangements in the one where they live.

**3. The status-change process.** A change in a man's position removes relationships in a specific way: the promotion that makes friendships with former colleagues awkward; the loss of a position that removes the men with whom the man's days were spent; the wealth that changes the terms of relationships in a community where he grew up; the poverty that makes the man withdraw. In each case, the arrangement was carried by the shared circumstances and does not survive their change.

**4. The grief and loss process.** Bereavement, divorce, and the loss of a parent or a friend remove the men's principal arrangements. The specific feature in men's accounts is that this process is frequently accompanied by a second mechanism: the man's own withdrawal during the period of the loss, which converts the temporary absence of arrangements into a permanent condition. The withdrawal is the material of Chapter 13, and the process is the reason that the material of that chapter addresses the network directly.

**5. The household process.** A man's arrangements are frequently carried, substantially, by his wife. She maintains the friendships with other couples, the family obligations, the community relationships, and the occasions at which the man's own relationships are sustained. Where the marriage ends, or where the wife dies, the arrangements are discovered to have been hers. This process is the most under-recognised in the material on men's isolation and it accounts for a substantial proportion of the isolation experienced by widowed and divorced men.

**6. The concealment process.** A man who has concealed material from his network over a period — the failure, the addiction, the loss of the position, the condition — discovers that the concealment removes the relationships themselves. He avoids the settings in which he would be asked, declines the occasions, and by the time the concealment is no longer necessary the arrangements have been dissolved. The process is the material of Chapters 9 and 16 combined, and it is the most insidious of the seven, because its operation is invisible to the man: he believes that he is protecting his position and he is dismantling it.

**7. The conclusion process.** The final process, and the one that operates after the others. A man who has been betrayed, dismissed, or disappointed arrives at a conclusion about other men — the wrong lesson described in Section 26.5 — and ceases to extend arrangements. The process then eliminates the participants rather than the relationships, and the man's isolation becomes a decision, which is why it is the most difficult process to reverse.

### The Three Accelerants

Three factors accelerate any of the seven processes, and they are worth identifying separately.

**Alcohol.** The most significant. Alcohol supplies the function of company and disclosure in a form that requires no arrangement, and it therefore removes the requirement to build one. Men who drink in the evening with the group at the bar frequently have no other arrangements, and the finding from treatment practice is consistent: when the drinking is addressed, the absence of any relationship capable of receiving the man's actual position becomes visible, and its construction becomes a required part of the work.

**Overwork.** The respectable accelerant. Work supplies activity, purpose, standing, and the appearance of connection, and it consumes exactly the hours in which arrangements are made. A man can be in the presence of thirty people for sixty hours a week and have no arrangement at all.

**Screens and the substitute arrangements.** The digital forms supply a partial version of each of the four functions from Section 26.1 — the group chat supplies membership, the forum supplies company, and, in a minority of cases, genuineness. The substitute is generally real as far as it goes, and its specific effect is that it removes the pressure to construct arrangements where the material itself could be delivered. The finding is not that digital connection is worthless; it is that the man who has replaced face-to-face arrangements entirely with digital ones has removed the function that survives a crisis.

## 27.3 What Isolation Does to a Man

The consequences of isolation are documented, and this section sets them out because the material is the strongest available argument against the assumption that a man's isolation is a private preference that costs him nothing.

### Health and Longevity

The research literature on social connection and mortality consistently finds that men with few social relationships face substantially elevated mortality risk, after accounting for other factors. The association is comparable in strength to well-established risk factors that receive far greater public attention, and the effect is larger in men than in women in most of the studies that report it.

Two mechanisms are documented.

**Physiological.** Social connection is associated with measurable differences in cardiovascular function, immune response, and stress hormone regulation. Isolated men carry higher physiological load, and the load is chronic.

**Behavioural.** Isolated men receive fewer prompts to attend to their health, present later with conditions, are less likely to be accompanied to appointments, receive less assistance with adherence to treatment and medication, and are more likely to continue a course of conduct that others would have interrupted. Men in treatment consistently identify this factor: somebody noticed.

### Mental Health

The findings are consistent and strong: social isolation is associated with elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and suicide. Men are already at substantially higher risk of suicide than women despite lower reported rates of depression and of treatment seeking, and isolation is among the most consistently identified factors in the analysis of that gap. The mechanism described in this book applies directly: a man with nobody to call holds the material in private, and the material compounds in the manner described in Chapters 8, 9, and 21.

### The Damage to Judgement

The least known consequence and, for the purposes of this book, the most important, because it explains why isolation damages men in the domain in which they most value their competence.

A man who has nobody with whom to think a matter through makes decisions from a single perspective, and the decisions have recognisable characteristics. The documented finding from the decision literature is that isolation produces risk-seeking in some domains and excessive caution in others, that it reduces the capacity for effective perspective-taking, and that it substantially increases the weight given to whatever framing the man has adopted.

Its practical forms in men's lives are the four described in this book's chapters.

The **large irreversible commitment** made without a second opinion: the guarantee signed for the friend, the business entered without documentation, the property purchased on an inside understanding, the investment made in a scheme that a colleague would have questioned in a single conversation.

The **concealment** continued beyond the point at which disclosure would have been affordable: the debt allowed to compound through the year in which it could have been declared, the condition allowed to develop through the period in which its management might have been simple.

The **adjustment** made in private and never revisited: the household expenditure reduced in a manner that the man's wife would have questioned, the retirement plan altered without any discussion, the decision to decline a position that a friend would have tested in ten minutes.

The **conclusion** formed without challenge: the conclusion that he is finished, that the betrayal proves what men are, that the business cannot be rescued, that his marriage is over. Men who have reached conclusions of this kind in isolation and who have then said them aloud in the presence of one person consistently report the same experience: the sentence, once spoken to somebody who takes it seriously, changes in the man's own hearing. The change is not produced by advice, and it is frequently not produced by disagreement. It is produced by the impression the sentence makes when it has to be stated to another person, and this is the smallest and most reliable benefit of the arrangement this chapter is asking men to build.

::: story
**A composite account, constructed from patterns reported in practice.**

A man of fifty-three, a bank branch manager, described the two months in which he made the decision that altered his household's circumstances for a decade. His employer had offered him a position in another city at a cost in income and in his children's schooling, and the decision was required within three weeks.

He described the three weeks in these terms: *I thought about it every night from eleven until two. I had all the numbers. I made a sheet. I decided to decline, and I declined it, and I told my wife after I had answered.*

His account of the outcome, which he gave eight years later, was that the position had been the last opportunity of its kind, that the household's circumstances had never recovered from his remaining in a declining role, and that his wife learned of the offer a week after he had refused it.

When asked what he would have done differently, he did not say that he would have accepted the position. He said: *I would have said it out loud to somebody who had nothing to gain. I have never been able to explain why I did not, and I have thought about it for eight years. There was nobody I could say it to. My wife would have had an interest in the answer. My colleagues were the office. My brother would have told my mother. I did not want to discuss it with my father because I did not want to tell him that I had an offer and might not take it. So I thought about it from eleven until two, for three weeks, and I decided.*
:::

The account's significance for this chapter is not the decision, which may or may not have been the correct one on the facts. It is the description of the deliberation: three weeks of solitary nightly reasoning with a single framing, no perspective available, and the decision delivered as a completed fact. This is what isolation does to a man's judgement, and it is the reason that the arrangements in Section 27.5 are described in this book as a requirement of competence rather than as a matter of comfort.
