## 1.3 The Emotional Cost of Appearing Strong

The phrase *appearing strong* is important, because this chapter is not about strength. It is about the labour of producing an appearance of strength, which is a different and more expensive activity. Real strength — the capacity to act, to endure difficulty, to carry responsibility — is a resource. It grows or depletes depending on how it is used and replenished. The appearance of strength, by contrast, is a performance, and performances have costs that are invisible to the audience.

### The Cost of Continuous Self-Monitoring

A man who has decided that his distress must not be visible takes on a permanent administrative task. He must monitor his own face, voice, posture, and answers, in every setting, all day. He must remember what he has told whom. He must select topics that will not lead towards dangerous ground. He must anticipate questions and prepare replies. He must notice when he is close to losing control of his expression and find a reason to leave the room.

This monitoring is effortful, and it consumes exactly the resource he most needs for the difficulties he is managing: attention. Men in this position describe a characteristic experience that will be familiar to many readers — performing adequately at work, and remembering almost nothing about the day; sitting in a meeting and hearing, as though from a distance, their own voice making sensible contributions; driving home without any recollection of the route. The capacity to think clearly is finite, and when a substantial fraction of it is spent on concealment, the remainder is not enough for good judgement. This is one of the most practical reasons to end the silence. Concealment does not merely hurt; it makes a man worse at his responsibilities.

### The Cost to Sleep and to the Body

The physical costs of sustained emotional suppression have been documented across several decades of research and are among the most robust findings in the field. Chronic stress activates physiological systems that are useful in emergencies and harmful when they never switch off. Men who habitually suppress emotion report and display higher rates of sleep disturbance, muscle tension, headaches, digestive complaints, elevated blood pressure, and fatigue that rest does not repair. In a general health setting, a man in this position is more likely to present with a physical symptom than an emotional one — headache, chest tightness, back pain, difficulty sleeping — and to be investigated, appropriately, for physical causes first. The emotional situation may never be mentioned at all.

The important implication for the reader is not medical but practical. If you are a man who has developed a set of physical complaints that no clinician has been able to explain, and you are managing a private burden that you have told nobody about, the two facts may be connected. This does not mean that the symptoms are imaginary. It means that the body is often the first place a suppressed problem becomes visible, and that physical treatment alone may miss the cause.

### The Cost to Judgement and Decision-Making

Sustained stress and poor sleep degrade precisely the capacities that men at the centre of families and organisations most depend upon: emotional regulation, the ability to consider consequences at a distance, tolerance for disagreement, and honest assessment of risk. The changes are gradual, and they usually appear first in the smallest decisions — irritability about domestic trivia, abruptness with subordinates, a tendency to dismiss advice, a sudden certainty that a particular plan is the only possible path. Men in this state frequently describe themselves as having become short-tempered, and are frequently described by others in the same terms, without either party understanding that they are describing the surface effect of concealed overload.

### The Cost of Emotional Numbness

When a man holds a difficult feeling out of view for long enough, he does not merely hide it. He weakens his access to feeling altogether. Emotional regulation is a skill, and it is learned by practice; conversely, the habit of suppressing one emotion reduces the ability to experience others. Men who have suppressed fear and grief for years report that they have also lost pleasure: they no longer enjoy music as they did, they do not laugh as readily, they do not find their children as entertaining, they have become unable to cry even at events that would once have moved them, and they cannot say with certainty what they want. This flattening is one of the most frequently reported experiences in the literature on male depression and male grief, and it is deeply confusing to the person living it, because it does not feel like sadness. It feels like the disappearance of colour from a life that is, by every external measure, going well.

### The Cost to Health Behaviour

There is a further cost that men rarely connect to concealment, and it matters greatly: the loss of routine medical care. A man who is maintaining an appearance of strength finds it difficult to attend a clinic. An appointment is an admission of need, and it produces a record. So routine checks are postponed, symptoms are minimised, and treatable conditions are discovered late. This is one of the clearest explanations for a well-documented pattern in men's health: men present later, with more advanced illness, and receive a diagnosis — whether of hypertension, diabetes, or depression — further along the natural history of the condition than women do. The behaviour looks like indifference to health. It is more often a consequence of the belief that a strong man does not need to be looked at.

The labour of appearing strong consumes attention, degrades sleep, erodes judgement, dulls feeling, and defers care. By the time the performance is recognised for what it is, its costs have usually already been paid — in a marriage, in a business, in a heart, or in a body.

## 1.4 The Loneliness Behind Achievement

There is a particular loneliness that accompanies a man's success, and it is difficult to discuss because it appears ungrateful. The conventional understanding of loneliness is about absence: nobody around, nobody available, nobody interested. The loneliness that this section describes is the opposite. It occurs in the middle of company, in the middle of responsibility, in the middle of a life in which a great many people need something from the man in question. It is the loneliness of being surrounded, counted upon, relied upon, and unknown.

### Why Achievement Produces It

Achievement and responsibility are structurally isolating, for four reasons that can be identified precisely.

**Achievement changes the nature of conversation.** A man who becomes the person who pays, decides, or mediates finds that conversations with him acquire a purpose. His wife needs a decision on the fees. His brother needs an answer on the loan. A colleague needs cover for a shift. A neighbour needs advice about a dispute. Every conversation is legitimate, and each one adds to the total quantity of contact while subtracting from the total quantity of relationship. At the end of a day of such conversations, a man has spoken to fourteen people and been known by none of them.

**Achievement creates an audience that must be maintained.** Once a man is known as the one who manages, that identity acquires expectations. To admit difficulty is perceived — by him and often by others — as a change in the terms. Men speak of an unspoken rule: *If I say I cannot, everything changes.* Whether or not the rule is real is an open question, and it is frightening precisely because it has never been tested.

**Achievement removes the structure in which confidences happen.** Friendships between men are frequently organised around shared activity — working, travelling, playing, farming, drinking, attending worship, and walking home together. When a man's advancement changes his daily patterns, he loses the intervals in which such friendships were conducted. What remains is the phone call, which is short and purposeful, and the occasional gathering, which is festive and public. Neither supports the kind of conversation in which a man says what is actually happening to him.

**Achievement alters the terms of comparison.** A successful man is compared with other successful men, which produces a persistent, low-grade sense of insufficiency even when his own position is objectively strong. This is the mechanism examined at length in Chapter 11, and it is one of the reasons that senior men are surprisingly difficult to console. They are not suffering from a shortage of achievement. They are suffering from a shortage of rest, of honesty, and of anyone who knows them well enough to say so.

### The Specific Loneliness of the Accomplished Man

The loneliness produced by achievement has three features worth naming.

It is *invisible to others*, because it contradicts the visible evidence. Nobody looks at a man with a functioning business, an intact family, and a respected name and concludes that he might be desperately alone.

It is *invisible to the man* for a long time, because he attributes the feeling to fatigue, to age, to a difficult quarter at work, or to the fact that everyone is busy. He may not use the word *lonely* about himself at all, because the word seems to belong to people with nobody, and he has everybody.

And it is *self-reinforcing*. Loneliness produces a characteristic set of behaviours: withdrawal from social contact in order to avoid exposure, shorter and more functional communication, and a heightened perception of others as unavailable. These behaviours produce further loneliness, which deepens the withdrawal. Over a period of years, a man can arrive at a situation in which he has hundreds of contacts in his phone and not one person to whom he would send a message at midnight.

### The Difference Between Recognition and Connection

A distinction that will recur throughout this book is set out here in its simplest form. **Recognition** is what a man receives for what he does: respect, titles, gratitude, deference, invitations, being asked for advice, being photographed at events, being named in acknowledgements. **Connection** is what a man receives for who he is: someone who knows what he is afraid of, someone who asks how he is and waits for the answer, someone who will tell him the truth, someone who will sit with him when there is nothing to be done.

Recognition and connection are not opposites, but they do not substitute for one another, and the second cannot be earned by increasing the first. This is the discovery that many successful men make in the middle of a period of achievement: they have become highly recognisable and entirely unknown. The tragedy of the pattern is that recognition is often pursued precisely because connection feels unobtainable, and each increase in recognition makes the pursuit of connection harder, since the man is now more, not less, exposed.
