## 45.10 Questions Readers Ask

The questions below are the last in the book, and they are the ones the case material records most frequently from men who have reached this point.

**"I have read the whole book. Why do I still feel that nothing has changed?"**

Because reading is not the material. The book has stated the distinction in every part, and it is restated here in its plainest form: the material is conducted, and its conduct requires a party, an instrument, and a date.

The case material's finding for this position is specific and it is uncomfortable. Men who read a book of this kind and conduct none of its exercises frequently report an increase in their isolation, because the reading has supplied them with a vocabulary for a position they are still conducting alone. The vocabulary is not an improvement in that arrangement. It is a more articulate form of it.

The remedy is the smallest possible act, and it is Part Four of the closing exercise: one call, within seven days, to a party who has offered and been declined, or to a party who has not been told. The book's finding is that the act, and not the reading, produces the change, and that the act is frequently smaller than the reader's estimate of it.

**"How do I begin if I genuinely have nobody?"**

Then the construction begins with your conduct toward others rather than with the search for a party, and the material is Section 45.4's four steps.

The specific beginning that the case material finds most reliable for a man without parties is a small office in an ordinary setting. The reason is mechanical rather than sentimental: an office supplies three of the four conditions at once. It supplies a setting, since the office is conducted somewhere with other people. It supplies repetition, since the office has a schedule. And it supplies an obligation, which is the component of belonging that a man without parties most lacks.

The office should be small and should be one the man can conduct: the treasurer's role, the secretary's, the organiser of a repair, the coach of a junior team, the keeper of a record, the visitor of the elderly, the collector of contributions. The case material's finding is that men who accept such an office and conduct it for a year report a specific and unexpected result: the parties who become available to them are the parties to whom they have been useful.

**"My wife says she has been telling me this for years. What do I say to her?"**

The honest answer, and the one the case material records as most effective, is the one that acknowledges the specific fact without either defending the past or collapsing into self-reproach: *you were right, and I did not hear it, and I am sorry that you carried that for as long as you did.*

The sentence is followed by an act rather than by a further apology, and the act is the statement of the position, at its true level, in the form of Table One. The case material's finding is that the wife's response to the acknowledgement is frequently less important than the act that follows it, and that the acknowledgement without the act is experienced as a further occasion of being told rather than of being joined.

**"I have told one person and nothing has changed. Was the book wrong?"**

The book has not claimed that a single disclosure changes a position, and the case material's finding is that the change is produced by the arrangement rather than by the disclosure. The disclosure's function is to establish the party who will hold the accurate position. The arrangement's function is to conduct the position with that party's participation: the second statement, the second opinion, the accompaniment, the instrument, the repetition.

The finding for the reader who has told one person and experienced no change is therefore the same as the finding for the reader who has done nothing, and it is stated in the same form: the next act is specific. What instrument did you require from the person you told? Did you ask for it? What date is on it?

**"My position is good. Do I need any of this?"**

The question is legitimate and the case material records it from men whose positions are, in fact, good: sound businesses, intact marriages, adequate health, competent children.

The material's answer has two parts. The first is that the tests of Section 45.1 are not tests of a man's position but of his arrangements, and a good position conducted without correction drifts. The finding records men whose positions were good at fifty-three and were not good at fifty-nine, and the change was conducted by decisions that nobody examined.

The second is that the material is not only for difficulty. The vision of Section 45.5 is a standard for a live position: the provision with the household's knowledge, the stated states, the instruments, the capacity that increases, the man who is not his work, and the endurance that is not alone. A man whose position is good can conduct that standard, and the case material records that the men whose positions were good and who conducted it frequently report the same benefit as men in difficulty, delayed by a decade and conducted without the crisis.

**"What if my household will not accept the changes?"**

Then the material is conducted at the scale the household permits, and the case material's finding is that the scale is generally larger than the man expects.

Three observations are recorded for this position. The first is that the household's resistance is frequently to the form rather than the substance: a household that will not conduct a Sunday conversation will frequently conduct an ordinary statement at the kitchen table. The second is that the household's resistance frequently reduces over time when the man's conduct is consistent and the consequences are visible, and the case material's finding is that the ordinary man's consistency is the instrument rather than his argument. The third is that the man's own conduct does not require the household's participation in order to be conducted: the provision with the household's knowledge requires the household to be told, and the telling is the man's act whether or not the household responds well.

**"I am not religious. Is any of this dependent on faith?"**

Nothing in this book is dependent on faith, and the material has been constructed without reference to any religious framework. The parties, the instruments, the arrangements, the practices, and the vision are stated in terms of conduct and evidence. Men who hold religious commitments will find that the material is compatible with them; men who do not will find that the material is complete without them. The book's concern throughout has been the man's arrangements, and the arrangements are conducted in the ordinary world.

**"What do I do for a man who will not accept any of this?"**

Three practices are recorded, and none of them requires the man's agreement.

The first is the availability, stated once and in ordinary language, without a programme. The sentence of Chapter 43.6's script is sufficient and it should not be repeated at every meeting.

The second is the specific instrument, supplied without a request where the man's position makes it obviously useful: the name of a professional, the offer of accompaniment, the practical assistance with one item, the telephone call on the anniversary. The case material's finding is that men who will not accept a general conversation will frequently accept a specific instrument.

The third is the protection of the man's position where the reader has the standing to protect it: the arrangement that is altered, the supervisor who is informed of the workload rather than the difficulty, the household's attention to the man's health, the party who is asked to make the call. The case material's finding is that the protection is frequently more effective than the persuasion, because the man's position is what is producing his conduct and the position is what the reader can alter.

The reader should note the limit. A man cannot be conducted into help against his will, and the reader's task is not to convert him. It is to remain available and to alter what can be altered, which is the material of Chapter 44.5's five functions in their ordinary form.

**"What happens after the book ends?"**

The book ends and the reader's position continues, and the material's finding about the period after the reading is stated in three observations.

The first is that the reader will conduct some of the exercise and not all of it, and the omissions will be the items in which the resistance is greatest. The omissions are the material for the next occasion, and the reader should record them rather than treat them as a failure of the book or of himself.

The second is that the change will be conducted over a period of years and will be visible in retrospect, which is the material of Chapter 38.5's indicative durations. The man who conducts the closing exercise and evaluates it in the following month will frequently conclude that nothing has happened. The man who evaluates it in the third year will frequently be able to name eight or ten alterations that were conducted and that he did not register at the time.

The third is that the material will require repeating. The case material's finding is that a man conducts the position statement, the parties, the instruments, and the terms at each significant change of condition: the new employment, the new household, the birth, the bereavement, the retirement, the illness. The book's programme is not a course that is completed. It is an arrangement that is conducted at intervals, and the intervals are supplied by the man's life.

## Final Word of the Main Text

The book has described a man who carried a great deal and told nobody. It has traced the architecture of that arrangement through the mind, the money, the marriage, the family, the identity, the society, the losses, the leadership, the recovery, and the rebuilding. It has ended with the one act that the whole architecture resists: the statement of an accurate position to a party who can hold it.

The reader who has reached this page has done something that most men who begin a book of this kind do not do. He has stayed with a subject that the culture supplies few occasions to examine. That is not a small thing, and it is not the end of the work.

The book's last word is not a resolution and it is not a promise. It is a sentence, and it is the same sentence with which the book's argument began and with which the chapter ends: **you can carry what you carry, and you do not have to carry it alone.**
