## 38.2 The First Period: The Four Tasks

The first period runs from the collapse to about three months after. It has four tasks, and each is small enough to be conducted in the state the collapse produces.

### Task One: Safety

**What it covers.** The man's physical safety and his household's; the immediate risk of any material from Chapter 34.3, including the risk to the man's own life; the shelter, food, and medical care of the household; and the containment of any active danger, including violence, the risk of self-harm, the risk to a spouse or a child, and the risk to a person the man's obligations concern.

**Why it is first.** Because nothing else can be conducted from a position of active danger, and because the risk categories of Chapter 34.3 are elevated in exactly the circumstances of this chapter. The chapter states the position plainly: a man who is in a collapse and who has any thought of ending his own life should treat the safety plan of Chapter 34.5 as the first task of this period, and the professional contact as the first action.

**The specific actions.** The accommodation for the following month, which is a matter of the next thirty days and not the next year; the food and the household's immediate requirements; the medical care, including the man's own; the assessment of any person at risk, with the appropriate professional route; and the safety plan in written form.

### Task Two: Information

**What it covers.** The man must establish what has actually occurred, in figures and in facts, before he acts on any of it. The specific content: the assets and their status; the liabilities, in full, including the guarantees, the personal obligations, and the sums owed to family and friends; the income that remains and what is required; the obligations with legal consequences, including the maintenance, the tax, the rates, and the contractual exposures; the position of any joint parties; and the deadlines.

**Why it is second.** Because the material of this book's preceding chapters is consistent on the point: the man's account of his position and the position itself are generally different, and the difference is generally in the direction of the position's being worse than the man's picture and the man's options being better. The practice material records the same finding in the collapse setting: the man's mental account is generally incomplete in both directions, and the written statement is generally the occasion on which he discovers the assets he had forgotten and the obligation he had minimised.

**The specific actions.** The written statement, in the form supplied in Section 38.4; the institution's records, obtained where they are obtainable; the professional appointment, being the lawyer, the debt adviser, the insolvency practitioner, or the financial counsellor, of which at least one should be arranged in this period; and the stopping of all new commitments until the statement is complete.

**The rule for this task.** No decision of consequence is taken before the statement exists. The rule is the same as the one in Chapters 27.3 and 34.2, and its purpose is the same: the decision that is taken from an incomplete account is generally the decision that produces the next collapse.

### Task Three: The Triage of the Obligations

**What it covers.** The obligations are sorted into three categories, and the sorting is a matter of consequence rather than of preference.

**Category A: the obligations that are legally or physically unavoidable.** The maintenance, the tax, the court orders, the rates, the obligations secured against property that must be protected, the household's basic requirements, and any obligation whose failure would produce a consequence the man cannot survive, including the loss of liberty, the loss of shelter, or the loss of a professional capacity.

**Category B: the obligations that can be deferred, negotiated, or restructured.** The unsecured debts, the trade accounts, the facilities, the obligations to parties who can be negotiated with, the arrangements with a period that can be extended.

**Category C: the obligations that are moral rather than legal.** The sums owed to family and friends, the commitments that were offered without terms, the arrangements of Chapter 33 that the man was carrying. This category must be identified and it must not be addressed before Category A.

**Why it is third.** Because the man's inclination in the first period is generally to pay the obligations that carry the most shame, which are frequently Category C, while the obligations that carry the legal consequence — Category A — are deferred. The practice material records the consequence precisely: the man who pays his sister and defers the tax has produced a second and larger problem, and the material of Chapter 33's inventory applies in its most consequential form.

**The specific actions.** The written list of every obligation, with its category, its deadline, its consequence, and the person or institution to whom it is owed; the negotiation of every Category B obligation by a stated date; the professional assistance for the negotiations; and the standing rule for Category C, which is that it is addressed after Category A and with the disclosure of the arrangement, in the form of Chapter 34.6.

### Task Four: One Ally

**What it covers.** One person who knows the whole position and who is available for the period.

**Why it is fourth, and why it is a task.** Because the material of Chapters 27 and 35 identifies this as the specific requirement in a collapse, and because the man's inclination is the opposite: to conceal the position until it is resolved, which in the case material produces the twelve-month period of solitary management described in Chapters 34 and 35. The ally's function is not to solve anything. It is the function supplied in Chapter 27.5: a recipient for the material and the correction of the man's own account.

**The specific actions.** The identification of the person, on the criteria of availability, capacity, and record; the delivery of the position in the honest form, including the parts that are the man's own failure; the arrangement's form, being the times, the means, and the extent; and, where no person exists, the professional route established as the ally of the period.

**The additional element for a collapsed man.** The ally should be told what the man is not to do, as well as what he is to do: the decision the man is inclined to take too quickly, the asset he is inclined to sell, the borrowing he is inclined to take, the confrontation he is inclined to have, and the amount he is inclined to drink. The practice material records this as the arrangement's most useful feature: the man's error in the first period is generally known to him in advance, and the ally is the mechanism by which it is interrupted.

::: keypoint
The first period of a collapse runs about three months and it has four tasks: safety, which covers the man's life and his household's; information, which supplies the written statement of the position on which every later decision depends; the triage of the obligations into the legally unavoidable, the negotiable, and the moral; and one ally who knows the whole position and who is told what the man is not to do. The rule for the period is that no decision of consequence is taken before the written statement exists.
:::

## 38.3 The Seven Errors of the First Period

The errors are described in the order of their cost in the practice material, and the reader should identify the ones toward which his own inclination runs, because the inclination is generally present before the situation that would act on it.

### Error One: The Concealment

**The error.** The man manages the collapse without informing the household, the ally, or the professionals, on the basis that he will resolve it and disclose it afterwards.

**The cost.** The mechanism of Chapters 34 and 35 in its most expensive setting: the household's arrangements are made on an incorrect account; the obligations that could have been negotiated are not; the assets that could have been protected are lost; the man's judgement operates without correction for the period in which the largest decisions are taken; and the disclosure, when it arrives, carries the additional material of the period of concealment and the household's loss of trust in the man's account of the household's affairs.

**The finding.** The men in the case material who concealed describe the outcome in uniform terms: *everything I did in that period that I did alone was wrong, and everything I did after I told her was better than what I had done before.*

### Error Two: The Panic Borrowing

**The error.** The man borrows to meet the immediate obligations: the high-cost facility, the informal lender, the sale-and-leaseback, the additional guarantee, the arrangement with terms that cannot be assessed in the state the man is in.

**The cost.** The obligation's size and cost, together with the asset or guarantee committed, and, most consequentially, the loss of the man's capacity to negotiate: the man who has taken the high-cost facility has removed his ability to negotiate the original obligation, because the negotiation requires the resources he has just committed.

**The finding.** The practice material's instruction is unambiguous: no new borrowing is taken in the first period, with the exception of the borrowing required to meet Category A, and that borrowing only where its terms have been assessed by the professional route.

### Error Three: The Rapid Sale of the Assets

**The error.** The man sells what can be sold in order to meet the obligations: the property, the vehicle, the equipment, the raw materials, the household's assets, the land.

**The cost.** The price obtained, which in a forced sale is generally well below the asset's value, together with the removal of the resources through which the reconstruction would be conducted. The specific case material finding: the man who sells the tools to pay the debt has removed the mechanism of his own income, which is the measure that determines whether the following years contain a reconstruction.

**The finding.** No asset is sold in the first period except where the professional route has advised it, where the asset's retention produces a risk that cannot be contained, or where the sale is required to meet Category A. The general rule is the one stated in Section 38.4: the assets' identification is a task of the reckoning, and their disposition belongs to the reconstruction's later stages.

### Error Four: The Isolated Major Decision

**The error.** The man takes the decision of consequence — the migration, the career change, the relocation, the marriage's ending, the business's dissolution, the property's disposition — in the first period and alone.

**The cost.** The material of Chapter 27.3 in its most consequential form, and the specific finding from the practice material: the decisions taken in the first period under the collapse's pressure are, in the men's retrospective assessments, the decisions they most regret. The reason is structural: the man's judgement is degraded by the state, the information is incomplete, the time pressure is generally less than it appears, and the alternatives are unexamined.

**The finding.** The decision is deferred until the statement exists, the professional has been consulted, the ally knows the position, and the household has been informed. The one exception is the decision required to meet Category A or to address an active risk.

### Error Five: The Revenge and the Litigation Impulse

**The error.** The man pursues the party he holds responsible: the partner, the debtor, the employer, the client, the institution, the relative. The forms include the litigation commenced in the first period, the public confrontation, the report and the complaint, and the correspondence.

**The cost.** The money, the period, and — the largest element — the man's attention, which is the resource the reconstruction requires and which the dispute consumes for years. The chapter's material on the claim is in Chapter 26.3 and the case in Chapter 26.7: the claim should be assessed on the documents, pursued where it is economic and where its conduct does not remove the resources required for the reconstruction, and placed in the professional's hands rather than in the man's.

**The finding.** The claim is assessed in the first period and not pursued by the man personally. Where the claim is economic, it is conducted professionally and in parallel with the reconstruction; where it is not, it is recorded and set aside, which the man should do explicitly rather than passively, since the alternative is the years of attention that the case material records.

### Error Six: The Drinking and the Numbing

**The error.** The alcohol, the gambling, the risk, the spending, the screens, and the behaviour described in Chapters 22 and 23, taken up as the means of managing the state the collapse produces.

**The cost.** The material of those chapters, together with the specific feature of the collapse setting: the numbing is generally accompanied by the man's reduced capacity to conduct the four tasks of Section 38.2, which is the period in which the reconstruction's conditions are established.

**The finding.** The reader's own report of his state in the collapse period should be treated as information, and the material of Chapter 34.4's checklist should be applied. Where the pattern is established, the professional route of Chapters 23.5 and 36.5 applies, and it is not deferred until the position is resolved, because the position's resolution requires the capacity.

### Error Seven: The Attempt to Restore the Position Immediately

**The error.** The man attempts to reconstitute his former position in the first period: the new business begun with the remaining capital, the position sought at the same level, the arrangement entered to demonstrate that nothing has occurred, the lifestyle maintained at the previous standard.

**The cost.** The remaining capital, which is the reconstruction's principal resource, together with the second failure and the additional material it supplies. The specific finding: the man's position is generally not restoreable in its previous form, and the attempt consumes the period and the resources that the reconstruction requires. The material is the practical form of Chapter 28.5's discussion of the dream's form and content: what the man requires is the content — the income, the standing, the capacity to provide — and the form in which he obtained it previously is generally gone.

**The finding.** The reconstruction begins at the level the position now permits: the income that is available, the work that can be obtained, the arrangements that can be maintained. The practice material records the men's own accounts consistently: the reduction to the lower starting point was the measure that made the reconstruction possible, and its avoidance is the reason that some men's collapses last for a decade.
