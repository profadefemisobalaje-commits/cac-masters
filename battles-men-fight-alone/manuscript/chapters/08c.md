## 8.5 Building Realistic Confidence

Confidence is not a feeling that arrives; it is an estimate that is built. A realistic confidence has three components — an accurate inventory of capability, a working tolerance of being seen, and experience of survivable failure — and each is constructed by a method.

### Method One: The Evidence File

The single most effective practice for men in this pattern is the maintenance of a written record that contradicts the critic's account. The method is as follows.

Once a week, record five items in a document kept for the purpose.

- **What I did.** The specific work performed, described without evaluation.
- **What resulted.** The outcome, as an observer would state it.
- **What was mine.** The decisions, skills, and judgements that were actually the man's contribution.
- **What others said.** Direct quotations, recorded at the time, rather than paraphrases from memory.
- **What I found difficult.** An honest entry, because a record containing only successes will be dismissed as fantasy.

The purpose of the file is not reassurance but evidence. Its function appears at the moment of a setback, when the memory supplies a selective account of failures and the file supplies the rest. Men who maintain such a record for six months consistently report that the critic's verdicts become harder to hold.

### Method Two: Changing the Standard of Evidence

Men with chronic self-doubt apply a higher standard of proof to evidence of their own competence than to evidence of their inadequacy. The remedy is to establish and apply a consistent standard.

The working standard proposed for this purpose is: **what would a fair observer, with the same information and no personal interest, conclude?** The standard requires the man to include in the evidence base the inputs (circumstances, resources, and luck) as well as the outcomes, and to exclude the special pleading in both directions. Men who apply it usually find that the fair conclusion is neither the critic's verdict nor a flattering one, but something specific: *he is competent at this kind of work; he is not experienced in that kind; he made a poor decision on this occasion.*

### Method Three: Separating the Task from the Self

The core operation of self-doubt is the collapse of a task evaluation into an identity statement. *The proposal was rejected* becomes *I am not capable.* The method consists of restoring the separation in language, systematically, until it becomes habitual.

| The critic's version | The accurate version |
| --- | --- |
| I failed. | The tender was lost. The pricing was wrong. |
| I am a fraud. | I do not know this subject as well as the two specialists in the room. |
| They will find out. | They already know I am not an expert; that is why I was hired for what I am good at. |
| I cannot manage this. | This is beyond my current capacity, and I know two people who could help. |
| I am useless at this. | I have not been trained in this, and I can be. |

Table: Rewriting verdicts as descriptions. The method is not positive thinking; it is the removal of a conclusion that the evidence does not support.

### Method Four: Graded Exposure to Being Seen

Confidence grows through experience, and the relevant experience is being visible and surviving it. Because the pattern is maintained by avoidance, the corrective requires a deliberate programme of graduated exposure, and it should be conducted in steps small enough that each is survivable.

A typical sequence for a man in a professional setting runs as follows, and readers should construct their own with steps calibrated to their circumstances.

1. **Ask one question in a meeting** where the answer is not known in advance, and state the reason for asking.
2. **Say "I do not know,"** once, in a professional setting, and add what you will do to find out.
3. **Delegate one item of work** and accept it back done differently.
4. **Present something imperfect** — a draft, a preliminary plan, an unfinished analysis — to a colleague for comment before it is complete.
5. **Take on a piece of work at the limit of your competence**, in the presence of someone experienced.
6. **Report an error** to a supervisor or client before it is discovered.

Men who carry out such a programme report a consistent result: the feared verdict does not arrive, the difficulty is smaller than anticipated, and the relief is considerable. When the feared verdict does arrive — a correction, a criticism, or a failure — the experience is equally instructive, because it demonstrates that a negative verdict is survivable, which is the fact that the whole pattern was constructed to deny.

### What Confidence Is Not

Two clarifications prevent misapplication. Realistic confidence is not the elimination of doubt: capable men remain aware of their limits, and the awareness is one of the reasons they are capable. And it is not a change in personality: a man may remain reserved, quiet, and unassuming, and still be confident in the sense used here, which concerns the accuracy of his self-assessment and his capacity to act on it.

## 8.6 Developing a Healthier Self-Concept

The final section widens the frame from confidence to self-concept, which is the underlying arrangement of beliefs a man holds about himself.

### The Structure of a Self-Concept

A self-concept is not a single opinion. It is a set of evaluations across multiple domains, and it is more robust when it is diversified. A man whose self-evaluation is concentrated in one domain — his professional results, his income, his physical capability, or his role in the family — is exposed to a total collapse if that domain fails. A man whose self-evaluation is distributed across several domains — work, family, friendship, health, faith, craft, community — possesses a portfolio, and the failure of one element is survivable.

A practical implication follows, and it is one of the most useful in the book: **a man in chronic self-doubt should deliberately invest in at least one domain of competence that is unrelated to his professional identity.** A skill practised for its own sake — a craft, a sport, a language, a musical instrument, gardening, cooking, a technical competence with no commercial application — provides an independent source of evidence about capability, and it is not vulnerable to the professional verdicts that dominate the primary domain.

### The Three Distortions to Correct

Three distortions are characteristic of the self-concepts of men with chronic self-doubt, and each can be addressed directly.

**The global self.** The tendency to evaluate the whole self from a single event. The corrective is specificity: statements about the self should be limited to the domain and the period to which the evidence applies. *I handled that negotiation badly* rather than *I am poor at relationships*. *I am behind on this project* rather than *I am disorganised*.

**The permanent self.** The assumption that present states are permanent: *I have always been like this; this is who I am.* The corrective is the historical record, which almost always shows change, development, and the acquisition of capabilities that the man did not previously possess.

**The compared self.** The tendency to define the self chiefly through comparison, as described in Chapter 7. The corrective is the establishment of a personal standard, written down, against which the man assesses himself before he considers anybody else's position.

### The Practice of Self-Compassion

The last element of a healthier self-concept is a practice that men frequently resist until they understand it, and it is worth explaining with care because it is misunderstood as self-indulgence.

Self-compassion consists of treating one's own failures with the same honesty and the same decency that one would apply to a colleague. It has three components: recognising one's own suffering accurately rather than dismissing it; understanding that failure and limitation are shared human conditions rather than personal defects; and addressing oneself with the accurate, constructive language one would use with somebody else rather than the language of contempt.

Self-compassion is frequently misread by men as lowering standards. The evidence points the other way: people who treat their own failures with decency are more willing to attempt difficult things, more willing to acknowledge errors, more persistent after setbacks, and less likely to conceal problems — all of which improve performance. Self-contempt, by contrast, produces concealment, avoidance, and overwork, which are exactly the behaviours that maintain the pattern this chapter describes.

### A Closing Note

The war against self-doubt does not end in a victory ceremony. What happens instead is that the accounting becomes fair. A man begins to know what he is good at and what he is not, to hold both with reasonable equanimity, to describe his work without a verdict about his person, and to be visible without anticipating exposure. The change is neither dramatic nor complete. It is durable, it is cumulative, and it is built entirely out of small, recorded pieces of evidence — which is why the exercise that follows this section consists of nothing more elaborate than keeping and examining a record.
