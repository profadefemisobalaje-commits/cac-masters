## 20.3 The Difference Between Appreciation and Praise

The distinction introduced in the introduction to this chapter deserves its own section, because men who seek the wrong thing frequently obtain it and remain unsatisfied, and because households that attempt to respond to this chapter's material frequently respond in the wrong currency.

### Three Terms

**Praise** is a general commendation of a person: *you are a good man; you have done well; we are proud of you.* It is pleasant, it is rare in most households, and it addresses the man rather than the contribution.

**Appreciation** is the valuation of a specific contribution: *the way you handled the matter with my family; the fact that you took the night work during the period when the children were small; what you did about the school fees in that year.* It is specific, it is tied to an action, and it addresses the contribution.

**Gratitude** is the expression of appreciation toward the man, in whatever form the household uses: words, a gesture, a favour, an arrangement, a public acknowledgement.

What men describe as missing is generally appreciation, and what they frequently receive, where they receive anything, is praise. The distinction explains a frequent and puzzling report: a man who has been praised for years and who continues to feel unrecognised.

### Why Appreciation Is Scarce in Households

Four reasons, and only the third involves a fault.

**The contribution is the baseline.** Households evaluate changes. The arrangement that has been in place for fifteen years is the baseline against which changes are assessed, and its continuation is not an event.

**The knowledge is absent.** Appreciation requires the household to know what the contribution cost, and the cost is concealed, as Section 20.1 established. A household cannot appreciate a cost it has never been told about.

**The cultural script omits it.** Most households have no practice of acknowledging a man's contribution. There are practices for acknowledging guests, relatives, and outsiders, and the man's contribution is regarded as the thing itself rather than as a contribution. This is not a fault of any individual; it is a gap in the household's repertoire, and gaps in repertoires are filled by adopting a practice.

**The man's own aversion.** Where the practice is attempted, men frequently reduce it, as described in Section 20.1, which extinguishes the attempt within two or three instances.

### What Appreciation Does

Three effects, all documented in the research on social support and family functioning, and all worth stating because they answer the objection that this material concerns feelings rather than outcomes.

**It changes the man's behaviour.** A man who knows that a specific contribution has been registered is more willing to repeat it. Men in this pattern frequently describe the mechanism accurately: they will do anything that somebody is aware they are doing, and they find it progressively more difficult to continue doing what nobody notices.

**It changes the household's decision-making.** A household that knows a man's costs can take them into account: the travel that will cost him two weeks of rest, the additional obligation that will consume the reserve, the request that arrives at a moment of genuine depletion. Households that do not know the costs continue to make requests, and their requests are not excessive in themselves; the household is simply operating without information.

**It changes the man's health behaviour.** Men report with consistency that medical care, rest, and the reduction of load become easier where somebody in the household knows the position. This is the practical route by which the material of Chapter 12 becomes achievable, and it is why this chapter and Chapter 12 should be read together: the reduction of load requires the disclosure of load, and the disclosure of load is frequently blocked by the man's fear of appearing to request recognition.

## 20.4 The Bitter Man: How Resentment Grows

The most consequential product of unrecognised contribution is the condition that men recognise in themselves with alarm and that others notice long before: bitterness.

### The Stages

Bitterness develops in six stages, and it is worth setting them out because they are recognisable and because the earlier stages are reversible at low cost.

**Stage One: the accumulation.** Years of competent, unacknowledged contribution. At this stage the man is not bitter and frequently not aware of any problem. His own description is that he is busy.

**Stage Two: the recognition of the pattern.** A specific event makes the pattern visible: someone else's contribution is thanked in his presence; a request arrives with no reference to what has been given; a family meeting is held and the man's position is not mentioned. The man's conclusion is formed: *it does not matter what I do.*

**Stage Three: the withdrawal of discretionary effort.** The man continues to meet his obligations, which are, after all, obligations, and ceases the contributions that were not required: the additional generosity, the readiness, the extra mile. The household notices the change, generally without understanding it, and frequently describes the man as having changed or become difficult.

**Stage Four: contempt.** This stage is the pivot, and it is where the condition becomes self-sustaining. Contempt is not dislike; it is the transfer of the man's grievance to the persons of his household. Its characteristic features: the internal dismissal of the wife and children as people whose acknowledgement would not be worth having; a manner that is dismissive, sarcastic, or condescending in the household and correct in public; and the construction of a private account of the family's unworthiness. Research on marital outcomes identifies contempt as one of the strongest predictors of marital dissolution, and its development here is the direct product of the previous stages.

**Stage Five: the alternative audience.** The man obtains the recognition elsewhere: a professional achievement, a community position, an organisation in which he is valued, a friendship, or, with some frequency, a person outside the marriage who supplies the appreciation the marriage does not. The last of these is the specific route to the situation described in Chapter 16.

**Stage Six: the arrival of the material.** At some point the man's obligations reduce or his circumstances change, and the material that was suppressed by the arrangement becomes available. The outcome is described in Chapter 24: a man in his late fifties, still functioning, considerably respected, and unable to describe a single relationship in which he is valued by the people he has spent thirty years supporting.

### The Signs of Bitterness in the Man

Men in Stage Four and beyond present with a recognisable set of statements and behaviours, and naming them is useful because they are frequently experienced by the man as realism.

- The public statement, made in the man's own household, that nobody appreciates anything.
- Enumerating contributions, frequently in arguments, in a manner that is defensible in content and destructive in form.
- Comparisons with other men whose families are described as more appreciative.
- A tendency to refuse requests that would previously have been accepted without comment, delivered with a specific explanation.
- The loss of pleasure in the household's achievements. A child's success is registered privately as an item that the man funded.
- A hardening in the assessment of the wife's character, from *she does not understand* to *she has never wanted to understand*.
- The construction of an audience outside the household in which the man's account of his marriage is conducted and from which the marriage is excluded.

### The Cost, and the Case for Early Action

Bitterness is expensive for everybody including the man, and the expense is worth stating precisely because the condition offers an internal justification — *I have earned the right to be like this* — which is difficult to argue with from inside.

The costs are: the man's own experience of his household, which becomes a performance of duty; the loss of the marriage's intimacy, which cannot survive contempt; the children's experience of a father whose regard is conditional on the family's acknowledgement — a form of pressure described in Chapter 17; the loss of the man's own pleasure in the family's achievements; the health consequences of a decade of chronic resentment, which the research on hostility identifies as substantial, particularly for cardiovascular risk; and the loss of the years in which the arrangement could have been corrected.

The case for early action is therefore practical rather than moral: the condition is repairable at Stage One and Stage Two at the cost of one conversation, and it is repairable at Stage Six at the cost of several years of work. The measure that prevents the escalation is the subject of the next section.
