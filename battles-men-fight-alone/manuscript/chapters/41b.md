## 41.1 The Definition He Inherited

The section establishes the material. The reader's task in this section is not to evaluate the definition but to write it down.

### The Sources

The definition a man holds is assembled from eight sources, and the reader should identify what each of his own supplied.

**Source one: the man's father, or the man who occupied that position.** The most powerful source, and the one that supplies the conduct rather than the words. The case material is consistent: men can recite their fathers' statements about manhood in the abstract, and their actual conduct is modelled on what their fathers did with pressure, money, grief, conflict, work, and the household. The specific material: what the father did with his own distress; whether he asked for anything; how he treated the mother; what he did when he was wrong; whether he was present.

**Source two: the man's mother, and the household's women.** The instructions: what a man provides; what a man is forgiven for; what a man is not permitted to be; what a man must protect. The specific material from the case reports: the mother's account of the father's character, and the sentence that accompanies a boy's distress — *do not cry, you are a man*, which the case material records as the single most frequently reported instruction of childhood in this book.

**Source three: the school, the training, the trade, and the profession.** The instructions of the setting: the tolerance of the difficulty, the prohibition on the complaint, the value of the competence, the marking of the softness, the rituals by which a boy was admitted into the category of men. The finding from the case material: the sources that carried the sharpest punishment for deviation were generally the institutions of the man's adolescence.

**Source four: the man's peer group, in the period of the definition's hardening.** Generally between fourteen and twenty-five, and the material: the terms used of the man who expressed or asked; the conduct that was rewarded; the men who were the group's models. The case material records that the majority of the men in this book can still reproduce the specific words.

**Source five: the arrangements of the man's household of origin.** Whether there was enough money; whether the man was the eldest son; whether a parent died, left, or was ill; whether the household held its difficulties privately. The source's material is the arithmetic of the period rather than the instruction, and its finding is the one recorded in Chapter 33.1: the man who was the eldest son of a household of scarcity generally holds the definition with the intensity of the arrangement that produced it.

**Source six: the culture's presentation of men.** The songs, the stories, the films, the proverbs, the religious instruction of the man's childhood, the public figures, the sporting material, the advertisement, and the community's accounts of its notable men. The source's material is not the man's obedience to these items but their ubiquity, and their effect is the establishment of the definition as the ordinary shape of things, so that a man's examination of it is conducted against a background in which the alternative appears deviant.

**Source seven: the man's own experience.** The most underrated source, and the one the case material records as decisive for many men. A man's definition is composed substantially of what happened to him and the conclusions he drew: the disclosure that was used against him; the expression that was met with ridicule; the relationship in which his softness was received and then exploited; the year in which the money was short and he was the one who was told; the occasion on which he asked for help and it did not arrive. The source's material is included because the chapter's central difficulty is located here: **the definition is partly a set of conclusions drawn from the man's own evidence, and its examination therefore requires that the evidence be re-examined rather than dismissed.**

**Source eight: the man's own conduct and its consequences.** The source that maintains the definition in the present. The man's position, his standing, his reliability, his household's reliance, and the rewards of the definition as he has experienced them. The finding: the definition is supported by the man's actual success, and the examination is not conducted against a tradition but against the man's own achievements.

### The Eleven Statements

The following statements are the ones most frequently recorded in the case material as the man's own account of his definition, and the reader should mark the ones he holds.

1. *A man provides for his family.*
2. *A man does not show weakness.*
3. *A man does not cry.*
4. *A man protects the people he is responsible for.*
5. *A man does not complain, and he does not explain himself.*
6. *A man keeps his word.*
7. *A man does not ask for help.*
8. *A man controls his household.*
9. *A man is measured by what he achieves and what he owns.*
10. *A man handles his problems himself.*
11. *A man does not talk about his feelings.*

The reader's instruction: **write the statements you hold, in your own words, and do not soften them.** The exercise's value depends on the accuracy of the material, and the case material records the finding that men write softened versions when they expect to be judged by them.

### The Five Classifications

Each statement the reader holds falls into one of five classifications, and the classification determines whether the clause survives the examination.

**Class one: the obligation.** The clause requires the man to do something for another person: to provide, to protect, to keep his word, to be present. The classification's material is the material of Chapters 3 to 6 and of Parts III to V, and its clauses are generally maintained through the examination with their content made specific.

**Class two: the prohibition on a state.** The clause requires the man not to feel or not to show a specified experience: not to fear, not to grieve, not to be uncertain, not to be tired. The classification's material is the difficulty at the centre of this book, and its clauses are the ones that the examination corrects, since a man cannot undertake not to feel.

**Class three: the prohibition on an act.** The clause requires the man not to conduct a specified act: not to ask, not to state, not to request assistance, not to be seen in the act of any of them. The classification's clauses are the ones Chapters 35 and 39 address, and their examination is conducted by distinguishing the act from its setting.

**Class four: the description of a status.** The clause asserts a position rather than a conduct: what a man is, what a man deserves, what a man is owed, what a man is entitled to determine. The classification's clauses are the ones that produce the material of Chapters 20, 26, and 40, and their examination is the chapter's principal work.

**Class five: the inheritance without a function.** The clause is held because it was transmitted and not because it produces anything the man can state. The classification's clauses are the ones that the examination releases, and the case material records the finding that the reader will generally identify two or three of them within an hour of writing the list. The frequently reported examples: a rule about a specific food, a rule about a specific task, a rule about the acceptable form of a man's clothing, a rule about the conduct a man permits his wife, a rule about the arrangement of the household's money that the man cannot connect to a purpose.

::: keypoint
A man's definition of manhood is assembled from eight sources — his father's conduct, his mother's and the household's instruction, the institutions of his adolescence, his peer group, his household of origin's arrangements, his culture's presentation, his own experience, and his own conduct and its rewards. The definitions are held as statements, and each is an obligation, a prohibition on a state, a prohibition on an act, a description of a status, or an inheritance without a function. The classification determines whether a clause survives the examination: a man cannot undertake not to feel, but he can examine what he has instructed himself not to do and what he has assumed he is owed.
:::

## 41.2 The Five Clauses and What They Actually Require

The section examines the five clauses most frequently reported in the case material and states what each actually requires — not what it is said to require, but what its holders' conduct shows it to require.

### Clause One: The Provision

**The common form.** *A man provides for his family. A man is the one who makes sure that the household has what it needs.*

**What it actually requires, in the case material.** In the overwhelming majority of the men described in this book, the clause requires that the man be **the primary or the sole source of provision, that he be the party to whom the household's shortfall is a personal failure, and that the provision's burden be borne silently, with no statement of its size, no request for reduction, and no disclosure of its effects.**

**The distinction the examination requires.** The clause's obligation is authentic. The household requires the provision, and a man's provision is a real good. What the clause adds to the obligation, and what is not in the clause's plain words, is the following four elements: **the exclusivity** — that the provision must come from him; **the silence** — that its burden may not be stated; **the personalization** — that a shortfall is a failing of his person rather than a change in the position; and **the anonymity** — that the act of providing is the whole of his relation to the household, so that his presence, his attention, and his person are not items the clause's holder considers himself to be providing.

**The consequence.** The material of Chapters 33 and 37: a load that cannot be stated, a burden that cannot be reduced, and a man whose value is computed at the point of his exhaustion.

**The revision the examination produces.** The obligation is maintained with its content made specific and its elements removed: the provision is the man's responsibility **with** the household's participation where the household has capacity; its size is stated as information and not as complaint; the shortfall is a change in the position rather than a verdict on the man; and the man's presence in the household is among the items he provides, as Chapters 18 and 39 established.

### Clause Two: The Composure

**The common form.** *A man does not show weakness. A man stays composed. A man does not let them see it.*

**What it actually requires.** The clause requires the man to conceal the evidence of his states from specified parties, at specified moments. The specification is generally: from his household always; from his wife in most matters; from his children in all; from his colleagues and staff in all; from his friends in the matters that concern him; and from himself, which is the clause's most consequential extension.

**The distinction.** The clause's obligation has a defensible content, and it is the material of Chapter 39.4's definition: **the regulation of one's states and their appropriate conduct in the setting.** The man who governs his conduct under pressure, who holds his fear through a briefing and states it afterwards, who does not discharge his anger into his household — that man is conducting a competence, and the clause names it.

**What the clause adds.** The clause's added content is the extension of the concealment to the settings in which no requirement exists for it, and its conversion into a rule about the man's own access to his states. The mechanism is Chapter 39.2's third stage: the technique is extended until it operates without a decision, and the man's registration of his own material is reduced to the point at which he cannot supply an account of his state to anyone, including himself.

**The consequence.** The material of Chapters 39.3 and 39.4: the man who is composed and unreachable; the household that constructs his absence; the information he does not receive; and the judgements he makes from an incomplete account of himself.

**The revision.** The regulation is maintained and the concealment is specified: the man states what is present in him to the parties entitled to it, on the occasions of his choosing, in the forms of Chapters 4.6, 27.5, 34.6, and 39.5's Practice Four.

### Clause Three: The Self-Sufficiency

**The common form.** *A man handles his own problems. A man does not ask for help. A man does not complain.*

**What it actually requires.** The clause requires that the man's difficulties be identified, managed, and resolved without the participation of another person, and that the man's need be invisible to the parties who might respond to it.

**The distinction.** The clause's obligation is the material of Chapters 31 and 35: the man's competence, his assumption of responsibility, and his conduct of the matters in his position. The clause's defect is not that it requires the man to be capable; it is that it requires the capability to be **conducted alone**, and it thereby removes the instruments on which capability depends. The research on help-seeking records the finding: the men who do not ask receive information about which they are wrong, carry positions longer than is necessary, and are treated later in the conditions that require early treatment.

**The consequence.** The material of Chapters 31.5 and 35.2: the delays, the undiagnosed conditions, the unnegotiated positions, the exhaustion, and the specific outcomes recorded in the men's health data.

**The revision.** The clause's obligation is maintained — the man handles his own problems — with the sentence added: **and he uses every instrument available, including other people's knowledge, because an instrument used alone is not a virtue.** The material of Chapter 35.4's five forms supplies the acts.
