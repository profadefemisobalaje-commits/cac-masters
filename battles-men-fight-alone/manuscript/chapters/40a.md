# The Man Who Finally Chooses Himself {.chapter num="40" short="The Man Who Finally Chooses Himself"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define what choosing oneself means in this book, and distinguish it from the versions that damage other people.
2. Describe the process by which a man's own life is deferred indefinitely, and identify the point at which his own deferral stands.
3. Analyse the four losses the deferral produces, including the loss of the capacity to know what he wants.
4. Apply the four permissions that men require, and test each against his own account of himself.
5. Conduct the audit of the second half of life: the list of what he will keep, what he will set down, and what he will take up, together with the conversations each requires.
6. Make the specific choices that a man's own life requires, using the criteria in Section 40.7, and conduct them without requiring the agreement of everybody affected.
:::

## Introduction

This chapter's title can be misread, and the misreading must be addressed at the outset because it is the reason that many men reject the material before examining it.

**Choosing oneself, in this book, does not mean the abandonment of the people a man is responsible for.** It does not mean the affair, the departure, the spending, the pursuit of the man's pleasure at his household's expense, or the reconstruction of his life on his own terms while the obligations of Parts III to V are left standing. The material of this book has been written against that pattern throughout: Chapter 33 established the obligations a man actually owes, Chapter 20 established the unspoken ledger, Chapter 26 established what a man owes the people who have relied on him, and Chapter 29 established the repairs.

What the chapter means is the opposite of that pattern, and it is the position the reader arrives at when the material of this book has been conducted.

**Choosing oneself means that the man's own life is one of the items the man is responsible for, that it is placed on the ledger with the other items, and that it is not the last item to be funded.**

Three findings from the case material establish why the chapter is placed here and why it is required.

**The men in this book have been choosing everything except themselves, and they have done it competently.** The obligations of Parts III to V, the work, the family, the community, the load of Chapter 33, and the dreams of Chapter 28 have been conducted in an arrangement in which the man's own life is what remains after the other items are funded — and the arithmetic, as the case material records, does not leave a remainder. The consequence is the position described in Chapters 24, 37, and 39: the man arrives at fifty-five, sixty, or sixty-five, and reports that he does not know what he wants, that he has no arrangements of his own, and that the years that remain are not identified as his.

**The deferral is produced by a rule rather than by the man's circumstances.** The rule is the one examined in Chapters 25 and 37: that worth is measured by what a man provides and that the man's own requirements are, by comparison, illegitimate. The rule is the reason that a man's own life is deferrable indefinitely, and it is the material of Section 40.2.

**The choice is available and it is not the abandonment of anything.** The audit in Section 40.5 has three lists rather than one, and the first is what the man will keep. In the case material of this chapter, the men who conducted the audit found that the majority of their obligations survived it, that a substantial minority were released with an arrangement rather than a rupture, and that a small number — generally two or three items — were the items that had consumed the man's own life without being either owed or wanted.

The chapter's approach has four further commitments.

**The permission is internal.** Section 40.4 is written as four permissions, and the finding from the case material is that the permissions are not available from the environment: the household generally requires the man's continuation in the arrangement, the family requires his assistance, the organisation requires his availability, and the culture requires the provision. The permission is granted in the man's own account, and the chapter supplies the argument by which it is granted.

**The choice must be conducted in the arrangement rather than announced.** The men whose changes held negotiated them: the timing, the disclosure, the arrangement, and the periods of transition. The men whose changes failed announced them and required the household's agreement, which is the pattern in which the change is met with the household's resistance and the man's own resentful withdrawal.

**Some people will resist, and the resistance is frequently legitimate.** The chapter records this finding honestly: the household that has been arranged around the man's provision has its own interest, and a member of the extended family whose obligation the man has been carrying has a real stake. The arrangement should be constructed with those interests stated rather than with the claim that the man deserves his own life, which is true and not an argument in a negotiation.

**The choice is conducted in the years that remain.** The chapter's practical orientation is the second half of the man's life: what he will do with it, whom he will be for, what he will keep, and what he will finally undertake. The material of Chapter 28's sixth section and Chapter 36's fourth condition supplies the substance, and the chapter's task is the man's decision that the years are his.
