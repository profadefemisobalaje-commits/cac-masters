# The Man with Nobody to Call {.chapter num="27" short="The Man with Nobody to Call"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise the forms that isolation takes in men's lives, including the forms that are concealed by activity, position, and marriage.
2. Identify the processes by which men arrive at a position in which nobody can be called, and trace their own course through them.
3. Explain the documented consequences of isolation for men's health, behaviour, and judgement, including the mechanism by which isolation damages decision-making.
4. Analyse the internal barriers that prevent a man from reaching out, and distinguish between the barriers that reflect the environment and those that reflect his own arrangements.
5. Apply a staged method for building a support network from a position of having none, including the first step, which is the one that stops most men.
6. Use the exercise at the end of this chapter to identify the person he would call, and to establish the arrangement.
:::

## Introduction

This chapter is about the position the book has been approaching since its first page, and the one that men describe to practitioners in almost identical terms: *There is nobody I can call.*

The sentence is not an account of loneliness in the ordinary sense. Men in this position frequently have wives, children, colleagues, employees, and acquaintances; they attend occasions; they are known in their communities and their names are on lists. The sentence describes a specific absence: there is no person to whom the man may deliver the material described in Parts I to VI of this book — the failure, the fear, the shame, the accumulated account, the conclusion he has reached about himself — without a permanent cost.

The absence is not a private matter. Its consequences are substantially documented. Men with few close relationships have higher mortality, worse recovery from illness, higher rates of depression, and higher rates of suicide; the effect is comparable in magnitude to well-known risks that receive far more public attention. Isolation also damages judgement, which is documented and less well known: a man who has nobody with whom to think a matter through makes decisions from a single perspective, and the decisions made in isolation are characterised by exactly the features this book has repeatedly described: the large irreversible commitment, the concealment, the adjustment that is never revisited, and the conclusion formed in the absence of challenge.

The chapter's approach has four commitments.

The first is to describe the positions accurately, including the concealments. A large proportion of men who have nobody to call do not know that they have nobody to call, because the fact is concealed by the activity of their lives. The description in Section 27.1 therefore distinguishes the forms.

The second is to take the internal barriers seriously, without treating them as the whole problem. Men who cannot reach out generally have reasons, and the reasons frequently concern concrete harm: what will be said, who will learn, what it will cost at work, what his wife will conclude, what his children will see. These are addressed directly rather than dismissed.

The third is to supply a practical construction. The chapter does not ask a man who has nobody to call to obtain support by an act of will; it supplies a sequence, with an entry point, and it is designed for a man who is unwilling or unable to talk about himself at the present time and who is not persuaded that talking would help.

The fourth is the book's central proposition in its final form: a man can carry the responsibilities described in Parts II to VI without being required to carry them alone, and the requirement to carry them alone is not a law of nature but an arrangement, and arrangements can be altered.

## 27.1 The Forms Isolation Takes in Men's Lives

Isolation is not a single condition. Six forms are common among men, and each has a different cause, a different outward appearance, and a different route out.

### Form One: The Man Who Was Never Asked

The first form, and the most straightforward. The man grew up in circumstances in which no adult attended to his difficulties: the household was occupied, the father was absent or harsh, the mother was managing an environment of scarcity, and the difficulties of childhood were managed privately. He acquired no experience of being attended to, and he therefore has no expectation of it.

**Appearance:** composed, self-reliant, frequently competent, and unable to identify anything missing. He will tell an interviewer that he is fine and that he does not need to discuss anything.

**Route:** the earliest and smallest available arrangement, and the requirement that the first attempt be described as experimental rather than as the man's actual position. Section 27.5's staged method is designed for this form.

### Form Two: The Man Whose Arrangements Were Removed

The man had arrangements, and they were removed: by bereavement, by divorce or separation, by migration, by retirement, by redundancy, by the departure of the children, by illness, or by the ending of a friendship. This is the form the first section of the chapter has most in common with Chapter 24's material: the man's network was substantially carried by one or two relationships, and circumstance removed them.

**Appearance:** frequently active and capable, and frequently unaware of the size of what was lost, because the arrangement was never counted. The discovery frequently occurs at a specific moment: the day of a diagnosis, a difficulty with a child, an emergency at night, when the man reaches for the telephone and finds that there is nobody to call.

**Route:** the reconstruction of arrangements, and the specific finding in Chapter 24's material: the identity-based material must be addressed first, because the man is frequently too reduced by the loss to initiate arrangements.

### Form Three: The Man Who Cannot Ask

The man has arrangements and uses none of them. He has friends, brothers, colleagues, and a wife; he knows that they would respond; and the requirement that he ask is one he cannot meet. The form is extremely common and it is the one whose failure rate is highest at the point of the first act.

**Appearance:** excellent networks, no utilisation, decline of the arrangements over time as the network becomes convinced that the man does not value them.

**Route:** the removal of the requirement that the first act be an act of asking. Section 27.5's entry points are constructed for this form: an offer of assistance to somebody else, a practical request, an attendance. The form's route is described at greater length in Chapter 35.

### Form Four: The Man Whose Isolation Is Arranged

A form that receives insufficient attention. The man's relationships are constrained by structure rather than by circumstance: the job that moves him every three years; the position from which friendships with subordinates are impossible; the marriage in which the man's own relationships have been the object of the wife's objection; the relocation to a new city; the care arrangement that occupies every hour; the shift pattern that removes the evenings. The man's isolation is a consequence of good conduct in other roles, and he frequently does not identify it as a problem until the cost becomes visible.

**Appearance:** frequently a man of high performance and substantial responsibility, who attributes the absence of friendships to having no time, which is accurate.

**Route:** the deliberate construction of arrangements as a structural requirement rather than as a consequence. The specific finding from men's accounts: the arrangements must be scheduled, as any other requirement of the position is scheduled, and the man's excellent organisational capacities are applied to a task they are not ordinarily applied to.

### Form Five: The Man Who Is Surrounded

The most concealed form. The man is in constant contact with people: family, staff, customers, congregants, patients, colleagues, and, in a substantial number of cases, an entire household that depends on him. He is spoken to all day. And the material of his own life has no recipient, because every available person is either dependent upon him, subordinate to him, the object of his responsibility, or an audience before which the material would cost him something.

**Appearance:** busy, connected, respected, exhausted, and frequently the last person in the room in whom anybody would suspect isolation.

**Route:** the identification of one person outside the man's responsibility, and this form's principal practical obstacle: the requirement that the person not be a dependent, a subordinate, or a member of the man's own household. Practitioners recommend, for this form, an arrangement that is explicit — a supervisory or peer relationship with a defined purpose — because the man's material requires a setting in which the discussion of it is the task rather than an act of disclosure.

### Form Six: The Man Who Chose It

A minority position that must be distinguished, because it is frequently not a problem. Some men's isolation is deliberate and functioning: they have a small number of relationships, they are content, and their arrangements supply what they require. The distinguishing test is not the size of the network but its sufficiency, and the test is this: whether there is at least one person to whom the man could deliver the material in the event of a serious difficulty, and whether he would do so.

Where the answer to both is yes, the form is not a defect and should not be treated as one. Where the answer to the second is no, the man's arrangement will not hold under load, and the material in Chapter 35 applies.

::: keypoint
Isolation among men takes at least six forms, and only one of them looks like isolation. The man who was never asked, the man whose arrangements were removed, the man who cannot ask, the man whose isolation is arranged by his other obligations, the man who is surrounded by people who depend on him, and the man who chose it. The route out differs by form, and the first useful step is the identification of which form is operating.
:::
