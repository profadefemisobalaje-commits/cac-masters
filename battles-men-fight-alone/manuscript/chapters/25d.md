## 25.5 Choosing Your Own Measure of Worth

The practical work of this chapter is the construction of a measure of worth that the man has chosen, that is available to him, and that does not require the audience's approval. The measure does not replace the world's assessment, which continues; it supplies a second assessment, held by the man, from which he acts.

### The Conditions

Four conditions must be met, and a measure failing any of them will not hold.

**It must be verdict-free.** The measure must not depend on outcomes the man does not control. A measure that requires the business to succeed is the audience's measure in the man's own handwriting.

**It must be available now.** The measure must be operable in the man's present circumstances, including the inadequate ones. *I will be a man who does not lie, who discharges what he owes to the extent of his capacity, who keeps himself present to his children, and who does not waste the day* is available to a man with no income.

**It must be specific enough to be checkable.** A man must be able to determine at the end of a day whether he has met it. The test used elsewhere in this book applies: if it cannot be answered with evidence, it is not a measure but a sentiment.

**It must be the man's.** Chapter 2's three-way test — kinship, work, own — applies to each element. A measure assembled from the community's requirements is the audience's verdict with different vocabulary.

### The Four Domains of a Personal Measure

**Conduct and integrity.** What the man will and will not do, stated specifically. The negative specification of Section 21.6 is the most durable form, because it is entirely within the man's control.

**Effort.** What the man intends to attempt, stated in units: the applications submitted, the hours worked, the training completed, the work performed properly. Effort is the domain in which the man has power, and it is the domain that the results-based measure ignores.

**Obligation.** What the man owes and to whom, discharged to the extent of his capacity, with the shortfall disclosed rather than concealed. This is the domain described in Parts III and V, and its importance here is that it supplies a standard the man can meet in conditions of scarcity: a man who cannot pay may still be a man who has told the truth about it and has paid what he could.

**Conduct toward his household.** The measure in Section 17.3: the four responsibilities of a father, and the specific attention items from Section 18.6. Its function is to hold the part of the man's worth which no market assesses.

### The Translation of Results into Effort

A specific technique, and the one men report as most useful. For each outcome in the man's circumstances, identify the corresponding effort and enter the effort into the measure.

| The outcome | The effort within my control | Is it in my measure? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The income I did not obtain | The applications I submitted, the meetings attended, the skills developed | |
| The promotion I did not receive | The quality of the work I performed, the competence I developed | |
| The business that failed | Whether the effort was made in full, whether the accounting was honest, whether the failure was contained | |
| My children's outcomes | My presence, my example, my conduct toward them | |
| My standing in the community | My conduct in public, my contribution at the level available to me | |

The technique's function is not to console the man. It is to restore a domain in which his effort counts, because the results-based measure has removed it, and because a man without a domain in which his effort counts stops making effort, which is the reduction described in Section 25.2.

### The Question of Ambition

One clarification: choosing a personal measure does not require the abandonment of ambition. Men who construct a personal measure report, with consistency, that their ambition becomes more sustainable and frequently larger, for the reason given in Chapter 7: ambition that is pursued without a verdict attached to each outcome can be sustained through failure, whereas ambition attached to a verdict is abandoned at the first verdict.

## 25.6 Living Without Needing Approval

The final substantive section addresses the practical arrangements by which a man conducts his life without requiring the approval of the judging audience. The objective is not indifference to other people, which is neither possible nor desirable; it is the reduction of the audience's authority over the man's decisions and his account of himself.

### The Four Arrangements

**1. The smaller audience.** A deliberate reduction of the number of people whose opinion is treated as authoritative. The practical form: identify by name the people whose assessment has genuine standing, on the criteria of knowledge, honesty, and concern, and treat the assessments from outside that group as information rather than as verdicts. The group is generally small, and men report that its identification alone produces a substantial change.

**2. The reduced exposure.** The unglamorous measure described in Chapter 11.5: the reduction of the ceremonies at which position is displayed, the reduction of the comparisons conducted by relatives, the reduction of the social media accounts through which other men's results are presented continuously. The measure's purpose is to reduce the frequency of the verdict's delivery, which is a matter of exposure rather than of attitude.

**3. The stated standard.** The man's measure, written and referred to. Chapter 11's exercise and Section 25.5 supply it, and its function in this context is to supply a second opinion at the moment the audience's judgement arrives.

**4. The relationship in which the man is not assessed.** Described in Sections 25.4 and 4.4. One relationship, at minimum, in which the man's position is known and his regard is not conditional on results. In the accounts of men who have maintained their footing through public failure, this is consistently identified as the decisive resource.

### The Three Prohibitions

**Do not argue with the audience.** The verdict is not produced by an argument and cannot be corrected by one. A man who defends his position against a relative at a family gathering has accepted the audience's authority and is conducting his case in its court. The practice is to state the position once, accurately, and to allow the verdict to stand unchallenged by the man's conduct over the following years. This is the material of Chapter 16.5, and its record in men's accounts is better than the argument's.

**Do not require the household to defend you.** A man who requires his wife and children to uphold his position in the community's conversations has transferred the audience's authority into his own household, which produces the arrangements described in Chapter 17.

**Do not construct a counter-audience in which the man is over-valued.** The opposite error, and a common one among men who have been judged: the deliberate construction of a circle in which the man's account is accepted entirely and in which the failure is attributed to others. The arrangement is comfortable and it is the mechanism by which men spend decades in the exposure response described in Chapter 16.3, conducting an unending case in a court that has already decided in their favour.

### What the Work Produces

Men who complete the work in this chapter describe four outcomes, and their consistency is worth recording because the outcomes are modest and the modesty is the point.

They report a reduction in the amount of their day consumed by the assessment of their position. They report an increase in the effort applied, for the reason given in Section 25.5. They report an improvement in their relationships with their households, because a man who is not defending his position at home is available to the people in it. And they report that their relationships with the extended family and the community improve, because an accurate and unapologetic statement of a man's position is substantially easier for others to work with than concealment, which is the finding of Chapters 9 and 20 in another register.
