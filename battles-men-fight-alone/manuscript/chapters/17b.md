## 17.2 The Father's Silent Sacrifices

Every father makes sacrifices, and the sacrifices a father makes are, in the majority of cases, entirely unknown to the people for whom they were made. This is not concealment in the ordinary sense — it is not deception, and it is rarely deliberate. It is the consequence of the way in which paternal sacrifice is structured: it is performed as ordinary duty, it is registered by the household as the normal state of affairs, and it is never converted into a statement.

Figure 17.1 shows the four fronts on which a father's obligation is distributed and the characteristic strain attached to each. The diagram is illustrative and generalised; the distribution differs substantially between households, and the purpose of setting it out is to make the whole arrangement visible rather than to describe any particular family.

### The Six Sacrifices

**1. The sacrificial hours.** The most visible to the man and the least visible to everybody else. A father's additional work — the overtime, the second job, the weekend contracts, the travel — is performed for the household and is registered by the household as absence. The paradox is exact: the sacrifice of time is made on behalf of the children and is experienced by the children as the withdrawal of the father.

**2. The sacrifice of ambition.** A substantial number of fathers abandon or postpone particular ambitions — further study, a business, a move, a career change — because the family's security requires stability. The sacrifice is made in a specific decision at a specific time, and it is rarely disclosed, because disclosing it would create an obligation that the man does not want to impose. Men frequently carry such decisions for a lifetime: the degree not taken, the offer not accepted, the city not moved to. Chapter 11 addresses the delayed and abandoned ambition directly.

**3. The sacrifice of health.** Sleep foregone, meals taken irregularly, medical care deferred, exercise abandoned. The sacrifice is defended on the grounds that there is no time, and the ledger is settled later in the form of conditions that are expensive to treat and that reduce the father's capacity at precisely the stage when his children need him in a different way.

**4. The sacrifice of the man's own interests and friendships.** The football, the instrument, the club, the army of friends from before the children. The sacrifice is gradual and it is rarely noticed until the man attempts to recover any of it, at which point he discovers that the friendships have lapsed, the interests have no currency, and his own leisure produces guilt.

**5. The sacrifice of emotional availability.** The element that the man least recognises as a sacrifice and that his children register most strongly. The suppression described in Chapter 3 is performed, in the paternal context, as a service: the man conceals his fear, his exhaustion, and his disappointment in order to keep the household steady. Its effect on children is the subject of Section 17.4, and it is the sacrifice most likely to be reproduced by the next generation.

**6. The sacrifice of the man's own account of himself.** The final sacrifice is the least discussed. A father who has given up particular ambitions, particular interests, and particular relationships in the performance of his role may find, at fifty-five, that he can no longer describe who he is apart from his function. Chapter 24 addresses the consequences; the point here is that the sacrifice is made gradually, in individual decisions each of which appears necessary, and that its cumulative effect is not visible until it is established.

### Why the Sacrifices Are Invisible

Five mechanisms account for the invisibility, and each is worth knowing because each is reversible.

**They are performed as duty rather than announced as gift.** A sacrifice that is described is the offering of a gift, and offering a gift creates an obligation in the recipient. Men avoid creating the obligation, and they therefore never mention the sacrifice, which the household accordingly registers as the ordinary state of affairs.

**They have no datable end.** A sacrifice made for a defined period is visible as a sacrifice. A sacrifice made indefinitely becomes the household's normal condition, and the condition is what the household adapts to, not the sacrifice.

**They are invisible in kind.** The sacrifice of ambition and health has no visible manifestation, in contrast to the visible manifestation of the money it produces. The household sees the provision; it does not see the offer that was declined.

**They are offset by visible rewards.** Where the sacrifice produces a vehicle, a house, or a standard of living, the rewards are read as the fruit of the man's choices and the sacrifices are read as the price of what he wanted. The reading is not unreasonable from outside; it is inaccurate, and the inaccuracy is the man's own doing.

**The man himself forgets them.** The most important mechanism, and the most important reason to write the material down. A man who has made a significant sacrifice and has never described it anywhere, including to himself, will gradually reconstitute his history as a series of ordinary decisions. The cost of this reconstruction is precise: the man loses the ability to state his own position, and therefore the ability to renegotiate it.

### The Ledger Problem, in the Paternal Context

Chapter 15 identified the unspoken ledger — the internal account of what a man has given and received — as one of the most destructive mechanisms in a marriage. The paternal version is related and distinct.

Its content is not what the man has given to the household; it is what the man has given up for the children. Its function is protective: it allows the man to justify the arrangement to himself during periods when the arrangement is producing nothing except fatigue. Its risk is that it converts the relationship with the children into an account, and an account held for twenty years produces, in middle age, a specific expectation that the children have not been told about: that they should be grateful, that they should consult him, that they should want what he wanted for them.

The remedy is the same as in Chapter 15 and it requires more courage in this context: the ledger must be stated. The practical form is not a demand for gratitude, which is the form the ledger takes when it is unmanaged. It is a statement of position: *I have done certain things for this family, and I am going to tell you what they were, not because you owe me anything, but because I have never said them aloud and they have been affecting how I behave towards you.*

## 17.3 Fear of Failing as a Father

The fear of failing as a father is more common, and more consequential, than the fear of failing professionally, and it differs in three respects: it cannot be declined, it is assessed by people who are not in a position to assess it, and its subject is somebody else's life.

### The Content of the Fear

Men's accounts make the content specific. A father fears:

- that his children will not be able to compete because of the education he could not fund;
- that his sons will reproduce his limitations and that his daughters will marry men who replicate his failures;
- that his working years were spent on the wrong measure — money rather than presence — and that the error will not be visible until it is permanent;
- that he has transmitted his own fears, his own temper, and his own patterns of silence;
- that a specific event — a separation, a period of unemployment, an illness, a business collapse — has determined his children's outcomes in ways he cannot correct;
- that he will die before his children know the important things about him.

### The Effects on Decisions

The fear distorts decisions in five specific ways, and each is common enough to be worth naming.

**Overwork justified by the fear.** The fear of failing as a provider produces additional work, which produces absence, which produces distance. The man is addressing one component of the role at the cost of another, and the arrangement is not registered as a choice.

**Discipline as over-correction.** A father who fears that his children will fail may apply a standard of discipline, education, and expectation that exceeds their capacity or their interest, in the belief that the standard is protection. The resulting pattern is documented in family research: high expectation without warmth, which is associated with the poorest outcomes of any combination of the two dimensions.

**Conflict with the children's other influences.** Where the father's fear concerns a particular outcome — a child's education, a daughter's marriage, a son's choice of career — the fear produces conflict with the child's mother, with the extended family, and with the child, and each conflict is conducted in the belief that the man is protecting the child's interests.

**Refusal to seek help.** A father who fears failing as a father interprets advice, counselling, or a family conference as an admission of failure. The result is that households with a serious problem — a child's illness, addiction, or difficulty at school — delay intervention for years.

**The projection of the man's own unmet ambitions.** The last and most common distortion. A father whose educational or professional ambitions were abandoned projects them onto a child, frequently onto the one whose capacities resemble his own. The projection is delivered in the language of the child's interest, and it is experienced by the child as a task on somebody else's behalf, which Chapter 11 addresses from the child's side.

### Where the Fear Comes From

**The absence of a model.** Many men are raising children in conditions very different from the ones in which they were raised, and they have no example of what fatherhood would look like in the new conditions.

**The absence of feedback.** As described in Section 17.1: the assessment arrives decades late.

**The public nature of paternal assessment.** A father's performance is assessed by his children's conduct, appearance, schooling, and success, all of which are visible, and by the comparison with other men's children, which is continuous. The mechanism is the same as that of Chapter 11 applied to the next generation.

**The man's own childhood.** A father who was raised under difficulty protects against its repetition, and frequently in a form that reproduces the other half of it: the father who was never praised produces children who are never praised; the father who was never permitted to speak produces children who are not listened to. The transmission of parental patterns is one of the better documented findings in the developmental literature, and the transmission is not a matter of intention.

**The economics.** The final source is structural rather than psychological: the cost of education, accommodation, and health care relative to the earnings available to most fathers. A man who cannot fund the education he believes his children require does not experience this as an economic constraint; he experiences it as a paternal failure, and the fear is grounded in fact.

### What Reduces the Fear

Three measures are available and all three are practical.

**Define what a father is actually responsible for.** The fear is sustained by a definition of responsibility that is unlimited. A workable definition has four items: to keep the children safe, to provide the necessities to the extent the man's capacity allows, to be a person whose conduct they may safely imitate, and to remain a person they know. Every other requirement is a preference, a custom, or an ambition, and a father who can distinguish the four responsibilities from the preferences will find the fear substantially reduced.

**Measure presence rather than only provision.** A man should know the actual number of hours he spends with each child per week, and each child should have a defined, scheduled arrangement with him. Chapter 18 and the exercise at the end of this chapter provide the method, and men consistently report that the measurement, rather than the total hours, is what changes the arrangement.

**Obtain the information the fear is substituting for.** Ask the children. A father who wishes to know the state of his relationship with his child has the option of asking a direct question, and the answers are frequently more informative and less damning than the fear's content. The question is: *what would you like more of from me, and what would you like less of?* The responses should be received without defence, which is the hard part, and acted on in one item.
