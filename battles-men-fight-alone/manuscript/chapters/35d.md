## 35.4 How to Ask

Asking is a technique, and this section supplies the form.

### The Four Parts of a Request

**Part one: the specific thing.** The request concerns a task, not a condition. *I need an hour of your time to look at these accounts* is a request; *I am not coping* is an announcement, and it generally produces either anxiety or advice rather than the assistance. The man should be able to state what he is asking for in a single sentence, in the language of a task.

**Part two: the reason, in one sentence.** The reason supplies the recipient with the context required to respond and does not require the man's full history. *I am in a position with the business that I do not understand and I have been managing it on my own.* The sentence is true, it is brief, and it does not require the material.

**Part three: the scale and the limit.** The request should be bounded: the time, the amount, the number of occasions, the period. *An hour on Saturday; a sum I will repay over six months; four sessions; one meeting.* The part's function is that the recipient can assess what is being asked and can agree to it, which is the arrangement that most requests lack.

**Part four: what the man will do.** The man's own part in the arrangement, stated: the information he will bring, the steps he will take, the schedule of the repayment, the decision he will make, the point at which he will report back. The part converts the request from a dependence into an arrangement, and it is the part that men most frequently omit because it appears to weaken the request. In practice it is the part that most reliably produces agreement.

### The Five Practice Forms

**Asking a friend.** *I need to talk to somebody about something that has been happening with me for about a year. I do not need you to solve it. I need an hour, and I need you not to repeat it. Saturday morning or any evening.*

The form's features: the material is named only as a category; the limit is stated; the requirement is stated specifically and it is small; and the setting is a recurring arrangement rather than an occasion.

**Asking a professional.** *I need an appointment about a matter that has been going on for some time and that I have not dealt with. I am not sure how to describe it in a line on a form.*

The form's features: the presentation is the appointment itself rather than the material; the sentence states that the material exists; and no diagnosis, history, or justification is required at that point. Where the man cannot make even this statement, the physical presentation is the available route, as Chapter 34.5 establishes.

**Asking an employer or an institution.** *I need to request X: the arrangement, the change of duties, the instalment plan, the leave, the assessment, the training. Here is the position in writing, here is what I am proposing, and here is what I will commit to.*

The form's features: the request is made in writing, with the position and the proposal; the employer is not asked to manage the man's state; the arrangement proposed is specific; and the request is made before the man's performance has been affected where that is possible.

**Asking a household member.** The four parts become the four sections of Chapter 34.6: the material, the reason for the silence, the effects already visible, and what happens next. The specific addition here is the request itself, which should be stated explicitly rather than left to be inferred: *I need you to know this, and I need to ask you for X.* The household's most frequent complaint about a man's disclosure is not the material but the absence of a request: the man has supplied the burden and left the household with nothing to do with it.

**Asking a man in a position to assist.** *I am asking you for one specific thing. Here is the position. Here is what I am doing about it. Here is what I will do with what you provide.* The form's features: the request is explicit that it is a request rather than an account of the man's troubles; the position is supplied, which is what a person with the capacity to assist actually requires; and the man's own part is stated, which is the arrangement that protects the relationship, as Chapter 26.5's rules establish.

### The Five Errors

**1. The vague request.** *Can you help me?* The recipient cannot assess it, and the common outcome is a general expression of support and the absence of assistance.

**2. The unbounded request.** The request for assistance *whenever you can* or the account of a difficulty with no stated requirement. The form's outcome is the deferral of the matter and the recipient's discomfort.

**3. The request that is an announcement.** The statement of the man's position with no requirement attached. The outcome is that the recipient must construct a response, which explains the frequency of the two responses men report: advice and reassurance, neither of which is assistance.

**4. The apologetic request.** The request that begins with the man's account of why he should not be asking. The form supplies the recipient with the man's own assessment of the request's illegitimacy, and it is frequently the mechanism by which the recipient declines.

**5. The request delivered at the worst possible moment.** The material delivered in a crisis, in a public setting, or to a person who is themselves in difficulty. The form's outcome is generally the arrangement described in Chapter 34.3's first category, in which the man's disclosure is received by somebody who cannot hold it, and the man concludes that asking does not work.

### The Timing

Two findings from practice, and they are worth recording.

**The first step should be a request the man can afford to have refused.** The graded form of Chapter 26.5 applied to the asking: the small request, the available recipient, the arrangement the man can survive. The finding is that the small request generally succeeds, which supplies the evidence that changes the man's assessment of the larger ones.

**The material should be delivered in an activity rather than at an appointment.** The material of Chapter 4.6 in the asking context: the drive, the walk, the workshop, the errand. The setting's function is that the man is not required to hold the recipient's eye for the duration of the material, which is the specific difficulty that the case material in Chapters 26 and 30 identifies.

## 35.5 Asking for What You Need

The chapter's final substantive section concerns the accuracy of the request, since a large proportion of the refusals men experience concern requests that were not what the man required.

### The Reduced Request

The most common form of the inaccurate request, and its mechanism is the wish to minimise the request's cost. The man requires money and asks for advice; he requires an arrangement and asks for information; he requires the household's participation and asks for tolerance; he requires treatment and says that he is tired.

The form's outcome is that the request is refused in the form in which it was made. The recipient answers the question that was asked, the man receives an answer that does not address the position, and the man's conclusion — that asking does not work — is drawn from a request that was not the one he needed to make.

**The correction.** The man should write what he actually requires and then, opposite it, what he is intending to say, and should identify the difference. The practice's finding is that the difference is frequently large and that the reduction was made in order to protect the recipient, to preserve the man's position, or to keep the request within a range the man could imagine being answered.

### The Inflated Request

The opposite error, and it is common in men who ask once: the request is made at the level of the man's whole position rather than the specific matter. *I need help with my life* is a request that cannot be answered; *I need four sessions with a practitioner and one person who will sit with me on Sundays* can be.

**The correction.** The decomposition: the whole position, divided into the specific matters, with an identifiable source and an identified requirement for each. The practice is the same as the inventory of Chapter 33.6: the written list, the item-by-item matching, and the arrangement for each item.

### The Request That Transfers the Load

The third form: the request delivers the man's material to a recipient who is required to carry it indefinitely. The recipient is the elderly parent, the child, the person in their own crisis, the subordinate, the person whose position requires them to act on the material.

**The correction.** The assessment of the recipient's capacity before the request, in the form of Chapter 34.6's second decision and with the same test: whether the recipient will be required to carry the material indefinitely, or whether they can place it. Where the recipient cannot place it, the professional or the group is the correct source, and the request to the household is limited to the fact.

### The Request Made Before the Man Can State It

The fourth form, and it is the most common in the material: the man knows that something is wrong and cannot yet state what it is. The available requests, in ascending order: the practical request (the appointment, the assistance with a specific task, the arrangement); the request for company (the standing arrangement, the activity); the request for an assessment (*I do not know what the matter is; I need somebody to establish it*); and the request for the arrangement (the group, the professional relationship, the supervision).

The chapter's instruction is that all four are legitimate and that the third is frequently the most useful: a man who can state only *I do not know what is wrong* has made a complete request to a general practitioner and a sufficient request to a peer arrangement.

## 35.6 When You Are Asked

The chapter's final section concerns the other direction, and it is included for a practical reason: the men who read this book are frequently the men who are asked, and a request that is badly received is the mechanism by which men stop asking.

### The Four Rules

**1. Receive it in the form it was delivered.** The man will frequently make a request that is not the request he needs, as Section 35.5 establishes. The recipient's task is to take what was delivered and to find what is beneath it: the question that is not the question, the complaint that is a request, the practical matter that is standing in for a material. The instrument is the follow-up question: *is it the accounts, or is it something else as well?*

**2. Do not supply advice as the first response.** The strongest finding in the practice material on men's disclosure: the man who is given advice receives the message that his position has a solution he had not thought of, which is generally inaccurate and which forecloses the delivery of the material. The correct first response is the acknowledgement and the request for more: *tell me what it is like from your side.*

**3. State what you can give, and state it as an offer rather than as a limitation.** The form: the specific thing the recipient can provide, with the time or the limit, and the alternative where the recipient cannot supply what was asked. The form's function is that the man's request is answered rather than deflected, and it establishes the arrangement that can actually be maintained.

**4. Keep the arrangement rather than the occasion.** The finding that most reliably determines whether the man will ask again: the recipient's contact over the following weeks. The single conversation, however well conducted, is generally insufficient; the arrangement — the calls, the meetings, the practical presence, the reporting back — is what the man's position requires.

### The Two Prohibitions

**Do not disclose the material.** The man's request is a confidence, and its disclosure terminates the arrangement and frequently produces the consequences the man's barriers predicted. The exceptions are the categories of Chapter 34.3, and the position there is stated plainly: a confidence does not hold where a life is at risk or where the material concerns violence or the safety of a person who cannot protect himself.

**Do not require the man to be grateful.** The requirement, frequently imposed in the form of a general expectation, converts the arrangement into a favour and establishes the obligation of Section 35.2 in the relationship. The man who is received without the requirement of gratitude will return.
