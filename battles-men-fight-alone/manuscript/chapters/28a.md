# The Battles of Loss, Regret, and Broken Dreams {.part num="VIII" img="/images/part8.jpg" summary="Confronting painful experiences that cannot simply be forgotten"}

# The Dreams That Died Quietly {.chapter num="28" short="The Dreams That Died Quietly"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Identify the categories of dream that men carry, and distinguish between a dream, a plan, and an obligation.
2. Explain the seven processes by which a dream ends without a decision, without a funeral, and without anybody being informed.
3. Name the particular form of grief that belongs to a dream that died quietly, and explain why it is frequently not recognised as grief by the man or by his household.
4. Assess the cost of holding a dead dream open, and the different cost of closing it.
5. Apply a structured reckoning to the life that was built in place of the dream, without dismissing either the loss or the achievement.
6. Use the exercise at the end of the chapter to decide, for each dream, whether it is live, closed, or genuinely pending — and to act accordingly.
:::

## Introduction

Almost every man carries the record of at least one thing he intended to become and did not become. It may be the education that was interrupted, the business that was never started, the country he meant to leave for, the work he meant to do, the house, the journey, the craft, the role, or the life he described to himself at nineteen and quietly stopped describing at thirty-five.

This chapter concerns those dreams, and specifically the ones that died quietly: the ones that were not abandoned in a decision and were not ended by an event that anybody observed. They were set down. They were postponed with a date that was never fixed. They were overtaken by obligations that were real, by money that was not available, by a father's requirement, by a wife's fear, by a failure, by a child's needs, by the slow accumulation of months in which the dream was not advanced.

The chapter is written on four commitments.

The first is that this is a loss, and that it should be named as one. Men are frequently told that dreams are for the young, that a man should be practical, and that a man who still speaks of the thing he meant to do is indulging himself. The instruction is applied by men to themselves more harshly than by anybody else, and its effect is that the loss is carried without being named. An unnamed loss behaves as an unnamed loss always behaves: it is present continuously, it is not processed, and it is available to be triggered by the smallest event — a former classmate's announcement, a child's decision, a song, a road.

The second is that the dream must be distinguished from the fantasy. A proportion of what men carry is not a dream but an image: a picture of a life with no plan attached, frequently adopted from a culture's account of success, and its loss is not a loss at all, because it was never held as a possibility. The distinction is developed in Section 28.2, and it matters because men frequently expend considerable effort grieving an image while neglecting a real dream.

The third is that the reckoning must include the life that was actually built. The chapter will not treat the man's obligations as the enemy of his life. In the material on men's dreams there is a strong cultural tendency to treat every abandonment as a tragedy produced by duty, and it is false to the accounts men give, in which the household, the children, the trade, and the position are frequently things they would not surrender. The work of this chapter is an accurate accounting of both, which is what Section 28.6 constructs.

The fourth is the practical objective. A dream that is kept open is expensive: it occupies the mental space of a live possibility while offering none of the activity of one, and it supplies a continuous standard against which the man's actual life is measured and found deficient. This is the mechanism described in Chapter 7, and it is the reason that a dream must eventually be classified — as live, as closed, or as genuinely pending — and acted upon in the appropriate manner. Closing a dream is a loss and it is also a relief, and the two are not in conflict.
