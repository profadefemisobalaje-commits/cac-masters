## 45.6 Beginning a New Chapter with Hope

The section's subject is the beginning, and it requires a careful treatment because the word hope is used in the culture to mean two different things, and only one of them is the material of this book.

**The first meaning is optimism.** The expectation that the man's position will improve, that the difficulty will resolve, that the outcome will be favourable. Optimism is a disposition and it is not within a man's control, and the case material finds that men who conduct their arrangements on optimism are frequently unprepared for the outcome that does not arrive.

**The second meaning is the one this section employs: hope as the conduct of a man's position in the belief that the conduct is worth undertaking, independently of the outcome's probability.** The definition is stricter than optimism and it is more useful, because it does not depend on a prediction. The man who conducts the arrangements of Section 45.7 does not know whether his marriage will be repaired, whether his business will recover, whether his son will speak to him, or whether his health will hold. He knows that the conduct is worth undertaking, and the case material's finding is that this knowledge is sufficient to begin.

The section therefore does not promise outcomes. It states the conditions of beginning, and it supplies the four that the material supports.

### The First Condition: The Position Is Stated

A man cannot begin a chapter whose position he has not stated. The statement is the material of Chapter 42.6's first step and Chapter 45.7's first table, and its components are the six domains: the money, the work, the marriage, the household, the health, and the standing. Each is stated in figures where the figures exist and in specific terms where they do not.

The finding is that the statement is frequently the whole of the beginning, because the man who has stated his position accurately discovers that it is smaller, larger, or differently arranged than his account of it, and each of the three discoveries alters what he does next.

### The Second Condition: The Parties Are Named

A man cannot begin a chapter alone, and the beginning requires the naming of the parties: the one person who knows the whole, the household who knows the arrangements, the professional who holds the domain that requires one, the two or three parties who would be called in a difficulty, and the party to whom the man is himself an instrument.

The finding is that the naming is frequently conducted before the parties have been informed, and that the informing is the act. The case material records men who wrote the list in an evening and made the first call within the week, and men who wrote the list and made no call for three years. The list is not the beginning. The call is.

### The Third Condition: The Instruments Are Identified

The man identifies, for each domain that is not in order, the instrument he requires: the professional, the treatment, the arrangement, the conversation, the qualification, the party who will read the position, the procedure he has not used. The identification is the material of Chapter 35.4's four forms of help, and its finding is that men frequently identify the instrument correctly and delay its use by months, which is the material of Chapter 35.2's accounting and is addressed by the date.

### The Fourth Condition: The Date Is Fixed

The beginning requires a date, and the date is not a figure of speech. The case material's finding is that a man's intentions are converted into arrangements by the fixing of a date and by the statement of the date to a party who will ask about it.

The date is fixed for the first act, and the first act is small: the appointment that is made, the call that is placed, the letter that is written, the statement that is made to the household, the professional who is engaged, the arrangement that is established. The date is recorded, and the party who will ask about it is named.

### What Hope Is Not Required to Be

The section closes with four statements that correct the reader's likely expectations, because the case material finds that they are the most frequent causes of the abandonment of the work.

**Hope is not required to be a feeling.** A man may begin without the feeling of hope, and the case material's finding is that the feeling frequently arrives after the conduct rather than before it. The man who waits for the feeling frequently waits for years, and the waiting is the material of Chapter 7's fear of failure in its most disguised form.

**Hope is not required to be confident.** The man does not know what will happen. He knows what he will do, and the confidence in the conduct is sufficient. The material of Chapter 32.4's authority without omniscience applies to the man's own life as much as to his leadership.

**Hope is not required to be constant.** The case material records men who began, faltered, resumed, and continued, and the faltering is not a failure of the beginning. The material of Chapter 38.5's stages applies: the reconstruction is conducted in stages and the stages are frequently interrupted.

**Hope is not required to be public.** The man's beginning does not require an announcement, a declaration, a public commitment, or a testimony. The case material's finding, recorded in Chapter 42.2's fourth cost, is that the public commitment and the private non-conduct are frequently conducted together, and that the man's beginning is more reliable when it is conducted quietly and recorded privately.

## 45.8 Illustrative Case: The Tuesday Afternoon

The case is composite and illustrative, and it is deliberately undramatic. It is constructed from the case material of this book and is presented as the book's closing example.

### The Man

Adebayo Ogunleye was fifty-three years old and was the managing director of a small engineering supplies company that he had founded at thirty-six. He was married to Kemi, a secondary school principal, and had three children: a daughter of twenty-four who was completing a postgraduate degree, a son of twenty-one who was in his final year, and a daughter of seventeen who was in her last year of secondary school.

His father had died at fifty-nine of a heart attack, having conducted a period of business difficulty in complete silence for two years. Adebayo had repeated the account of his father as a strong man who had carried a great deal. He had, in the two years before the case, begun to hear the account differently, and he had not said so to anybody.

### The Position

The company employed eleven people and had an annual turnover that had been declining for three years. In the year before the case, the company had lost its principal customer, which had represented forty-one per cent of its revenue, and Adebayo had replaced approximately half of the revenue with three smaller customers at a lower margin.

The company's bank facility was three months from its review, and the company's position depended on the facility's renewal. Adebayo had not told Kemi the size of the facility, the identity of the guarantor, or the fact that the guarantee was personally secured against the family house.

His health had changed. He had been waking at three or four in the morning for approximately eight months. He had been drinking two or three bottles of beer on most evenings, which he had not done before, and he had stopped attending the gym in the previous year. He had not had a medical examination in four years. His father had died at fifty-nine.

His marriage was not in difficulty in the sense the phrase usually carries. It had become an arrangement of two people conducting parallel responsibilities with efficiency and without material. Kemi had twice asked him, in the previous year, whether something was wrong, and he had said that he was tired.

He had two friends. One, Emeka, had been his colleague at his first employer and had telephoned every month for eleven years. In the previous eight months, Adebayo had not returned four of the calls. The other, a man named Tunde, had asked him in the sixth month whether he wanted to talk about the business, and Adebayo had said that there was nothing to talk about.

### The Occasion

The occasion was not a collapse. It was a sentence.

On a Tuesday afternoon in the eleventh month, Adebayo attended a meeting at his bank. The meeting was routine and it concerned the facility's review. At the end of it, the bank's officer, a woman of about forty named Mrs. Adio, asked him a question that was not on the agenda. She asked whether he had considered what would happen to his household if the facility were not renewed.

Adebayo had prepared an answer. He had used it before. The answer was that the position was manageable and that he did not anticipate a difficulty.

He did not give the answer. He stated, in Mrs. Adio's office, at about ten past three on a Tuesday afternoon, that he had not considered it, that he had not told his wife the size of the facility, and that he had been waking at three in the morning for eight months.

The statement took perhaps ninety seconds. Mrs. Adio's response was ordinary and it was the response the case material records most frequently as decisive. She did not express alarm. She stated that this was useful information, that the review's outcome was not affected by it, and that the bank had a process for exactly this position. She then asked him whether he had a party who could read the position with him. He said that he did not. She gave him the name of an adviser who was not connected to the bank, and she suggested that he speak to his wife.

### The Tuesday Evening

Adebayo did not go home. He drove to Emeka's office and waited for him in the car park for forty minutes, which he had not planned. When Emeka came out, Adebayo stated that he needed to talk, and Emeka's response was to get into the car.

The conversation lasted two hours. Its content, recorded later, had four components. Adebayo stated the company's position, with the figures. He stated the guarantee, and the house. He stated the drinking and the waking. And he stated the sentence that the case material treats as the case's centre: *I have been behaving like my father, and I have known it for two years.*

Emeka's conduct was the material of Chapter 44.5's five functions. He was present. He supplied information, including the name of an accountant who had conducted a similar restructuring two years earlier. He accompanied Adebayo to the first meeting with the adviser three days later. He stated his limit: he did not know enough about the guarantee to advise, and the lawyer was required. And he continued: the monthly calls resumed, and Emeka added a second call each month for the following year.

### The Household

Adebayo stated the position to Kemi on the Saturday. The statement took fifty minutes and included the figures, the guarantee, the drinking, and the waking.

Kemi's response was not the one he had predicted. She did not express shock at the position, and she did not respond with anger at the concealment until the following day. Her first response was a question: *for how long.* His answer was two years. Her second question was whether he had seen a doctor. He said no.

Her response the following day was the one the case material treats as the household's contribution. She stated that she was angry about the two years, that she understood what he had been doing, and that she would not conduct the arrangement in which she was informed after the consequences had arrived. She then stated the household's position from her own side, which Adebayo had not known in its detail: the household's expenditure, her own pension, the amount that could be released from their daughter's school fees, and the fact that her brother had offered a loan eighteen months earlier which she had not mentioned because she had not known the position.

The household conducted three arrangements within the month. The first was the medical examination, which Adebayo attended, and which resulted in treatment for hypertension and a referral for the sleep. The second was the adviser's engagement, which produced a restructuring plan for the facility. The third was the household's ten minutes on Sunday evening, which the household conducted for the following year and which the seventeen-year-old daughter, according to the case material, used first.

### The Year After

The case's outcome is stated with the restraint the material requires. The facility was renewed on restructured terms. The company's revenue in the following year was approximately level with the preceding year. The house was not lost. The guarantor arrangement was altered, at a cost, so that the household's house was no longer secured.

Adebayo's drinking reduced to two or three occasions in a month, and the waking reduced but did not cease, and was treated. His medical examination produced a diagnosis of hypertension that was being managed, and a further examination in the second year produced no new findings.

The marriage did not become a different marriage. It became a marriage in which two people stated their positions, at intervals, and in which the parallel arrangement was interrupted. The case material records Kemi's account in the second year: *we argue more than we did, and I know him better than I did.*

The two friendships changed. Emeka's monthly calls continued, and their content changed. Tunde, who had asked in the sixth month and had been told that there was nothing to talk about, received a call from Adebayo in the third month after the Tuesday, in which Adebayo stated that he had been wrong to decline, and stated what had been happening. The case material records Tunde's response in a single sentence: *I have been waiting eleven months for this call.*

The daughter of seventeen, at the end of the year, said something that the case material records as the case's most significant measure and that is reported here without commentary: *Baba, you have started telling us things before they happen instead of after.*

### The Finding

The case's finding is stated in four parts, and the fourth is the book's closing statement.

**The first part is that the occasion was ordinary.** There was no crisis, no collapse, no diagnosis, no ultimatum. There was a question at a routine meeting and a man who did not give the answer he had prepared. The case material's finding is that this is the ordinary form of the day, and that men who wait for the crisis to conduct the statement frequently conduct it after the consequences have arrived, when the statement is no longer available in its useful form.

**The second part is that the first statement was the hardest and it was not the most important.** The statement to Mrs. Adio took ninety seconds and produced the instrument. The statement to Emeka took two hours and produced the accompaniment. The statement to Kemi took fifty minutes and produced the household's participation. The case material's finding is that the first statement's value is that it makes the second possible, and that men frequently believe they must conduct the largest statement first and therefore conduct none.

**The third part is that the parties were already there.** Mrs. Adio was a bank officer who asked a question outside her agenda. Emeka had been telephoning for eleven years. Tunde had asked directly. Kemi had asked twice. The case material's finding is that the position described in Section 45.2 is the ordinary one: the support was available, and the man's conduct had maintained its absence.

**The fourth part is the book's closing statement.** Adebayo did not become a different kind of man. He remained reserved, private, and disinclined to discuss his interior life at length. He continued to run his company, to be accountable for eleven employees, to carry his household's obligations, and to conduct the difficulties that his position supplied. What changed was not his temperament and not his standard. What changed was that on a Tuesday afternoon in the eleventh month, he stopped conducting his position as though its accurate statement were a defeat, and stated it to a party who could help. The rest of the case is the consequence of ninety seconds.

## 45.9 Chapter Summary

**Chapter 45 in brief.** Self-reliance is a competence with a domain, and the domain has been expanded beyond its usefulness. The expansion has four mechanisms: success, which hides the failure; the absence of a party, which makes the arrangement the only one available; the script, which makes self-sufficiency an identity rather than a competence; and the cost of a previous request, which governs twenty subsequent years. The competence becomes a liability at the point where the man's position is no longer subject to correction, and the point is identified by five tests: the unexamined decision, the unstated figure, the unregistered change, the grief without an audience, and the collapse without a witness. What is released is the requirement of exclusive conduct. What is retained is the responsibility, the standard, and the obligation.

The difficulty is not generally the absence of support but the refusal of support that is available. The six reasons are the imposition, the incomprehension, the change of standing, the burden, the futility, and the identity, and each is answered by a distinction rather than a reassurance. Beneath them are four fears: dependence, exposure, the party's alarm, and the insufficiency of the difficulty. Acceptance is not dependence: dependence is an arrangement in which a party conducts a function the man cannot conduct, and acceptance is an arrangement in which a party supplies an instrument the man uses. The distinction is tested by who holds the decision, what remains if the party withdraws, and whether the man's capacity has increased or decreased. A support that is specific is an instrument and can be accepted; a support that is general is a rescue and must be declined.

A limitation is a boundary that is not within the man's power to remove and is not a matter of effort. The four categories are the body, the capacity, the past, and what others will give. The three false settlements are denial, resignation, and bargaining. The reconciliation has four components: the accurate statement, the inventory of what remains, the reallocation of the arrangements, and the grief for what the limitation has cost. The reconciliation does not reduce the standard. It relocates it.

Purpose is the answer to what the man's position is for, and it requires a position, a contribution that is not his own benefit, and an outcome that can be observed. Its four foundations are the household, the work, the community, and the transmission. Belonging is the condition in which parties hold the man's accurate position, their estimate of him is not conditional on a favourable appearance, and he has obligations to them that he conducts. Its construction has five steps: the man's own conduct as the instrument, the setting, the repetition, the disclosure, and the obligation.

The vision of manhood retains six obligations and removes six additions: a man provides, and the provision is conducted with the household's knowledge; a man regulates and states; a man is capable and uses the instruments that competence requires; a man is accountable and increases the capacity of those he answers for; a man's work is real and the man is not his work; and a man endures, and he does not endure alone.

Beginning requires four conditions: the position stated, the parties named, the instruments identified, and the date fixed. Hope, in this chapter's sense, is the conduct of a man's position in the belief that the conduct is worth undertaking, independently of the outcome's probability. It is not required to be a feeling, to be confident, to be constant, or to be public.

::: summary
**The chapter's central claim.** The day a man realises that he does not have to fight alone is not the day he understands it. It is the day something in his arrangements changes: a statement made to a party who can hold it, an instrument accepted rather than declined, a limit stated rather than denied, a call placed rather than intended. The act is small, it is frequently conducted on an ordinary afternoon, and it is available to the reader this week. The battle does not become smaller. The number of parties in it changes, and the change is the whole of the book's argument.
