## 15.3 Causes of Marital Dissatisfaction

Men frequently describe their marriages as unsatisfying without being able to identify what is missing, and the vagueness prevents action. The classification below is intended to convert a general dissatisfaction into a specific finding. There are eight causes, and they have different remedies.

**1. An unequal distribution of load.** One partner is carrying materially more of the household's work, financial management, child care, and emotional labour than the other, and the imbalance is not acknowledged. Its remedies are structural: the division of tasks, the reassignment of responsibilities, the reduction of an external obligation. This cause is the most common and the most tractable, and it is the one whose remedy men most often resist, because it requires the reassignment of obligations the man regards as his own.

**2. An absence of intimacy.** The absence of conversation, physical affection, and one-to-one time. Its remedy is arrangement rather than disposition: scheduled time, a change in routine, and the removal of the conditions that prevent it.

**3. A conflict of priorities.** The struggle over priority described in Section 15.1. Its remedy is a written and specific allocation, as described in Section 15.4.

**4. A specific and unaddressed injury.** An infidelity, a humiliation, a financial concealment, a decision made unilaterally, a failure to attend a significant occasion, an intervention by the man's family. Where an injury of this kind has not been addressed and repaired, it operates as a permanent background condition, and no amount of general improvement will affect it. Its remedy is Chapter 13's material on betrayal and Chapter 16's on the repair of injury: acknowledgement, accountability, and time.

**5. A difference in temperament or expectation that will not change.** Some differences are not problems to be solved. They are features of two people, and they include differences in sociability, in orderliness, in religious observance, in ambition, and in the desired level of family involvement. Its remedy is accommodation: an explicit agreement about how the difference will be managed, rather than a continued attempt to convert the other person.

**6. An external strain acting on both parties.** Financial difficulty, illness, a bereavement, employment insecurity, a difficult child, a burden from the extended family. Its remedy is the joint management of the strain, which requires it to be named as a shared condition rather than experienced as a private problem by each party, which is what most commonly occurs.

**7. An untreated condition in either party.** Depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, alcohol dependence, a hormonal or medical condition, or a chronic pain condition. This cause is the most frequently missed, and it is the one with the largest specific remedy, because treatment frequently improves the marriage more than any relational intervention could. Chapter 36 addresses men's treatment, and men should consider that their wives' conditions fall under the same principle.

**8. An incompatibility at the level of the marriage's purpose.** The least tractable of the eight. Two people may have fundamentally different expectations of what a marriage is for — companionship versus dynasty, partnership versus hierarchy, an exclusive unit versus a node in a wider family system. Where such a difference exists, the practical objective is not agreement but an explicit and workable arrangement, and where it cannot be reached, the decision framework in Section 14.6 applies.

The practical use of this classification is direct: a man who has identified the cause can identify the remedy, and a man who has identified neither will continue to address occasions.

## 15.4 A Communication Framework for Serious Marital Disagreements

This section sets out a method that has been assembled from the practice that appears repeatedly in the marital research literature and in clinical practice. It is written for serious disagreements rather than for daily irritations, and it is set out as rules because rules are easier to follow when the temperature rises.

### Before the Conversation

**Choose one subject.** A single matter, stated in one sentence, agreed in advance. Where a man approaches his wife with a list, the exchange will become a scoring of the list.

**Set the conditions.** A time when both are available and nobody is tired, hungry, or late for something; a place where children cannot hear; a defined duration of about forty-five minutes; and no audience.

**Decide the objective.** One of three: to inform, to obtain agreement on a specific change, or to understand her position. Deciding the objective in advance is what prevents a conversation from becoming a general assessment of the marriage.

**Prepare one specific proposal.** Not a complaint but a proposal: what the man will do, what he is asking the other person to do, and the period in which it will be tried. Proposals are the units in which marriages change.

### During the Conversation

**Begin with the specific situation and the man's own position.** *When my mother came for six weeks in July, I did not ask you whether that was workable, and I should have.*

**State the finding rather than the accusation.** *We have not had an evening together in about four months* rather than *you never want to spend time with me.*

**Ask, and listen to the answer, before defending.** *What is it like for you?* followed by silence while the answer is given in full.

**Do not introduce the past.** The rule is strict: the subject is the present arrangement, and previous incidents may be referenced only where a single example is required. Where the past is admitted in quantity, both parties will leave with their positions confirmed and nothing changed.

**Do not score.** Statements of the form *I do this and you do that* convert the conversation into an assessment, and assessments produce defence rather than agreement.

**Allow the silence.** The most important of the rules in practice. Where a point has been made, the agreement to stop talking for a minute or two is what allows the other person to consider it. Men who fill the silence with further evidence are the men who describe their conversations as unproductive.

### Ending the Conversation

**Agree on one thing.** Not a general understanding; one specific change, with a date and a person attached. The observed pattern in successful marital change is a large number of small, specific agreements rather than a small number of general ones.

**Agree the review.** *We will look at this again in a month.* Reviews convert agreements into arrangements and prevent the drift described in Section 15.2.

**Close it deliberately.** A sentence, a physical gesture, an ordinary activity together. The ending matters because it establishes that the conversation was a matter between two people rather than a verdict on the relationship.

**Record it.** A note, kept privately, of the agreement and the review date. Men should note the guidance in Chapter 16 about record-keeping and its risks, and confine the record to the specific agreements.

### Rules of Engagement for High-Conflict Moments

When the temperature rises, four rules prevent permanent damage. They should be agreed in advance, in a calm period, as rules rather than as accusations.

1. **No character statements.** The subject is behaviour. *You did not tell me* is admissible; *you are a liar* is not.
2. **No audience, and no third party brought in.** Relatives, children, and friends are not participants, and, in particular, the wife's family and the man's family are not to be used as allies. Where an ally is required, the ally should be a counsellor.
3. **A stopping rule.** Either party may end the conversation by stating the fact rather than by leaving the room: *I am not able to continue this well; let us continue on Thursday.* The rule works only if it may be used without penalty and if the continuation actually occurs.
4. **No irreversible acts.** Decisions made in a conflict — about the household, the money, the children, or the marriage — are provisional. Nothing is implemented for a defined period.

## 15.5 Responsibility, Sacrifice, and the Point of Injury

Sacrifice is intrinsic to marriage. A married man's life contains a large quantity of work performed for the benefit of other people, and this is generally a source of meaning, not of injury. The question this section addresses is the point at which sacrifice becomes damaging — to the marriage and to the man.

### Four Kinds of Sacrifice

**Sacrifice that is chosen, understood, and reviewed.** A man works additional hours for a defined period to clear a debt, and both parties know the period and the reason. This kind of sacrifice is sustainable and it strengthens the marriage, largely because the requirement is shared.

**Sacrifice that is chosen but not disclosed.** The man carries something the household does not know about: a debt, an obligation to a relative, a health condition, an ambition, a failure. This sacrifice is the material of Chapter 9, and its effect on a marriage is corrosive in a specific way: the man's contribution becomes invisible, and his becoming unappreciated is the direct consequence of his own decision not to be known.

**Sacrifice that is imposed and not negotiated.** The requirement arises from the environment rather than from the couple's decision: a parent's illness, a sibling's obligation, the community's expectation, the employer's demand. Where this arrangement persists, the couple's shared life is substantially determined by parties outside it, and resistance is expressed indirectly, in the form of the struggles described in Section 15.1.

**Sacrifice that has become an identity.** The most dangerous of the four. The man defines himself by what he gives up, and the giving becomes continuous, unmeasured, and increasingly resented. Its features: the man's exhaustion; his sense of being unappreciated; his comparison of himself with other men who "do less"; and the tendency of the household to accept the arrangement as permanent. Its specific consequence is described in Chapter 12, and its marital consequence is that the man holds an unspoken ledger, which produces the pattern of sudden, disproportionate anger described below.

### The Unspoken Ledger

This is worth naming separately because it is the most destructive and least understood of the mechanisms in this section. A man who sacrifices without the sacrifice being known, acknowledged, and reviewed begins to accumulate an internal account: what he has given, what it has cost, and what he has received in return. The account is not disclosed, and it is periodically consulted.

The consequences are recognisable. The man becomes more sensitive to small instances of ingratitude. He becomes disproportionately angry about minor matters, since the response is calibrated to the accumulated balance rather than to the event. And he becomes increasingly isolated, because the material that would correct his account is hidden from the person who holds it.

The remedy is direct and it requires the ledger to be stated: what the man has been carrying, what he has not said about it, and what he would like the arrangement to be. Men consistently report discomfort at the prospect of describing their contributions, on the grounds that a man should not need recognition. The discomfort is worth crossing: the sentence *I have been carrying X for two years and I have not told you, and it is affecting how I behave at home* is the standard and effective form.

### The Test for Injurious Sacrifice

Four questions determine whether a sacrifice has crossed into injury. Where three or more are answered in the affirmative, the arrangement requires attention.

1. **Is it permanent rather than time-limited?** Has a defined period ever been set, and has it been reviewed?
2. **Is it invisible?** Could the man's household state what he has given up in the last two years, in specific terms?
3. **Is it producing resentment?** Does the man find himself keeping account, or reacting to small matters with disproportionate anger?
4. **Is it harming the person making it?** Are there measurable costs: health, sleep, presence, work, sanity, or the capacity to enjoy anything?

Where the answer to question four is affirmative, the guidance in Chapter 12 applies and should be followed before any further relational work is attempted, because a man operating at the point of collapse cannot conduct a difficult marital conversation well.
