# Carrying Other People's Problems {.chapter num="33" short="Carrying Other People's Problems"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Identify the role of the man who carries other people's problems, and describe the forms it takes in families, communities, workplaces, and friendships.
2. Explain how the role is acquired without a decision, and why it is rarely questioned by the man who occupies it or by the people who benefit from it.
3. Analyse the five costs of the role, including the accumulation of other people's material, the erosion of the man's own resources, the resentment that cannot be stated, and the transfer of the cost to his household.
4. Distinguish between the obligations a man actually owes and the demands that have attached themselves to him, using the four tests in Section 33.4.
5. Apply the distinction between assistance and rescue, and recognise the pattern in which a man's help maintains the difficulty it is intended to solve.
6. Construct arrangements that distribute the load instead of concentrating it: the referral, the shared arrangement, the family's own capacity, and the professional route.
:::

## Introduction

There is a position that men occupy in families, communities, workplaces, and networks of friends, and it is rarely named: the man who is everyone's solution.

He is the person who is called when a relative's fees are unpaid; when a cousin needs a place to stay; when a brother's court matter requires a guarantor; when a neighbour's son needs a reference; when a colleague's marriage is failing; when the association's accounts are in disorder; when a death leaves a household without arrangements; when a young man in the trade has nowhere to go. He is the person consulted on property, employment, schooling, health, disputes, marriages, and burials, and he is the person who is expected to know what to do and to be available to do it.

This chapter concerns him, and it is written on four commitments.

The first is that the role is a genuine good. Men who occupy it are the load-bearing structures of their families and communities, and the work they perform is essential; in settings where formal provision is thin, they are frequently the only provision. Nothing in this chapter proposes that a man should withdraw from his obligations, decline his responsibilities, or become unavailable to the people who depend on him. The chapter's subject is the specific arrangement in which the role has become unbounded, unexamined, and carried by one person, and the arrangements that allow it to be carried without the man's collapse.

The second is that the role is acquired rather than chosen. No man in the case material of this chapter decided to become the family's solution; the position developed through a sequence of small demands, each of which he met because he could. The absence of a decision is what makes the role resistant to examination: there is no moment to which the man can point and no boundary that was ever agreed, and the arrangement is therefore experienced as a fact about the family rather than as a pattern that was constructed.

The third is that the cost is real and is systematically underestimated, including by the man. The accumulation of other people's material is the fifth burden of Chapter 31, and this chapter treats it as a principal subject: a man who holds the confidences, the difficulties, the debts, the disputes, and the futures of forty people is carrying forty pieces of material, and there is generally no arrangement anywhere in which his own is held.

The fourth is that the correction is not withdrawal but distribution. The final section concerns the construction of arrangements in which the load is carried by more than one person: the relative who can be brought in, the family's own council, the professional, the institution, the man who can be trained to carry part of it. The chapter's objective is that the man becomes one of several load-bearing points rather than the only one, which is a benefit to everybody including the people he supports.
