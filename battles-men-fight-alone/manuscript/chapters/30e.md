## 30.7 When Grief Becomes a Condition

The chapter must supply the distinction between the course of grief and the point at which a grief requires treatment, because the material in this chapter is the material most likely to go unattended and because men in this position frequently attribute a treatable condition to the loss itself.

### What Ordinary Grief Does

The literature describes a course that includes the following, all of which are ordinary and none of which indicates illness: the material's intensity varies and frequently increases at certain points; it arrives in waves; sleep is disturbed; appetite changes; the man's concentration is reduced; he is irritable, flat, or restless in periods; he experiences the physical symptoms associated with distress; and the material is engaged by reminders. The course generally eases over months, with recurrence at anniversaries and milestones, and this pattern continues, in a reduced form, indefinitely.

### The Six Patterns That Require Assessment

**1. The material does not vary at all.** Constant intensity with no waves, no relief, and no capacity for the man to be engaged by anything else, sustained without change, is the pattern that most frequently indicates a condition requiring treatment.

**2. The duration exceeds the ordinary course without any reduction.** Where the man's functioning has been substantially impaired for a period of a year or more after the loss, with no reduction in intensity, an assessment should be obtained.

**3. The functioning has collapsed.** The man cannot work, cannot discharge his obligations, cannot maintain the ordinary arrangements of his life. This is the clearest practical indicator, and it should be assessed without delay.

**4. The material has become a condition of the man's whole account of himself.** The loss has become the organising fact of the man's existence, and every matter is interpreted through it. This pattern is the one associated in the literature with the risk of a prolonged and impairing course.

**5. The self-destructive conduct has begun and is continuing.** The drinking, the driving, the risk, the prolonged absence from the household, the suicidal thinking. The specific and urgent point: men in later life have the highest suicide rate of any group, and the loss in this chapter — the friend, the wife from whom the man was separated, the colleague, the person from the concealed period — is frequently the triggering event. A man with an active wish to die should seek help immediately and should regard the urge as a clinical emergency rather than as an insight.

**6. The material is presenting in the body.** Persistent physical symptoms that have not been evaluated, insomnia over an extended period, the cessation of eating, sustained exhaustion. The presentation is common in men and it is frequently the channel through which the man can be brought into an assessment.

### The Distinctions to Be Obtained

There are four, and they are diagnostic questions rather than matters for the man to conclude on his own.

**Whether the man's condition is grief or depression.** The two overlap substantially and differ in important respects: grief's material is oriented toward the loss and arrives in waves; depression's is general and persistent, with the loss frequently secondary. The distinction matters because the treatment differs.

**Whether the loss has prolonged the grief beyond its ordinary course.** The category of prolonged or complicated grief is recognised, is associated with substantial impairment, and responds to specific treatment. Men who have carried a loss for years without the possibility of acknowledgement are exactly the group in which it is most likely to occur and least likely to be assessed.

**Whether there is an associated condition requiring treatment.** Alcohol dependence, anxiety, a physical condition, or a condition that predates the loss and has been unmasked by it.

**Whether the loss is connected to a matter requiring its own treatment.** The material of Chapters 22, 23, and 29: an addiction, a remorse, or an injury that the loss has activated.

### The Practical Guidance for Men

The following four points are stated plainly, because the material in this chapter is the material about which men most frequently decide not to act.

**An assessment is not a diagnosis.** A consultation establishes what is present and what is required, and a substantial proportion of men who obtain an assessment are told that their material is within the ordinary range of grief and that what they require are the arrangements in Section 30.6.

**The presenting complaint can be something else.** A man who will not discuss the loss can present the sleep, the chest, the drinking, or the exhaustion. Primary care is organised to accept exactly these presentations, and the assessment questions used in routine practice — sleep, appetite, how long, and whether the man has somebody he speaks to — supply the route into the material, as the case in Chapter 27.6 illustrates.

**The arrangements in Section 30.6 do not require the assessment and should be conducted either way.** The date, the place, the account, the recipient, the conduct, and the disclosure to the household are worth constructing in every case, including where treatment is also required.

**The man's household should be informed at the level of the fact.** Where the material is being treated or where an assessment has been sought, the household's arrangements are substantially improved by knowing that something is being carried and attended to, even where the content is not disclosed. The case for this position is in Section 30.6 and it is a matter of accuracy: the household's alternative explanation for the man's state is generally worse than the truth.
