# The Battles Men Face in Society {.part num="VII" img="/images/part7.jpg" summary="Public expectations, private wounds, and the struggle for dignity"}

# The World That Judges a Man by His Results {.chapter num="25" short="The World That Judges a Man by His Results"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the social practice of measuring a man by his results, and identify the specific mechanisms through which the measurement is applied.
2. Analyse the price of being publicly assessed as a failure, including its effects on standing, opportunity, and self-regard.
3. Explain what occurs when a society has no place for a man's version of success, and identify the particular difficulties of unemployment, informal work, disability, and interrupted careers.
4. Define dignity in a context that values achievement, and distinguish it from recognition and from status.
5. Apply a structured method for choosing his own measure of worth without denying the reality of his circumstances, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Construct a way of living that does not require the approval of the judging audience.
:::

## Introduction

There is a question that every man is asked, at every gathering, by almost every person he meets for the first time: *What do you do?* The question is not an inquiry about a man's occupation. It is an evaluation's first instrument, and the answer determines how the conversation proceeds: the seat offered, the interest taken, the respect extended, and the estimate formed.

This chapter is about a world in which that question is the principal means of assessment, and in which the answer is received as a verdict. It is not about an imagined society; it is a description of the arrangements in which most of the men who read this book conduct their lives. In these arrangements, a man's worth in public is a function of his results: what he has built, what he earns, what his children achieve, what he contributes, and how his position compares with the positions of the men against whom he is measured.

The chapter's purpose is to describe the machinery accurately, because a man who understands it can work with it, and to address three specific situations that the machinery handles badly.

The first is the man whose results are publicly inadequate: the man who has not built, who does not earn, whose children have not prospered, and whose position is visibly below his contemporaries'.

The second is the man whose version of success the society has no place for: the man in informal work in an economy that recognises salaried employment; the man whose competence is in a trade for which there is no market; the man with a disability in an environment built entirely for the able-bodied; the man who chose a path that his community does not recognise as success.

The third is the man whose results are adequate and who has discovered that they do not produce what he was promised. His position is understood least of all, because the machinery asserts that results produce satisfaction, and he is the evidence that it does not.

The chapter proceeds from a position that should be stated at the outset. The measurement of men by their results is not a conspiracy and it is not solely oppressive. It is a system of evaluation that a society requires in order to allocate standing, opportunity, credit, marriageability, and trust, and it has supplied, over generations, a powerful incentive for men to build and provide. The difficulty is that the system is applied to every part of a man's worth, that it produces permanent categories of failure, and that it offers no procedure by which a man judged inadequate can be restored to his own account of himself. The work of this chapter is the construction of that procedure.
