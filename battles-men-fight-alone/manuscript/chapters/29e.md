## 29.7 The Prohibition on Transferring the Debt

Before the case material, the chapter addresses one practice directly, because it is common, it is well-intentioned, and it damages people who were not involved.

### The Pattern

A man carries an obligation he cannot discharge — to a dead parent, an estranged sibling, a person he failed, a family he left — and he converts it into an obligation imposed on somebody else. The forms are recognisable.

The father who was absent for his eldest son's childhood becomes the man who cannot permit his youngest daughter to leave the house.

The man who did not attend his father's funeral becomes the man who insists on the family's attendance at every obligation, enforced with disproportionate force.

The man who failed to protect a younger brother becomes a man who monitors his own sons' friendships, schools, and whereabouts so closely that the boys' accounts of childhood are dominated by surveillance.

The man whose own education was interrupted becomes a man who requires a particular course of study from a child who has a different capacity and a different wish.

The man whose honesty failed in a specific matter becomes a man who insists on a rigidity of procedure in his household which produces, in the case material, children who conceal ordinary matters in order to avoid the man's response.

### Why It Occurs

The mechanism is straightforward and the chapter states it without accusation. The man's obligation is real, his capacity to discharge it is absent, and his attention is consequently organised around the material permanently. When a person becomes available who resembles the injured party, the man's obligation attaches to that person, and the attachment feels like the discharge of it. The man is frequently aware of the intensity of his attention and frequently unaware of its origin.

### Why It Fails

**It does not discharge the obligation.** The debt belongs to a specific person or event, and the recipient of the transfer was not the injured party. The material's operation is unchanged, which is why the pattern tends to intensify over the years.

**It imposes a debt that was not the child's.** The mechanism is exactly the one described in Chapter 19: a young person carrying an obligation that was not his own, which produces either compliance and resentment, or refusal and the man's response to the refusal, which is experienced by the child as a judgement on his character rather than on his conduct.

**It produces concealment in the household.** Where the transferred obligation is enforced with intensity, the household's response is concealment rather than honesty, which is the arrangement described in Chapters 18 and 22, and which has the effect of removing the man's access to the very information through which his attention could be appropriately directed.

**It removes the possibility of the man's own repair.** The conversion of the material into the management of another person is a practice that consumes his attention without conducting any part of the work described in Section 29.6.

### The Correction

The correction is specific and it consists of three acts.

**Identify the transfer.** The man writes out the obligation and, separately, the intensity of his attention to the person in his household, and states the connection in his own hand. Men report that the statement is generally sufficient to reveal the pattern: the intensity, once described, is recognised by the man as disproportionate.

**State the obligation to its actual object.** Where the injured party is available, this is the repair of Section 29.5. Where the injured party is dead or absent, the man states the obligation in writing, in the form of a letter that is not sent, or of a page in a private record, and he states what he owes and to whom.

**Establish the household on the arrangement it actually requires.** The specific forms of attention and requirement that the man's household and children actually require are the ones set out in Parts III and V, and they are generally different from the transferred obligation in two respects: they are smaller, and they are about the people concerned rather than about the man's material.

**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in counselling, mediation, and men's group practice. The material is a case of irreparable regret.

**Damian is forty-eight, a civil engineer, married to Ngozi for twenty years, with three children. He was born in a village about three hours from the city in which he works, and he is the second of five children.**

The matter at the centre of Damian's case occurred when he was twenty-three. His eldest brother, Peter, who was twenty-eight, had been responsible for the family's small transport business, one lorry and a car, and had, in the year of the events, been diagnosed with a heart condition and warned to reduce his work. The family required an additional driver and Peter asked Damian to take over the business's second vehicle for a period.

Damian had received an offer of employment in the city that year and he accepted it. He describes the conversation: *He asked me in the yard. I told him I had the job in the city and that I would be going in two weeks. He was quiet. He said it was fine. I did not tell him that I had not applied for the job yet and could have taken the lorry. I could have told him I would drive for six months and then go. I did not want to delay my own thing by six months and I did not want to say that out loud.*

Peter continued with both vehicles for eight months, and during that period his condition worsened and a further cardiac episode occurred. A replacement driver was found eventually, the business was not sustained, and Peter died at thirty-nine, eleven years later, of a heart attack.

Damian's account of the material contains the elements of Section 29.3 in their punitive form: the counterfactual (*if I had driven that lorry, the business would have held, he would not have worked, he would be alive*); the verdict (*I was a man who chose his own thing over his brother and I have known that about myself for twenty-five years*); the debt with no creditor he can reach (Peter died eleven years later, and Damian has no way of establishing what the intervening years would have contained); and the management, which consisted of four practices: he did not return to the village except for obligations, he did not discuss Peter in the household, he did not accept a management position offered to him at thirty-eight which would have required relocation, and he drank heavily at the period of the year in which the conversation in the yard had occurred.

The transfer described in Section 29.7 was also present and Damian was unaware of it until it was named: his second son, aged nineteen, wanted to study in another state, and Damian had refused to discuss it for two years, on grounds that were described in the household as *he is too young*, and which Damian was, in his own words, *unable to explain to my wife without raising my voice*. The son's account of the position, which emerged in a family consultation, was: *My father will not let me go anywhere. He says it is about my studies. I have known for four years that it is about my uncle.*

The work Damian conducted occupied about five months and consisted of four elements.

**The account.** Written over three sittings: what occurred, what he was offered, what he refused, what he did not say, and what he actually controlled at twenty-three. The document distinguished, on the counsellor's instruction, three columns: the things I did; the things I could have done; and the things about which I decided nothing.

**The witness.** Damian gave the account in full to a counsellor and subsequently, at his own decision, to his sister, the eldest surviving sibling, in a conversation that lasted four hours. His account of the sister's response: *She said that she had known that Peter had asked me, and that she had never thought about it since, and that the family's view was that Peter's heart was the family's problem and not mine. She said it three times because I did not believe her the first two times.*

**The stated position on self-punishment.** Damian wrote a page in which he answered the question of what his suffering had achieved over twenty-five years. Three items in the page are worth recording. First, that Peter was not assisted by it. Second, that the position he had declined at thirty-eight had cost him an income that his household required. Third, that his son had been refused a course of study for two years because of a conversation held in a village yard when the son was not born.

**The redirection.** The form Damian chose came from the fact of the case: his son's position. He gave the permission, assisted with the enrolment, and took on, over the following three years, the mentorship of two young men in his workplace who were supporting their families and studying part-time. He describes this as *the lorry I did not drive, in a different shape*, and states that the correspondence is deliberate and that he does not discuss it with the young men.

The outcome Damian reported two years after the work was described in the following terms: *I have not stopped knowing what I did. I said no to my brother because I did not want to lose six months and I could not say that out loud, and he was dead eleven years later and I have no way of knowing whether it made any difference. What I stopped doing was paying for it every year with my own life. The reckoning is on the anniversary; my wife knows about the anniversary; my son is in another state and I am helping him, and I intervened for two young men at the office who do not know why I help them. There is nothing else I can do and I have done all of it.*

Four features of this case deserve isolation.

**The material was an omission of thirty seconds in a yard.** The man's own description of the case, and the reason it is included: the chapter's material is frequently of this size, and the man's sense of disproportion — *I have carried this for twenty-five years and it lasted half a minute* — is itself a component of the punitive mode and not a reason to consider the matter small.

**The counterfactual was unfounded and the man could not test it.** Peter died eleven years later of a heart condition, and the imagined alternative — that six months of driving would have altered the outcome a decade later — cannot be established. The case illustrates the finding from the counterfactual literature: ease of construction is not evidence, and men who construct the alternative with the greatest certainty are frequently constructing it on the least information.

**The punitive mode was signalled by the escalation and the transfer.** The drinking at the period of the year of the conversation, the declined position, and the two-year refusal of a son's plan. These are the markers of Section 29.3 and the transfer of Section 29.7, and the case is included because its most consequential feature was not the twenty-five-year rumination but the harm that the rumination was producing in the man's own household — a debt charged to a young man who was not involved.

**The work did not remove the material; it altered what it produced.** Damian's account two years later retains the knowledge, the regret, and the absence of any possibility of knowing the counterfactual. What has changed is its operation: it has a date, it is known to his wife, it has been described to one witness, it has been converted into conduct of a corresponding kind, and it is no longer charged to a child. This is precisely the position described at the end of Section 29.6 and it is the most that the chapter claims.
