## 14.7 Illustrative Case: Twenty-Two Years in the Same House

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in couple counselling, family practice, and research on marital communication.

**Yusuf is fifty-four, a retired customs officer, married to Rukayat for twenty-two years, with four children, the youngest of whom is nineteen.**

Yusuf's account, given in a counselling session that he had entered for an unrelated reason, was that his marriage was stable, that his wife was a good woman, that they had not quarrelled seriously in years, and that he had not spoken to her about anything that mattered to him since the year after their marriage.

The origin, which he identified precisely, was an evening twenty years earlier. He had been passed over for a promotion that he had been promised, and the loss had been substantial: a grade, an increase of about a third in his earnings, and, more importantly, the position he had been working towards for six years. He came home and told his wife. Her response, which he remembered verbatim, was: *Well, you should have followed up with the man when I told you to. Anyway, it is not the end of the world; my sister's husband has been in the same position for nine years.*

Yusuf's conclusion, formed that evening, was that his wife was not a person to whom difficulties could be taken. His behaviour afterwards was entirely rational: he stopped taking them. Over the next twenty years, he handled each difficulty with the institutions involved, with two colleagues, and, at times, with a brother in Kano who died in 2016. He had no conversation with his wife about his work, his health, the household's finances beyond the practical, or his own state, and the marriage was, by his account, harmonious.

Rukayat's account, obtained separately, contained three pieces of information that Yusuf did not have.

First, she recollected the same evening, and she recollected it differently. She had been in the middle of a difficult period: a miscarriage six weeks earlier that she had not told him about in any depth, a conflict with her mother-in-law that was at its height, and the management of four children under eight in a house with no assistance. Her sister's husband, whom she mentioned, had been unemployed for nine years and the family discussed it constantly. Her remark was, in her description, an attempt to reduce his disappointment by placing it in context, and she had not intended that he should stop speaking to her.

Second, she had noticed. She said: *For the first four or five years, I used to ask him. He would say it is nothing, or it is well. After a while I thought, this is the kind of man he is, he does not talk. I thought I had married a quiet man. I did not know that he had decided something about me. I thought it was his character.*

Third, she had her own arrangement of silence, and it had been established earlier than his. She had never told him about the miscarriage in full, because when she began to speak of it he had said that such things happen and that they should be grateful for the children they had. She had, for eleven years, managed a condition for which she was receiving treatment at a private clinic, which he did not know about. And she had, for the previous six years, been managing the household's finances with less money than she had reported, covering the difference from her tailoring work, because she did not want him to feel that the retirement was insufficient.

The intervention, in the session, took an unusual form. The counsellor asked each of them, in the other's presence, to describe the last time they had wanted to say something to the other and had not, and what they had feared. Yusuf described a medical test in the previous year whose result he had waited for alone for eleven days. Rukayat described a request from her sister for assistance that she had refused without consulting him and then concealed. Neither had any difficulty describing the material. The difficulty, in both cases, was the sentence each had formed about the other, twenty years and four years ago respectively, and neither had ever said it aloud.

The outcome over the following two years was specific and limited. They began a weekly conversation, on Sunday evening, of about an hour's length, and it was not about household logistics, which had filled their conversations previously. Yusuf attended two of Rukayat's clinic appointments. Rukayat ceased the concealment of the household's finances, which required a difficult conversation in which the six-year shortfall was disclosed and, eventually, understood as an act that had been intended protectively and had caused injury. And in the second year, Yusuf told her four things about his years at the customs service that he had never told anybody.

His assessment at the end of the process was: *I lost twenty years to one sentence, and she did not say it to me to make me stop. I have been carrying a conclusion about a person who was in the room, and I never tested it. What I have, now, is not a new marriage. It is the same marriage with the door open.*
:::

Six features of this case deserve isolation.

**The conclusion was formed from one exchange.** A single remark, made under the specific conditions of a bad evening in a difficult period, formed a conclusion that governed twenty years. This is the most common mechanism of the condition described in this chapter, and it is worth stating plainly: men frequently base a twenty-year decision about their marriages on one conversation.

**The remark was not intended to close the subject.** Rukayat's remark was an attempt at comfort, addressed partly to her own difficulty, and delivered in a form that carried additional meanings for the man. Both parties were operating on incomplete information; neither was acting in bad faith.

**The wife had noticed and had formed her own conclusion.** Her conclusion — *I thought I had married a quiet man* — is the crucial piece of information, because it demonstrates that she had interpreted his silence as character rather than as a decision about her. Men consistently assume that their withdrawal communicates rejection; it is frequently read as personality, which means the message men intend to convey is not the message received.

**Her silence predated his.** The miscarriage, the treatment, and the household's financial management were all undisclosed, and the first of them had been closed by an inadequate response from Yusuf. The case is therefore not one of a silent man and an available woman; it is one of two people who had each attempted disclosure and had each been met with a response that ended the practice.

**The intervention was a single structured question.** The counsellor's question — the last time you wanted to say something and did not, and what did you fear — produced material that neither party had concealed deliberately and that neither had ever been asked for. The format is worth noting: it required no diagnosis, no interpretation, and no emotional performance.

**The recovery was partial and practical.** A weekly arrangement, two clinic appointments, the correction of a concealment, and four disclosures over two years. The marriage continued to contain a gap, by the man's own account. What changed was that the gap contained a known position rather than an untested conclusion.

## 14.8 Practical Exercise: The Disclosure Plan and the Two-Chair Conversation

This exercise has four parts. Parts One and Two are completed alone; Part Three is completed with the partner; Part Four is a maintenance arrangement.

### Part One: The Conclusion I Formed

Write out, in one sentence, the conclusion you formed about your partner's willingness or capacity to hear you — for example: *she is not interested in anything that is not about the household;* *she has enough of her own problems;* *if I tell her, it will become an argument.*

Then answer four questions about it.

- When, precisely, did I form this conclusion, and what happened in that conversation?
- What did she actually say, in her own words, and what did I understand her to mean?
- What have I tested since? If the answer is nothing, say so.
- What would have to happen for me to revise the conclusion?

### Part Two: The Record

Write out the material that has gone undisclosed, as a list. Do not write the content; write the subject and its duration: *my work situation, four years; my health, one year; the loan I took, eight months; my father's illness, two years; how I feel about this marriage, nine years.*

The list is usually longer than the man expects, and producing it is frequently the point at which the work becomes urgent.

### Part Three: The Structured Conversation

Choose one item from the list. One. Apply the four conditions: purpose, time, quantity, form. Then write four sentences and say them.

1. **The opening.** The factual, present-tense statement of the situation.
2. **The material.** What happened, what you did, where you are. Two minutes, no more.
3. **The request.** One specific and modest thing you are asking for.
4. **The reciprocal question.** Ask what she is carrying that you do not know about, and listen without responding defensively.

Then hold the conversation, at the arranged time, in the arranged place. Record afterwards: what was said, how it was received, what was requested, and what will happen next.

### Part Four: The Arrangement

Establish a recurring occasion — weekly, fortnightly, or monthly — for the conversation, at which the following four questions are asked in the same order each time:

- How are you? (asked of the other person first)
- What is the hardest thing about your week?
- Is there anything I have done that has made it worse?
- What do you need from me in the coming week?

Men who adopt an arrangement of this kind report that the structure does most of the work, because it removes the requirement to initiate at a moment of difficulty, which is precisely the moment at which a man in this situation is least able to speak.
