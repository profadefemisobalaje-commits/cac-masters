## 28.3 The Ache of a Dream Nobody Knows About

The distinguishing feature of the loss described in this chapter is that there is no audience for it. This section sets out what follows, because the consequences are practical and because they explain the form the grief takes.

### Nobody Was Informed, and Therefore Nobody Can Offer Anything

The ordinary arrangements by which a loss is processed require that the loss be known. A bereavement is announced, attended, and spoken about; a financial collapse is described to the people who are affected by it; an illness is disclosed to the household. In every case the acknowledgement by others performs four functions: it establishes that something has happened, it permits the man to speak of it, it supplies the company of people who know, and it marks a transition.

For a dream that died quietly, none of these operate. Nobody knows, so the man cannot speak of it without first explaining that there was something to speak of; and the explanation itself invites the response described below. There is no company, because the people who know the man do not know the dream — frequently including his wife, which is the most consequential and the most common form of the absence. And there is no transition, because there was no event.

### The Five Responses the Man Anticipates

Men who consider disclosing a dead dream anticipate five responses, and the anticipation is accurate in a substantial number of cases.

**The practical response.** *You can still do it.* Delivered with goodwill, it treats the disclosure as an invitation to solve a problem, and it is frequently the most difficult response of the five, because it requires the man to explain why the plan is no longer available — the age, the money, the route that has closed — which is a description of defeat. The response is also inaccurate in the important respect: the man is not reporting a plan he has failed to make; he is reporting a loss.

**The minimising response.** *There are people with worse problems* or *you have a good job and four children; be content*. The response is common, it is frequently accompanied by an accurate assessment of the man's circumstances, and it removes his permission to grieve by comparing his loss to a larger one.

**The accusation.** *You should have done it when you had the chance* or *you always had an excuse*. This response is less common than the man fears and it is the one he most expects, which is a measure of how much of the accounting has already been conducted in his own mind.

**The retrospective verdict.** *So it was a waste, then* — the response that converts a life's decision into a failure, which is the material of Chapter 25 applied to the man's private record.

**The silence.** The response that is most common of all: the hearer says something brief, moves to another subject, and does not return to it, because he does not know how the material should be handled. The man reads the silence as a confirmation that the material should not have been raised, and it is generally this response that prevents a second attempt.

### Why the Man Does Not Grieve It

Four reasons, and they are worth setting out because they must be answered before the work of Sections 28.4 and 28.5 can proceed.

**Because there is no dead body, no loss to be recovered, and no event to be described.** The ordinary grief processes are triggered by events; the process in this case has no trigger, and men are not generally equipped to grieve a thing that simply stopped being possible.

**Because he chose it.** The man's own participation in the loss is the principal obstacle. A man who set the dream down in favour of his children's schooling does not feel entitled to describe the outcome as a loss, because to do so implies that the alternative would have been better, which would have required him to have chosen differently, which he would not.

**Because he is supposed to be over it.** The cultural instruction described in the introduction is applied internally with force. The man's own position is generally this: *I decided this at thirty-one and I am fifty-two; a man of fifty-two who still speaks of a medical career he did not begin is not being honest about his age.* The instruction suppresses the material without resolving it, and the material remains available to every comparison described below.

**Because the loss is not the loss of a thing but the loss of a version of himself.** The most important of the four reasons. The dream was not an object the man possessed; it was a description of who he was going to be. Its death is therefore an identity loss, and the material of Chapter 24 applies directly: the man must reconstruct an account of himself that includes what he became and that does not require him to pretend that the other account was never held.

### The Two Comparisons That Operate Continuously

Two comparisons supply the persistent occupation that men describe in connection with this material, and both are available at any moment.

**The comparison with the men who went.** The former classmate, the colleague, the cousin, the man from the same street who took the path. The comparison operates through whatever evidence is available — the professional listing, the announcement, the return visit, the social media account, the school reunion — and it is the mechanism described in Chapters 11 and 25. Its specific feature in this material is that the comparison is not with a stranger's general position but with a specific alternative to the man's own life, which makes it considerably more painful and considerably less accurate, since the man compares his actual life, in its whole and visible detail, with an account of another life that is composed of its public successes.

**The comparison with the version of himself who took the path.** The stronger of the two, and the one that operates without any external evidence. The man imagines the life he would have had: the position, the income, the household, the respect, the children raised elsewhere, the parents cared for differently. The imagined version is constructed entirely from the dream's advantages and contains none of its costs, and it is therefore a comparison between a real life and an ideal one, which cannot be won. The material of Chapter 24 on the ideal standard applies, and this comparison is the specific reason that a dead dream occupies the mental space of a live one.

::: story
**A composite account, constructed from patterns reported in counselling and in men's group practice.**

A man of fifty-six, a school teacher who had taught for thirty-two years, described during a men's group meeting the dream that had occupied him since the age of twenty-two.

*I was going to be a lawyer. I had the papers, I had the entry, and I had the fees for the first year. My father fell ill in the year I was to go, and the fees became the hospital's fees. When he recovered — he recovered fully, he lived another twenty-one years — I was twenty-four, and by then I had taken the teaching job, and my younger two were in secondary school, and there was nobody else to pay for them. I never decided not to be a lawyer. There was no day. I just did not register for the second year, and then the years had gone.*

*The thing nobody knows is that I have been a lawyer in my own head for thirty-four years. When a case is in the news, I decide it. When I hear an argument, I think about how I would have put it. I have written judgements in my head during lessons. I have never told my wife any of this. She believes that I am a man who wanted to be a teacher. She asks me sometimes why I am quiet in the evening, and I say I am tired, and I am tired.*

*What I could not say in this room until tonight is that I chose it. I chose my father's recovery and I chose my brothers' schooling and I would choose them again, tomorrow. So what am I grieving? I am grieving a man I invented, who I have carried for thirty-four years and who I have kept alive in my own head, and who was never going to exist, because the man who did all those things — the one I have been imagining — he would have had to be a man who let his father die and his brothers leave school. I have been envying a man who is not worth being.*
:::

The account is included because it supplies the chapter's central mechanism in the man's own words. The imagined alternative is not the life that would have followed from the dream; it is the dream's benefits without its costs. The imagined self is a man who took the path and did not pay for it. And the resolution the man discovered is one of the two the chapter develops in Sections 28.5 and 28.6: the dream is closed by an accurate reconstruction of what the dream would actually have required, which is not a consolation but a correction of the record.
