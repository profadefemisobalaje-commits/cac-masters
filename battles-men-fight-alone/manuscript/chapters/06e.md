## 6.8 Practical Exercise: The Two-Week Symptom Log and the Decision Point

This exercise has two purposes: to produce an accurate picture of your own state over a defined period, and to produce a decision about what to do with the picture. It takes five minutes a day for fourteen days, plus thirty minutes at the end.

### Part One: The Daily Log

For fourteen consecutive days, record the following five items at the same time each evening. Use a notebook or the table below. Score each item from zero to three, where zero means absent, one means mild, two means moderate, and three means severe.

| Day | Sleep quality | Energy | Irritability | Interest in things I usually enjoy | Alcohol or sedatives used to cope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |
| ... | | | | | |

Three additional lines should be completed once a week:

- *This week, the thing that most affected my state was ______________________.*
- *This week, I spoke to ______ people about how I am actually doing.*
- *This week, I did the following thing that I used to do and had stopped doing: ______________.*

### Part Two: The Scoring

At the end of the fortnight, total the columns. Then apply the following interpretation, which is a screening aid and not a diagnosis.

- **Total irritability of 14 or more, or a decline in interest averaging 2 or more, or an average sleep score below 1:** the pattern is consistent with a depressive presentation and should be assessed professionally.
- **Any use of alcohol or sedatives specifically to sleep or to settle the state, on more than four nights out of fourteen:** warrants professional assessment, because self-medication is a recognised marker of a treatable condition.
- **Any thought during the fortnight that you would be better off dead, that others would be better off without you, or that you cannot continue:** requires immediate professional contact. Do not wait for the fortnight to end. Chapter 34 and Appendix Q set out what to do.

### Part Three: The Account

Write five sentences that translate the fortnight into information a professional can use. Use the three principles from Chapter 5: describe behaviour and body rather than feelings; attach everything to a date; report consequences rather than causes.

An example of the form:

> *Over the last four months I have been waking at about three and cannot return to sleep. My temper has changed: I shout at my children over small things, which I did not do before. I have stopped playing football and stopped attending my association. I am drinking three or four bottles most evenings to sleep. In the last two weeks I have twice thought that my family would be better without me.*

### Part Four: The Decision Point

Write your answer to each of the following, and then act on the last one.

1. **Have I had these symptoms for more than two to four weeks?** If yes, the decision is not *whether* to seek assessment but *where*.
2. **Which route will I use?** Physician, clinic, counsellor, hospital service, telephone line, or online service. Write the specific name and place, not a category.
3. **Whom will I tell?** One person who will know that this is happening, and who will ask about it. Chapter 4 explains why this single step is decisive.
4. **What will I say in the first thirty seconds?** Write the sentence down and rehearse it aloud three times.
5. **What is the date and time of the contact?** Write it in your calendar now, and treat it as you would treat a meeting with a major client.

### Part Five: If the Answer Is That You Cannot Do It

A proportion of readers will complete the exercise and will not make the contact. That is a real and foreseeable outcome, and three things help.

**Lower the first step until it is trivial.** The first step does not have to be an appointment. It can be a telephone call to a helpline, a message to a physician's receptionist, a question asked of a trusted person about who they would recommend, or the purchase of a notebook. Any of these moves the situation forward, and the second step is easier than the first.

**Ask somebody to accompany you.** Being taken to an appointment by a wife, a brother, a friend, or a colleague removes most of the friction, and men report that this is the single most effective support they received in the whole process.

**Treat the resistance as a symptom.** The reluctance to seek help is part of the condition, not a judgement about whether help is deserved. A man who says *I will do it when I feel stronger* is describing a promise that depression does not keep, because the strength arrives through the treatment rather than before it.
