## 4.7 Illustrative Case: Ninety Minutes in a Car Park

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in counselling and in the men's health literature.

**Kunle is forty-seven and owns a medium-sized electrical contracting business in a state capital.** He employs fourteen people. He has been married for nineteen years; his wife, Bola, is a branch manager in a bank. They have three children, one at university. His father died four years ago, and since then Kunle has become the effective head of an extended family of twenty-three people, seven of whom are dependent on him in some form: two of his late brother's children whose school fees he pays, his mother's medical costs, and a cousin who works in his business and whose salary is, in practice, an arrangement rather than a wage.

Kunle works from seven in the morning until nine or ten at night, six days a week. He describes himself as "not a man who talks about things," and would not have used the word lonely about himself until the evening described below.

His three closest friends are men he has known since university. The pattern of the relationship is this. He attends the funerals. He contributes to the weddings. He gives professional advice freely. He has lent money to two of them and written it off. In the last three years he has seen each of them fewer than five times, and he has not been alone with any of them for more than an hour since 2019.

In February, Kunle's business loses a major contract. In March, he learns that a client owing a substantial sum has taken proceedings against his company. In April, he begins to have difficulty sleeping. In May, during an argument over a trivial matter, Bola tells him that she feels she is living with a stranger, and that this is the fourth year she has felt it. He does not answer, because he does not know what to say.

In June, a Thursday, he drives from a supplier's yard to a site and finds, on arrival, that the client has cancelled the visit. He sits in the car park with the engine off. It is half past four. He stays there for ninety minutes. He cannot say afterwards what he thought about, except that at some point he took out his telephone, looked through the contacts — there are four hundred and eleven names — and could not identify one person to whom he could send a message describing his situation. Not one.

What he noticed was not sadness. It was a cold, factual realisation, described in his own words later: *I have spent twenty years looking after everybody and there is no one on the other side of the counter.*

The following week he did two things. He telephoned a former colleague — not his oldest friends, but a man he had worked with eleven years earlier and trusted — and asked whether they could meet. He told that man, in the course of a two-hour conversation, most of what he had told nobody: the lost contract, the proceedings, the loans, his brother's children, and the finding that he had no one to call. The second thing he did was to telephone a counselling service recommended by a relative, and to make an appointment. Of that call he said afterwards, *It was the most frightened I have been since I was a boy, and I was only making an appointment.*
:::

The case illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout this book, and four features of it are worth isolating.

**The loneliness was not produced by failure.** Kunle's isolation was at its most severe in a period when he was, by all external measures, successful: a functioning business, a marriage, a respected position in a large extended family. His network of obligations had grown precisely as his network of support had shrunk, and the two curves had crossed at some point that nobody noticed.

**The obligation load crowded out the friendships.** The extended-family responsibilities were legitimate, and he had accepted them honourably. What nobody had audited was the effect on the remaining relationships: with each year, the time available for friendship decreased, and the probability of any of those friendships becoming intimate decreased with it.

**The moment of realisation was not dramatic.** He did not lose his family, his health, or his business. He sat in a car park for ninety minutes with his telephone in his hand and found that there was nobody to call. This is not unusual. For many men, the recognition of loneliness arrives in an unremarkable private moment, and its force comes precisely from its ordinariness.

**The remedy began with a stranger and a former colleague.** Not with his oldest friends, and not with his wife. Kunle's first disclosure was to a man he had not seen for eleven years, and his second step was professional. This is worth noting because it is the more common order in practice: men frequently tell somebody outside the immediate circle first, precisely because the stakes are lower. The choice is not a failure of courage; it is a sound piece of tactical judgement, and it can be repeated with the people who matter once the first sentence has been said once.

## 4.8 Practical Exercise: The Connection Audit

This exercise takes about ninety minutes and requires only a notebook. It has five parts. Readers who work through it with a counsellor or a trusted friend report considerably better results, because the exercise tends to produce a decision that is difficult to make alone.

### Part One: The Inventory

Draw a table with four columns and answer for each person in your life with whom you have had contact in the last twelve months.

| Name | What we talk about | What they know about my actual situation | Who initiates contact |

The first two columns will be filled quickly. The third is the revealing one. Against each name, write one of four letters:

- **N** — knows nothing of my real situation.
- **S** — knows something, but only the public account.
- **K** — knows a great deal.
- **A** — knows everything that matters.

Most men who complete this audit find that the K and A columns are close to empty, and that the majority of names are marked N, including the names of people they would describe as close friends.

### Part Two: The Reciprocity Check

For the three to five people who matter most to you — spouse or partner, closest friend, closest relative, an adult child — write two short paragraphs: what they currently receive from you, and what you currently receive from them. Do this for each person separately, and be specific. *They receive my income and my availability* is specific. *They receive my support* is not.

### Part Three: The Trace

For each of those people, answer one question: *When did I last tell this person something that made me feel exposed?* Write the month and year. If the answer is *I cannot remember*, record that as the answer and resist the temptation to substitute a lesser event.

### Part Four: The Decision

Answer three questions in writing.

1. **Who is the one person most likely to receive a difficult disclosure well?** Choose one. Not the ideal person; the most likely.
2. **What is the one item I will disclose first?** Choose something of moderate weight: a worry about money, a concern about health, a difficulty at work, the fact of a specific loss, or the fact that you have been sleeping badly for months. Do not choose the heaviest item; it can wait for a second conversation.
3. **What is the date, and what is the sentence?** Write both. The sentence should be short and specific. *I want to tell you something I have told nobody. I am not asking you to fix it. I just need you to know.* Then do it on the date.

### Part Five: The Structure

Finally, set up the structure that prevents the relapse into isolation. Choose two recurring arrangements and write them into your calendar for the next twelve months.

- **A standing meeting:** one person, one activity, one interval, fixed time. Monthly is achievable; fortnightly is better. Examples: a walk, a meal, a two-hour call, a shared weekly obligation such as taking a parent to a clinic.
- **A group or association:** a men's fellowship, a professional association, a sports team, a study or discussion group, a community committee with which you are already involved. The requirement is not the subject matter; it is regularity, familiarity, and the possibility of conversation that is not transactional. Appendix J sets out a plan for building such a group from nothing, and Appendix S provides material for its meetings.
