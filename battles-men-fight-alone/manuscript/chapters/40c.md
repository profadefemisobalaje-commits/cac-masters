## 40.3 The Cost of Never Choosing

Four losses. The first two are external and the last two are borne by the man.

### Loss One: The Lives He Was For

**The material.** The men in this book who have deferred their own lives have generally conducted the obligations of Parts III to V competently, and the case material records the finding that the people they were for were frequently not required to conduct the relationship with them.

**The specific forms.** The children who describe the father as a good man they did not know, as Chapter 39 established; the wife who has lived with the provision and not the man; the friends who were maintained as arrangements; the family that received the assistance and not the man; the household whose members selected what they brought to him.

**The finding from the case material, and it is the hardest in this chapter.** The men who discover the deferral's size at the end frequently report that the obligations were discharged and the relationships were not constructed: that they were, to their children, the position and the money and the instruction; that the accounts in Section 39.7 are reproducible in their own households; and that the years of provision did not produce the material they had believed the provision would produce.

**The corrective.** The material of Chapter 39.5's sixth practice and Chapter 29.6's fourth element: the repairs, the practices, and the audits. The finding is that the repairs are generally available, that the household waits for the account rather than the correction of the past, and that the years remaining are sufficient for the work — which is the second half of the chapter's material in Sections 40.5 to 40.7.

### Loss Two: What Was Not Built

**The material.** The work a man intended, the business, the qualification, the book, the school, the land, the trade, the practice, the craft, the thing he was going to do. Chapter 28's material applies in full, and its accounting is the one this section conducts.

**The two findings.** First, that a proportion of the deferred items are still available: the men in the case material who took up an item at fifty-eight or sixty-two and produced it. Second, that a proportion are not: the qualification whose requirements have altered, the route that has closed, the partnership that has died, the years whose accumulation was the item itself.

**The instruction.** The audit of Section 40.5's third list is conducted on what is available. The chapter's finding is that the available items are generally more numerous than the man's account of them, and that the accounting is properly conducted with the ally and with the professional rather than alone — the material of Chapter 30.4's rule that the man should not audit his own position without an instrument he has not written.

### Loss Three: The Capacity to Know What He Wants

**The material.** The fifth cost of Chapter 39.3, in its most consequential form, and the loss that makes the chapter's own exercise difficult for the reader who has deferred longest.

**The specific presentation.** The man reports that he does not know what he wants; that he has no preference; that he would not know what to do with the time; that the question is unanswerable. The case material records the presentation in the man's own words: *The doctor asked me what I enjoy and I sat there for a minute and I said, I do not know. I have never been asked that. I do not think I have ever asked myself that.*

**The mechanism.** The capacity to identify preference is a competence, and it is developed by its exercise and reduced by its disuse. A man who has not exercised it for thirty years has the position of a man who has not used a limb: the capacity's apparatus is intact and the man's access to it is not.

**The corrective, and it is specific.** The capacity returns with the practice and it returns in a sequence: first the physical and practical preferences (what he wants to eat, where he wants to sit, when he wants to sleep); then the matters of conduct (what he is willing to do and to refuse); then the larger matters (the work, the place, the relationships, the years). The finding from the case material is that the sequence takes months rather than weeks and that it should be recorded rather than assessed, which is the reason the exercise of Section 40.8 includes the preference register.

### Loss Four: The Man's Own Account of His Life

**The material.** The final loss and the one the chapter's reflection questions address directly: the man's own account of the years.

**The presentation.** The man's account of his life is composed of the obligations: the children educated, the house built, the position held, the family carried, the debts cleared. The account is accurate and it is not the man's own. The specific finding from the case material: the men who report the position describe the account as somebody else's, and the material of Chapter 24's empty season is its presentation.

**The mechanism and the corrective.** The mechanism is the identity anchor of Section 40.2: the man's account of himself is the provision, and the provision is not the man. The corrective is the audit and the choices: an account of a life requires the man's own items, and the items are constructed by the conduct and not by the accounting. The chapter's instruction is therefore that the audit is the beginning and the choices are the work.

## 40.4 The Four Permissions

Men in the case material report that the barrier to the chapter's work is not the arrangement but the permission: the man does not believe that he is entitled to conduct it. The section supplies the four permissions and the arguments that establish them.

### Permission One: The Permission to Have a Life

**The statement.** *My life is a thing in itself and not the residue of my obligations.*

**The argument, in four parts.**

**Part one: the man's life is the instrument through which every obligation is discharged.** Chapter 37.5's argument in its broadest form: the health, the judgement, the capacity, the relationships, and the years are the resources; the discharged obligation is the product; and a man who consumes the instruments to produce the products is a man who is consuming his own capacity. The case material contains the demonstration: the men whose health failed under the load of Chapters 33 and 37 discharged fewer obligations in the following ten years than the men who maintained their capacities.

**Part two: the man's life has its own term.** The years are finite, the deferral's condition may never be met, and the death of Chapter 28's material arrives in the case material of men in this book at ages between forty-four and seventy-one. The argument is not an appeal to urgency; it is the statement of the position, and the reader's own experience of the deaths in his cohort supplies its evidence.

**Part three: the account of the man's life is required.** The reflection questions at the end of every chapter of this book, the material of Chapter 24.4, the letter of Chapter 28.5, and the man's own position when his years end. The argument is the material of Chapter 44 in its preliminary form: a man's conduct is a life, and the life is either conducted with the man's participation or without it.

**Part four: the people he is for require him as a person.** The material of Chapter 39.3's first and second costs, in the household's own words: the wife who has everything the man supplies and does not have the man; the son who would rather report his death than his unemployment. The argument's form for the reader: the obligations' principal beneficiaries are the people in the household, and the household's accounts, taken separately, are the evidence of what it requires.

### Permission Two: The Permission to Not Be Everybody's Solution

**The statement.** *I am not the solution to every problem in my family, my community, and my organisation, and my pretending otherwise has cost the people who should have had to solve their own.*

**The argument.** The material of Chapters 33.4 and 33.6: the man's assistance that prevents another person's capacity; the household's material that is consumed by the extended family's requirements; the man's over-availability that produces the organisation's dependence; and the specific finding from the case material that the men who conducted the reduction found that the receiving parties' capacity appeared. The argument's household form: the man's wife and children have a claim on the arrangement that the man's other obligations have been consuming.

### Permission Three: The Permission to Be a Beginner and to Be Bad at It

**The statement.** *I may take up something I am not competent in, in public, at this age, and be seen doing it.*

**The argument.** The material of Chapters 24.5 and 28.6: the self-measure that makes a man's own pursuits unavailable to him; the competence that has been the man's protection and the reason that he has not been a beginner since his twenties; and the case material's finding that the men who conducted a new pursuit reported the incompetence as the element that produced its effect. The argument's mechanism: a man's own life consists of the items he has chosen, and an item chosen at forty-eight is an item at which he is not competent, which is the price of the item and not its disqualification.

### Permission Four: The Permission to Ask for What He Wants

**The statement.** *I may state what I want, in the ordinary matters and in the large ones, and the statement is legitimate whether or not it is granted.*

**The argument.** The material of Chapters 30, 35, and 39.4's third competence: the man's requests, at the level of the practice material in Chapter 35.4, conducted in the four-part form; the finding that men's first contact with the material's sources follows a prolonged silence; and the specific finding of the case material for this chapter: the men who stated what they wanted to their households reported that the household's response was generally available, and that the household's own arrangements had frequently been waiting for the man's statement in order to be constructed.

**The permission's distinction.** Stating what he wants is not the same as requiring that it be granted. The chapter's rule: the statement is the man's responsibility and the answer is not.
