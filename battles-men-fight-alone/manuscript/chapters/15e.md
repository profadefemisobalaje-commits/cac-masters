::: summary
**Chapter 15 in brief.** The battles in a marriage are rarely the visible ones. The visible conflict — about money, a child's school, a relative's visit — is an occasion on which an older and less specific difference becomes audible, and it is usually a difference about what the marriage is for. Most of what damages a marriage is not conflict but accumulation: a pattern that repeats, unexamined, for years.

Seven struggles are carried inside marriages without being raised: the struggle over priority, in which the man distributes himself by obligation and his wife assesses the distribution by priority; the struggle with desire, managed rather than discussed; the struggle over money and standing, with four recurring forms — obligation against provision, present against future, control against participation, and provision against presence; the struggle with self-worth inside the home, where the man's professional standing is irrelevant; the struggle with the man's own family, in which the wife's complaint is usually that she is not defended; the struggle with silence, diagnosed by the fact that the couple converse competently about logistics and rarely about anything else; and the struggle with the conclusion that the marriage has become an arrangement.

Silence takes over a marriage through seven stages: the small decision not raised; the decision not raised because of the last time; the accumulation of unraised items; the reinterpretation of the man's behaviour on incomplete information; the withdrawal of reciprocity by both parties; the establishment of an efficient, predictable arrangement containing no intimacy; and, years later, the incident that appears to come from nowhere.

Marital dissatisfaction has eight causes with different remedies: unequal distribution of load; absence of intimacy; conflict of priorities; a specific unaddressed injury; a temperamental difference that will not change; an external strain acting on both parties; an untreated condition in either party; and an incompatibility at the level of the marriage's purpose. Naming the cause identifies the remedy; men who name neither continue to address occasions.

Serious disagreement is conducted using four preparations (one subject, set conditions, a decided objective, one specific proposal), six rules during the conversation (begin with your own position, state findings rather than accusations, ask before defending, do not introduce the past, do not score, allow the silence), three endings (agree one thing, agree the review, close deliberately), and four rules of engagement for high-conflict moments (no character statements, no audience, a stopping rule, no irreversible acts).

Sacrifice is intrinsic to marriage and becomes injurious in four forms, of which the most destructive is sacrifice that has become an identity. The unspoken ledger — an internal account of what has been given and not acknowledged — produces disproportionate anger and progressive isolation, and its remedy is to state the account. Four questions test whether a sacrifice has crossed into injury, and an affirmative answer on three or more requires attention.

Help should be sought at defined indicators rather than at the point of collapse: a single significant injury, the same argument recurring for more than two years, symmetrical silence, an untreated condition, escalation, the involvement of children, or any consideration of a third party or of leaving. The available routes are couple counselling, individual counselling, treatment of an individual condition, structured programmes, and family or community mediation, each with its own indications and limits.

## Reflection Questions

1. From the seven silent struggles in Section 15.1, which is present in your marriage? Which one have you never named, even to yourself?
2. Where in the seven stages of Section 15.2 is your marriage now? What was the decision you did not raise that started the sequence, if you can identify it?
3. Complete Exercise One, Part A. Was the distribution of load ever agreed, or has it evolved? Which single item would make the most difference if it were reassigned?
4. From the eight causes in Section 15.3, which two are present in your marriage, and what does the corresponding remedy require of you specifically?
5. Have you been keeping an internal account — a ledger of what you have given and not been acknowledged for? Write out the balance, then answer: what would it cost you to state it aloud?
6. Applying the four questions in Section 15.5, has any sacrifice of yours crossed into injury? If three or more answers are affirmative, what will you do this month?
7. Which of the indicators in Section 15.6 applies to your marriage, if any? What is the specific step you will take, and by what date?

## Further Reading

- **On the empirical study of marriage:** the major longitudinal research programmes on marriage and its stability, including the observation studies that identify the specific communication behaviours distinguishing marriages that endure from those that end, and the findings on the predictive value of how couples handle disagreement.
- **On the demand-withdraw pattern:** the research on the common pattern in which one partner seeks discussion and the other withdraws, including the findings on its association with marital dissatisfaction and its resolution.
- **On the effects of financial arrangements within marriage:** the literature on money management, concealed expenditure, and financial decision-making within couples, including research indicating that separate and concealed financial arrangements are associated with reduced marital satisfaction.
- **On couples and illness:** the substantial literature on how individual mental and physical health conditions affect marital satisfaction and stability, and how treatment of the individual condition improves the marriage.
- **Practical resources:** couple counselling services, including subsidised and community-based providers; structured marriage enrichment and communication programmes; mediation services for couples; and legal advice, which should be obtained early — before decisions are made — where separation is under consideration, so that the man's position, and his children's, is known rather than assumed.
