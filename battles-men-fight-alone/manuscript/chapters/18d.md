## 18.6 Building a Marriage Where Emotion Is Allowed

The final substantive section sets out the arrangements by which a marriage becomes a relationship in which a man's interior life is permitted, discussed, and known.

### The Premise

The premise is that permission is granted by arrangement rather than by affection. Households do not become emotionally open because the parties love each other more; they become open because a structure exists in which difficult material can be raised without the household being destabilised. The structure is the work, and it is more reliable than the intention.

### The Six Arrangements

**1. The recurring conversation.** A scheduled occasion — weekly, fortnightly, or monthly — in which the exchange described in the exercise in Chapter 14 is conducted. Its four questions, in the same order: How are you? What is the hardest thing about your week? Is there anything I have done that has made it worse? What do you need from me in the coming week?

Two design features matter. The first question is asked of the wife first, which prevents the arrangement from becoming the man's channel and confirms that it is reciprocal. And the conversation is scheduled rather than arising, because material of this kind does not emerge spontaneously in a household with children, work, and obligations.

**2. A designated hour with nothing to administer.** Time together in which there is no task, no logistics, and no children. Men frequently respond that this is impossible, and the practical answer is the one in Chapter 12: the hour has to be taken from something and the decision about what to take it from has to be made. The arrangements that work are specific and unglamorous: a Saturday morning, a Wednesday evening, a monthly day.

**3. A rule about third parties.** An explicit agreement about what may be shared outside the marriage — with the wife's family, with the man's family, with friends. Disclosures made inside the marriage and subsequently circulated are the most common cause of a man's decision to close the channel permanently, as Chapter 14 established.

**4. A rule about conflict.** The rules of engagement in Section 15.4: no character statements, no audience, a stopping rule, no irreversible acts. These should be agreed in a calm period and referred to in the difficult one.

**5. A sharing of the load.** The division of household functions, including the emotional and administrative labour. This arrangement is the precondition of the others in the majority of households, because a wife who is exhausted cannot conduct the recurring conversation, and the consequence is described in Chapter 22.

**6. The man's own support outside the marriage.** Described in Chapters 4 and 14: one confidant, one group, or one professional relationship. The purpose of this element is frequently misunderstood — it is not a substitute for the marriage and it is not a channel for complaint. Its function is to prevent the marriage from carrying the whole of the man's requirement, which is the arrangement that fails when the first difficulty between the couple is also the first time the man has needed to speak.

### The Six Prohibitions

The arrangements above are frequently defeated by six practices. Naming them is intended to make them avoidable.

**Do not use the conversation for scoring.** A recurring conversation in which past grievances are submitted as evidence will be discontinued by both parties within a month.

**Do not disclose and then withdraw.** A man who discloses a matter and then behaves as though the disclosure did not occur, or who becomes unreachable for a period, teaches the household that his disclosures precede difficulty.

**Do not require the response to be perfect.** The material of Section 14.4 applies. The first responses will be clumsy, and the clumsy response should be corrected in one sentence rather than treated as evidence about the marriage.

**Do not make the wife responsible for the man's state.** A wife is not the solution to her husband's depression, exhaustion, or grief. She is a participant in the marriage. Where a condition is present, its treatment belongs with a professional, and the wife's role is support and knowledge rather than management.

**Do not conduct the arrangements only while they are improving.** The arrangements have value at the point at which the marriage is not improving, which is when most of them are abandoned.

**Do not treat the disclosure as a single event.** A man's disclosure to his wife of what he carries is not the conclusion of the work; it is the opening of a channel that then has to be used, over years, with respect to matters that are small as well as large.

## 18.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Loved His Wife and Did Not Know Her

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in couple counselling and in the clinical literature on men and marriage.

**Emeka is fifty-four, a senior manager in a logistics firm in Lagos, married to Bisi for twenty-six years, with three children, two of whom are adults and one in secondary school.**

Emeka's presenting concern, when he sought counselling, was that he no longer knew why he was married. He was clear that he loved his wife, that the marriage was peaceful, that nothing had occurred, and that he had no intention of leaving. He described the position as follows: *We run the house. We talk about the children, the money, the family, the arrangements. I do not know what she thinks about anything that is not logistics, and, more importantly, I do not know what I think. If she died tomorrow I would be destroyed and I would not be able to tell anybody what we had.*

The history that emerged had the following structure.

Over twenty-six years, Emeka had constructed an arrangement in which he was the household's function: its income, its decisions, its negotiations with two extended families, its medical administration, its representation at community functions, and, for the previous seven years, the manager of his mother's care arrangements. He had performed these functions competently. He had, in addition, carried a private set of matters that his wife did not know about: a correspondence with a former university classmate in which he had, for four years, disclosed more of himself than he had to his wife (the correspondence was not romantic and had never been romantic; it was, in his description, a friendship conducted by message, and he was aware that his wife would find it difficult to understand); a private concern about his middle child's mental state, which he had investigated on his own initiative and about which he had decided not to worry his wife; and a persistent sense of having become a competent administrator of a family rather than a participant in one.

The marital arrangement had three features that Emeka had never named. First, he and his wife had not had a conversation about anything outside the household's administration for eleven years. Second, the household had not contained any criticism of Emeka for the same period: the absence of conflict, which he had regarded as a sign of a good marriage, was, in the counsellor's assessment, a sign that disagreement had become impossible, and, as Bisi later said, she had stopped raising matters because "there was no point in it; he would do what he thought was right in the end". Third, the family's decisions were made entirely by Emeka, who consulted after deciding, a pattern that had been established in the first year of the marriage when the household had negligible income and there was no capacity for deliberation.

The intervention, conducted over about eighteen months, consisted of four elements, and Emeka's account of the order of their effects is instructive.

He began with the transfer of functions — the family's medical arrangements and one set of relative relationships — and he described this as the most difficult step and the most useful. It required teaching Bisi the administrative details, accepting her handling of them differently from his own, and, on two occasions, watching an outcome he would have managed differently.

He then conducted the recurring conversation, initially weekly and later fortnightly. Its first three months were, in his description, awkward and nearly empty, because neither party had practice. By the sixth month the conversation had acquired content and had, on two occasions, produced disagreement, which he noted as a change in the right direction.

He then disclosed the three matters he had been carrying. The correspondence was received badly and required three separate conversations; Bisi's assessment was that the content was not the difficulty but the practice of having spoken to somebody else for four years while not speaking to her, which is the finding of Chapter 14 in a specific form. The concern about the middle child was received with relief: Bisi had reached the same conclusion independently and had also decided not to worry anybody. And the third matter — his sense of having become the administrator of a family — was received as plain information and produced, in his words, the only moment of the whole process at which his wife cried.

He then, at the counsellor's suggestion, wrote the account described in Section 18.5: what had happened in his working life, what he had wanted at various ages, what he regretted, and what he intended for the remaining period.

His summary, at the conclusion of the work, was: *I thought a marriage with no quarrels was a good marriage. What we had was a project with two staff. I am not claiming to have become a romantic. I am claiming that my wife now knows three things about me that she did not know after twenty-six years, and that I have booked a Wednesday evening for twenty years that I had been giving to nothing in particular.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The absence of conflict was the symptom, not the health.** Emeka had interpreted the marriage's peacefulness as evidence of success. The counsellor's assessment — that a marriage with no disagreement over eleven years had removed the possibility of disagreement — is a recurring finding in couple work, and it distinguishes the marriage of agreement from the marriage of administration.

**The consultation-after-decision pattern was established in the first year.** The household's decision structure had been set when there was no income and no capacity for deliberation, and it had never been revised. This is the most common history of the pattern described in Section 18.4: a functional arrangement established early, under constraint, and maintained by inertia.

**The undisclosed correspondence was a symptom as much as a fault.** The correspondence was not romantic and had not been concealed for its content; it had been concealed because it was the channel through which Emeka was known, and its disclosure would have exposed the fact that such a channel was necessary. This is the mechanism described in Chapter 14 as competition between support and marriage, and it is worth noting that the marriage, rather than the friendship, was the subject that required the intervention.

**The child's difficulty had been assessed twice, independently, and not discussed.** Both parents had reached the same conclusion and had both decided to protect the other. The arrangement is an exact illustration of Section 18.1: two people performing a protective function for each other and neither in possession of the information available to the other.

**The order of the interventions produced most of the effect.** The transfer of functions came before the conversation, and the disclosure came after both. Emeka was clear that he could not have conducted the disclosure first, because he had nothing to disclose it into: a marriage in which decisions were made alone had no channel in which a disclosure could be received. This is the practical answer to the question men most frequently ask — *what do I say?* — and it is that the arrangements frequently require attention before the words do.
