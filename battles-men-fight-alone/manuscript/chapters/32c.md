## 32.3 The Knowledge Trap

There is a specific position that carries the requirement to its maximum and that is experienced by men in every walk of life: the man whose standing rests on his knowledge.

### The Forms

**The self-taught expert.** The man who acquired his competence without formal instruction: the mechanic, the builder, the technician, the trader, the farmer, the driver, the administrator, the small-business owner. His standing in his field is entirely his knowledge and his results, and there is no credential to fall back on. The form's specific difficulty is that his authority is personal: nobody assigned it to him, and there is no document that establishes him as the person who knows. His standing is therefore vulnerable in a way that a certified professional's is not, and he manages the vulnerability by never being seen not to know.

**The professional whose expertise is narrow and whose authority is broad.** The specialist whose formal competence is in one domain and whose position requires him to speak in several. The doctor asked about the law, the engineer asked about the contract, the accountant asked about the strategy, the teacher asked about the child's health, the officer asked about the market. The man answers in the adjacent domain, because the question has been addressed to him and because the alternative is a referral.

**The man whose marginal knowledge is being tested.** The man among men, in the group, the meeting, the argument: the man who must be able to state the figure, the name, the date, the outcome, the record. Its specific form in men's settings is the competitiveness of the exchange, which recruits the requirement even in men who are not otherwise subject to it: the man who can answer quickly acquires standing in the conversation, and the man who says he does not know loses a point.

**The man whose history is his knowledge.** The man who has the record of the family, the community, the institution, and the trade: the one who knows what happened, who was involved, what was agreed. This form is common in the man's later years and it carries the same trap in its most complete version, because the man is the sole holder of material that others require and because his standing in the present is frequently his knowledge of the past.

**The man who has been the answer's supplier to a person who has died.** The specific and reported form in which a man's father, wife, or brother was the person who knew, and the man now occupies the position and must supply what he does not have. Men in this form describe a specific and prolonged difficulty: *he was the one who knew all of this and now people ask me.*

### The Trap's Mechanism

The trap has five components, and they compound.

**1. The man is identified with his knowledge.** His standing in the setting, his income, his household's position, and his own account of himself are all substantially composed of what he knows. A question he cannot answer is not merely an unanswered question; it is a reduction in the man's standing, and the reduction is visible to everyone in the room. The fact that this is somewhat true is what gives the trap its force.

**2. The setting reinforces the avoidance.** Every time the man answers, the group's behaviour confirms that he is the one who knows, and every time he declines to answer, an unspoken uncertainty is registered. The arrangement is a stable equilibrium: the more the man answers, the more he is asked, and the less anybody asks anybody else.

**3. The questions are addressed to the wrong person.** Because the man is known as the one who knows, all questions of any kind come to him, including the questions belonging to other people's domains. The man therefore answers, or refers, or deflects. The correct response — that the question be addressed to the person whose competence it concerns — requires the man to admit that there is such a person and that he is not it.

**4. The information required to check the man does not exist.** The man's answers are frequently not verifiable in his setting: the others do not know enough to check him. The verification comes, where it comes, from the consequence, which arrives later and at a cost that is generally paid by somebody else.

**5. The man's own supply of knowledge degrades while his standing requires that it not.** The most durable component. A self-taught expert's competence decays with the technology, the market, the regulation, and the practice. The decay is universal and it is normally addressed by asking questions and by learning, which the trap forbids. The man's position therefore deteriorates in the domain on which his standing depends, and the deterioration is invisible to everybody, including frequently to himself, until a consequence arrives.

### The Way Out

Four measures, and the fourth is the one that changes the term of the arrangement.

**Move the standing from what the man knows to what the man does.** The man's account of himself — and his environment's account of him — should rest on his conduct, his obligations, his results, and his reliability rather than on his information. The measure is the one described in Chapter 25.5: the personal measure in the domains of conduct, effort, obligation, and household conduct. A man whose standing is his conduct can be asked a question he cannot answer without any change in his position, and the arrangement was in evidence in the case material of Chapter 25.

**Establish one practice in which the man is demonstrably learning.** The trade's new methods, the qualification, the professional association's technical sessions, the course, the reading, the tool, the machine, the software. The practice's function is to place the man in the position of a learner regularly and publicly enough that the position is normal, which converts the occasional admission of ignorance from an event into a habit.

**State the domain of your competence.** The measure that most effectively ends the trap, and it is a sentence: *that is outside what I do; the person who knows is X*. The sentence's use requires the man to possess an answer of a different kind, which is the referral, and the professionals who operate best in the material are the ones who refer fluently.

**Supply the correction yourself before somebody else does.** The man who finds an error of his own announced, in his own account, in the setting in which it matters, acquires the position of a man whose information can be trusted. The measure is the practical form of the recovery described in Chapter 25.6, and men who have adopted it describe a specific result: the organisation begins to bring the man's errors to him, which is the arrangement that prevents the most expensive failures.

## 32.4 When the Answer Is Not Yours to Give

A large proportion of the questions a man receives in a position of authority are not questions that his authority equips him to answer, and the failure to distinguish the classes is what produces the bluff. This section supplies the classification.

### The Four Kinds of Question

**1. The question within your authority and your information.** The decision that only the man can make, with the information available to him. His answer is required and it is his to give. The requirement is legitimate, and the element the man should retain from this chapter is the discipline of saying what he is deciding on: *This is a decision I am taking on what I know, and here is what I am not certain of.*

**2. The question within your authority and outside your information.** The decision that the man must take, and that requires information he does not have. His answer is required and its content is not: the correct response is the enquiry, the assessment, or the referral, with the decision timetable stated. *I will decide this by Friday, and I cannot decide it until I know X.* The form is described in Section 32.5.

**3. The question outside your authority.** The matter belonging to somebody else's function: the technical question belonging to the engineer, the medical question belonging to the practitioner, the legal question belonging to the lawyer, the financial matter belonging to the accountant, the discipline matter belonging to the institution's procedure, the domestic matter belonging to a spouse. The answer required is the referral, and the referral should be specific: the name, the institution, or the procedure, and not a general instruction to seek advice.

**4. The question that is not actually a question.** The most common of the four in a man's household and in his working life. The person is not requesting information; the person is requesting presence, acknowledgement, attention, an ally, or an account of the man's position. The questions in this class include the household's *what are we going to do about your son*, the subordinate's *who is going to support me in this*, the friend's *what do you think of what he has done*, and the wife's *is this how the next ten years are going to be*. The man's instinct is to supply information, which is not what is being requested, and the household's experience of the exchange is that the man answered a question that was not asked.

The correct response is the one in Part V of this book: the acknowledgement first, the orientation second, the help third. *That is a hard thing to be carrying. Let me hear how it is from your side.*

### The Distinctions to Apply Before Answering

Three, and they should be applied in the moment rather than afterwards.

**Is this my domain?** The fastest question in the classification and the one that catches most of the avoidable bluffs.

**Is the information required available to me, or is it available to somebody else?** If the answer is the latter, the correct response is the referral, and the referral is a complete answer to the question of what the man can do.

**Is the person asking for information, or for something else?** If the latter, the answer required is presence rather than content, and supplying information in its place is the reason the man is experienced by his own household as unreachable.
