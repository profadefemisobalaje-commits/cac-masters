## 22.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Two People After Six

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in counselling practice, family practice, and the research literature on anger and behaviour change.

**Idris is forty-four, a regional sales manager for a pharmaceutical distributor, married to Zainab for sixteen years, with three children aged between seven and fourteen.**

The case was presented by Idris's wife, through a family counselling service, with the following account: for approximately eight years, Idris had been a different person in the two hours after he returned from work. In that period he was irritable, quick to shout, intolerant of noise, and, on about four occasions each year, would shout at one of the children in a manner that left the child distressed for days. After about nine o'clock in the evening, the behaviour would change: he would become quiet, apologetic, and, in Zainab's description, unreachable.

Idris's own account, obtained separately, had three parts.

First, he did not accept that he had a problem with anger. He described himself as a man with a short temper after a long day, which he regarded as ordinary and which he stated was shared by most men in his position. He noted, with some accuracy, that he had never been violent and that his anger was expressed in words.

Second, he had attempted to change the behaviour four times. Each attempt had taken the same form: a decision, made after an incident, that he would not shout at the children again; a period of between one and three weeks during which the decision held; and a return to the previous pattern, generally after a day of particular difficulty at work. Each return was followed by a period of increased shame, during which he did not discuss the matter at all.

Third, and after about four sessions, the material that gave the case its form emerged. Idris's working day had a specific structure that he had never described to anybody: he spent the final three hours of each day, between four and seven in the evening, in his vehicle, driving between two towns to deliver stock and collect payments, and it was in that period that the pressure of the day accumulated — the shortfalls, the customers who did not pay, the targets he had not met, and the knowledge that his income depended on figures that he had never once, in eight years, met at the level expected of him. He arrived at his house, in his description, "with about three hours of accumulated things that I had nowhere to put." The house was noisy, the children were present, and the first request of the evening frequently produced the shout.

The assessment and the plan had the following components.

The function was identified in the third session: the anger was the discharge of an accumulated state, and the state was not the day's events but the man's assessment of himself against a target he had never met. The shout was, in his own account once he had described it, *the one moment of the day when I am the one deciding something.*

The trigger map was completed over four weeks and produced three findings that Idris had not anticipated. The shouts occurred, without exception, in the first ninety minutes after his arrival. They occurred on the days when he had received a payment shortfall, which was the majority of days. They did not occur on the days when he stopped at the second town, where a colleague kept an office, and spent twenty minutes there — a practice he had begun years earlier and had abandoned.

The plan was constructed along the five stages and consisted of the following measures.

**Environment.** A stop of twenty minutes was reinstated at the second town, at the colleague's office, on every working day. Idris described this measure as the single most effective element and as the one he had resisted, on the grounds that it delayed his arrival at home. The counter-argument, which was decisive, was that the twenty minutes produced an arrival that his household could live with.

**Substitute.** For the first ninety minutes after arrival, a fixed arrangement: Idris would change, would spend fifteen minutes alone, and would then take the children's bathtime, which was the loudest and most demanding period of the evening and which he had previously spent in another room attempting to be undisturbed. The change placed him, deliberately, in the situation he had been avoiding, but at a point after the transition.

**Measurement.** A daily record of the state on arrival, from one to ten, and of any incident. The record produced the case's most instructive finding within five weeks: the state on arrival correlated much more strongly with whether he had stopped at the second town than with the day's actual figures.

**Psychological work.** The material about the target was addressed directly, and it required about four months. Idris's position, which he had never stated to anybody including his wife, was that he had been recruited into a role requiring a level of sales performance that he did not have and could not obtain, and that he had spent eight years concealing this from his employer and from his household. The plan included a specific step: a conversation with his employer about a change of role, which he conducted in the fifth month and which resulted in a move to a technical support position at a lower level of variable pay.

**Accountability.** A monthly arrangement with a male counsellor and, at Idris's own initiative, an arrangement with his brother-in-law, whom he described as the only person in the family who would tell him the truth. The brother-in-law's function was specific: Idris telephoned him on the days when an incident had occurred, without exception, and reported it.

The outcome at eighteen months was: no shouts at the children in about fourteen months, with one incident in month four; a change of role with a reduction of about nine per cent in income and, in Idris's description, the removal of the daily basis of his self-assessment; and a change in the household's arrangement of the evening, which Zainab described as the period in which she stopped managing the children's behaviour around her husband's state.

Idris's summary was: *I did not have a temper. I had a nine-to-seven situation that I could not tell anybody about, and a house that I arrived at with nowhere to put it. Four attempts to decide that I would not shout produced three weeks each. What worked was a twenty-minute stop and telling my wife what the last eight years had actually been.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The man's own account was accurate in its content and wrong in its conclusion.** Idris was correct that he had never been violent and correct that his anger was expressed in words. His conclusion — that this constituted an ordinary temper — was the obstacle, and it is the most common obstacle in this material: the man assesses the behaviour against a threshold that he sets, and the threshold is generally set below his own conduct.

**The four attempts followed the same mechanism as Section 22.3.** A decision made in the aftermath of an incident; a period of abstinence; a return; an increase in shame; and no change in the arrangement. The pattern is set out in the chapter as the reason resolve fails, and the case illustrates it without embellishment.

**The measurement produced the finding that the man could not have reached by reflection.** The correlation between the twenty-minute stop and the state on arrival was established from Idris's own record, and the finding is the kind that reflection systematically misses. This is the reason Section 22.5 places measurement at the centre of the method.

**The weakness was sustained by an undisclosed situation.** Eight years of concealed performance shortfall. The case belongs in this chapter as an example of the rule in Section 22.4: a weakness that began in a specific situation is frequently not addressable without addressing the situation, and the arrangement changes — the twenty-minute stop and the change of role — did more of the work than the psychological material.

**Accountability was supplied by a person with the right properties.** The brother-in-law was selected for a specific reason — that he would state an assessment to the man's face — and the arrangement's function was narrow: the report of an incident, on the day it occurred. The narrow scope is the reason the arrangement survived, and it is the practical form of Section 22.6's fourth prohibition.
