## 40.9 Practical Exercise: The Page and the Year

The exercise is conducted over four sittings and produces one page in the man's own hand.

### Sitting One: The Position

**Section A: The assets of the second half.** Record, as figures, what the man has that the years can be constructed on: the income and its requirements; the savings and the period they would cover; the house; the health and the two warnings; the capacities and the qualifications; the relationships he has; the standing he holds; and the years, counted at his own table from his actual age.

**Section B: The obligations currently carried.** Every item, in the form of Chapter 33.4's budget: the person, the arrangement, the cost in money and time per month, and the period it has been conducted.

**Section C: The preference register.** For two weeks, record each preference as it arrives, without assessment: what he wanted to eat, where he wanted to sit, what he wanted to refuse, what he did not want to attend, what he wanted to say. The register is required material for Sitting Two.

### Sitting Two: The Audit

Complete the four questions of Section 40.5 for every item in Section B, and record the item's classification.

Then write the three lists.

**The first list: what I keep.** With the arrangement for each, stated specifically.

**The second list: what I set down.** With the form of the release — the closure, the transfer, or the reduction — the date, the notice, and the arrangement.

**The third list: what I take up.** Four items at most, each specific, each dated, and each with a first step written as an act rather than as an intention.

### Sitting Three: The Household and the Instruments

**Section A: The household statement.** Write it in the four-part form of Section 40.6's Matter One, in no more than one page: the position, what I have found, what I intend to keep, set down, and take up, and what I require of the household's account of what it does to them.

**Section B: The resistance.** For each item on the second list, record the party, their interest, the arrangement, the notice, and the sentence the man will use. Record also the two prohibitions: no argument with the resisting party, and no requirement of their agreement.

**Section C: The professional route.** Record which of the choices — the work, the household's arrangements, the marriage, the children — requires legal, financial, clinical, or relationship advice, and record the appointment's date.

**Section D: The ally.** Name the ally, record what the ally is told, and record what the man is not to do.

### Sitting Four: The Page and the Year

Write one page, in the man's own hand, dated, containing:

1. The accurate statement of his position, in four sentences.
2. The three lists.
3. The seven choices of Section 40.7, with his decision on each, stated in one sentence each.
4. The first step of the third list, with its date.
5. The sentence that states what the years are for.

Then fix the reviews: three months, six months, and a year, with the dates written, with the ally present where possible.

## 40.10 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is this not selfish?"**

The distinction of Section 40.1 governs, and the test is supplied there: what the household's position looks like at the end of the work. The obligations owed are conducted; the repairs are made; the arrangements are disclosed; the household's account is sought. The specific finding from the case material is the one that answers the question: the men who conducted the audit were better able to discharge their obligations over the following decade than the men who did not, and their households report the position as improved. The chapter's position is that the account of the man's own life as selfish is the rule of Section 40.2's worth anchor in the man's own voice, and that the rule has been examined in Chapters 25 and 37.

**"I do not know what I want. The exercise does not work for me."**

This is the presentation of Section 40.3's third loss, and it is expected. The sequence is supplied: the preference register first, in the physical and practical matters, for at least two weeks; then the registration practice of Chapter 39.5; then the questions, recorded as they arrive and returned to in the following month. The finding from the case material is that the capacity returns, that it returns before the larger questions can be answered, and that the register should not be abandoned on the basis of the first week's result.

**"My wife will not agree to this."**

The chapter's position requires the household's account and not the household's agreement. The disclosure in the four-part form; the household's material sought in specific terms; the arrangements conducted with notice and with the household's position addressed; and the resistance endured. The case material records two findings: the household's specific objection is frequently to one or two items rather than to the audit, and the household's objection to the man's own arrangements is generally addressed by the man's conduct in the household's arrangements, which is the material of Chapters 39.5 and 38.6.

**"I have obligations I cannot set down. The children, the parents, the debts."**

The chapter does not propose their release. The first list exists for this material, and the instruction is that the owed obligations be named accurately and arranged rather than conducted as the whole of the man's life. The audit's effect for the men in this position is generally not the release of an obligation but three changes: the release of the items that were not owed, the arrangement of the items that were, and the addition of the third list. The case material records the finding that the third list's addition is what alters the position, and that the reduction of the obligations frequently follows it.

**"I am fifty-eight. Is it not too late?"**

The case of Section 40.8 is included for this question, and its man was fifty-eight with a course he had intended for twenty-one years. The chapter's material on the point: the third list's items are generally available at fifty-eight and at sixty-two and are generally small; the years' number, counted at the man's own table, is generally more than his account of it; and the finding from the case material is that the men who conducted the work at sixty report the same outcome as the men who conducted it at forty-eight. What is not available is the previous decades, which is the material of Section 40.3's second loss and of Chapter 28's accounting — and the accounting's purpose, as that chapter established, is not the man's punishment but the accurate statement of what is left.

**"What if my household's position is altered by my choices and a member of it suffers?"**

Then the choice is being conducted correctly, because every choice of consequence alters somebody's position, and a choice that alters nobody's is not a choice. The chapter's requirement is that the alteration be known, stated, and arranged: the disclosure, the notice, the period of transition, and the party's capacity addressed. The specific finding from the case material for readers who hold this question: the household member whose position is altered by the man's neglect of his own life is the member who has been receiving a man who is not there, and the alteration produced by the audit is frequently the first change in that member's position that either party can describe.

::: summary
**Chapter 40 in brief.** Choosing oneself does not mean the abandonment of the people a man is responsible for, the man's pleasure as a criterion, the ending of a marriage for a better one, or the announcement that he is putting himself first. It means that the man's own life is an item on the ledger with the other items, that his requirements are legitimate rather than a reward, that the obligations are audited rather than assumed, that his preference is information he is required to have, and that the choice is conducted in the years that remain. The test of which version is operating is what the household's position looks like at the end of the work.

A man's deferral is a process in seven stages — his requirements subordinated early, his first obligations accepted, the arrangement established and rewarded, the deferral given a date, his own requirements no longer formulated, his own material not counted, and the discovery — and it is anchored to one of five rules: worth, duty, fear, permission, or identity. The costs are four: the lives he was for, in which the obligations were discharged and the relationships were not constructed; what was not built; the capacity to know what he wants, which returns in a sequence when it is exercised; and his own account of his life, which has been composed of other people's items.

Four permissions are required, and they are granted by argument rather than by the environment: the permission to have a life; the permission not to be everybody's solution; the permission to be a beginner and to be bad at it in public at this age; and the permission to ask for what he wants, with the distinction that the statement is his responsibility and the answer is not.

The audit is one page of three lists, classified by four questions: whether the item is owed, whether he wants it, whether he would take it up now, and what its ending would require. The second list's releases take three forms — the closure, the transfer, and the reduction — and the third list's items must be specific, dated, and stated with a first step. The choice is then conducted in four matters: the household, with the disclosure and its account required and not its agreement; the people who will resist, whose resistance is frequently legitimate and is endured rather than argued; the timing, in which the third list is conducted first and the changes are distributed; and the instruments, which are the ally, the professional route, the written record, and the reviews at three months, six months, and a year.

The seven choices are the work, the household's arrangements and the extended family, the marriage, the children, the man's own arrangements, the standing, and the years — and the product of the work is one page, in the man's own hand, dated, containing the three lists, the seven decisions, the first step, and the sentence that states what the years are for.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. What has your own life been deferred to, and what is the condition? Has it been met, and was it replaced?
2. Which of the five anchors of Section 40.2 holds your deferral in place, and what is the sentence in your own words?
3. Complete the audit's second list. How many of the items are actually owed, and what would the release of the remainder require?
4. Which of the four permissions are you withholding, and what is the argument you use?
5. Write the third list. Which item has been there longest, and what is its first step?
6. Consider the last five years. Which of your obligations were conducted as arrangements and which consumed the years? What would the audit have altered in 2021?
7. Write the sentence that states what your remaining years are for, and read it to the ally.

- **On the second half of life:** the research and practice literature on midlife and later-life development, including the findings on the reconfiguration of purpose and identity in the second half of life, on the evidence concerning the pursuit of long-deferred aims, and on the patterns of satisfaction reported in later life.
- **On goal pursuit and the deferral of personal aims:** the research on personal goals, including the findings on the effects of unattained goals, on the conditions under which long-deferred aims are taken up, on the value of specific and dated intentions, and on the role of social support in their conduct.
- **On caregiving burden and the reduction of obligations:** the literature on caregiving and on the burden of family obligation, including the findings on the effects of over-commitment on health and household relationships, and the evidence on the outcomes of reducing or transferring commitments.
- **On preference and self-knowledge:** the research on the identification of preference and on self-knowledge, including the findings on the effects of chronic self-neglect on the capacity to identify one's own states and preferences, and on the methods by which the capacity is recovered.
- **Practical resources:** career and financial counselling; individual counselling and psychological services; couple's counselling; legal advice on property and family arrangements; and the material in Appendices D, G, H, and P.
