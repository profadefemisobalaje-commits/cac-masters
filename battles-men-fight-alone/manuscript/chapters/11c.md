## 11.5 The Hidden Cost of Constant Comparison

Constant comparison is the mechanism by which the definition of success described in Section 11.1 operates on a man's daily experience, and its costs are frequently invisible to the man paying them. Six costs are worth naming.

**The loss of intrinsic satisfaction.** A man who measures himself against others loses access to the satisfaction that comes from the work itself: solving a difficult problem, developing a skill, doing something well. The work becomes a means of keeping position, and its intrinsic rewards are no longer registered.

**The distortion of decisions.** Comparison does not merely produce feelings; it produces assessments, and assessments guide decisions. Men have taken on unmanageable debt, expanded businesses beyond their capacity, bought vehicles they could not maintain, and accepted roles for which they were not suited, in each case on the basis of a comparison rather than an analysis.

**The erosion of generosity.** Comparison converts peers into rivals. A man who is measuring himself against his colleagues and contemporaries cannot celebrate their successes without cost, and this produces a specific and unpleasant change in character that men frequently notice in themselves with alarm: a reluctance to congratulate, an interest in others' difficulties, and a tendency to recount one's own achievements more often than before.

**The narrowing of options.** Where a man's reference group is defined by a single occupation or a single community, comparison reduces the range of paths he can consider, because any deviation from the group's standard is read as failure. Men in this position frequently decline opportunities that would have suited them, because the opportunities do not compare favourably against the group's definition of advancement.

**The exhaustion of the competitive posture.** Continuous comparison requires continuous effort, and it produces the state described in Chapters 5 and 12: permanent readiness, degraded sleep, and a low-grade anxiety that the man attributes to work volume rather than to the ranking activity that is generating it.

**The transmission to children.** A father who measures himself against his contemporaries transmits the same measurement to his children. Children in such households are frequently aware, from a young age, that they are competing on their father's behalf, and they carry the arrangement forward.

### The Four Practical Measures

Four measures reduce the cost of comparison without requiring the man to stop noticing others, which is not within voluntary control.

**Fix your own reference point.** Establish a personal standard, in writing: an income sufficient for defined obligations, a level of saving, a quantity of time at home, a state of health, and a defined contribution. Judging yourself against a standard you have chosen is the single most effective measure, and it is the exercise at the end of this chapter.

**Change the reference group deliberately.** Men frequently belong to reference groups that were assembled by circumstance — school cohorts, family networks, occupational peers. Deliberately adding a reference group with different priorities (a community organisation, a professional body with a service orientation, a group of men who have made an unusual choice) provides alternative standards and reduces the domination of the existing one. Chapter 27 develops the practical method.

**Reduce exposure.** This is unglamorous and effective: reducing the social media accounts that present curated achievement, reducing attendance at events whose primary function is display, and reducing the number of occasions on which the question *what are you doing now?* is likely to be asked. The point is not isolation but the removal of unnecessary and continuous exposure to the comparison mechanism.

**Convert comparison into information.** Where a comparison is genuinely relevant, it can be used rather than suffered. The questions worth extracting are specific and practical: how did he obtain that contract; what qualifications did she pursue; who introduced him to that market; what did the move cost? A comparison converted into information is a research activity, and research is not corrosive.

## 11.6 Creating an Individual Definition of Success

The final section is the constructive work of the chapter. It consists of constructing a definition of success that is personal, specific, verifiable, and calibrated to the man's actual circumstances — and then using it.

### The Requirements of a Workable Definition

A personal definition of success must satisfy five conditions.

**It must be specific.** *To be successful* has no operational meaning. *To have a home in my own name, to educate all four children through secondary school, to have an income sufficient to meet my household's requirements and my mother's medical costs, and to have three months of reserve* is a definition that can be assessed and acted on.

**It must be verifiable.** A man must be able to determine, at any point, whether he is meeting it. Definitions that depend on other people's opinions cannot be verified and therefore cannot deliver satisfaction.

**It must be yours.** The exercise in Section 8.5 of Chapter 2 applies here: each element should be marked as kinship, work, or own. A definition assembled entirely from the expectations of others will be abandoned or resented.

**It must be calibrated to capacity.** A definition that exceeds what is achievable under the man's circumstances is a sentence rather than a plan. Where a man's definition includes elements he cannot achieve on the present path, the definition should specify the change of path rather than the impossible objective.

**It must include what the man is not willing to sacrifice.** This is the element most frequently omitted, and it is the one that makes the definition useful. *I will achieve X, and I am not willing to lose my health, my marriage, or my children's regard in order to do it* is a definition that includes a boundary, and the boundary is what prevents the definition from consuming the man's life in the way described in Section 11.2.

### The Domains of a Personal Definition

A workable definition covers seven domains, and men who construct one are consistently surprised by how few of the seven they have ever considered deliberately.

| Domain | The question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Income and work | What level of income, from what kind of work, is sufficient for the obligations I have chosen to carry? |
| Housing and assets | What do I need to own, and what am I able to maintain rather than merely to acquire? |
| Family and marriage | What kind of husband and father do I intend to be, in terms of presence and behaviour rather than provision alone? |
| Children | What do I want them to be able to do, and to know about me, by the time they are adults? |
| Health | What state of health do I need to sustain, and what am I doing that endangers it? |
| Community and contribution | What contribution do I intend to make beyond my household? |
| Rest and life | What do I intend to do with the time I have, and what gives me satisfaction independently of achievement? |

Table: The seven domains of a personal definition of success. A definition that omits the last two domains is not a life; it is a schedule.

### Using the Definition

A definition of success is only useful if it is applied. Three applications are recommended.

**As a standard for decisions.** Before accepting a new obligation, a new role, a new debt, or a new commitment, the definition provides the test: does this move me towards my own standard, or towards somebody else's ranking?

**As a defence in conversation.** A man with a written definition can answer the family's comparison with a statement that is difficult to argue with: *my plan is to do X by the age of Y, and I am on schedule.* Whether or not the interlocutor accepts it, the man is no longer conducting the conversation in the terms of the comparison.

**As a measure of progress, reviewed annually.** An annual review, on the man's birthday or at a fixed date, comparing position with definition, serves two purposes: it produces evidence of progress, which counters the mechanism described in Section 11.3, and it identifies the deviation, which permits correction before the deviation becomes permanent.

### The Comparison That Is Worth Making

Not all comparison is corrosive, and the distinction is worth stating precisely, because men who attempt to abandon comparison altogether usually fail, and they fail for a reason: comparison is the ordinary procedure of any learning process.

What distinguishes the damaging form from the useful one is the object of the comparison. The damaging form compares **positions**: who has more, who has advanced further, who is ahead in the ranking. The useful form compares **methods**: how was that result obtained, what did it require, and what part of the requirement is available to me? The first produces a verdict about the man; the second produces information about the work. The first is conducted at ceremonies, on social platforms, and in family conversations; the second is conducted in a direct conversation, in a professional relationship, or in study.

Three practical consequences follow from the distinction.

First, a man who wishes to reduce the cost of comparison should not attempt to stop noticing other people's positions, which is not within voluntary control. He should redirect the comparison to the method, which is a learnable act and which converts a corrosive experience into a productive one.

Second, the men who are most useful to consult are not the men who are furthest ahead, whose circumstances may be entirely unlike the man's own, but the men who are one or two steps ahead on a path comparable to his own. The information available from the second group is practical and reproducible; from the first, it is usually a description of an outcome without its conditions.

Third, a man should have at least one comparison that is internal to himself: his own position of three or five years ago, measured in the specific terms of his own definition. This comparison is the only one that is entirely within the man's control, and it is the only one that produces evidence of progress rather than evidence of position. Men who adopt it consistently report a reduction in the sense of being behind that no amount of external achievement had previously produced.

### What This Section Is Not Recommending

Two clarifications prevent misunderstanding. The chapter does not recommend the abandonment of ambition. Men who construct personal definitions frequently find that their ambition increases, because it is no longer being consumed in the management of comparison, and because the objectives are now achievable and their achievement is satisfying. Nor does it recommend the rejection of family or community expectations, which are legitimate and frequently valuable sources of standards. It recommends only this: that a man should hold a definition of success that he has examined, that fits his circumstances, and that he would defend — rather than carrying one that was assembled from comparisons and applied without his consent.
