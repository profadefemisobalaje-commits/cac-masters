## 24.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Retired on a Friday

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in occupational health, geriatric and family practice, and the research literature on retirement and identity.

**Alhaji Ganiyu is sixty-three, a retired assistant director in a federal ministry, married to Rukayat for thirty-six years, with five children, all of whom are adults and four of whom live outside Nigeria.**

Ganiyu retired in March, on his sixty-third birthday, having served for thirty-seven years. He had planned for the retirement for four years: the house was complete, a property in the town had been built for rental income, the pension arrangements were in order, and two of the children were settled. He described his plan for the retirement, in the assessment, as follows: *I had done everything properly. There was nothing left to arrange.*

The condition emerged over the following fourteen months and was described by Rukayat, who requested the assessment, in the following terms: he had stopped leaving the house except for Friday prayers and the market; he had begun to drink at about four in the afternoon, which he had never done during his working life; he had become angry about small household matters and had, on two occasions, raised his voice at his grandson; he had stopped attending the meetings of the professional association of which he had been a member for thirty years and had, in November, declined a position in the community's development association; and he had begun to telephone his children abroad at intervals of a few days with no subject.

The assessment identified three components.

First, a depressive episode of moderate severity, which had been present for about nine months. It had presented in the male register: irritability, alcohol use in the afternoon, sleep disturbance, weight loss, and, in his own account, a persistent sense that there was nothing to get up for.

Second, a physical component: assessment identified hypertension, which had been untreated since a routine examination two years earlier, and a testosterone deficiency, which was confirmed on two separate measurements and which his physician regarded as contributing substantially to the loss of energy and drive.

Third, and the component that required the work of this chapter, a structural absence: a role inventory, completed in writing, established that of eleven roles Ganiyu had held at the age of sixty, nine had been removed by the retirement, and the two that remained — husband and grandfather — had been conducted entirely within a household that had been organised for thirty years around his absence during working hours.

The further history had a specific feature that Ganiyu had never described. During the four years of planning his retirement, he had not planned for the retirement's content. He had planned for its finances, its housing, and its obligations, and he had described his intention for the time itself, whenever asked, as *resting* and *taking things easy* — phrases that he had repeated so often that he had not examined them. He said, in the assessment: *I planned everything except the part where I am still alive at eight in the morning.*

The work extended over about two years, and its components were as follows.

The medical component came first: treatment for hypertension, treatment for the testosterone deficiency, treatment for the depressive episode, and the reduction of alcohol use, which did not require a withdrawal programme and which was addressed with the methods of Chapter 22.

The grief work came second, and it took the form of a written account of the working life: thirty-seven years of it, in sequence, including the positions, the colleagues, the disputes, the two appointments he had not obtained, and the period of eight years in which he had been posted to a state in which he had been unhappy. Ganiyu described this task as the most substantial element of the whole period, and he wrote approximately forty pages by hand.

The materials were identified in the fourth month and were, in his own account, entirely unpredicted. He identified a competence he had never regarded as a competence: he had spent seventeen years, in two of his postings, negotiating with state-level officials on budget matters, and he had become, in that period, unusually good at it. He identified an interest he had abandoned at the age of twenty-eight: the repair and maintenance of mechanical equipment, which he had learnt from his father and had stopped when his career became demanding. And he identified a relationship that had lapsed: a former colleague, with whom he had worked for twelve years and with whom he had not spoken since his colleague's own retirement four years earlier.

The arrangements were established as follows. Ganiyu undertook to provide three sessions of advice each month, without payment, to the small business association of a neighbouring town, whose members were negotiating collectively with suppliers. He established a workshop in a room at the back of the house, in which he repaired and maintained small equipment, initially for his household and later for a small number of paying customers, and which he describes as the single most consequential element of the whole reconstruction: *I had forgotten that I am a person who can make a thing work with his hands. It was the first thing in fourteen months that had nothing to do with who I used to be.* And he re-established contact with his former colleague, with a fixed arrangement to meet on the first Saturday of each month to discuss machinery, which both men described as a friendship rather than an appointment.

Two years and four months after the retirement, the position was as follows: the depressive episode had resolved; the hypertension was controlled; the alcohol use had returned to occasional; and Ganiyu's own description of the arrangement was: *I have four things in the week. On Tuesday I go to the association. On Thursday the workshop is open. On the first Saturday I see Aliyu. Every morning I walk for forty minutes and I do not sit down when I come back. That is all. It took me fourteen months and a doctor and forty pages of writing to find out that the answer was that small, and I had been telling everybody for four years that I would be resting.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The retirement had been planned in every respect except its content.** The financial, housing, and family arrangements were complete. The man's own description — that he had planned everything except the part where he is still alive at eight in the morning — is the chapter's central statement, and it identifies the specific gap that produces the condition in men who are otherwise well organised.

**Three conditions were present simultaneously and required separate treatment.** The depressive episode, the untreated hypertension, and the testosterone deficiency each contributed to the presentation, and the case is included because the assessment of men in this condition is frequently conducted as though it were solely psychological. The medical assessment came first in the sequence, and its placement is deliberate.

**Nine of eleven roles were removed at once.** The role inventory is the instrument that made the situation legible, and its output is the reason the work was directed at materials rather than at activity in general: the man's initial attempts at *keeping busy* had failed for the reason given in Section 24.3.

**The materials were not the ones the man would have predicted.** A negotiation competence of seventeen years' standing, a repair workshop abandoned at twenty-eight, and a lapsed friendship. The identification of the materials from a written history rather than from reflection is the case's most transferable feature, and the workshop — an activity of no relation to the man's former profession, in which he was a beginner — produced the largest effect.

**The arrangement was small and specific.** Four items in a week, each scheduled. This is the pattern in the accounts of men who reconstruct successfully, and it stands against the expectation that the second half of life requires a project of substantial scale, which is the expectation that most frequently produces a period of activity without conviction followed by a return to the empty season.
