# When Everyone Depends on You {.chapter num="10" short="When Everyone Depends on You"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the expectations placed on a family provider in extended-family systems, and distinguish between obligations that are genuinely his and those that have simply accumulated.
2. Map the network of dependency in his own life, in terms of money, time, decisions, and emotional availability, and assess what the network currently costs.
3. Explain the emotional burden of being financially indispensable, including the resentment, fear, and isolation it produces.
4. Apply a method for setting boundaries that preserves responsibility without accepting unlimited liability, and rehearse the conversations that boundaries require.
5. Establish shared financial planning with a partner and a household, including the specific mechanisms — the budget meeting, the priority list, and the written schedule of family support.
6. Recognise and prevent provider burnout, using the warning sequence and the protective practices set out in this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

Consider what a man in his late forties typically supports, and the list is usually longer than he first reports. His own household: a wife or partner, and between one and five children, some of them in tertiary education. His parents: a mother's medication, a father's funeral costs, the maintenance of a house in the village. His siblings, in some cases: school fees for a brother's children, a hospital bill for a sister, a contribution to a nephew's wedding. An extended network: a cousin who needs capital for a business, an uncle's medical treatment, a family meeting's levy, a community association's dues, a church or mosque project contribution. And, frequently, another household entirely: a late brother's family, an elderly aunt, a niece in the city for her education.

Each individual obligation is legitimate. Each has a reason, a history, and a moral claim. A man would find it difficult to refuse any single one of them, and would be criticised if he did. What no one ever does is add them up. When they are added up, the total frequently exceeds the man's entire income, and the difference is met by borrowing, by the sale of assets, by the deferral of his own medical treatment, by the neglect of his own retirement, and by the quiet assumption that he will work until he dies.

This chapter deals with that situation. It is not an argument against family responsibility, which is one of the most valuable features of the societies in which extended-family systems operate, and which protects millions of people against hardship that no state in the region is yet able to absorb. It is an argument against unlimited liability, unexamined accumulation, and the assumption that a man's usefulness has no limit — and against the arrangement in which a man's own household, his own health, and his own future quietly become the item that absorbs every shortfall.

## 10.1 The Expectations Placed on Family Providers

To change an arrangement, a man must understand it. This section identifies the expectations that produce provider overload, in the order in which they usually arrive in a man's life.

### The First Expectation: The Household

The obligation to support a wife and children is the one that most men accept without question and often without negotiation. In practice, it has three components that are worth separating, because they impose different burdens.

**Subsistence:** food, shelter, clothing, transport, and medical care. This obligation is continuous, non-negotiable, and demanding.

**Investment:** education, family assets, and the establishment of children in adult life. This obligation is large, deferred, and frequently unpredictable in cost: a university fee, a dowry, a business start-up, a medical episode.

**Standard:** the level at which the household lives relative to comparable households in its community. This component is rarely named as an obligation, and it is frequently the largest source of financial pressure, because it is set by comparison rather than by need.

### The Second Expectation: Parents

Support for parents is among the most strongly held obligations in the societies examined in this book, and it is generally understood as a repayment of the investment made in the man's upbringing. It carries a distinctive emotional weight: a man who fails to support a parent experiences a moral failure of a different order from a man who cannot pay a supplier.

Three features make this obligation difficult to manage. It is frequently open-ended, because medical costs in old age are unpredictable and can absorb almost any income. It is asymmetrically distributed among siblings, because the obligation falls in practice on the one with the visible capacity rather than being shared. And it is rarely discussed in the terms in which it is carried: no family sits down to establish what the support will consist of, what the other siblings will contribute, and what will happen as the needs increase.

### The Third Expectation: Siblings, Their Children, and the Wider Kin

The third band of expectation is the one that expands indefinitely. It includes school fees for siblings' children, support for widowed relatives, capital for a relative's business, funeral contributions, community levies, and the informal role of the family's banker.

Its features are as follows. The obligations are individually modest and collectively very large. They are frequently generated by other relatives' decisions rather than by need in any objective sense. They arrive with a moral vocabulary — *you are the one who can; the family is relying on you; do not let us be disgraced* — that makes refusal socially expensive. And they are permanent, because each support successfully provided establishes a precedent for the next request.

### The Fourth Expectation: The Community

Finally, the community expects contributions: development associations, hometown unions, religious bodies, cooperative societies, and the ceremonies and funerals at which presence and contribution are recorded. These obligations are integral to a man's standing and to his family's ability to call upon community support in a crisis. They are, in a real sense, insurance premised on reciprocity, and a man who withdraws from them entirely will find that the community is less available to him when he needs it.

The difficulty is that the expectations are set by visible capacity and are frequently adjusted upwards as a man's position improves, while the reduction that follows a decline in his fortunes is never correspondingly adjusted.

### The Cumulative Effect

The four bands of expectation, taken together, produce a position that a man frequently has never examined, and its main features are these.

- **The obligations exceed the income.** This is not a marginal imbalance; in many cases the sum of family and community obligations is one and a half to two times the man's actual income, and the difference is met by debt.
- **The largest obligations are invisible.** Nobody in the family knows what the man spends on the other obligations. The wife knows the household's element; the mother knows hers; the siblings know theirs. No person, including the man, holds the complete picture.
- **The obligations are permanent, while the income is not.** Support arrangements made during a period of good earnings frequently continue after the earnings have fallen, because the reduction would require a disclosure that the man is unwilling to make.
- **Personal and retirement needs are the residual.** A man meeting these obligations typically funds his own medical care, pension provision, and housing only if something is left. In practice, nothing is left.
- **The burden is borne in silence.** The most damaging feature of the whole arrangement is that a man carrying two households, four dependants beyond his own children, and community obligations typically discusses it with nobody, on the grounds that the situation is normal and that complaining would be shameful.

::: keypoint
The problem is not that a man supports his extended family. The problem is that he does so without a total, without a limit, without a discussion, and without any adjustment when his capacity changes — and the household that absorbs the shortfall is his own.
:::

## 10.2 Supporting Parents, Spouses, Children, and Relatives

Having described the expectations, it is worth examining each band of obligation in practice, because the interventions differ.

### Supporting Parents

Three practices materially improve the arrangement without reducing the support.

**Establish the plan with the siblings.** A family conversation establishing what each sibling can contribute, in what proportion, and how the arrangement will change as needs increase. This conversation will not be straightforward, and it is far easier than the alternative: thirteen years of unilateral support, followed by a crisis in which every sibling discovers the position for the first time.

**Separate the predictable from the unpredictable.** Routine monthly support can be planned. A medical emergency cannot, and provision for it should be made deliberately: an agreed fund, an insurance arrangement where available and affordable, or an explicit understanding among siblings of how such costs will be shared.

**Give time as well as money.** Men often assume that the obligation is financial. In practice, parents frequently report that presence matters more than funds, and time spent on visits, transport to appointments, and practical assistance is both less costly and more valuable.

### Supporting a Spouse

The obligations a man carries towards his own partner are of a different kind from those towards his parents, and two specific problems recur.

**The unilateral allocation of income.** A man who allocates income to extended-family obligations without consultation is not merely spending money; he is making a decision that affects his partner's household. The most common and most damaging pattern in provider overload is the quiet transfer: support given to relatives from the household's resources, without discussion, on the grounds that a man is entitled to exercise his own judgement about his own income. This is the mechanism by which many marriages accumulate resentment that surfaces years later in a financial crisis.

The remedy is not to stop the support but to make it a shared decision, with a figure, a duration, and a review date. Couples who do this report a marked reduction in conflict, and — this surprises many men — partners are frequently more generous than their husbands expect, once the support is part of a plan rather than a private expenditure.

**The assumption that support flows in one direction.** Extended-family systems are frequently experienced by women as one-directional: money leaves the household for the husband's relatives, and the wife's relatives are expected to manage without. Where both partners have family obligations, the arrangement should be symmetrical and explicit, and where they do not, the asymmetry should be named and agreed rather than assumed.

### Supporting Children

Children's needs are the least negotiable, and the difficulties that arise are of three kinds.

**The educational commitment exceeding present capacity.** School and university fees are frequently the largest single item in the budget, and the commitment extends for fifteen to twenty years. Where income is volatile, the commitment should be structured as far as possible — education savings arrangements, family contributions agreed in advance, and a realistic choice of institutions that reflects the capacity to sustain the payments, rather than a choice reflecting the family's position relative to its neighbours.

**The differential treatment of children.** Where a father supports some children fully and others not at all — between households, between marriages, or between children with differing abilities — the consequences are long-lasting and are borne chiefly by the children. Chapter 17 addresses this; the relevant point here is that obligations to children are cumulative rather than optional, and that a man cannot offset financial support to one child against the emotional consequences for another.

**The parental obligation to be present.** Time is a form of provision, and it is the one that most frequently gets displaced by the financial obligations described in this chapter. A man working sixty hours a week to support nine people has made a decision about his children's upbringing, whether or not he recognises it as one.

### Supporting Relatives

Three practices reduce the cost and increase the effectiveness of relative support.

**Convert repeated support into defined arrangements.** A relative who requires ongoing support will require it indefinitely. Converting an ad hoc series of payments into a defined monthly arrangement with a duration and a review date gives the man control over the total and gives the recipient something more useful than uncertainty.

**Prefer capacity-building to consumption support.** Where the relative is capable of income, assistance in the form of capital, training, equipment, or an introduction is frequently more effective in the long run than the provision of consumption. It is also more accurately accounted for: a business loan with a repayment schedule is a different instrument from a monthly subsidy.

**Establish the principle that other relatives exist.** The single most effective structural change in provider overload is the redistribution of a specific obligation among the relatives who are capable of contributing. This is frequently resisted, and it is frequently more successful than the man expects, because the relatives who have contributed nothing were, in most cases, never asked.

### The Household Conversation

The interventions above all require one conversation to begin, and that conversation is the one with the man's own household. Its content is straightforward: the total income, the total obligations, and the allocation. Its effect, in the experience of men who undertake it, is at least as significant as the financial improvement it produces: for the first time, the whole position is visible to the people who are most affected by it, and the man is no longer carrying the arithmetic alone.
