## 14.5 When Understanding Cannot Be Found in the Relationship

This section addresses the case in which the attempts described in Section 14.4 have been made and the understanding has not been obtained. It begins with a distinction that determines everything that follows.

### Unwilling and Unable

**Unwilling.** The partner is capable of the conversation and declines it. The evidence of unwillingness includes: the capacity to hold comparable conversations about other matters; an active avoidance that appears when the subject approaches; a consistent pattern across years and contexts; and a stated position, sometimes explicit, that the man's interior life is not her concern.

**Unable.** The partner cannot conduct the conversation, for reasons that are not a refusal: her own mental health condition or trauma history; a load that has genuinely exhausted her capacity; an upbringing and cultural formation that equipped her with no means of receiving such material without interpreting it as a threat; or a personal history in which her own disclosures were received in a manner that makes them impossible for her.

**The distinction matters practically.** Where the partner is unable, the appropriate response is generally to obtain the understanding elsewhere while maintaining the relationship, and to consider whether anything can improve her capacity — treatment for her own conditions, a reduction in her load, counselling for the couple. Where the partner is unwilling, the appropriate response involves a decision about the marriage itself, and the assessment in Section 14.6 provides the framework.

**The distinction is difficult to make from inside.** Men are poor judges of their wives' capacity in this specific respect, for two reasons: they have usually attempted disclosure once or twice rather than consistently, and they are usually unaware of the load their wives are carrying, because their wives have also stopped disclosing.

### Obtaining Understanding Elsewhere

Where understanding is not available in the marriage, it must be obtained elsewhere, and it must be obtained without deceiving the partner. Four sources are available and each has specific properties.

**One confidant.** A friend, a sibling, or a colleague. The practical arrangements are the same as those described in Chapter 4: a mutual arrangement, a regular occasion, and a rule of confidentiality. The value is not advice but the presence of one person who knows the actual situation.

**A peer group.** A group of men in comparable circumstances. The advantage is that it removes the requirement for explanation: men in a group of this kind do not need to be persuaded that a difficulty is real. Chapter 27 addresses how such groups are found and how they are conducted.

**A professional.** A counsellor, therapist, or, in some cases, a physician or a religious minister, where the man's own framework includes one. The advantage is capacity and method; the disadvantage, for many men, is cost and the requirement to begin with a stranger. Men should note that a counsellor is generally the most effective option for material that is serious, long-standing, or connected to a condition such as depression, and that the confidant is not a substitute for treatment.

**A community or an activity that supplies something other than understanding.** Understanding is not the only requirement. Structure, usefulness, competence, and pleasure also matter, and a man whose household supplies none of them may obtain a great deal from an organisation in which he is useful and a practice at which he is competent. Chapter 27 covers this.

### The Ethics of External Support

Three rules apply, and they protect both the man and the marriage.

**The partner should know that the support exists.** Not the content, but the fact: *I have been speaking to somebody about what I am carrying, and I would not be able to tell you who or what about.* This preserves the marriage's honesty without breaching the confidentiality that makes the support effective.

**The support must not be a substitute marriage.** A relationship with a person of the opposite sex in which the man receives emotional intimacy that his marriage does not provide is, regardless of intent, a relationship that competes with the marriage and that will tend towards the situation described in Chapter 16. This is not a moral judgement about the situation; it is an observation about the mechanism, and it is based on the consistency with which this trajectory is reported.

**The support must not be used to accumulate a case.** Counsellors and confidants are frequently placed in the position of receiving a man's account of his marriage over years while the marriage itself never hears the material. The result is a file of grievances and no attempt at resolution. The useful test: if a conversation with a confidant would become a conversation with the partner where the partner were willing, the material belongs in the marriage, and the man should ask himself why it has never been delivered there.

## 14.6 Decision Framework: Staying, Renegotiating, or Leaving

Some men arrive at this point having attempted, repeatedly and reasonably, to obtain understanding, and having found that the relationship will not supply it. This section provides a framework for the decision that follows. It does not presume the answer, and it deliberately sets out all three options.

### The Assessment

Answer the following, in writing and honestly. The answers, not the advice of others, should determine the decision.

**Part One: The relationship as a whole.**

1. What does the relationship supply that matters: children, companionship, practical partnership, financial stability, family connection, reliability, affection, sexual relationship, shared history, care in illness?
2. What does it not supply, and how central is that to my wellbeing?
3. Are there other serious problems, or is the difficulty confined to the man's interior life?

**Part Two: The disclosure attempts.**

4. How many genuine attempts have I made, and in what form? Were they structured, scheduled, and limited to one subject, or were they sudden, indirect, or made during a crisis?
5. What did I actually ask for, and was it specific and deliverable?
6. Did I ask about her own difficulties, and did I hear them?

**Part Three: Capacity.**

7. What is her load, and what is she carrying that I know about and have not addressed?
8. Does she have a condition, a history, or a formation that makes this conversation impossible for her?
9. Has her capacity changed over the years, and is it changing now?

**Part Four: The alternatives.**

10. What would I obtain outside the marriage that I do not obtain inside it, and can it be obtained without damaging the marriage?
11. Would ending the marriage produce the understanding I want, or would it produce a different set of difficulties?
12. What would the consequences be, for each child, for the household, and for the man's own circumstances?

### The Three Options

**Staying, with an external source of support.** This is the most common viable answer and it should not be treated as a failure. A man who maintains his marriage, obtains the support he needs elsewhere, and manages the arrangement with honesty and without substituting the external relationship for the marriage has made a legitimate decision, and many men describe it as satisfactory over a long period. The condition attached to it is the maintenance of honesty about the support's existence, and its risk is the competition described in the previous section.

**Renegotiating the relationship.** This option involves a deliberate change in the terms: the man states what he requires, states what he will supply in return, and proposes the arrangement — the regular conversation, the counselling, the change in the household's division of labour, the reduction of an external obligation. Renegotiation succeeds more often than men expect where the man's request is specific, where he offers a corresponding change, and where the couple obtains help. Chapter 15 addresses the practice in detail, and couple counselling is generally the most effective route where it is available and affordable.

**Leaving.** This is a legitimate option in cases where the relationship supplies nothing of significance, where the man's attempts have been genuine and repeated, where the partner is unwilling rather than unable, where the arrangement is harming the children, or where the man's health is being damaged. Leaving should be decided on the basis of the full assessment rather than the immediate feeling, should be prepared before it is announced, and should be conducted, where children are involved, with the guidance in Chapters 13 and 17. The chapter does not recommend leaving, and it does not recommend staying at any cost. It recommends a decision that has been examined.

### The Most Common Outcome

The most common outcome, in the experience of practitioners, is not any of the three options in pure form. It is a partial improvement: two or three successful conversations, a modest change in the arrangement, a better division of load, and a continuing gap that both parties have acknowledged. Men who expect a complete transformation are frequently disappointed by this outcome and abandon the whole work as a failure. Men who recognise that a marriage may contain a substantial remaining gap and still be worth maintaining are generally the ones who report an improvement five years later.
