## 43.7 Creating Emotionally Supportive Communities

The previous section supplied the individual and institutional conditions for help-seeking. This section turns to the community, because the individual boy is never conducting his development in isolation, and because the community determines whether the conditions that a household establishes survive the boy's adolescence.

The section's subject is therefore not a campaign, an awareness day, or a slogan. It is the design of the ordinary arrangements through which a community conducts its business, and the specific question of whether those arrangements permit a boy to speak.

The section proceeds in three parts: the five institutions that constitute a community's emotional curriculum; the six design principles that the case material supports; and an illustrative account of a community that conducted the design.

### The Five Institutions

**Institution One: The Household.** The household is the first institution and the one with the greatest influence, and its design is the material of Section 43.3. Its specific features are the vocabulary, the allocation of attention, the treatment of the adults' states, and the repair.

**Institution Two: The School.** The school is the institution through which a boy's peer group is most frequently constituted, and it therefore has the greatest influence on the enforcement of the propositions described in Section 43.4. Its design features are the classroom's response to error, the presence of a named adult, the curriculum's treatment of emotion, and the school's response to a disclosure.

**Institution Three: The Workplace, the Workshop, and the Trade.** For the young man of sixteen and above, the workplace conducts a curriculum that the household and the school no longer control. Its design features are the supervisor's conduct in the presence of difficulty, the workplace's response to error, the availability of the professional route, and the ordinary conversation of the trade, which is frequently the most powerful enforcement of the propositions in a young man's life.

**Institution Four: The Association and the Community Body.** The association — the trade association, the cooperative, the residents' body, the alumni group, the sports club, the social club — conducts a curriculum through its meetings, its ceremonies, its language, and its account of its members. Its design features are the language of its proceedings, the conduct of its officers, the response to a member's difficulty, and the arrangements it supplies at the moments of loss that the case material of Part VIII describes.

**Institution Five: The Peer Group.** The peer group is not an institution in the formal sense, and it is the most powerful of the five for the boy between roughly twelve and twenty. Its design is conducted indirectly, through the settings in which it forms and the adults who supervise those settings, and its features are the currency of its standing, the treatment of a member's disclosure, and the presence of at least one member who conducts a different standard.

The reader should note that these five institutions overlap and that a boy's conduct is determined by the combination. A household that establishes the conditions of Section 43.6 and a peer group that punishes their use will produce a boy who speaks at home and nowhere else, and the adult form of that position is the material of Chapter 18.4's compartmentalisation. The community's task is therefore not the improvement of one institution. It is the alignment of the five, and the alignment is conducted over years.

### The Six Design Principles

The case material and the practice literature support six principles, and each one is stated with the specific arrangement that conducts it.

**Principle One: The permission is stated in the community's own language.** A community's language is the medium through which its permissions are transmitted, and a permission stated in the language of a campaign will frequently be received as external. The principle's conduct is the community's own vocabulary, used by its own officers, in its own settings: the phrase in the local language that a father uses to his son, the proverb that the association's chairman cites at the meeting, the sentence that the coach uses at the end of training. The finding in the case material is that the community's own language is more effective than any imported formulation, and that it is frequently available already, since most communities possess a vocabulary for endurance and a smaller but existing vocabulary for shared difficulty.

**Principle Two: A named person is available in each institution.** The principle is the community form of Section 43.6's first condition. Every institution should have a person who is known, available, and trained, and the person should be named in ordinary communication rather than in a policy document. The finding in the case material is that the named person is the single most effective element in a community's arrangement, and that it costs less than any other element.

**Principle Three: The arrangements are routine rather than occasional.** A community that conducts a programme once a year conducts an event. A community that conducts its arrangements weekly conducts a culture. The principle's conduct is the routine: the ten minutes at the start of the association's meeting at which members report; the weekly conversation at the workshop; the termly assembly at the school; the monthly household conversation described in Section 43.8.

**Principle Four: The community supplies settings in which boys form peer groups of a different kind.** The principle addresses the enforcement described in Section 43.4. The settings are ordinary: the team, the club, the choir, the workshop's apprentice group, the reading group, the volunteering arrangement. The design requirement is that the setting should have an adult who conducts the standard, a currency of standing that is not dominance, and at least one occasion on which members speak about difficulty. The finding in the case material is that the setting's effect is substantial and that it is frequently produced by a single adult's conduct over a single season.

**Principle Five: The community's leaders conduct the material in public.** The principle is the community form of the modelling channel, and it is the one that communities most frequently omit. The leader's disclosure should be proportionate, should be followed by action, and should be conducted in the leader's ordinary setting. The finding in the case material is that a single proportionate disclosure by a respected man — a chairman, a coach, a head teacher, a senior colleague — changes the community's permission more than a year of programming, and that the disclosure's effect depends on its being followed by the leader's visible action.

**Principle Six: The community's arrangements survive the individual who established them.** The principle is the one that determines whether the work endures. The arrangements should be written into the institution's ordinary practice, attached to a role rather than a person, reviewed annually, and supplied with the resources they require. The finding in the case material is that community programmes frequently end when their founder leaves, and that the arrangements which survive are the ones that were built into the institution's routine before the founder's departure.

### The Community's Specific Difficulties

The section states four difficulties that the case material records, because the reader who attempts this work will meet them and will otherwise interpret them as failure.

**The first difficulty is the fear of imported agendas.** Communities frequently receive emotional programmes as an external criticism of their culture, and the reception is frequently accurate, since many programmes are designed elsewhere and conducted in a language that the community does not use. The remedy is Principle One: the work should be conducted in the community's own terms, by the community's own people, and should be presented as the recovery of something the community already possesses rather than as the importation of something it lacks.

**The second difficulty is the exposure of the first person who speaks.** The first man who discloses in a community bears a cost that the later members do not, and the cost is frequently the material of Chapter 26's betrayal. The remedy is the protection of the first person: the leader's explicit support, the community's stated norms, and the absence of any arrangement that makes the disclosure the community's material. The finding in the case material is that communities which fail to protect the first person do not produce a second.

**The third difficulty is the substitution of the programme for the arrangement.** A community that has conducted a workshop frequently believes that it has conducted the work, and the belief is the most common failure in the practice literature. The remedy is Principle Three and Principle Six: the routine and the institution, rather than the event.

**The fourth difficulty is the professional boundary.** A community arrangement is not a clinical service, and the case material records harm where untrained members conducted material that exceeded their competence. The remedy is the professional route: a stated referral arrangement, a trained named person, and the community's explicit acknowledgement of its limits. The material of Chapter 35.6 and Appendix Q applies.

### An Illustrative Account: The Association That Changed Its Meetings

The section closes with an account that is composite and illustrative, constructed from the case material and presented as an example rather than as a report of a particular body.

A trade association in a mid-sized town had, for eleven years, conducted its monthly meeting in the same form: the chairman's opening, the reading of the minutes, the financial report, the old business, the new business, and the closing. Its membership was approximately ninety, most of them men between thirty-five and sixty, and its ordinary conversation concerned contracts, prices, and the conduct of competitors.

In the year of the account, two of its members died within four months. The first died suddenly; the second, according to the account that circulated afterwards, had been unwell for a year and had told nobody. At the meeting that followed, the chairman departed from the agenda and spoke for four minutes. He stated that he had known the second member for twenty years, that he had not known he was unwell, and that he had been asking himself what in the association's arrangements had made it easier for a man to conduct a year of illness alone than to mention it at a meeting. He did not answer the question. He proposed one change: that the meeting should begin with ten minutes in which any member could report on any matter, personal or professional, with the assurance that nothing reported would be minuted.

The change was adopted, and its first three months were difficult. In the first month, nobody spoke. In the second, a member reported a difficulty with a bank facility, and the association's ordinary business resumed. In the third, a member stated that his wife had been unwell for six months and that he had not known how to say so. The association's response was not therapeutic and it was specific: two members who had experience of the hospital's arrangements spoke to him afterwards; the association's welfare officer made an appointment with him; and the ten minutes continued.

At the end of the year, the association's account of the change was recorded in the case material in three statements by its members. The first: *nothing has changed in the business, and the meetings are longer.* The second: *I know three things about my colleagues that I did not know, and I am less alone in this town.* The third, from the chairman: *the ten minutes cost us nothing, and it is the only part of the meeting that anybody remembers.*

The account is presented because it illustrates the section's principles in their ordinary form. The permission was stated in the community's language. The named person existed already, in the welfare officer, and was used. The arrangement was routine rather than occasional. The leader conducted a proportionate disclosure and followed it with an action. The arrangement was attached to the meeting rather than to the chairman. And the community's limits were acknowledged, since the association referred its member to a professional and did not attempt to conduct his position itself.

The reader should note what the account does not contain. It contains no campaign, no slogan, no external programme, and no claim that the association had solved the problem. It contains a change in an agenda and a decision by a chairman. The case material's finding is that this is the ordinary form of the work, and that the extraordinary forms — the national programme, the awareness campaign, the public figure's testimony — are effective in proportion to the number of ordinary forms that accompany them.
