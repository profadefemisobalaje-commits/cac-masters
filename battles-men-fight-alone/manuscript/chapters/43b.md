## 43.3 The Influence of Parents, Teachers, and Mentors

The previous section described what is transmitted. This section describes who transmits it, and it proceeds institution by institution because each one has a different mechanism, a different point of leverage, and a different failure.

The reader should note the chapter's working assumption here. The question is not which institution matters most. The research literature and the case material agree that the household is the earliest and the most formative, and they also agree that the influence of a single adult outside the household is frequently decisive for a boy whose household cannot supply what he requires. The section therefore treats the institutions as a set of overlapping arrangements rather than as a ranking, and it supplies for each one the specific practice that the reader can conduct.

### The Household and Its Emotional Curriculum

The household is the boy's first curriculum, and its subject is not what it says about feelings. Its subject is what it does with them.

Four features of the household's ordinary conduct constitute the curriculum, and each is present in every household in some form.

**The first is the household's vocabulary.** The words that are available in the household for interior states determine what the boy can report. A household with two words — *fine* and *angry* — produces a boy with two words, and the boy will use *angry* for everything that is not fine, which is the material of Chapter 12.5's man whose only permitted state is irritation. A household with a wider vocabulary — disappointed, ashamed, frightened, lonely, relieved, humiliated, grief-stricken, exhausted — produces a boy who can discriminate, and the discrimination is the first competence of Chapter 39.4.

**The second is the household's allocation of attention.** The boy learns which of his states receive attention and which do not. A household that attends to achievement and ignores fear teaches the boy to present achievement and to conceal fear. A household that attends to physical injury and ignores humiliation teaches the boy to report his body and not his standing. The allocation is rarely deliberate, and it is always legible to the boy.

**The third is the household's treatment of its adult members' states.** The boy observes what happens when his father is afraid, when his mother is grieving, when his uncle is ashamed, and when a visitor is in difficulty. The observation is the material of Section 43.1's fourth channel, and the household has more control over it than it believes, because the household chooses what is conducted in the boy's presence and what is conducted elsewhere.

**The fourth is the household's repair.** Every household damages its members at some point, and the difference between households is not the damage. It is the repair. A household in which the adult names what he did, states its effect, and conducts the repair produces a boy who knows that failure is survivable. A household in which the damage is never mentioned produces a boy who knows that certain events are permanent and unmentionable, which is the material of Chapter 15.4's injury.

The practical consequence for the reader is that the household's curriculum is conducted by ordinary means and does not require a programme. It requires the adult to state one true sentence about his own state at a frequency the household can bear, to name the boy's states with precision when they appear, to conduct one repair in the boy's observation, and to keep the household's account of itself accurate, which is the material of Chapter 42.6.

### The Father and the Specific Mechanism of His Influence

The father's influence is the subject of a large literature and a great deal of mythology, and this section states only what the case material supports.

The father's influence operates through three mechanisms that are distinct from the mother's, and the distinction is empirical rather than evaluative.

**The first is the mechanism of the boy's identification.** The boy's question is not *what does my father say a man is* but *what does my father do, and am I becoming that*. The question is asked with particular intensity between the ages of roughly six and sixteen, and it is asked of the father's conduct in the presence of difficulty. A father who is present, warm, and available in ordinary circumstances and absent, cold, or explosive in difficulty supplies the boy with a definition that the boy will spend his adult life either reproducing or resisting.

**The second is the mechanism of the father's permission.** The father's own disclosure — a sentence about his fear, his failure, his grief, or his uncertainty — supplies the boy with a permission that no other party can supply with the same force. The finding in the case material is consistent and specific: men who can speak about their difficulties frequently identify a single occasion on which their father, or a father-substitute, stated a difficulty in their presence. The occasion is generally brief, frequently awkward, and remembered for decades.

**The third is the mechanism of the father's standard.** The father supplies the boy's sense of what is required of him, and the standard is transmitted as much by what the father accepts in himself as by what he demands of the boy. A father who accepts exhaustion, concealment, and self-neglect in himself transmits those as the standard, whatever he demands of his son. A father who accepts help, rest, treatment, and repair transmits those as permitted, and the transmission is stronger than any instruction.

The reader should note the case material's finding on the absent father, because it is frequently misread. The finding is not that an absent father produces a silent son. It is that the absence produces a specific question that the boy cannot answer — *what would he have done* — and that the boy answers it himself, generally with the most severe version of the culture's script. The adult son of an absent father is frequently more rigid in his manhood than the son of a present and difficult one, because he has had to construct the standard without a model and has constructed it from the least forgiving materials available.

The practical consequence is stated without sentiment. A father who is present can correct his conduct. A father who is absent cannot, and the adult son's work is then the material of Chapter 41.5's substitution exercise: the identification of the standard he constructed, the examination of its sources, and the deliberate substitution of a definition he has chosen.

### The Mother and the Understated Mechanism

The mother's influence on the boy's emotional conduct is frequently understated in the culture's account of manhood and is substantial in the research literature and in the case material.

Three mechanisms are recorded consistently.

**The first is the mechanism of the boy's first vocabulary and his first audience.** The mother is frequently the party to whom the boy's first states are reported, and her response establishes whether reporting is safe. The finding in the case material is that men who can speak about difficulty frequently describe a mother, an aunt, or a grandmother who received their reports without ridicule and without alarm.

**The second is the mechanism of the household's emotional conduct.** The mother's management of her own states, and her management of the household's, is the boy's first observation of what an adult does with difficulty. Where the mother conducts her difficulties alone, privately, and without the household's knowledge, the boy learns a lesson about adult life that the father's conduct may confirm or contradict.

**The third is the mechanism of the mother's protection against the father's severity.** The finding in the case material, recorded without judgment, is that the boy's capacity to speak is frequently preserved by a party who intervened between the boy and a severe response: a mother, an aunt, an older sibling, a grandmother. The intervention did not need to be dramatic. A sentence — *he is tired, leave him* — or a change of subject was frequently sufficient to preserve the boy's willingness to report.

The reader should note the caution that the case material also records. Where the mother is the boy's only permitted audience, the boy's conduct develops a specific split: full expression in the mother's presence, and complete inexpression everywhere else, including in his adult marriage. The finding is that the split is not resolved by the mother's continued availability; it is resolved by the boy's acquisition of a second and third audience, which is the material of Section 43.5's named person.

### The Teacher and the Classroom

The school's influence on the boy's emotional conduct operates through four features, and only the fourth is ordinarily recognised.

**The first is the classroom's response to error.** A classroom in which error is met with ridicule produces a boy who will not attempt, will not ask, and will not report. A classroom in which error is treated as information produces the opposite. The mechanism is the same as the household's third channel, and the school conducts it for six hours a day.

**The second is the teacher's own conduct in the presence of difficulty.** The teacher who names her own uncertainty, who corrects her own error aloud, and who states that a subject is difficult teaches more about the management of difficulty than any lesson on the subject.

**The third is the curriculum's treatment of emotion.** A curriculum that treats emotion as a subject for girls, or as a matter for the guidance counsellor rather than the classroom, transmits the belief that emotion is a specialist's business rather than an ordinary competence. A curriculum that includes the material of Section 43.5 within ordinary subjects — a discussion of a character's fear in a literature lesson, an account of a scientist's failure in a science lesson, a history lesson that examines the private cost of a public decision — transmits the opposite.

**The fourth is the named adult.** The finding that matters most for this chapter is the one the case material records most consistently: a boy who can name one adult in his school to whom he would speak is a boy with a substantially different trajectory from a boy who cannot. The adult does not need to be a counsellor. The adult needs to be present, to be known to the boy, to have stated that he is available, and to have responded well once.

The practical consequence for the reader who teaches is direct. He should ensure that every boy in his care can name an adult. He should state the availability in ordinary language and in an ordinary setting. He should respond to the first disclosure in the manner of Section 43.5, and he should record nothing that he has promised to keep confidential, except where the law and the boy's safety require otherwise, in which case he should say so in advance.

### The Coach, the Artisan, and the Master

The coach, the artisan, the master of a trade, the music teacher, and the driver of a vehicle are frequently the most influential adults in a boy's life, and their influence is exercised through a mechanism that the classroom does not supply: **the mechanism of demonstrated competence under difficulty.**

The apprentice observes his master conducting a difficult task badly, correcting it, and continuing. He observes the master's frustration, its regulation, and its conduct. He observes the master's admission of a limit — *I do not know this, we will ask somebody who does* — and he observes the consequence of the admission, which is generally that the work proceeds. The observation teaches the boy that competence includes the admission of a limit, which is the material of Chapter 32.4's distinction between authority and omniscience.

The mechanism has a specific failure mode that the case material records frequently: the master who conducts his competence as a performance of infallibility, who cannot be seen to be uncertain, and who punishes the apprentice's error as a threat to his standing. The apprentice learns the trade and learns, at the same time, that a man's competence is conducted by concealment. The finding is that this is one of the most efficient transmissions of the pattern in the book, because the apprentice's dependence on the master prevents him from questioning the model.

The practical consequence is that the reader who supervises young men in any trade should conduct two things in their observation: one correction of his own error, stated aloud, and one admission of a limit, followed by the request for the instrument that supplies it. These two acts cost the master very little and are frequently the most valuable instruction the apprentice receives.

### The Mentor and the Single Adult

The mentor is the subject of a large practice literature and a smaller research literature, and this section states only what the material supports.

The mentor's function, for the purposes of this chapter, is not advice. It is the supply of three things that the boy's household may be unable to supply: a second model of manhood, a second audience for disclosure, and a party whose interest in the boy is not derived from the boy's performance.

The finding in the case material is specific and worth stating plainly. **One adult is generally sufficient.** The boy does not require a network, a programme, or a formal arrangement. He requires one adult who is not his parent, who is reliably present, who is not scandalised by what the boy reports, who does not report the boy's material to the household without the boy's agreement, and who continues the relationship after the disclosure. The presence of that adult is associated in the case material with substantially better outcomes in the boy's adult capacity to speak, and the association does not depend on the adult's qualifications.

The reader who is asked to be that adult should note three cautions.

The first is that the relationship is not therapy and should not be conducted as therapy. The mentor's competence is presence, ordinary conversation, and the willingness to be a party to whom a boy can speak. Where the boy's material indicates a disorder, a trauma, or a risk, the mentor's task is to assist the boy to the professional route, which frequently requires the mentor to accompany him to the first appointment.

The second is that the mentor's own material is not the subject of the relationship. A mentor who conducts the relationship as an audience for his own difficulties has reversed the arrangement.

The third is that the relationship requires boundaries that are stated and kept: the times at which the mentor is available, the matters that the mentor will not keep confidential, and the duration of the arrangement. The boundaries are not a reduction of the relationship. They are the condition on which a boy can rely on it.

### What the Institutions Have in Common

The section closes with the common structure, because the reader who is conducting this material in more than one institution will find that the same five features determine whether the institution teaches speech or silence.

**The first is a vocabulary.** The institution supplies the words for interior states, or it does not.

**The second is a permission.** The institution states that a difficulty may be reported, in ordinary language, by a named person, in a stated way.

**The third is a response.** The institution's response to the first report determines whether the permission survives. The response should be attentive, unhurried, non-punitive, and followed by an action.

**The fourth is a consequence the boy can observe.** The institution conducts at least one repair, one disclosure, or one admission of a limit in the boy's observation, and it permits the boy to see that the party survived it.

**The fifth is a repetition.** The institution conducts the four features routinely rather than once, because a single conversation is an event and an institution is a pattern.

The reader may test any institution against the five features in a single meeting, and the test is supplied in Section 43.8's second column. The result is frequently surprising: institutions that describe themselves as caring for boys' emotional development frequently lack the third and fourth features entirely, and institutions that describe themselves as strict and traditional frequently possess them.
