## 7.3 Financial Failure and Wounded Self-Esteem

Financial difficulty is the most common serious failure in the lives of the men this book is written for, and it wounds self-esteem in a manner that men describe in almost identical terms across countries, classes, and ages. It is worth being precise about what happens.

### Three Losses in One Event

A financial failure delivers three distinct losses, and they arrive together, which is why the event is so disorganising.

**Material loss.** The loss of income, savings, property, or business capacity, with the practical consequences that follow: unpaid fees, delayed medical care, reduced food quality, the withdrawal of children from school, the sale of a vehicle, or the loss of a house.

**Social loss.** The loss of standing in a system of regard that is partly economic. Men report the specific social experiences that accompany financial trouble: avoiding gatherings at which they would be expected to contribute; declining invitations because of the cost of attendance; being asked for assistance and having to refuse; hearing that something has been said about them; the shame of a child being sent home from school.

**Identity loss.** The third loss is the most consequential, and it involves the fusion described in the introduction to this chapter. The man's sense of himself was constructed substantially out of his capacity to provide, and the loss of that capacity is experienced as the loss of himself. The experience is not metaphorical, and it is not an overreaction. It is the predictable result of a long process in which a man's worth was repeatedly identified with his usefulness.

### The Specific Wounds and the Specific Thoughts

The thoughts that accompany financial failure are consistent, and it is worth listing them, because a man who reads his own thought in print is a man who is more able to examine it.

- *I have let my family down.* Frequently the first and most persistent thought, and usually the one that carries the most charge.
- *I am not the man I said I was.* The collapse of the public account described in Chapter 1.
- *I will lose them.* A fear concerning the family: that a wife, children, or relatives will revise their regard, or in some cases leave.
- *Everyone will know.* The anticipation of public knowledge, and the loss of standing that will follow.
- *I must have been foolish.* A retrospective reconstruction in which past decisions are re-read as evidence of stupidity, regardless of the information available at the time.
- *What is the point of continuing?* In severe cases, a risk of self-harm. This requires immediate professional attention, as Chapters 6 and 34 set out.

### The Behaviour That Follows, and Why It Is Costly

The pattern of behaviour that follows a financial failure is highly predictable, and, in the great majority of cases, it makes the failure worse. Four behaviours recur.

**Concealment.** The failure is hidden from the spouse, the family, the community, and frequently from professional advisers. Concealment prevents the four interventions that most help: shared planning, cost reduction, restructuring of debt, and emotional support.

**Over-trading to recover.** The man increases risk in an attempt to recover quickly, frequently with borrowed money. The impulse is understandable — speed appears to be the only way to prevent discovery — and it is one of the most reliable routes from a difficulty into a catastrophe.

**Withdrawal from social obligations where the cost is visible.** This produces the isolation described in Chapter 4 at the very moment when company and information are most needed.

**Self-punishment through work.** Men frequently respond by working longer hours, reducing sleep, and abandoning leisure entirely, which reduces their decision-making capacity at the point where clear decisions matter most.

### The Recoverable Part and the Unrecoverable Part

A distinction that assists recovery is the separation of what can and cannot be restored.

What is frequently recoverable: income, business capacity, assets, and, over a period of years, standard of living. Men of forty-five, fifty-five, and sixty-five recover from serious financial losses in large numbers, and the recovery is usually slower and less spectacular than the collapse.

What is not recoverable, and what requires separate work: the years spent in concealment; the trust damaged by dishonesty; the health damaged by the stress; the relationship with children during the period of withdrawal; and, where it occurred, the loss of a marriage that could have survived the money problem but not the secrecy. These losses are addressed by honesty, by the repair methods described in Chapters 29 and 36, and by the professional support described in Chapter 35. They are not addressed by earning more.

## 7.4 Perfectionism and Fear of Judgment

Perfectionism and the fear of judgement are related but distinct, and the distinction determines the treatment. Perfectionism concerns the standard a man applies to his own work. Fear of judgement concerns his expectation of how others will respond to it. A man may have either without the other, and many men have both.

### Perfectionism

Perfectionism is generally understood as a disposition in which the person sets standards that are extremely high and evaluates performance against them, with the consequences that satisfaction is rare, effort is excessive, and the person is perpetually dissatisfied with results that others consider entirely adequate.

Four consequences matter for the reader.

**It consumes time disproportionately.** The last ten per cent of quality frequently costs as much as the first ninety. Perfectionists deliver better work than they can afford, in exchange for sleep, relationships, and additional capacity that would have produced more overall value elsewhere.

**It produces avoidance.** Where the standard is unreachable, the most rational response is not to begin. Procrastination in perfectionists is not laziness; it is the avoidance of an anticipated verdict. Chapter 22 examines the pattern in detail.

**It destroys delegation.** A man who requires that work be done to his own standard cannot hand work to others, which limits the size of anything he can build and guarantees his own overwork. In occupational terms, this is the most expensive feature of perfectionism.

**It transmits.** Children of perfectionist fathers acquire either the same standard or a rebellion against it, and in both cases the family's emotional life is organised around a standard rather than around its members. Chapter 43 examines what boys learn from this.

### Fear of Judgement

The fear of being judged is a social anxiety with a specific male character. Its features are as follows.

**It attaches to exposure.** The man is not afraid of hard work or of difficulty; he is afraid of being seen failing, being corrected in public, being asked a question he cannot answer, being revealed as less competent than his position implies.

**It produces a characteristic set of behaviours.** Over-preparation; reluctance to speak in meetings unless certain of the answer; avoidance of questions in the audience; refusal to ask directions, to seek technical help, or to admit ignorance; postponement of performance reviews; and a marked reluctance to be examined, tested, or assessed in any form.

**It has a clear mechanism.** The fear is generated by the belief that a single observed failure will produce a global reclassification: not *he got that wrong* but *he is not competent*. This is the same fusion of act and identity described in Section 7.3, and it operates in the social domain.

**It produces an important and under-recognised cost.** Men who fear judgement fail to seek help for precisely the problems that would benefit from it, because asking for help is an admission of a gap. A man may spend two years attempting to resolve alone a tax problem, a legal matter, a medical symptom, or a technical fault that a professional would have resolved in a week. Chapter 35 addresses this directly.

### What Helps

Four measures have a good record with both patterns.

**Define the standard of sufficiency in advance.** For each task, write down what would be good enough, and why. Judging a piece of work against a standard established beforehand is considerably easier than judging it against an imagined ideal afterwards.

**Adopt the professional standard rather than the ideal.** Professionals in every field work to a standard that is adequate for the purpose and appropriate to the budget. Architects, engineers, surgeons, and accountants all do this, and men who insist on more than the professional standard are frequently confusing self-protection with quality.

**Test the judgement fear against evidence.** Ask three people whose judgement you respect, and who know your work, whether they have ever observed the failure you fear. The answer is, in the author's experience, almost always no, and the reason is instructive: other people are occupied with their own exposures.

**Expose yourself deliberately, in graduated steps.** Speak once in a meeting without complete certainty. Ask one question publicly that reveals a gap. Delegate one item of work that will be done differently from how you would do it. The evidence accumulates, and the fear diminishes — a method described in Appendix H.
