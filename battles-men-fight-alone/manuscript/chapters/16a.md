# The Pain of Rejection and Betrayal {.chapter num="16" short="The Pain of Rejection and Betrayal"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Identify the many forms that rejection takes in a man's life, including those outside the romantic domain, and describe the specific features of each.
2. Explain the psychological mechanisms by which rejection damages a man's self-assessment and behaviour, including the phenomenon of social pain, the rise of rejection sensitivity, and the two opposing responses of withdrawal and aggression.
3. Analyse the anatomy of betrayal, including the distinction between the injury inflicted and the deception that surrounded it, and the effect of the number of people who knew.
4. Describe the pain of being misjudged, falsely accused, or publicly discredited, and the specific problem of restoring a standing that has been taken without a hearing.
5. Apply a structured recovery method covering the acute period, the reconstruction of the account, the restoration of standing, and the role of forgiveness understood as an internal decision rather than a reconciliation.
6. Rebuild the capacity to trust selectively, using the assessment and plan at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

Chapter 13 examined the end of a relationship, and it included the rejection and the betrayal that frequently accompany it. This chapter treats rejection and betrayal as subjects in their own right, because they occur in every other domain of a man's life and because their effects there are less well understood and less discussed.

The two experiences are related and distinct. **Rejection** is a decision by a person or a group that the man is not to be included: not selected, not admitted, not consulted, not invited, not retained. **Betrayal** is a violation of an agreed or assumed trust by a person who was inside it: a friend who uses what he was told, a partner in business who transfers the assets, a relative who petitions against him, a colleague who takes credit and removes him, an association that spends the money and constructs a story.

Both produce pain that is frequently invisible and that is frequently carried for entire decades. Both are poorly handled by men, for a reason that this chapter will make explicit: the standard male response to rejection is to withdraw, and the standard male response to betrayal is to withdraw and become hard, and both of these responses preserve the injury rather than resolve it.

A note on scale is necessary before proceeding. Rejection and betrayal are not equivalent experiences across their forms, and the reader should not treat them as such. A man who is not invited to a meeting is not in the same position as a man who is falsely accused and cut off from a community of forty years. This chapter distinguishes the incidental, which is ordinary and requires no intervention, from the substantial, which requires work. The distinction is important because men who have been taught to dismiss all such matters as unimportant frequently fail to notice that a substantial injury has been sustained, and because a man who treats every minor rejection as though it were a betrayal will exhaust himself and the people around him.

## 16.1 The Many Faces of Rejection

The romantic rejection examined in Chapter 13 is the form most discussed. The following are equally consequential and far less often named.

### Rejection by Family

Forms: the parent who favours another child and does so visibly over decades; the family that decides collectively on a matter affecting the man without consulting him; the exclusion from a family's decision-making despite his contribution; the withdrawal of a family's recognition in a dispute; the family that receives his assistance and does not record it. In customary settings, where extended-family decisions determine property, marriages, and obligations, exclusion from family decisions is a substantial loss of standing and of practical interest.

The particular feature of familial rejection is that it cannot be repaired by replacement. A friend can be replaced and a community can be joined; a family of origin cannot. A man who is rejected by his family therefore carries a permanent structural feature, which is the reason that familial rejection produces, in men's accounts, a distinguishing and durable grief.

### Rejection by Peers and by a Group of Men

Forms: exclusion from a group of colleagues or contemporaries; the loss of a place in a circle through which the man obtained his standing; exclusion from an association, a cooperative society, a professional body, or a development union; being spoken about in a group and not defended by those present.

The effect on men of exclusion from a group is frequently more severe than the effect of exclusion from an individual relationship, because men's social lives are frequently organised around groups, and because a group's exclusion is public, which is to say that it is known to everybody. The research on ostracism is consistent on this point: exclusion from a group produces measurable pain, a reduction in the sense of belonging, and, in the period immediately following, a reduced capacity for considered reasoning.

### Rejection in Work and Profession

Forms: a promotion or appointment given to another, particularly where the man was led to expect it; exclusion from a project, a contract, or a role; dismissal, including dismissal that is treated as routine; a scholarly or professional setback; a business partnership's dissolution with the man's contribution unacknowledged; the refusal of a loan, a grant, or an opportunity for which he was qualified; being passed over repeatedly for a reason that is never provided.

Two features of occupational rejection make it particularly damaging. It is frequently unexplained — the reason is never given, and the absence of a reason invites the man to supply one, which is usually about himself. And it is frequently communicated publicly or semi-publicly: the appointment is announced, the dismissal is known, and other people's reaction to the man's position becomes a continuing source of the injury.

### Rejection by One's Own Children

A form that is among the most painful and the least discussed. Forms: estrangement after a separation or divorce; a child's preference for the other parent; the adult child who reduces contact; the child who adopts an accusation made by others; the child who communicates only when something is required. For men whose paternal identity is central — which, on the evidence in Chapter 17, is the majority of men — this is a specific and serious injury, and its management requires the material in Chapters 17 and 24.

### Rejection by a Community or a Public

Forms: the destruction of a professional reputation; exclusion from a community after an accusation, a scandal, or a failure; the loss of the standing obtained over decades through a single event; being the subject of a settlement or a story that continues to circulate.

Where a man is rejected by a community, the practical consequences are extensive: social relationships, business opportunities, marriage prospects for his children, and access to informal credit and support all depend on standing.

### Self-Rejection

The final form, and the mechanism by which the others become durable. A man who has been rejected repeatedly begins to anticipate rejection, to read neutral events as rejections, and to act on the reading, which is the process described in the next section. The subjective experience is that the world has rejected him; the more accurate description is frequently that the anticipation has begun to do the rejecting, and this is the part of the process over which a man has some influence.

## 16.2 What Rejection Does to a Man's Mind

Six mechanisms operate, and understanding them is what allows the effects to be recognised and reduced.

### Social Pain

Research into the neuroscience of social rejection indicates that exclusion and being evaluated negatively activate regions of the brain also involved in physical pain, and that the experience is not a metaphor. Two practical consequences follow: the pain is genuine and should be treated as such rather than dismissed as an overreaction; and the ordinary methods of managing physical pain — rest, support, time, and the avoidance of further injury during the acute phase — apply.

### The Rapid Loss of Belonging, Self-Esteem, Control, and Meaning

A consistent finding in the experimental research on ostracism is that exclusion produces an immediate drop across four dimensions at once: the sense of belonging, self-esteem, the sense of control over one's situation, and the sense of a meaningful existence. This accounts for the severity of the experience: it is not a single injury but a simultaneous reduction across four of the five or six things that a man relies on to feel that his life is working. The same research indicates that the effects attenuate with time and support, and that the most effective intervention in the acute phase is the re-establishment of one or more of the four dimensions by other means: one relationship in which the man belongs, one activity in which he can act effectively, one contribution that is meaningful.

### Self-Blame and the Search for the Defect

Men respond to rejection by examining themselves for the cause, and the examination is conducted with the standards described in Chapter 8. The typical content: a specific defect, usually in the man's character or worth; a generalisation from this instance to a pattern; and a discounting of the evidence that contradicts it. This is the mechanism by which an event becomes an identity.

### Rejection Sensitivity

Where a man has experienced repeated rejection, he develops a heightened readiness to detect it: ambiguous signals are read as rejection, neutral remarks are scanned for exclusion, and an expectation is established in advance. This produces two observable patterns, both of which are recognisable to the men themselves once they are named.

**Accommodation.** Excessive effort to secure inclusion: agreeing with everything, giving to the point of injury, apologising before anything has occurred, and remaining in arrangements that are damaging in order to retain a place.

**Anticipatory withdrawal.** Leaving before being refused: declining invitations, removing oneself from contention, ending contact, and describing the position as choice rather than as protection.

### The Two Opposing Responses

The research on rejection identifies two effects that appear contradictory and are both present, depending on circumstance: the tendency to withdraw, and the tendency to aggression. Withdrawal protects against further injury and increases isolation. Aggression — irritability, hostility, an eagerness for confrontation — restores a sense of control and produces precisely the responses that confirm the man's expectation of exclusion. The two responses alternate in the same man, frequently over the same period, and each produces the outcome the other is designed to prevent.

### Rumination

The final mechanism is the most difficult to interrupt and the most responsible for the durability of the injury: the repeated mental review of what occurred, what was said, what should have been said, and what the man should have done. Rumination has two properties that make it particularly adhesive: it feels like problem-solving, which makes it difficult to abandon, and it produces a fresh experience of the injury each time it occurs, which prevents the attenuation that would otherwise take place.

**The practical interruption** is not the instruction to stop thinking about it, which does not work. It is the two-part method used for the internal critic in Chapter 8: an appointment with the material (a defined period each day in which it may be considered, and no other time), and the conversion of the material into a specific item — one written conclusion, one action, or one question that requires an answer from a person rather than from the mind.
