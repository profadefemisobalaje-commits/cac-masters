## 7.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"How do I tell my wife that the money is gone?"**

Prepare the facts before the conversation: what is owed, to whom, at what rate, what the income is, what the obligations are, and how many months the position can be sustained unchanged. Then say what you want from her — to hear it, to help you think, or to act with you — and say when you will update her. The most common mistake is to disclose without a plan, which transfers panic rather than information, and the second most common is to disclose a softened version, which will be discovered and will cost more than the original difficulty. Appendix D provides the worksheet, and Appendix E provides the scripts.

**"Should I sell an asset or borrow to cover the gap?"**

This is a question for a competent financial adviser, and the general principle is that borrowing to maintain an unsustainable standard converts a difficulty into a disaster. Where a borrowing decision is genuinely marginal, three questions assist: does this loan reduce the total cost of the problem; does it have a defined repayment source; and does it postpone a necessary adjustment that will be larger later?

**"What if I have already concealed it for a long time?"**

The cost of concealment continues to rise after disclosure, which is the reason to disclose now rather than after one more quarter. Expect the first weeks to be difficult: anger, fear, questions, and — frequently — a demand to know why you did not say earlier. The answer should be the truth, which is usually that you were ashamed and did not want to disappoint anybody. The repair is completed by consistent honesty about the position from that point onwards, which is a matter of months rather than one conversation.

**"How do I stop comparing myself with men who are doing better?"**

Not by trying to stop noticing, which is not in your control, but by choosing the reference group deliberately and auditing the comparison with the four questions in Section 7.2. In practice, two changes do most of the work: identifying one or two men in your own field who have made the trade you refuse to make, and writing down your own standard rather than measuring yourself against other people's visible results.

**"Is it a failure to reduce the family's standard of living?"**

It is a decision with costs, several of which are real and visible. It is not a failure, and the evidence from men who have made it is that the consequences are rarely as severe as anticipated: the reduction is absorbed, some obligations are renegotiated rather than abandoned, and the family's regard for the man is far more closely related to honesty during the process than to the standard maintained.

::: summary
**Chapter 7 in brief.** The pressure to succeed operates through four components at once: economic necessity, social standing, the belief that one's value to the family is conditional on provision, and an internal standard harsher than anything externally imposed. Its distinctive feature is the locked-in effect: success raises expectations, costs rise faster than income, and the reduction of ambition comes to require a public admission that the man has organised his life to avoid.

What men fear when they fear failure is rarely poverty. It is the loss of standing, of usefulness, of the family's trust, and of self-respect. Each has a different remedy, and only the first responds to earning more.

Comparison harms men in two ways: through a spiral in which each attainment raises the reference standard rather than producing satisfaction, and through distorted assessments of one's own position that lead to bad decisions. The remedy is not the instruction to stop comparing but the systematic exposure of the comparison's five defects — incomparable inputs, selective presentation, trajectory versus moment, the trade one would not accept, and a reference group that can be chosen.

Financial failure delivers three losses at once: material, social, and identity. The behaviours that follow — concealment, over-trading to recover, withdrawal, and self-punishment through work — reliably make the failure worse. What can usually be recovered is income, capacity, and assets over time; what frequently cannot be recovered are the years spent concealing, the trust damaged by secrecy, and the relationships damaged during withdrawal.

Perfectionism and the fear of judgement are distinct patterns with a shared root: the fusion of act with identity. Perfectionism costs time, produces avoidance, destroys delegation, and transmits to children. The fear of judgement produces over-preparation, silence in meetings, avoidance of assessment, and — most expensively — the refusal to seek help for problems that would be resolved in a week by a competent professional. Four measures help: defining suffciency in advance, adopting the professional rather than the ideal standard, testing the feared judgement against evidence, and graduated exposure.

Recovery from a serious failure follows five stages: stop the bleeding by halting concealment-driven expenditure and protecting basic functions; establish the actual position on paper, including the number of months the situation can be sustained; report to one person with a prepared account; act on the two largest items rather than fifteen; and set a review date. The account given to others should contain the facts, the uncertainty, the plan, and the ownership of decisions — without the transfer of shame.

Finally, worth and performance can be separated by practice rather than by belief: changing the language from verdict to description, establishing a domain of value that does not depend on results, keeping an evidence file, and accepting praise. Men who separate the two can lose a business, a job, or a contract and still act. Men who do not must protect an identity while managing a crisis, and the two requirements conflict — which is why concealment, over-trading, and collapse so often occur together.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Which of the four components of the pressure to succeed — economic, social, relational, or internal — is strongest in your life? How much of your current load would remain if the social component disappeared?
2. Take the four objects of the fear of failure in Table 7.1. Which one do you actually fear? What would addressing that specific fear require, and what would it not require?
3. Write down the last comparison that affected a decision you made. Apply the four questions from Section 7.2. Would you now make the same decision?
4. Section 7.3 lists the six thoughts that accompany financial failure. Which of them have you had, and how recently? Which one is doing the most work in your decisions at present?
5. Consider the perfectionism and judgement patterns in Section 7.4. In what area of your work do you refuse to hand over a task, and what has that refusal cost you in the last two years?
6. Complete Stage Two of Section 7.5 for your own position: how many months can the present situation be sustained unchanged? What did you feel on discovering the number?
7. In one sentence, describe the relationship you want between your performance and your worth. Then write what you will do this week that only a man holding that relationship could do.

## Further Reading

- **On the psychology of failure:** the research literature on achievement motivation, attribution, and self-worth, including work on the difference between self-worth contingency on success and self-acceptance, and studies showing that people who attribute failure to specific and changeable causes recover more effectively than those who attribute it to fixed deficiencies of the self.
- **On comparison and social media:** the substantial recent literature on social comparison and well-being, including experimental studies showing that comparison with curated presentations of others' lives lowers mood and satisfaction.
- **On perfectionism:** the research on perfectionism and its consequences, including work distinguishing perfectionistic striving from perfectionistic concern, and studies on its relationship to procrastination, anxiety, and burnout.
- **On financial recovery:** practical literature on debt restructuring, personal financial planning, and small-business recovery, together with the financial counselling materials published by consumer and cooperative organisations in Nigeria and elsewhere.
- **On bankruptcy and insolvency:** the literature on the psychological effects of insolvency on men, including the effects on identity, marriage, and health, and the evidence that early disclosure and legal advice materially improve outcomes.
- **First-person accounts:** published accounts by men who lost businesses, professional standing, or savings, and rebuilt. These accounts supply the most convincing evidence available for the practical claim that serious failure is survivable and frequently instructive.
