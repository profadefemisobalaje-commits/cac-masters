## 13.5 Healing and Learning to Love Again

This section sets out the process of recovery, and it is written with a specific caution: men recover from relationship loss along a recognisable sequence, but the sequence is not a schedule, and a man who attempts to accelerate it by force of will is likely to produce a delay.

### The Three Stages

**Stage One: Stabilisation.** The first weeks to three months. The objectives are physical stability, protection of functioning, and the avoidance of irreversible decisions. Sleep, food, exercise, work, and the ordinary structure of the day should be maintained or re-established. Contact with the former partner should be reduced to what is necessary, and where children are involved, contact should be confined to matters concerning them, in writing, with a fixed format if necessary. Alcohol should be reduced rather than increased, which is contrary to the instinct of the period. One confidant should be identified. Work obligations should be performed at a standard that is defensible, which frequently requires telling a supervisor that something personal is happening, without detail.

**Stage Two: Understanding.** One to six months, and frequently longer. This is the stage at which the meaning of what happened is processed: the actual reasons, the man's own contribution, the genuine features of him that are worth preserving, and the alterations that follow. It is the stage in which the two destructive hypotheses — *I am insufficient* and *all women are alike* — should be examined and replaced with something specific and accurate. It is also the stage in which most of the practical arrangements are settled and in which the legal and financial matters should be concluded, in the interests of leaving them behind.

**Stage Three: Re-engagement.** The stage at which interest returns in other people, in activities, and in the possibility of a new relationship. This stage cannot be scheduled. It occurs when the previous stages have been performed and when the man has re-established some structure of life that is his own rather than a reaction to the loss.

### The Two Destructive Responses

Two responses are common and both prolong the difficulty.

**The first is retaliation in kind.** The man enters a period of conspicuous activity designed to demonstrate that he has recovered, to prompt regret, and to establish his own standing. Its features are recognisable: sexual activity pursued for its demonstrative effect rather than its value, conspicuous spending, statements made in public, and social media presentation. The response is understandable, and it delays recovery for two reasons: it keeps the man's attention on the former partner, and it displaces the work of Stage Two.

**The second is entrenchment.** The man concludes that relationships are unwise, converts the loss into a general principle, and organises his life around self-sufficiency. This is the more common response among men over forty-five, and it is the more difficult to notice, because it is frequently presented as maturity: the man is focused on work, on his children, on his health, and has no interest in the complications of a relationship. In some cases this is a settled and satisfactory choice. In many it is a defensive arrangement whose cost appears later, in the form of isolation, as described in Chapters 4 and 25.

### Learning to Love Again

Three pieces of work are required before a man enters a new relationship in a position to conduct it well.

**The completion of unfinished material.** Most men carry three items from a previous relationship: a story about what happened, a belief about themselves that resulted from it, and an unresolved grievance. Each should be written out, examined for accuracy, and revised. The test is specific: can the man describe the previous relationship to a new partner in a manner that is fair to everybody involved, that includes his own contribution, and that does not require the new partner to take sides? A man who cannot do this brings the previous relationship into the new one.

**The establishment of a life that does not require a partner.** The most reliable predictor of a good subsequent relationship, in the accounts men and women give, is that the man had a viable life of his own: work, friendships, interests, health, and structure. A relationship formed out of need is under pressure from the beginning, and a man who has been through a loss is particularly prone to forming one from need.

**The examination of the pattern.** Where the relationship's end involved something the man did or failed to do — the withdrawal described in Chapter 15, the absence described in Chapter 17, the management described in Chapter 9 — the pattern should be identified and addressed before the new relationship is formed. The contribution is not a matter of blame; it is a matter of identifying the specific behaviour that produced difficulty and confirming that it has changed.

### The Re-entry

Three practical points, based on men's accounts of forming relationships after a loss.

**Expect the comparison, and do not act on it.** A new partner will be compared involuntarily with the previous one, frequently unfavourably, and this is normal in the first months. The comparison should be observed rather than used.

**Do not conceal the history, and do not lay it out in detail.** The honest and workable position is acknowledgement without elaboration: the relationship ended, here is what happened in one sentence, and here is what I learned. A full history delivered early is a burden; a history concealed is a lie that will be expensive later, as Chapter 35 describes.

**Address the practical arrangements before the relationship becomes serious.** Obligations to children, support payments, family obligations, and other standing commitments should be disclosed as the relationship becomes serious, and before any formal commitment. This is the material covered in Chapter 9, in a different context, and the same principle applies.

## 13.6 Illustrative Case: The Widower and the Man Who Was Left

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in counselling practice, bereavement research, and family practice.

**Emeka is fifty-six, a secondary school principal, and his wife of twenty-nine years died twenty months ago, after an illness of seven months.**

Emeka's response followed the pattern that his community expected. He took three weeks of leave, arranged the funeral, received visitors for eleven days, and returned to work. He spoke at his wife's memorial service with composure, and his composure was repeatedly remarked upon by the family, usually in the form of approval.

What followed was the arrangement he did not discuss with anybody. He retained the household exactly as it was: her clothes, her side of the wardrobe, the arrangement of the kitchen, and the items on the dressing table. He began to sleep on the sofa. He took on additional duties at the school, including Saturday examinations supervision, which he had never done before. He stopped attending the monthly meeting of his professional association. He answered questions about his welfare with the sentence *God knows best*, which was true and also served as a terminator. And he had, for eleven months, a period of what he described as discipline: no alcohol, early rising, daily walking, and an emotional flatness he maintained as an achievement.

Fourteen months after the death, his eldest daughter visited for two weeks and observed the following: that her father did not enter the main bedroom, that he had kept everything in place, that he had not once mentioned her mother by name in her hearing, and that he became distinctly different — quicker, harder, less tolerant — whenever the subject approached. She raised it with him directly on the last evening of her visit, in the terms that adult children use when they have decided to be direct: *Papa, what happened to you has happened. It is not in the wardrobe. You are.*

Emeka's account of what occurred in the following period is instructive. He described an evening, approximately three weeks after his daughter's visit, when he entered the main bedroom for the first time since the death, intending to clear the wardrobe, and found himself unable to do anything other than sit on the bed for approximately two hours. He said: *I sat there and I understood that I had been organising my life as though she had travelled. I had been living in an arrangement that expected her to come back. I had not permitted myself to know that she was not coming back, because if I knew that, I would have had to feel it, and I did not have the strength to feel it while there were things to do. There were always things to do.*

He began, in the following months, three things, each ordinary. He attended a weekly gathering of widowed men at a social centre in the town, which he had avoided for a year. He began to keep the bedroom as his own, including the removal of the wardrobe contents, a process that took him five evenings. And he began to speak about his wife, at first in the family and then more widely, in ordinary sentences rather than in tributes.

Twenty months after her death, he describes his state as follows: *I am not over it. I do not expect to be over it. I can hold it now without it taking the whole of me. The year when I was strong was the year I lost, and I did not know it.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The composure was read as recovery.** The community's approval — reinforced by cultural expectation and by the man's own standard — provided a strong incentive to maintain the appearance of having coped, and the appearance was maintained for fourteen months.

**The frozen household was a physical expression of the unfinished grief.** Retaining the wardrobe, sleeping on the sofa, and avoiding the bedroom constituted a daily arrangement that presumed the possibility of return. This pattern is common in bereavement and it is not a sign of pathology; it becomes a problem when it is maintained for a prolonged period without the grief being processed.

**The overload was a strategy.** The additional duties at the school, the Saturday supervision, and the stopped association meeting operated as the same arrangement described in Chapters 5 and 12: the removal of the unstructured time in which grief becomes unavoidable.

**The disclosure came through a direct question.** The daughter's sentence was short, specific, and did not permit the standard answer, and it enabled the man to reconsider, over weeks, a decision he had made without having recognised it as one.

**The three interventions were entirely ordinary.** A group of men in the same position, a practical task conducted at home, and the beginning of ordinary speech about the person who died. This is what recovering from serious loss generally looks like: unremarkable, repetitive, and conducted over a long period rather than achieved in a single insight.

Now a second case, deliberately brief, because it produces a different lesson.

**Tunde is thirty-eight, a civil engineer, and was left by his fiancée five months before the wedding, by telephone message, with no explanation beyond a statement that she could not continue.**

For three months, Tunde behaved in a manner that his friends described as exemplary. He increased his working hours. He organised a trip for six friends, which he paid for. He began a fitness programme with visible results. He attended every family function and answered questions about the wedding with the statement that it was not to be, delivered with a smile.

In the fourth month, an incident occurred at a family gathering. A cousin asked, jokingly and within range of several relatives, whether Tunde had been told the reasons for the cancellation. Tunde rose and left the gathering. He did not return for nine months, and during that period he reduced his contact with his family to telephone calls on important occasions.

What emerged, over two years and in the course of treatment for what he initially described as stress, was a pattern that he had not previously identified. His relationship with his fiancée had been conducted for four years in an arrangement of constant demonstration: he had increased his earnings substantially, taken on a loan to fund the wedding and the housing, adjusted his church membership and family commitments to match her family's expectations, and suppressed three specific disagreements — about the location of the family home, about the number and timing of children, and about the financial assistance he gave to his mother — rather than risk a conflict. He had, in his own description, been managing the relationship as a project. When the project was cancelled, he had no idea who he had been for four years.

Tunde's recovery involved two things that he found difficult and useful: an examination of the four years in which he wrote out the disagreements he had suppressed and what he had actually wanted in each case; and a deliberate practice, in his family and at work, of stating disagreement about small matters, which he had to learn as a technique rather than exercise as an inclination. Two years later, in a relationship that he describes as about sixty per cent as romantic and twice as honest, he reported the sentence that this chapter would identify as its central teaching: *I was not heartbroken because I lost the person. I was broken because I had given up the person I was in order to keep her, and I never told her that I had done it.*
