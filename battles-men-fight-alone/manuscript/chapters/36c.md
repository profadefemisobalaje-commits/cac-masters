## 36.2 What Healing Is Not

Seven misconceptions operate in men's material on this subject, and each prevents the work by setting a standard that cannot be met.

### The Seven Misconceptions

**1. Healing is forgetting.** A man who believes this will measure his progress by the material's absence, and the material's recurrence — which is the ordinary course of memory — will be read as failure. The accurate position: the material remains and its operation reduces. Chapter 30.2's finding on continuing bonds applies: the goal is not the removal of the connection but its integration into the man's arrangements.

**2. Time heals.** The statement is partly true and it is dangerous in the form in which it is used, because the man's conclusion is that no work is required. The accurate position: time assists when the wound has an arrangement and assists considerably less when it has none, which is the finding on prolonged grief in Chapter 30.7 and on the persistence of the material in Chapter 29.3. A wound with no arrangement frequently becomes more established with time, because the arrangements around it — the household's account of the man, the compensations, the avoidance, the identity — acquire their own structure.

**3. Healing means becoming a different kind of man.** The instruction that a man's recovery requires him to become expressive, soft, available, or verbal in a form that is not his. The accurate position: the forms of the work are varied, as Chapter 30.2 established, and the objective is the man's arrangements and the reduction of the wound's operation. A man who is not a talker can conduct this work through the written account, the physical form, the group with a defined purpose, and the conduct of Chapters 29 and 30.

**4. Healing requires forgiveness.** The material on forgiveness in Chapters 16, 26, and 29 applies. The accurate position: the release of the account and the repair of the man's own obligations are the man's work; the injured party's forgiveness and the resumption of the relationship are separate matters; and a man may conduct the whole of his recovery with the wrong unrepaired in the other party's hands.

**5. The single breakthrough.** The expectation that the material will be resolved by one conversation, one appointment, one disclosure, one insight. The expectation is a product of the culture's accounts of recovery, and its operation in practice is that the man conducts the first step, finds that the material remains, and concludes that the work failed. The accurate position: the work proceeds in stages with relapses, as Section 36.5 sets out, and the change is measured over years rather than occasions.

**6. The permanent reduction of the man's capacity.** The belief that healing requires the man to accept a reduced version of himself: less decisive, less capable, less ambitious, permanently marked. The accurate position is the opposite in the case material: the men who conduct the work report an increase in capacity, because the attention that was occupied and the judgement that was degraded are returned, and because the arrangements of Chapters 31, 33, and 35 make the man more effective rather than less.

**7. The requirement to be grateful for what the wound taught.** The instruction that a man should find the benefit in what was done to him. The instruction is applied by well-intentioned people, and its operation is that the man's own account of the harm is not permitted. The accurate position: some consequences of a hard experience are real — the competence, the caution, the capacity to endure — and the man is not required to regard the harm as beneficial in order to retain them.

### The Effect of the Misconceptions

The seven share a common mechanism: each sets a standard that the work cannot meet and therefore converts the man's ordinary course into evidence of failure. The man who expects forgetting reads the material's return as a setback; the man who expects a breakthrough reads the absence of resolution as a failed treatment; the man who expects a reduced self declines to begin, because the price appears to be his capacity.

The chapter's practical instruction is therefore that the reader should select a measure that the work can actually produce, and the following five are the ones used in the practice material.

**The measures:**

- a reduction in the frequency with which the material arrives unbidden;
- the availability of a person who knows the material;
- the absence of the material's operation in the observed places — the sleep, the temper, the drinking, the avoidance;
- the man's ability to describe the material without managing the room;
- the reduction of the material's cost to the man's household.

Each of the five is observable, none requires the wound's removal, and all five are within reach of the arrangements in Section 36.4.

## 36.3 The Four Conditions of Recovery

The research and the practice literature identify four conditions that are consistent across the settings in which recovery occurs, and the chapter presents them as arrangements rather than as attitudes.

### Condition One: Safety and Stability

**What it is.** The material must be addressed from a position that can hold it. The condition has four components: the man's physical safety, which concerns violence, the immediate risk, and the settings in which he is not safe; his basic stability, which concerns sleep, food, shelter, medical care, and the material of Chapter 34.5; the absence of the immediate emergencies of Chapters 22, 23, and 34 — an active addiction, a financial collapse in progress, a risk to life; and the availability of at least one arrangement that can hold what the man will surface.

**Why it comes first.** The evidence on recovery is consistent: the material should not be surfaced before there is somewhere for it to go. The mechanism is practical: a man who opens the material without an arrangement will experience the material's full intensity, will have no place to put it, and will do what any person does in that position — close it again, generally more tightly, which produces the position described in Chapter 9 and which frequently produces the conclusion that the work is dangerous.

**What it means for the reader.** Chapters 18 to 23 are conducted first where the man's position includes an active addition, a financial emergency, a risk to life, a marriage in acute crisis, or a recent bereavement. This is the sequencing principle of the whole book, and it is the reason the book's structure places the material as it does.

### Condition Two: The Account

**What it is.** The material must be described, in a form that the man can produce, and the description must be available to at least one recipient beyond the man. The forms: the written account of Chapters 29.6 and 30.6; the spoken account to a witness or a professional; the account conducted in a group; the account rendered as the record of Chapter 25.3; the account constructed with the assistance of the professional in the structured forms used in treatment.

**Why it matters.** The research on the operation of memory for difficult material identifies the mechanism: an event that has not been described in language is frequently held in the form of sensory fragments, physiological responses, and generalised associations, and its representation in language is what allows it to be situated in the man's past and distinguished from his present. This is the reason that a man's material arrives as a physical state, a mood that appears without a cause, a specific fear, or an image, and the reason its description in words changes its operation.

**The specific note for men.** The account does not require the man's feeling to be narrated. The description of what occurred, in sequence, with the facts, is the operative element. Men who believe that they cannot describe the material because they cannot describe their feelings about it should note that the feeling is not the required content.

### Condition Three: Mourning and the Conduct of What Was Lost

**What it is.** The material has a cost, and the cost requires acknowledgement. The forms are those of Chapter 30.6 and Chapter 13: the acknowledgement of what was lost, the marking of the loss, the conduct of the grief in the man's own form, and the incorporation of the loss into the man's account of his life.

**Why it matters.** The material on loss and on the adaptation to irreversible circumstances is consistent: the reduction in the wound's operation is associated with the man's acknowledgement of the loss's actual size, and the failure to acknowledge it is associated with the prolongation of the material and with the compensations described in Chapter 29.7. The man who has not mourned the loss may not conduct anything else, because the material remains invested in the maintenance of an account in which the loss has not been counted.

**The conduct of losses that were not acknowledged.** The categories of Chapter 30.1 are the chapter's most frequent material: the loss with no occasion, the shared dream that ended, the child lost before birth, the friend whose death nobody in the man's household knew about. Each requires the four elements of Chapter 30.4: the place, the visit, the account, and the witness.

### Condition Four: Reconnection and the Construction of the Life

**What it is.** The work's final condition, and the one that determines whether the material becomes a place in the man's life or a project in its own right. Its components: the man's re-engagement with the arrangements of an ordinary life — work, household, relationships, the community, the future — in a form that includes the material rather than requiring it to be resolved first; the construction of the arrangements of Chapter 27; the undertaking of the measures of Chapters 24, 25, and 28; and the deliberate conduct of what the man has identified as owed.

**Why it matters.** The finding from the practice literature and from the case material is consistent: recovery is not completed in the material but in the man's reconnection. The men whose recovery holds are the men who, at some point in the process, took up something — the arrangement, the responsibility, the work, the mentorship, the measure — that required them and that was not the material. This is the reason that the material of Chapter 28.6, Chapter 29.6's element four, and Chapter 33.6's arrangements are the substance of recovery rather than an appendix to it.
