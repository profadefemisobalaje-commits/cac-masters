## 16.6 Rebuilding Trust in Yourself and Others

The final substantive section addresses the question that men ask most frequently after a betrayal, and it addresses it as a technical problem rather than a moral one: how is trust rebuilt, in a man who has learned that his trust was misplaced?

### Two Distinctions

**Trust is not a single quantity.** A man who says *I no longer trust anybody* is describing a generalisation that his own behaviour does not fully implement. In practice, men who have been betrayed continue to trust in specific domains — they trust their driver with the vehicle, the bank with the salary, a colleague with a report — and their difficulty is confined to the domain in which the betrayal occurred and to its closest analogues. Identifying the domain is the first practical step, and it usually reduces the problem from a general condition to a specific one.

**Trust is not a decision, it is an accumulation.** Men frequently attempt to resolve the matter by deciding to trust again, which fails for the same reason that a decision to trust in the first place would fail: trust is established by experience, in graduated steps, and it is not a setting that can be adjusted by will.

### The Graduated Method

The practical method is an escalating sequence of small exposures, conducted deliberately, with a decision point after each.

**Step One: identify the domain.** In which area did the injury occur — money, confidences, professional cooperation, family loyalty, a relationship? The rebuilding is confined to that domain and does not require the man to alter his conduct in the others.

**Step Two: choose low-stakes exposures.** Identify people who are appropriate for particular kinds of trust, and begin with items of small value: a small sum, an ordinary confidence, a minor responsibility. Where a person has been reliable in that domain previously, this is the correct place to begin, since the accumulated history is the material from which trust is rebuilt.

**Step Three: verify without concealment.** Verification is legitimate and should be visible rather than secret. A statement of the form *I am going to ask you to send me the record each month, because of what happened with the previous arrangement* is honest, and it is considerably easier to maintain than covert checking, which produces the man's own dishonesty alongside his injury.

**Step Four: record the results as data.** A simple written record of the specific exposures and their outcomes, reviewed at intervals. Its function is to correct the mechanism described in Section 16.2, in which any ordinary unavailability is read as evidence and the positive instances are discounted.

**Step Five: extend where the results support it, and stop where they do not.** The last part is important. Graduated trust does not mean trusting everybody at every level; where the record indicates untrustworthiness in a domain, the correct response is a reduction in exposure, not an increase in vigilance. Men in the aftermath of betrayal frequently continue to deal with the same person, at the same level, and maintain a permanent condition of watchfulness instead. Vigilance is more expensive than a boundary.

### The Man's Own Judgement

The fourth injury from Section 16.3 is the injury to the man's confidence in his own judgement. Three measures are specific to it.

**Separate the assessment from the outcome.** A person who was trusted and proved treacherous does not demonstrate that the assessment was unreasonable; it demonstrates that the person's conduct was concealed, which is the one variable the man cannot observe. The test is the test of information available at the time, and men should apply it honestly: what was observable, and what would a reasonable person in the same position have concluded?

**Record the signals that were genuinely present.** Not to blame, but to build capacity. The useful output of this measure is a short list of specific features to be more attentive to: the timetable that did not add up, the refusal of documentation, the pattern of other relationships, the treatment of previous partners or associates, the reluctance to be introduced to the person's family.

**Introduce one reviewing relationship.** A man who has been betrayed should have one person with whom a significant arrangement is discussed before it is entered: a business investment, a loan, a commitment of trust. The purpose is not permission but review, since a second assessment corrects the specific vulnerability — the man's own judgement in a domain where he is interested and therefore partial.

### Trust and the Household

Where the man's marriage and children have observed the injury, three measures apply. The man should state what happened at a level appropriate to their age and to their involvement; he should not conduct the recovery as a private operation with the household uninformed, since the household's anxiety, if unexplained, becomes a second injury; and he should avoid the general lesson that bitterness supplies. The most damaging thing a betrayed man can transmit to his sons is the proposition that trust is foolishness. A more accurate and more useful transmission is available: that trust is extended in stages, verified, withdrawn where the evidence requires, and restored where it is earned.

## 16.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Accused Without a Hearing

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in community affairs, cooperative-society administration, legal practice, and counselling.

**Segun is fifty-eight, a retired primary school head teacher, and was for nineteen years the secretary of the development association of his community in Ogun State.** He is married with four children, the eldest of whom is a medical doctor in the United Kingdom.

In the fifth year before his retirement, the association undertook the construction of a borehole and a small water project, with a total budget of about eleven million naira, raised through members' contributions, a grant from the local government, and a donation from a member's business. Segun, as secretary, received the contributions, kept the records, and paid the contractors.

Eighteen months after the project's completion, during a period of dispute over the association's leadership, an allegation appeared in the form of a document circulated to members and submitted to a group of elders: that about 2.4 million naira of the project funds had not been accounted for, and that Segun had been responsible for the receipts.

Three features of what followed determined the case.

First, Segun was not informed before the document circulated. He learned of the allegation from a neighbour who asked him whether it was true, at his gate, on a Saturday morning.

Second, the allegation was framed in a form that could not be answered. The document did not specify a transaction, a date, or a receipt. It stated a total that "could not be accounted for," and requested that Segun respond to the general question, which is precisely the form of allegation described in Section 16.4 as impossible to disprove.

Third, Segun responded in the way that most men respond. He removed himself. He stopped attending the association's meetings, stopped attending the community's functions, and, within two months, stopped attending church services and the weekly market, arranging for his wife to go instead. He described his reasoning as: *What is the point of being there while people look at you? I would rather be absent than be watched.*

Two years were lost. In that period, Segun: applied for and obtained the association's records, twice, and was refused both times on procedural grounds; consulted a lawyer, who advised that the matter had no legal cause of action because no legal proceeding had been initiated against him; lost a rentable property arrangement in the community, which he had intended to develop; and stopped speaking to two members with whom he had been friendly for thirty years. His wife was, in his description, his only confidant, and his withdrawal was interpreted as confirmation: in the words used by two members subsequently, *if the man had nothing to hide, he would have come to the meeting and answered.*

The turn came through his eldest child, who came home for a month in the second year and did three things that Segun described as the reasons for the recovery. She went, with her father, to the association's chairman and obtained the complete file of the project's accounts. She then, with Segun's permission, produced a reconciliation: contributions received, receipted and banked, and the payments made, item by item, against the contractors' invoices and the bank statements. The reconciliation identified the source of the 2.4 million discrepancy: it was the local government grant, which had never been received in full — approximately 2.1 million had been paid in two instalments and 300,000 remained unpaid at the time the accounts were drawn, with the balance still on the donor's ledger — and the project had been completed at a lower specification in consequence, a decision taken at the association's own meeting, minuted and signed by the chairman.

The third thing she did was the one Segun regarded as decisive, and it involved no document. She told him that his absence was the case against him, and she required him to accompany her to the following general meeting.

Segun attended. He did not make a speech. The chairman, who had by then left the office and been replaced, presented the reconciliation at the meeting, and the matter was recorded as closed. Two of the three principal figures behind the allegation attended the same meeting; one apologised to Segun privately, and one did not.

Segun's account of the recovery, four years after the meeting, identified three changes. He resumed his participation in the association and is currently its treasurer, an office he accepted at the request of the members after a period of refusal. He has not resumed his friendship with the two members who circulated the document, and he described this as a boundary rather than a grievance: *I greet them. I do not work with them and I do not discuss my affairs with them.* And he stated the change in himself in the following terms: *For two years I behaved as though I had been judged. Nobody had judged me. Nobody had even looked at the paper. What I did by staying away was provide the evidence. My daughter made me come back into the room, and in the room it took one meeting.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**Segun learned of the allegation from a neighbour.** The absence of a hearing was total: the document circulated, the elders received it, and the man at the centre of it was not informed. This structure — an allegation that acquires standing without the person being heard — is the mechanism of Section 16.4, and it is common in community, family, professional, and employment settings.

**The allegation was framed so that it could not be answered.** A total without transactions, dates, or documents cannot be refuted by denial, and denial in that form is read as evasion. The only effective answer was the one that eventually worked: a reconciliation of the accounts, item by item, produced from the original records rather than from the man's account.

**The withdrawal was the decisive error, and it was entirely rational.** Segun's reasoning — that presence meant being watched — is the reasoning that the injury produces, and its consequence was that the association's inference (*if he had nothing to hide he would have come*) became the case's principal evidence. This is the reason the method in Section 16.5 places such emphasis on continuing to be present, and the reason that men find the instruction difficult to accept.

**The daughter's intervention was a document and an escort.** Two actions: she obtained the records and produced the reconciliation, which supplied the factual answer the allegation had been designed to resist; and she accompanied her father into the room, which supplied the one thing he could not supply alone in the second year, which was a reason to be present. The pattern recurs throughout this book: practical assistance given by one person, at a specific point, changes a decade-long course.

**The boundary was maintained without estrangement.** Segun did not resume friendship with the two members who circulated the document, and he did not denounce them, refuse to greet them, or attempt to exclude them from the association. The position — ordinary contact, no cooperation, no discussion of his affairs — is the form of boundary that is sustainable in a community where continued contact is unavoidable, and it is the option identified in Section 16.5 as appropriate in most family and community situations.
