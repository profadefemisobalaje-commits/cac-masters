## 23.4 What Addiction Costs

The costs of dependence are extensive, and they are set out here in four categories because each requires different attention in the recovery.

### The Cost to the Man

**Health.** The direct effects of the substance or behaviour; the indirect effects of sleep loss, poor nutrition, and neglect of existing conditions; the elevated rate of accidents and injuries; and the specific effects on the liver, cardiovascular system, and nervous system in the case of alcohol, which is the most common case among the men this book addresses.

**Mental health.** The condition and the mental health condition are related in both directions. Depression and anxiety are more common among men with dependence, dependence produces and maintains both, and the two are frequently treated as one. The practical implication is that both should be assessed, because treating one and not the other is one of the common causes of relapse.

**Capacity.** The reduction in judgement, memory, and reliability is frequently noticed first by colleagues. Men with concealed dependence frequently work longer to compensate, which accelerates the condition.

**Self-regard.** The specific injury described throughout this chapter: the man's own account of himself is organised around a matter he cannot discuss.

**Time and money.** The direct expenditure, which is frequently larger than the man admits even to himself, and the ancillary costs: the absences, the losses, the payments, the interest.

### The Cost to the Household

**The atmosphere of the house.** The most consistently reported effect by the families of men with dependence: the household is organised around the man's condition. The children learn to read the man's state; the wife manages the arrangements to avoid the trigger; the household's plans are made conditional on the man's capacity to participate.

**The financial consequences.** The direct expenditure, the losses, the debts, and the diversion of household funds, as described in Chapter 9. Where gambling is the form, the losses can be catastrophic, and their recovery takes years.

**The relational consequences.** The deterioration of the marriage; the wife's assumption of the household's management; the children's formation of their own patterns; and the specific development described in the marital literature, in which the unaffected partner becomes the household's manager of the condition and the man becomes its subject.

**The physical risk.** Where the condition involves a substance, the risk to the household is not only financial and relational. The safety considerations in Appendix G and the guidance in the safety sections of this book apply where a household contains violence or the threat of it, which is more likely in the presence of alcohol.

### The Cost to the Man's Position

Employment consequences; professional consequences, including licensing matters where the profession regulates conduct; the loss of reputation; and, in the specific case where the condition becomes known to the community, the loss of standing described in Chapter 16. Men should note that the position is rarely as irreversible as the fear suggests, and that the pattern for the men who lose the most is concealment maintained until the condition declares itself on its own terms — the incident, the dismissal, the offence — at which point the man has no control over the disclosure's form.

### The Cost That Is Frequently Overlooked: The Cost of Concealment

The final category is the one this chapter exists to name. Concealment has its own costs, and they are frequently larger than the costs of the condition.

**The delay.** Every year of concealment is a year in which the condition progresses and in which the treatment has a lower prospect of success. This is the single most consequential cost.

**The household's wrong conclusions.** Where the behaviour is known in its effects but not in its cause, the household constructs an explanation, and the explanation is generally about the man's character or about its own inadequacy. The children of fathers with concealed dependence frequently carry an account of themselves as having been the reason their father withdrew.

**The compulsion to compensate.** The man works longer, provides more, and performs reliability to demonstrate that nothing is wrong. The compensation produces exhaustion, and it produces the material of Chapters 12 and 20.

**The loss of the years in which the man could have been known.** The most general cost, and the one that men in recovery most frequently describe as irrecoverable.

## 23.5 The Routes to Recovery

This section sets out the routes, with an honest account of what each involves. It is deliberately specific, because the vagueness of the advice offered to men on this subject is a substantial part of the problem.

### Route One: Assessment

The first step in every case, and not an optional preliminary. An assessment establishes: whether dependence is present, which determines the treatment; whether a co-occurring condition — depression, anxiety, a sleep disorder, a physical condition — is present, which is the case in a large proportion of men with dependence; whether withdrawal carries medical risk, which determines the safety of the initial period; and what the appropriate level of treatment is.

Where assessment is obtained: a physician, a psychiatrist, a clinic specialising in substance use, a hospital emergency department where the situation is acute, or a confidential telephone service offered by health authorities in many jurisdictions. The specific content of the discussion can be limited to the material the man is willing to disclose; the assessment does not require the household's involvement at this stage.

### Route Two: Professional Treatment

**Outpatient treatment.** The most common form for men in employment and family life: regular sessions with a counsellor, a psychologist, or a physician, frequently weekly, sometimes with group participation. Its advantages are confidentiality and compatibility with work. Its requirement is a genuine reduction in use alongside the treatment, which should be established at the outset with the practitioner.

**Medication.** Where indicated, for withdrawal management, for the reduction of craving, and for co-occurring conditions. The evidence on medication for alcohol dependence is substantial, and the specific point for men is that the medication is not a substitute for the psychological work and the psychological work is frequently insufficient without medication.

**Residential treatment.** Where outpatient treatment is insufficient, where the environment cannot be altered, where the condition is severe, or where the household's safety requires separation. Its advantage is the removal of triggers and the provision of a structured environment. Its costs are the time, the expense, the disclosure, and the return to an unchanged environment at the end.

**Group programmes and peer support.** The structured group programmes, including the long-established twelve-step approach and its variants, and the professional group programmes. Their specific advantage is the presence of other men with the same condition, which produces the evidence against the shame described in Chapter 21 more rapidly than any other route. Men who are reluctant to attend a group frequently describe the experience afterwards as the point at which the condition lost its uniqueness.

### Route Three: What the Man Can Do Himself

Three measures that a man can take without treatment, and that are appropriate in cases of harmful use rather than dependence.

**The record.** Two weeks of honest recording: the quantity, the time, the situation, the state before and after. The record is the most reliable instrument available, and most men who complete it find that their use is higher than their estimate.

**The environment.** The removal of the substance or the trigger from the house; the removal of the application; the change of route; the change of company. The environmental change is not a permanent solution and it produces a substantial reduction, which establishes the man's position.

**The substitute.** A specific activity at the time and in the place in which the use occurred: exercise, a meeting, a call with a person, a task. The substitute must serve the same function — rest, company, relief, sleep — and it must be arranged in advance rather than decided at the moment of the urge.

### Route Four: The Conversation With the Household

The subject that brings most men to this chapter's page last, and the one that determines the durability of the recovery.

The conversation should not be conducted first, and it should not be conducted alone. The order that practitioners generally recommend: assessment, treatment begun, the household informed with the support of the practitioner, and the household's arrangements adjusted with professional input. The conversation's content should be: what the condition is, what has been occurring, what treatment is in place, what the household's role is, and what the man needs — which is frequently specific and modest, such as the removal of alcohol from the house or the management of a trigger.

The household's reaction is not controllable and should not be predicted. Men who have conducted the conversation with professional support report a range of responses: relief, anger, a period of crisis followed by support, and, in a minority of cases, a decision that the marriage cannot continue. The last outcome is a genuine possibility, and its probability is considerably higher where the disclosure is made after an incident than where it is made before one.
