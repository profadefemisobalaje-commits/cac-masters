## 17.8 Practical Exercise: The Fatherhood Inventory

This exercise takes about two hours and is completed in writing. It is designed for fathers of any age, including fathers of adult children, and it produces the most value where it is completed honestly in Parts Two and Three.

### Part One: The Record

For each child, complete the following without consulting anybody.

| Item | Child 1 | Child 2 | Child 3 | Child 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Age | | | | |
| Hours I spent with this child last week | | | | |
| The last thing this child told me that mattered to them | | | | |
| What this child worries about, to my knowledge | | | | |
| What this child wants to do, to my knowledge | | | | |
| The thing I most admire in this child | | | | |
| The last time I said it to them | | | | |
| What this child would say if asked what I want from them | | | | |
| What I know about this child that nobody has told me | | | | |

Two or three blanks in the last four rows indicate the pattern described in Section 17.5 rather than a deficiency of memory.

### Part Two: The Sacrifice Ledger

Write out, in specific terms, what you have given up or deferred on behalf of your children. Include the year and the amount where they exist: the position not taken, the study not undertaken, the business not started, the move not made, the health check deferred, the friendship allowed to lapse, the six years of night shift.

Then answer three questions.

- Have I ever stated any of these aloud, to anybody?
- Which of them did I choose, and which arrived?
- Which of them am I currently expecting something in return for, without having said so?

### Part Three: The Fear

Write out your fear of failing as a father in your own words, in no more than 200 words. Then classify each element of it using the four responsibilities in Section 17.3.

- **Keep them safe:** which of my fears concerns this?
- **Provide the necessities to the extent of my capacity:** which?
- **Be a person whose conduct they may safely imitate:** which?
- **Remain a person they know:** which?

Anything that does not fall into one of the four categories is a preference, a custom, or an ambition. Mark it as such. Men consistently find that between a third and a half of the fear's content is in that category, and the marking alone reduces it.

### Part Four: The One Change

Choose one item and specify it.

- **The change:** what will be different.
- **The unit:** the activity, the call, the hour, the meeting — stated precisely.
- **The frequency:** weekly, monthly, or annual.
- **The period:** for how long before it will be judged.
- **How the child will know:** what will be said to them, in one sentence.

Write the one sentence that will be said to the child and say it this week. Men who postpone the sentence for more than a month rarely say it.

### Part Five: The Notebook

Begin the two records described in Section 17.6.

- **Record One: the facts.** Family medical history, dates, positions held, property, debts cleared, and the events of the man's working life.
- **Record Two: the material never said.** Per child: what they were like, what the father noticed, what he feared for them, what he wishes them to know about him, and one item of his own conduct that requires correction.

Neither record should be shown to anybody until it is complete, and it need not be shown at all. Its function is not the document but the work.

## 17.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I have worked away for years. Is it too late?"**

No, and the measure that matters is not the recovery of the past but the establishment of the present arrangement. A father who begins a monthly call with an adult child at sixty is doing the work at the correct time: the child's need for a father's knowledge and regard does not expire, and the adult relationship is the one that lasts longest.

**"My children do not want to talk to me. What do I do?"**

Do not require conversation. Establish an occasion and keep it without requirement: the regular call, the shared meal, the journey, the practical assistance offered without conditions. Where there is a specific injury, the correction in Section 17.6 should be delivered, once, without a request for forgiveness, and then the arrangement should be maintained. Withdrawn children frequently re-engage when the requirement of the exchange is removed, and the process takes years rather than months.

**"My wife says I am present but not involved. She is right, and I do not know where to begin."**

Begin with an activity and a child, individually, weekly, chosen to suit the child. The purpose of the activity is to create the occasion; the conversation follows from the occasion and not the reverse. Men find that the second occasion is easier than the first and that the tenth occasion is entirely ordinary. Where the man has never spoken about himself in the household, the material of Chapters 3 and 14 provides the prerequisite.

**"Is it wrong to want recognition for what I have done?"**

No. What is unworkable is an undisclosed expectation of recognition, which is the ledger described in Section 17.2. State the position and let the household decide what to do with it. Note that the recognition that fathers most commonly report as valuable is not gratitude for provision, which children frequently supply in the form of respect that feels formal, but interest in the man himself.

**"I am the only one providing for twelve people. Where do I find time for presence?"**

The time does not exist and will not be created by reorganisation. It has to be taken from something, and the practical sequence is the one in Chapter 12: the hour count, one category removed, the minimum allocations scheduled. Where the obligations are genuinely non-negotiable for the present year, the correct approach is a defined period: an arrangement stated in advance — *this year I am on the contract and I will be absent; next year the arrangement changes* — which the children can understand, because children manage an explained absence far better than an unexplained presence.

**"My father is dying and we have never spoken. Can it be done now?"**

Yes, and the form matters. Begin with something practical, which gives both parties a subject: a document, a photograph, a journey, a family matter requiring discussion. Then say the specific things that require saying, in the structure of Section 17.6: what he did for you, what you wish he had done differently, and what you know about him that you value. The conversation does not need to resolve anything; it needs to occur, and men who have had it report that the main effect was on themselves.

::: summary
**Chapter 17 in brief.** Fatherhood is discussed publicly in two registers — authority and provision — and neither describes what fathers experience from the inside. The weight of paternal responsibility consists of five components: the permanent consciousness of dependence; a standard derived from comparison rather than from capacity; the absence of any interim feedback; the expectation of shielding the household from bad news; and the fear for the world the children will inhabit.

The load is distributed across four fronts — children, partner, the father's own parents, and the extended family and community — and the characteristic strain of each competes with the others for the same finite hours. The competition, rather than the volume, is what men describe when they say they are stretched. Three things about the weight are rarely said: that the children are the source of pressure as well as the reason for effort; that the marriage has been deprioritised deliberately; and that the father does not know his children's interior lives.

Silent sacrifice takes six forms: the sacrificial hours, which the household registers as absence; abandoned ambition, rarely disclosed for fear of imposing an obligation; health; own interests and friendships; emotional availability; and the man's account of himself. The sacrifices are invisible because they are performed as duty, have no end date, leave no visible trace, are offset by visible rewards, and are eventually forgotten by the man himself. The paternal ledger — the internal account of what has been given up for the children — is protective in function and damaging in effect, converting the relationship into an account the children have never been told about.

The fear of failing as a father distorts decisions through overwork, discipline as over-correction, conflict with the child's other influences, the refusal to seek help, and the projection of the man's own abandoned ambitions. It is reduced by defining what a father is actually responsible for, measuring presence rather than provision, and obtaining the information the fear is substituting for.

A father who cannot show emotion produces six documented effects in his children: uncertainty about his regard, the reading of silence as disapproval, the learning of the same repertoire, difficulty with authority and conflict, discomfort at receiving affection, and the painful consolidation of distance in his old age. The measures that change the pattern are one true sentence about the man's own state, the explanation of his silences, a recurring activity with each child, the reception of what the child offers, and the direct statement of regard.

What a father leaves is of three kinds: material, competence, and relational. The relational inheritance is the most durable and the least likely to be chosen deliberately. It is built by transmitting the actual history, teaching competence explicitly, writing what is not said, establishing a recurring obligation of contact, and saying the three things children need to hear while they can still be said. Where a father's conduct has caused injury, the correction — conduct, impact, reason, and present arrangement — is delivered once, without a request for forgiveness, and its purpose is the removal of the child's self-directed explanation.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. From the five components of the weight in Section 17.1, which is heaviest for you — and which have you never said aloud to anybody?
2. Which of the four fronts in Figure 17.1 is currently being neglected because another is absorbing the hours? What is the cost, in specific terms?
3. Complete Part Two of the exercise. How many of your sacrifices have never been stated to anybody? What are you currently expecting in return for one of them without having said so?
4. What is your fear of failing as a father, in your own words? Applying the four responsibilities, how much of it falls outside them?
5. How many hours did you spend with each child last week, and what is the number you would consider adequate? What is the difference, in hours per month?
6. If you cannot show emotion as your father could not, what is the one sentence about your own state that you could say in front of your children this week?
7. Which of the three statements in Section 17.6 — that you are proud, that a failure does not change your regard, that the child has affected you — have you never said to each child? When will you say it?

## Further Reading

- **On father involvement and child outcomes:** the substantial research literature on the association between paternal involvement and children's cognitive, social, and emotional development, including the findings on the specific contribution of fathers' engagement distinct from mothers'.
- **On father absence and presence:** the research distinguishing physical presence from emotional availability, and the findings on the outcomes associated with presence that is not accompanied by engagement.
- **On intergenerational transmission:** the literature on the transmission of parenting patterns across generations, including the findings on the transmission of emotional unavailability and the factors that interrupt it.
- **On fathers' mental health:** the research on paternal depression and anxiety, including the finding that paternal mental health conditions affect children's outcomes independently of maternal health, and the evidence on the under-recognition of these conditions in fathers.
- **On men's grief and bereavement as fathers:** the literature on paternal grief, particularly after the death of a child and after separation, including the finding that fathers' grief is frequently unrecognised and unsupported.
- **Practical resources:** structured fatherhood programmes, many of which are delivered through community organisations, schools, and local government; the material in Appendix L on the father's legacy document; and Chapters 12, 18, and 19 of this book, which address the working life, the marital arrangement, and the father's own history respectively.
