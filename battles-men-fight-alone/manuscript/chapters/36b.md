## 36.1 The Wounds Men Carry

This section is an inventory, and its purpose is that the reader can identify what is still operating before selecting the work. The categories are drawn from the research and the practice literature and are presented in plain terms.

### The Ten Categories

**1. The wounds of childhood.** The absence of attention; the harsh or unpredictable parent; the parent who was present and unwell; the violence in the household; the sexual or physical abuse; the humiliation; the poverty that required the man to work from an early age; the parentification described in Chapter 19; the separation or the death of a parent; the household's treatment of one child as the problem. The category's feature is that its effects are frequently invisible to the man because the household's arrangements were normal to him.

**2. The wounds of violence.** The man as the victim of violence in adulthood: the assault, the robbery, the armed attack, the police or institutional violence, the violence in a conflict, the violence in a marriage or by a partner, the violence at work. The category carries the injury's physical consequences and the material described in the research on traumatic exposure: the intrusion of the event, the avoidance of the setting, the hypervigilance, the alteration of the man's assessment of his safety and his world.

**3. The wounds of humiliation.** The specific category in the research on men and shame: the public degradation; the failure in front of the men whose regard mattered; the position lost; the man's body, speech, or status mocked at a formative age; the family's arrangement that made him the object; the treatment at an institution. Its feature is that it operates through the man's conduct in the setting rather than through his memory of the event, and its consequences are those of Chapters 8 and 21.

**4. The wounds of betrayal.** The material of Chapter 26: the confidences used, the trusts broken, the abandonments, the parent who left, the partner's conduct, the colleague's betrayal, the institution's conduct toward a man who had served it. The category's specific feature is the alteration of the man's general model of other people, which is the reason the correction requires evidence rather than argument.

**5. The wounds of loss.** The deaths and separations of Chapter 30 and the losses of Chapter 28. The category includes the losses whose acknowledgement was not available, which are the ones the chapter's work most frequently concerns.

**6. The wounds of failure.** The business lost, the position removed, the examination failed, the man's own conduct that produced a consequence for his household or for others. This category overlaps with the material of Chapters 25 and 29 and its feature is the man's own participation, which is what makes it resistant to the form of healing that requires the man to be a victim of circumstance.

**7. The wounds of the position.** The material of Part IX: the decisions that harmed people, the material of forty people received and never placed, the man's own conduct while carrying a load he could not support — the severity, the drinking, the neglect of his household. The category is identified in leadership practice and occupational health and its feature is that the man's responsibility is real and the arrangement is absent.

**8. The wounds of the household.** The man's own conduct as a husband and father: the years of absence, the severity, the marriage that failed, the child who is estranged, the disclosures the household made and the man did not receive. This category is the material of Parts III and V, and its inclusion here is deliberate: a man's own conduct is a wound he carries, and the work on it is Chapter 29's.

**9. The wounds of the body.** The injury, the disability, the chronic condition, the illness, the loss of the man's physical capacity, the material of Chapter 32.7 in its wider form. The category carries the man's relation to his own body and to the position his body supplied, and its treatment is described in Chapter 32.3's first measure.

**10. The wound of the man's whole account of himself.** The specific category that the book has identified repeatedly: the conclusion the man has formed about himself. It may be the material of Chapter 8's core belief, Chapter 21's private account, Chapter 24's lost identity, or Chapter 25's accepted verdict. Its feature is that it operates as the frame through which all the other categories are interpreted, and its work is the reconstruction described in Chapters 24 and 25.

### What the Inventory Produces

Four outputs, and each is required before the work of Section 36.4 can be conducted.

**The identification of what is still operating.** A wound that is not operating requires no work; a wound that is operating must be identified. Its markers are the ones used throughout this book: the sleep, the temper, the avoidance, the drinking, the attention, the specific subject that cannot be raised, the specific setting that is declined, the man's own account of his conduct toward his household.

**The identification of the categories, which determines the work.** The categories are not interchangeable. Betrayal requires the material of Chapter 26.5. Loss requires Chapter 30's arrangements. Failure requires Chapter 25's measure and the moral material of Chapter 29. Violence and traumatic exposure require the professional route of Section 36.6. Humiliation requires the reconstruction of the man's account of himself, which is Chapter 21's work.

**The identification of the sequence.** Where several categories are present, the work is ordered: the safety and stability first, then the material that is most available, then the material that requires support. The ordering matters and its principle is supplied in Section 36.4.

**The identification of what requires a professional.** The categories of Section 36.6 — traumatic exposure, depression that has not lifted, the material connected to substances, the material of Chapter 34.3's first category — should be referred to the professional route at this point rather than after the man has attempted the private work. The finding from practice is consistent: the man who attempts the traumatic material without support generally surfaces it and then withdraws, which produces the position described in Chapter 9 and frequently the conclusion that the work does not help.

::: keypoint
The wounds men carry fall into ten categories: childhood, violence, humiliation, betrayal, loss, failure, the position, the household, the body, and the man's whole account of himself. The inventory's function is to identify what is still operating, which categories determine the work, the sequence in which the work proceeds, and what requires a professional. A wound that is not operating does not require work, and a man should not conduct an inventory of his history in order to produce a conclusion about himself.
:::
