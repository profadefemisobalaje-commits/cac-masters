## 21.6 Becoming the Man You Want to Be

The final substantive section converts the preceding material into a construction: not the reduction of a gap but the building of a person.

### The Problem With the Image as a Goal

A man who attempts to become the man his image promised will fail, for a structural reason: the image was assembled for public legibility and it contains other people's requirements. Its standard is comparative, its timetable is borrowed, and its criteria are the six requirements described in Chapter 2. A man pursuing it will be conducted for a lifetime by an assessment he did not write.

The alternative is not the abandonment of ambition. It is the substitution of a self-authored specification, which is the work described in Chapter 11, conducted here with respect to the man's person rather than his position.

### The Specification

The specification has five parts and is written in one sitting of about two hours.

**Part One: What I will not do.** The negative specification, which is the most useful part and the one most frequently omitted. A list of the conduct the man will not undertake, in specific terms: the ways he will not treat his household, the matters on which he will not compromise, the conduct in his work he will not undertake, the arrangements he will not conceal. The negative specification has the advantage of being entirely within the man's control, which distinguishes it from any objective that depends on circumstance.

**Part Two: The capacities I intend to hold.** Not achievements but capacities: the ability to describe his own state; the ability to obtain assistance; the ability to remain in a relationship in which he is known; the ability to keep himself functioning over a decade. The four unrecognised capacities from Table 2.1 form the core, and additions should be specific to the man's life.

**Part Three: The relationships I intend to have, and what I will do in them.** Specific: the arrangement with each child, the arrangement with a partner, the number of friendships he will maintain and their form, the participation in the family of origin, the contribution to a community. Stated as arrangements rather than as wishes.

**Part Four: The work and the provision.** What the man intends to do for income, at what intensity, for how long, and with what limits. This part should state the ceiling and the boundary from Chapter 11, and it should state the transition: what the man intends to be doing in the decade after full working life.

**Part Five: The record I intend to leave.** The material of Section 17.6, applied to the man's whole life: what he intends to be known for, by whom, and in what form. Writing this part has a specific effect that men report with surprise: it makes the previous four parts easier to write, because it establishes the standard against which the arrangements are being chosen.

### The Three Practices That Convert the Specification Into a Man

**1. The daily sentence.** One sentence, written at the end of each day, on how the day was conducted with respect to one element of the specification. Not a diary and not an assessment; one sentence. The practice operates in the manner of the measurements elsewhere in this book: it makes the specification visible daily, and its effect is disproportionate to its size.

**2. The monthly review.** Thirty minutes, on a fixed date, against four questions: which part of the specification did I keep; which did I fail; what caused the failure; what is the one change for the coming month? The review converts the specification from a document into an arrangement.

**3. The annual account.** Two hours, once a year, in which the man writes an account of the year as he would wish somebody who knew him to be able to give it. The account is not for publication and it is not an assessment. Its function is the one identified throughout Part VII and Part XI of this book: a man who writes down what his year contained is in a position to notice what it did not contain.

### The Relationship Between the Specification and the Household

Two observations are important.

First, the specification should be known by at least one person, and preferably by the man's household. A specification held privately is a plan; a specification known by the household is an arrangement, and the difference in outcome is substantial, as the case below demonstrates.

Second, the specification will conflict with other people's expectations in at least one respect. The conflict should be expected and should not be treated as evidence of error. A man who has written a specification that requires no change in his arrangements has written a description rather than a specification.

## 21.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Was Two Men

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in counselling practice and in the literature on shame and male identity.

**Kunle is forty-eight, a senior lecturer at a polytechnic, married to Folake for nineteen years, with three children.**

The case was presented in counselling for insomnia and a general loss of interest, and the material that emerged concerned a discrepancy that had been maintained for twenty-two years.

Kunle's public position was as follows: he held a doctorate, a senior lectureship, a position on two professional committees, and the respect of his department; he had supervised eleven postgraduate students; he was his extended family's principal representative; and he was known as a calm, principled, and highly competent man. His household was well regarded and his three children attended good schools.

Kunle's private account contained four items, none of which had been shared with anybody.

First, his doctorate had been obtained at a private institution of modest standing, at a period when his first choice had been unaffordable; his thesis had been supervised at a distance and had been completed with limited support. He had, at the time, described the situation to colleagues in terms that omitted the institution's standing, and the omission had never been corrected. Over twenty years, the omission had been maintained through a series of small avoidances: a reference to "my university" rather than its name; an unwillingness to discuss the periods of study; a decline of two invitations to speak at events where the question would arise.

Second, he had, in his first year of teaching, obtained a position for which another candidate was better qualified. The appointment had been determined by a family connection, and Kunle had not been told about the connection until after the appointment. He had accepted the position, had performed well in it, had applied for nothing further through connections, and had regarded his own career since then as an attempt at restitution.

Third, his second child, aged sixteen, had a health condition — a diagnosis of epilepsy made three years earlier — which the household had managed carefully and which Kunle had not disclosed to his extended family, his department, or any friend. The concealment had required a continuing series of arrangements: the child had been withdrawn from a swimming programme, his absences had been explained in other terms, and the household had declined two invitations that would have required the disclosure of the diagnosis.

Fourth, Kunle had lost interest in his subject. He had entered the discipline with enthusiasm at twenty-two and had, over two decades, become an administrator of a subject rather than a student of it. He had published nothing in four years, and he described his lecturing as competent and empty. He had not told his wife, and he had presented the loss of interest as workload.

The insomnia and the loss of interest were assessed as a depressive episode, which was treated. The four items were then worked through in the manner described in Sections 21.3 and 21.5.

The doctorate was addressed first, and it produced the case's most instructive finding. Kunle disclosed the matter to a colleague of fifteen years' standing, in the structure of Section 21.3, and the colleague's response was that he had known the institution for years, that it had never occurred to him to regard it as significant, and that two other members of the department had completed their postgraduate work at institutions of comparable or lower standing. The disclosure took about four minutes. Kunle recorded, in his own words, that what he had carried for twenty-two years was *a fact that four thousand words of other people's biographies also contain and that nobody but me had ever cared about*.

The position obtained through a family connection was disclosed to his wife, who had not known, and was resolved by a specific decision: Kunle placed his name on a professional register of available assessors, in which appointments were made by open procedure, and has since taken two such appointments.

The child's diagnosis was disclosed to the extended family and, in the form appropriate to the child's interest, to the department. Its effects were entirely practical: two relatives with medical knowledge were identified, the swimming concern was resolved through a supervised arrangement, and Kunle reported that the item had occupied a position in his account that its actual weight did not support.

The loss of interest was the last item and the most consequential. Its resolution required two things: an admission to Folake, which was received with the observation that she had known for about three years, and a change in the arrangement of his work. Kunle reduced his administrative commitments by one role, negotiated a reduction of his teaching load, and took on a research collaboration with a colleague in another institution with the specific requirement that he write rather than administer.

His summary, two years later, was: *I was two men. The one in the department was competent, principled, and didn't care about any of it, and the one at home was ashamed of four things, three of them invisible to anybody but me. The four things took about a year to work through, and the largest of them was not the doctorate or the appointment. It was that I had stopped being interested in anything and had decided that this was what a man of my age is.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**Three of the four items were invisible to everybody, and their weight was maintained entirely by the man.** The doctorate, the appointment, and the loss of interest had no external consequence. What gave them their force was the belief in exposure: that the disclosure would end the man's standing. The disclosure's outcome — a colleague's four-minute response — is the mechanism of Section 21.3 demonstrated in a single exchange.

**The concealments required continuing arrangements.** The avoidances, the withdrawals, the declined invitations, and the terms used to refer to the institution. This is the maintenance cost described in Section 21.1, and it is the reason that concealed material is expensive even when it is never discovered.

**The child's diagnosis was being carried as shame rather than as care.** Kunle's management of his son's condition was competent, and it was conducted inside a framework of concealment that belonged to the man's account rather than to the child's welfare. The case is included because this pattern is common: a household's legitimate privacy about a child's condition frequently merges into the man's private account of his own inadequacy.

**The wife had known about the loss of interest for three years.** The most frequently reported finding in cases of this kind: the material that the man believes is concealed is frequently visible to the people who live with him, and what is concealed is the man's acknowledgement of it. Chapter 14's material on the partner who cannot be told is the corresponding subject.

**The final item required an arrangement rather than a disclosure.** The loss of interest was resolved by reducing an administrative role, negotiating a teaching load, and entering a collaboration. This is the pattern in Section 21.6: the specification produces arrangements, and the arrangements produce the man.
