## 31.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is this not simply the price of the position? Everybody who leads is alone."**

The isolation is general and the price is not fixed. The arrangements in Section 31.5 are used by leaders in every sector, and the men who use them report specific differences in their judgement, their health, and their households. The proposition that leadership requires total isolation is a description of the current arrangement rather than a law, and its practical consequence is the degradation of the judgement of the person responsible for the outcomes of everybody in the organisation.

**"I cannot tell anybody anything. Anything I say will get out."**

The concern is legitimate in a small market or a small town, and it is the reason that Arrangements One and Two are constructed as they are: a peer group whose members are outside the man's own market, with a rule of confidentiality; and a professional arrangement whose confidentiality is an obligation rather than a favour. Where the man can obtain neither, the question to be answered is what the alternative costs: the Chapter 27.3 material on judgement applies, and the man's decision-making is being conducted without correction.

**"I do not have time for a group and a counsellor and a boundary."**

The arrangements take less time than the man's current position consumes in managing the consequences of his condition: the deferred decisions, the meetings cancelled, the episodes of illness, the household's crisis, the matter that was not raised with him for two years. The case material is consistent on the arithmetic: the arrangements consume a few hours a month and the condition consumes the man's evenings, his health, and frequently a year of his work.

**"My household does not need to know about my work."**

The household does not require the content. The arrangement in Section 31.5 is the disclosure of the fact: the load, what the man is doing about it, and what he requires. The specific finding from men who have conducted it is that the household's alternative explanation for the man's state is generally worse than the truth, and that the disclosure alters their conduct toward him in the direction he requires.

**"I have been leading for twenty years and I am not in difficulty."**

Then the arrangements that apply are the ones that keep it that way: the peer group, the retrieved information, the boundary specification, the place where the man is not the leader, and the household arrangement. The specific value for a man who is not in difficulty is the calibration in Arrangement Three: the information about his own conduct that nobody in his organisation will supply. Men who have operated the practice over years report that it is the measure that has prevented the position from altering them without their noticing.

**"I think I am at the end of it. What do I do first?"**

Apply Section 31.4's ninth presentation and treat it as an emergency: obtain a professional assessment now, and do not conduct the matter privately. Then, in the order in which the case material reports them as most useful: the professional arrangement, since it is the earliest available; the household arrangement, since the household is already affected; the boundary, since the position will not reduce itself; and the peer arrangement, which requires more time to construct. The chapter's position on the ninth presentation should be recorded plainly: the conclusion that a man cannot continue, held in isolation by a man whose judgement has been degraded by that same isolation, is the most dangerous configuration in this book, and it requires contact with a professional rather than further private deliberation.

::: summary
**Chapter 31 in brief.** Every arrangement of authority separates the man who holds it from the people subject to it, and three structural effects operate: the boundary of rank, through which his words, moods, intimacies, dependence, self-management, and history acquire a weight determined by the hierarchy; the information asymmetry, through which he receives selected information, is not told the matter that most concerns him, and is not informed of his own state by anybody; and the position as the container of other people's anxiety, in which the group's uncertainty is deposited and none of the man's own material is returned anywhere.

The isolation takes five forms: the man with nobody above him, whose judgement is therefore unbounded; the man whose nearest peer is his competitor; the man promoted from the group; the man in the small setting without a professional community; and the man whose position is not recognised as leadership at all. The position makes four things impossible: complaint in the ordinary direction, the visible expression of uncertainty, reciprocal friendship, and the man's own state being available to him.

The load consists of five burdens, of which the first four are the decision burden, the example burden, the morale burden, and the boundary burden, and the fifth — carried by many leaders and rarely named — is the accumulation of other people's personal material, brought to the man because he has the standing to act and left with him because there is no arrangement in which his own position concerning it can be held. Carrying the load alone has two consequences: the degradation of judgement, which affects the people the man is responsible for, and the accumulation of the material in his household and his body.

The presentations of a leader who has nothing left are exhaustion that sleep does not correct, decision avoidance, intolerance of other people's material, cynicism, the loss of the position's meaning, the performance of performance, the body, the conduct at home, and a privately reached conclusion that he cannot continue. The final item is the most serious configuration in this book and requires professional assessment rather than private deliberation.

Six arrangements allow a man to hold authority without being consumed by it: the peer arrangement of equivalent leaders with a rule of confidentiality; the professional arrangement outside the reporting line; the five questions asked monthly of named people to retrieve the information the organisation withholds; the boundary written, dated, and given to somebody who will notice; the place where the man is not the leader and refuses office; and the household arrangement, in which the man's spouse and children know the fact of the load, what he is doing about it, and what he requires. Finally, a leadership arrangement must not be transferred into a household: the reporting line, the asymmetry, the emotional discharge, and the standard all produce a household that functions and whose members are not known to the man, and the correction is the separation of the two positions, the renegotiation of the household in its own terms, and the supply of the man's own position to the people who live with him.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the confidences list in Part One, Section B. How many matters are you holding that were brought to you by other people, and who holds your own?
2. From Section 31.1's second effect: what have you learned in the last year about your own conduct from somebody else, and who would tell you if you had altered in a way that mattered?
3. Which of the nine presentations in Section 31.4 are present in your case? Which has your household noticed, and what have they concluded about it?
4. Which of the five forms of isolation in Section 31.2 describes your position, and what does it make impossible in your arrangements?
5. Construct the five questions from Arrangement Three and name two people you will ask them. What do you expect the first month's answers to contain?
6. Write the boundary specification. Which item in it is currently being violated, and by whom?
7. Answer the five questions in Part Four about your household. What does your household do differently when you are not in the house?

## Further Reading

- **On leadership and isolation:** the literature on the loneliness of leadership, including the findings on the structural sources of leader isolation, the effects of isolation on leader judgement and wellbeing, and the practices that leaders use to obtain candid feedback.
- **On decision fatigue:** the research on decision fatigue and on the depletion of judgement across a working day, including the findings on the conditions under which judgement is preserved and the practical routines that have been developed in professions where the consequences of poor decisions are severe.
- **On executive and leadership coaching, mentoring, and supervision:** the practice literature on the arrangements available to men in positions of authority, including the evidence on their use, the conditions under which they are effective, and the confidentiality obligations that govern them.
- **On leader wellbeing and burnout:** the research on burnout in leaders and in managers, including the findings on the association between leadership responsibility and psychological strain, the evidence on exhaustion, cynicism, and the loss of meaning as its components, and the risk factors that predict more serious outcomes.
- **On family business and on the household of the leader:** the literature on family dynamics in which one member holds the principal authority, including the findings on how leadership conduct transfers into household arrangements and its effects on spouses and on children.
- **Practical resources:** peer groups and professional associations for leaders; executive coaching, mentoring, and counselling services with defined confidentiality; occupational health services; family and relationship counselling; and the material in Appendices I, J, N, and O.
