## 12.4 Workplace Stress and Chronic Overwork

Chronic overwork is frequently treated as a personal choice, and it is frequently produced by conditions the individual man does not control. This section separates the two, because the interventions differ.

### The Organisational Conditions

Six conditions account for most chronic overwork among men in employment. A man who examines this list will usually find that his hours are produced by at least four of them.

**Understaffing.** The work of the establishment is distributed among fewer people than the establishment was designed for. Every reduction in headcount increases the load on those who remain, and the increase is rarely accompanied by a reduction in the volume of work.

**Insecure employment.** Where employment is casual, contractual, or renewable at short intervals, availability becomes a condition of continued employment. Men in this position accept hours they cannot sustain, because declining is read as unwillingness.

**Performance measurement.** Where performance is assessed by output, hours, or availability, the rational response is to increase them. Where the measurement is continuous and visible — the dashboard, the ranking, the target — the response continues indefinitely.

**The availability expectation.** Digital communication has removed the boundary between working and non-working hours. The expectation that a man will respond in the evening, at the weekend, and on leave is now established in many organisations, and it is frequently unwritten and therefore unnegotiable.

**The promotion structure.** Where advancement depends on visibility and availability, the men who reduce their hours remove themselves from consideration. This is a rational assessment of many organisations, and it means that the reduction of hours carries a career cost that the man must weigh rather than one that he can avoid.

**Supervision by absence.** Many managers, themselves overloaded, supervise by exception: they intervene when something is late, which means that the overloaded man receives attention only for failure, and receives no attention at all for the arrangement that is producing the failure.

### The Personal Conditions

Four personal conditions compound the organisational ones, and these are the ones within the man's control.

**Inability to delegate.** Described in Chapter 7. A man who requires work to be done to his own standard will perform the work of three people and will therefore be given it.

**Acceptance of every request.** A man who has never declined a request will be given more requests, because he is the reliable option. The reliability that earns respect also generates the load.

**The substitution of work for other parts of life.** Work is frequently the least complicated domain: it has defined tasks, measurable outcomes, and clear feedback. A man whose marriage has become difficult, whose children have withdrawn, or whose friendships have thinned may find work the more comfortable place, and will therefore spend more time there. The overwork is not the cause of the difficulty elsewhere; it is frequently the response to it.

**Financial commitments requiring continuous high earnings.** Described in Chapters 9 and 10. Where the household's standard and the family's obligations are calibrated to peak earnings, every reduction in hours produces an immediate financial consequence.

### The Effects on Performance

Chronic overwork is frequently justified by its results, and the justification is partly accurate for short periods and inaccurate for long ones. The research on working hours points to a consistent pattern: output per hour falls as hours rise; error rates increase; and the additional hours produce diminishing and eventually negative returns. In addition, the effects on judgement are the ones that matter most for men in responsible positions: reduced capacity for complex reasoning, increased reliance on quick and familiar responses, reduced tolerance for disagreement, and a reduced capacity to recognise one's own deterioration.

Two specific effects are worth naming because they affect the reader's own decisions about the quantity of work he accepts.

**The unrecognised deterioration of judgement.** A man in the fourth month of sustained overwork does not experience himself as impaired; he experiences the work as harder and his colleagues as less competent. The assessment is impaired, and the impairment is invisible to the person making it.

**Accumulated error.** In occupations with high consequences, the research on fatigue shows a marked rise in error rates and in the severity of errors after prolonged hours. This is why the industries with the largest risks — aviation, medicine, heavy transport, and process industries — impose limits on working hours, and it is why men in other fields should impose their own.

### The Effects on the Household

The effects of chronic overwork on a man's household are frequently more severe than its effects on his work, and they are systematically under-reported because the household cannot make a complaint in the way that an employer can.

**Financial volatility produced by health.** The income argument for overwork assumes continuous capacity. Where the overwork produces an illness, an injury, or a hospitalisation, the household's income stops abruptly while its expenditure rises, and the reserve that would have covered the interruption has not been built, because the earnings were absorbed by obligations. The household that was said to depend on the man's income is therefore most exposed precisely at the moment the income ceases.

**The transfer of strain to the people at home.** A man operating at his limit arrives home with nothing left for regulation. Irritability, impatience, and silence are the registered effects, and they are directed at the people who are least responsible for the pressure. In the accounts of wives and adult children, this is the most consistently reported feature of a man's overworking years, and it is frequently remembered long after the economic circumstances have improved.

**The absence of the father at the years that cannot be repeated.** The father's availability matters most in the years of the children's childhood and adolescence, which is precisely the period in which men work hardest. The absence is rarely experienced by the father as a loss at the time, because there is nothing with which to compare it, and it is very frequently experienced as a loss in later life, when the children are adults and the relationship is one of politeness rather than intimacy. Chapter 17 addresses this in detail.

**The modelling of a pattern.** Children learn from what they observe. A son who sees his father's arrangement as the definition of a man's life will reproduce it, and a daughter who sees it will expect it. This effect is the reason that several men, having completed the capacity audit in the exercise at the end of this chapter, identify their own fathers with more accuracy than they had managed in thirty years.

### Practical Measures

Five measures reduce the level of chronic overwork, and they are ordered by the size of the effect.

**Establish a ceiling on hours, and measure it.** A man who does not know how many hours he works each week cannot reduce them. Two weeks of honest recording usually produces a figure that the man himself does not believe.

**Reduce in one category rather than across the board.** Choose one: the weekend work, the evening communication, the additional engagements, or the travel. A general intention to reduce produces nothing; the removal of a single category produces a measurable effect and is defensible socially, because it can be explained as a decision rather than a withdrawal.

**Delegate one function that is currently yours.** Identify a function, not a task: the supervision of a particular process, the management of a particular client, the preparation of a particular report. Delegation of a function transfers the whole of the load associated with it; delegation of tasks transfers only the task.

**Manage the availability expectation explicitly.** A stated arrangement — *I do not respond after nine; if something is urgent, telephone* — is respected more often than men expect, and it is considerably more effective than an unstated reduction in responsiveness, which is read as unreliability.

**Address the personal conditions.** Only the reader can determine whether his overwork is chiefly organisational or personal, and the test is simple: if the organisation offers the arrangement and the man declines it, the condition is personal. The two personal conditions that most commonly maintain overwork are the inability to delegate and the substitution of work for other parts of life, and each has a corresponding section elsewhere in this book.

## 12.5 Balancing Ambition with Personal Well-Being

The word *balance* is frequently used and rarely defined, and the vagueness allows men to dismiss the whole subject. This section defines it precisely and offers a method.

### What Balance Is Not

Balance is not an equal division of hours, which is neither achievable nor desirable. It is not the absence of ambition, and it does not require a man to reduce his commitment or his standards. And it is not a permanent state; there are legitimate seasons of intense work: a business start-up, a professional qualification, a young family's early years, a crisis in the household.

### What Balance Is

Balance is the condition in which four things are simultaneously true.

1. **No essential domain is deteriorating.** Health, marriage, relationship with children, income, and obligations are each, at minimum, not worsening.
2. **Every domain receives a minimum allocation.** Not an equal share, but a defined minimum below which the domain is not permitted to fall: an hour a day with children, one evening a week with a partner, seven hours of sleep, three sessions of exercise, one friend contacted each week.
3. **The intensity has an end.** Intense periods are legitimate; permanent intensity is not. Each period of intensity should have a defined end point, after which the arrangement is reviewed.
4. **The arrangement is sustainable by the man himself.** Not by a version of him with more resources, better sleep, and no illness — by him, as he actually is.

### The Three Practical Mechanisms

**The minimum allocation register.** Write the minimum allocation for each domain, and review it weekly. A domain that has fallen below its minimum for two consecutive weeks is treated as a fault requiring correction, in the way that a shortfall in payments would be.

**The seasonal structure.** Arrange the year so that periods of intensity alternate with periods of recovery. A man who plans four intense months followed by a reduced month will manage it; a man who works at seventy hours every week of the year will not, and the difference is not the total hours but their distribution.

**The defined trade.** Where a reduction is genuinely impossible, the man should choose deliberately what will be reduced rather than allowing the reduction to be decided by whichever obligation shouts loudest. A defined trade — *I will work these hours for the next two years, and during that period I will not take on any community position, and my health checks will continue* — preserves control and, in the experience of men who use it, reduces the resentment that accompanies an undifferentiated overload.

### The Question of Well-Being for Men Who Distrust the Subject

Some men will object at this point, and the objection is legitimate: the language of well-being is frequently promoted by people whose circumstances are easier, and it is frequently used to recommend rest to men who cannot afford it. Three points answer the objection.

First, the material in this chapter is not primarily about comfort; it is about capacity. A man who sleeps six hours, never rests, and has not attended a clinic in four years does not merely feel worse than a man who does; he performs worse, makes more errors, and is more likely to be incapacitated at a moment he cannot predict. The argument is instrumental.

Second, every measure in this chapter is compatible with continued high performance. None of them requires a man to accept a lower standard or to abandon his obligations.

Third, the consequences are borne by other people. A father who collapses from untreated hypertension does not spare his household his illness; he delivers it to them at the worst possible moment, along with the loss of income, the medical costs, and the burden of care. This is the consideration that changes men's behaviour more often than any argument about their own welfare, and Chapter 17 develops it in the context of fatherhood.
