# The Silent Battles of Marriage {.chapter num="15" short="The Silent Battles of Marriage"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the hidden struggles that occur inside marriage, and identify those that are specific to men in long-term relationships.
2. Analyse the mechanisms by which a marriage becomes silent, including the role of accumulated grievance, avoidance, and the exchange of small decisions.
3. Identify the causes of marital dissatisfaction and distinguish between the dissatisfaction that can be repaired and that which cannot.
4. Apply a communication framework for serious disagreements, including the rules of engagement that prevent an ordinary conflict from producing permanent damage.
5. Assess the role of responsibility and sacrifice in a marriage, and identify the point at which sacrifice becomes injurious to both parties.
6. Recognise when professional help is required and evaluate the specific options, including counselling, structured programmes, and separation where appropriate.
:::

## Introduction

The battles in a marriage are not, for the most part, the battles that are visible. Visible conflicts — the argument about money, the disagreement about a child's school, the dispute about a relative's visit — are the occasions on which an underlying conflict becomes audible. The underlying conflict is usually older, less specific, and rarely named at all. It is a difference about what the marriage is for.

This is why so many men report that they do not understand what the problem is. They address the visible occasions one after another, competently and in good faith, and the marriage becomes no easier. They conclude that their wives are difficult, or that they themselves are inadequate, or that marriage is like this. What they have not identified is that the argument about the school fees was never about the school fees, and that no settlement of the school fees will end it.

The purpose of this chapter is to make the invisible conflict visible, and then to set out what can be done about it. Its approach is deliberately practical. It addresses five subjects: the hidden struggles that men carry inside marriage without disclosing; the mechanism by which a marriage becomes silent; the causes of dissatisfaction and their classification; the practical rules for conducting serious disagreement; and the question of sacrifice — specifically, how to tell the difference between the sacrifice that sustains a marriage and the sacrifice that quietly destroys one of its parties.

One general observation should be made at the outset, and it applies throughout. Most of what damages a marriage is not conflict. It is accumulation. A single disagreement about a mother's visit is a difficulty; the same disagreement occurring in the same unpromising form for eleven years is a structural feature of the relationship. What men and women describe when they finally speak to a counsellor is rarely a recent event; it is an account of a pattern, and the pattern's defining property is that it repeated.

## 15.1 Silent Struggles Inside Marriage

Seven specific struggles are carried inside marriages without being raised. Each is described below with the signs that indicate its presence.

### The Struggle Over Priority

The most common and the least discussed. A man is required to distribute himself between his wife, his children, his parents, his siblings, his work, and, in some cases, his community. He performs the distribution on the basis of obligation, and his wife assesses it on the basis of priority. Where the two assessments differ, the disagreement is rarely about any single instance. It is about the answer to a question that neither party asks: **who comes first?**

The signs: recurrent disputes about time; the wife's references to specific past occasions; the man's sense of being unappreciated despite substantial effort; the presence of a mother-in-law or sibling-in-law in every disagreement; and the wife's statement, delivered in one form or another, that she is not important to him.

The man's position in this struggle is genuinely difficult, because in many cultural settings the requirements are not parallel. Assistance to parents and siblings is frequently a moral obligation whose neglect carries public consequences, while attention to a wife is a private matter whose neglect carries none. A man who manages the public obligation correctly and the private one poorly is behaving rationally in the terms of his environment, and is producing a marriage in which his wife concludes, accurately, that she is not a priority. The resolution is not to abandon the obligations, and it is not to instruct the wife to accept less. It is to make the priority visible and specific, as Section 15.4 describes.

### The Struggle With Desire

Men and women frequently differ in the quantity, form, and meaning of sexual desire, and the difference is frequently managed rather than discussed. Its effects, when unmanaged, are recognisable: a prolonged reduction in physical intimacy; approaches that are declined and are then not repeated; resentment that is never expressed; the use of the television or separate bedtimes as an arrangement that avoids the possibility; and, in some marriages, a settled and jointly maintained fiction that the subject is unimportant.

Men rarely raise this subject directly, and there are three reasons: the fear that the request will confirm a pattern of rejection; the belief that raising it will produce compliance without desire, which is a worse outcome than the present one; and the absence of language for discussing the subject that is neither a complaint nor a demand. Chapter 21 addresses the subject specifically, including the marital difficulties that arise from sexual function problems that men conceal rather than treat.

### The Struggle Over Money and Standing

Chapter 9 established that men conceal financial hardship. In marriage, a second dimension is added: the assessment of what the household's money should be used for. Four recurring conflicts are identifiable.

**Between obligation and provision.** Whether available funds should be directed to extended family obligations or retained for the household's own security and standard. This is the single most common financial conflict in the marriages described in this book.

**Between the present and the future.** Whether the household should consume at the present level, which is frequently demanded by the environment, or save against a future that is not visible.

**Between control and participation.** Who decides, and how the decision is arrived at. Where one party decides and the other implements, the implementation is frequently accompanied by concealment and the concealment by a slow erosion of trust, in the pattern described in Chapter 9.

**Between provision and presence.** Where increased income requires absence, the household must choose, and the choice is frequently made by the man alone in the form of additional work, and evaluated by his wife on the basis of the absence rather than the income.

### The Struggle With Self-Worth Inside the Home

A specific and painful struggle, reported by men with some frequency: the man is assessed at home by a standard that differs from the one applying at work, and he is frequently found wanting. A man who has a position of respect in his workplace is a man whose household knows his failures, his irritability, his unavailability, and his difficulty in the specific matters that arise at home. The home is the one setting in which his professional standing is irrelevant.

The consequences are two, and they are mutually reinforcing: the man is more comfortable at work, where his competence is recognised, than at home, where it is not; and he therefore spends more time at work, which confirms the household's assessment. The arrangement is described in Chapter 12 as one of the personal conditions producing overwork, and it is one of the most reliable findings in the accounts of men who describe their marriages as unhappy.

### The Struggle With the Man's Own Family

Where a man's family of origin and his wife are in a settled, low-level conflict, the man occupies an impossible position: he is required to adjudicate, he is required not to adjudicate, and every position he takes is read as a betrayal by one party or the other. Chapter 26 addresses the specific dynamics. Within a marriage, the central difficulty is that the man frequently fails to recognise that his wife's complaint is not that his mother is unpleasant; it is that he does not defend her.

### The Struggle With Silence

The battle of Chapter 14, occurring inside a marriage that is otherwise functioning. Its presence is indicated by the following sign, which is diagnostic: **the man and his wife converse competently about logistics and rarely about anything else.** Logistics includes the children, the money, the schedule, the relatives, and the household's operational matters, and it is capable of filling many years. Its sufficiency as a subject is a measure of distance rather than of partnership.

### The Struggle With a Diminished Sense of the Marriage's Value

The seventh struggle is not with a subject but with a conclusion. Men in long marriages described as stable by their surroundings frequently carry a private assessment that the marriage has become an arrangement: housing, children, an economic partnership, and a division of labour, conducted efficiently and without anything else. The assessment is usually correct in some part and is frequently a description of a stage rather than of a verdict, since marriages do have periods of reduced intensity and these periods are not necessarily permanent. What distinguishes the men who recover from those who do not is, in the accounts available, whether the assessment was ever examined or simply accepted.

## 15.2 How Silence Takes Over a Marriage

The mechanism is gradual, and it operates through a series of individually rational decisions. It is worth setting out in sequence, because a man who recognises his own marriage in the sequence is generally able to identify the point at which the change can still be reversed.

**Stage One: the small decision not raised.** A minor dissatisfaction arises. It is not raised, correctly, because the cost of raising it exceeds its importance. This is normal and necessary; no marriage can process every annoyance.

**Stage Two: the small decision not raised because of the last time.** A second dissatisfaction arises, and it is not raised for a different reason: the last time something similar was raised, the conversation went badly. This is the point of departure, and it is usually invisible at the time.

**Stage Three: the accumulation of unraised items.** Over months, a set of unraised matters accumulates. Each is individually small. Together they constitute a position: the man's view of the distribution of time, effort, money, and consideration, which his wife does not hold and which he has not described.

**Stage Four: the reinterpretation of behaviour.** Because the unraised material is unknown to the wife, she interprets her husband's moods, absences, and irritabilities on the basis of the information available to her, which is incomplete. Her interpretations are frequently wrong, and the interpretations she most commonly supplies are the ones that are most damaging to the relationship, because the possibility that is most accessible to a person who is being treated with distance is the possibility of another person.

**Stage Five: the withdrawal of reciprocity.** Each partner reduces their investment. The wife stops raising some matters, for the reasons in Chapter 14. The man stops attending to some of the household's emotional requirements. Neither decides to stop; both respond to the reduced environment with reduced output, which is the ordinary logic of any exchange.

**Stage Six: the establishment of the arrangement.** The couple settles into a functional arrangement containing three properties: it is efficient, it is predictable, and it contains no intimacy. The man is comfortable with it, in the sense that it produces no conflict, and he frequently reports, accurately, that his marriage is peaceful.

**Stage Seven: the incident that appears to come from nowhere.** Some years later, an event occurs — a disclosure, a discovery, a remark made in public, an ultimatum, the appearance of a third party — that appears to be the beginning of the marriage's difficulty. It is not the beginning. It is the point at which an arrangement established at Stage Two becomes audible.

::: keypoint
The silent battle in a marriage is not the absence of conflict. It is the presence of an unexamined arrangement that has been maintained by the daily decision not to raise matters that are individually too small to raise. The remedy is not confrontation. It is a scheduled arrangement that raises the class of matters rather than the individual items.
:::
