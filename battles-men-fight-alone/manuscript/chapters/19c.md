## 19.5 Reclaiming the Lost Years

The constructive work of this chapter is conducted in two parts: what can be recovered, and what can be grieved. Men frequently attempt the first without the second, and the attempt is unstable, because the grief returns each time the recovery is incomplete.

### What Can Be Recovered

**1. The experience of being looked after.** The capacity to receive is not fixed in childhood, and it can be developed in adulthood, though it requires practice and a deliberate arrangement. The practical method, in the sequence in which men report success:

- **Choose one person and one specific form of care.** Not a general intention to accept help, but a specific arrangement: a meal prepared by somebody else, a journey in which the man is driven, a task performed on his behalf, a payment made for him. The form matters less than the fact that it is somebody else's contribution to his life.
- **State the difficulty.** Men in this pattern find receiving uncomfortable, and the discomfort is best named in advance. *This is difficult for me and I am going to do it anyway* is the sentence that converts the discomfort from an obstacle into a subject.
- **Record the outcome.** After several instances, the man should write what actually happened: what the other person's response was, what the cost was, and whether anything deteriorated. The record is usually decisive, because the anticipated cost of receiving — that it will create an obligation, that it will alter the relationship, that it will be resented — is systematically larger than the actual outcome.

**2. The practice of unstructured time.** The capacity for leisure is developed by scheduling it, which is a paradox that men in this pattern find workable. One activity per week that produces nothing, held for eight weeks, is the practical measure, and the activity should involve the hands or the body rather than a screen. Men commonly report that the first weeks produce discomfort and that the discomfort diminishes without disappearing entirely, and that the activity produces a specific effect that is worth describing: the restoration of an hour in which the man is not performing a function.

**3. The return to abandoned interests.** The interests that were discontinued between the ages of twelve and twenty are frequently still available: the sport, the instrument, the drawing, the reading, the making of things. Their return has a value beyond recreation, because they are the man's own in the sense described in Section 18.5: they were chosen by the boy for no reason connected with the household's requirements.

**4. The completion of interrupted education or training.** Where education was interrupted, its resumption in adulthood is frequently both possible and valuable, and men over forty complete qualifications and training at a considerable rate. The obstacle is not capacity but the arrangement: the time and the cost have to be found, and the justification for spending them on the man himself has to be constructed against a lifelong objection to self-expenditure.

**5. The acquisition of the ordinary competences that were never taught.** A specific and frequently overlooked item. Men who became responsible early frequently lack competences that were nobody's responsibility to teach: the ability to swim, to drive, to manage a bank account properly, to cook, to negotiate a salary, to handle a formal institution. The list is the man's own to compile, and the addressing of items on it produces a disproportionate improvement in confidence.

**6. The practice of failure without consequence.** The most valuable and the hardest to arrange. A child who was carrying consequences did not have the option of failing safely, and the adult competence at the man's disposal is frequently built on an over-developed avoidance of error. The measure is to take on one activity in which the man is a beginner and in which the consequences of failure are trivial: a league football match, a class, a public performance, a new business skill, the learning of a language in front of others. The specific and observable effect is a reduction in the fear of attempting unfamiliar things, and men report that this effect extends into more consequential domains.

### What Must Be Grieved

The second part of the work is the one men most frequently attempt to skip, and the skipping is the reason the recovery stalls.

The material to be grieved is specific, and it should be established in writing.

**The time that was spent working instead of being a child.** This is not an abstraction: it is the years, the seasons, the games not played, the friendships not formed, the school years whose detail the man cannot supply because he was not attending to it.

**The absence of a person to whom the man could be a child.** The most commonly reported and the most difficult item. Men in this pattern frequently describe the realisation, in middle age, that there was never a moment in their lives at which an adult was reliably managing their situation.

**The loss of the parental relationship in its protective form.** A parent who required care is not a parent who protects, and the loss is frequently grieved not at the parent's death but decades later, when the man observes another relationship between a man and his father and recognises what was absent.

**The version of himself that did not develop.** The bookish boy, the athletic one, the sociable one, the one with plans that had no relation to a household's requirements. Men frequently retain a precise memory of who they would have been, and the memory should be written down, because its content is frequently of use — it identifies the interests of Section 19.5 in their original form.

The grief work itself follows the pattern described in Chapter 13: naming the loss in writing, confirming which parts are permanent and which are addressable, and permitting the material to be present without converting it into a grievance against a person who was, in most of these cases, doing what they could with no better options.

### The Two Rules

**Rule One: recover what is available and do not use the recovery as evidence of what was taken.** Men in this pattern frequently experience each new capacity as a demonstration of the loss — football at fifty as a reminder that football was not played at fifteen. The rule is to hold the two apart: the loss is real and permanent, and the recovery is real and worthwhile, and neither reduces the other.

**Rule Two: do not require the recovery to be understood by anybody.** The wife, the siblings, and the surviving parent frequently do not understand the work, and their incomprehension is frequently expressed as amusement or as a suggestion that the man is behaving childishly. The work is the man's own, and it proceeds better without the requirement of anybody's approval, particularly the approval of the people whose arrangements benefited from the original sacrifice.

## 19.6 Healing the Boy Inside the Man

The final substantive section addresses the material that this chapter's title names, and it is written with a specific caution. The phrase *the boy inside the man* is used in popular writing in a way that is frequently unhelpful: it suggests a hidden child requiring rescue, or an unaddressed injury requiring catharsis. What is described in this section is different, more modest, and more useful.

### What the Material Actually Is

The material consists of three things, each of which is identifiable and each of which is addressable.

**An expectation of the man from himself.** The sentences that were formed in the period in question and have operated ever since: *If I do not do it, nobody will.* *I must not add to the load.* *I cannot rely on anybody.* *They are depending on me and I must not fail.* These are not memories; they are current operating instructions, and they are frequently the reason a man in his fifties cannot rest.

**An unaddressed requirement.** The requirement the boy had and did not present: to be noticed, to be helped, to be allowed to fail, to be looked after, to be told that things would be managed. It persists in adult life in the form of the reception problem in Section 19.5 and in the form of a specific and recurring pattern in marriages: the man who cannot tell his wife what he needs and who is disappointed when she does not supply it.

**A version of the self that is not the function.** The interests, temperament, and dispositions of the boy, which are frequently still available and which are frequently the material from which the identity work of Chapters 18 and 24 is conducted.

### The Four Steps

**Step One: name the instructions.** Write the sentences that operate in you, in the form in which you say them to yourself. Then test each one for accuracy and current applicability: is the sentence true now, and was it true then? The sentences frequently survive their circumstances by decades: a man of fifty-five operating on an instruction formed at fourteen, in a household that ceased to exist in 1989.

**Step Two: revise them into workable forms.** The instruction *if I do not do it, nobody will* becomes *if I do not do it, somebody else will do it differently, and that is acceptable*. The instruction *I must not add to the load* becomes *I am permitted to require something of the people who require things of me*. The revision is not an affirmation; it is an accurate statement about the man's present circumstances, and it is written down and referred to, in the form described in Chapter 8.

**Step Three: state the requirement.** The step that requires the most courage, and the one that produces the most change. The man identifies one requirement he has carried without presenting, and states it to the person from whom it is due: a sibling, a parent, a wife, a relative. The statement should be specific, should not be framed as an accusation, and should not require a response: *I was fourteen when I started carrying the household, and I have continued to carry it, and I am telling you that because nobody has ever been told.* Men report that the statement's effect on themselves is considerably larger than its effect on the listener.

**Step Four: give the boy something he did not have.** The final step, and the one that makes the others durable. The measures in Section 19.5 constitute the giving: the activity, the interest, the competence, the received care, the safe failure. Men frequently attach a specific plan to this step — the driving lessons, the swimming, the completion of the qualification, the football — and it should be specific, dated, and small enough to complete.

### When Professional Support Is Indicated

Two indications are worth stating. Where the material is connected to a specific form of abuse or to a household containing violence or addiction, the work described in this chapter is not a substitute for professional treatment, and Appendix B and Chapter 37's guidance applies. And where the material has produced a depressive condition, an anxiety disorder, or a pattern of alcohol use — all of which occur at elevated rates in men who became responsible early — treatment should precede the reflective work, because a man who is depressed will conduct this exercise as an additional account of his own inadequacy.
