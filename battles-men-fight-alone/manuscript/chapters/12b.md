## 12.2 The Fear of Losing Income

The fear of losing income is the engine of most of the behaviour described in this chapter, and it deserves to be examined as a specific fear rather than as a general anxiety. The examination has two parts: what would actually happen, and what the man believes would happen. The gap between the two is frequently substantial, and it is the space in which the man's remaining freedom lies.

### The Actual Consequences

For a household with a defined set of obligations, a reduction in income has five consequences, and they arrive in a specific order.

**Immediate.** The obligations that are legally or socially enforced at short intervals — rent, transport, food, school fees at the start of a term, loan instalments — are affected first, and the household's capacity to meet them falls within weeks.

**Short-term.** The household enters a period of adjustment: expenditure reduction, borrowing, the use of reserves if any exist, and the deferral of non-urgent costs, including medical treatment.

**Medium-term.** Assets are consumed or sold, credit standing deteriorates, and the standard of living is revised downward. This is the period in which the household's structure changes.

**Long-term.** The consequences depend on the recovery of income. If income is restored within a period that the household's assets and credit can absorb, the consequence is a period of difficulty followed by restoration. If it is not restored, the household enters a different trajectory, in which the children's education, the couple's security, and the man's health are permanently affected.

**The secondary consequences.** It is the secondary consequences that the fear of losing income is actually about, and they are not primarily financial: the loss of standing, the questions from relatives, the revision of the family's position in the community, the change in the household's view of the man, and, in some cases, the loss of the marriage.

### The Systematic Overestimation

Research and practice both indicate that the anticipated consequences of income loss are systematically overestimated, for four reasons.

**The imagining is done at the point of maximum fear.** A man contemplating the loss of income normally does so in a state of fear, and fear produces catastrophising — the attaching of total consequences to specific risks, as described in Chapter 8. The imagined outcome is therefore the worst available scenario: the loss of the house, the withdrawal of the children from school, the end of the marriage, and permanent disgrace.

**The household is more capable than the man assumes.** Families adjust. Expenditure falls, work is taken on by other members, the relatives who are asked frequently assist, and the school that is approached about fees frequently negotiates. Men who have been through a genuine reduction in income report, with remarkable consistency, that the household's response was materially better than they had feared.

**The social consequences are overestimated in the short run and underestimated in the long run.** In the short run, the community is less attentive to a man's position than he believes; most people are occupied with their own. In the long run, the consequence of a man's handling of his difficulty — with honesty or with deceit, with effort or with an attempt to conceal — matters considerably more than the difficulty itself.

**The fear ignores the alternative.** A man who continues working to the point of collapse does not avoid the loss of income; he defers it and increases its probability, and he arranges for it to arrive at a moment when he cannot control its timing and has less capacity to respond.

### The Real Question About Income Loss

The discussion above does not establish that the loss of income is harmless. For many households it is a serious event, and this book does not minimise it. What it establishes is that the relevant question is not *how do I avoid any reduction in income* but a set of more specific and answerable questions.

1. **What is the smallest reduction that would make the work sustainable?** Not a change of career, and not a sabbatical. One fewer engagement, one fewer weekend, one fewer additional role, or a reduction of hours from eighty to sixty-five.
2. **How long can the household sustain a reduction of that size?** Here the reserve calculation from Chapter 10 and Appendix D is decisive. A man who knows that he has four months of capacity has a materially different range of options from a man who has none.
3. **What is the earliest point at which the arrangement can be changed?** Not *never*, which is the answer men usually give, but a date: after the fees are paid, at the end of the contract, in the next quarter, when the eldest completes school.
4. **What is the household's position if I become unable to work for three months?** This is the question that converts the fear into a planning exercise, because it produces a specific gap that can be addressed by insurance, by a reserve, by a sibling arrangement, or by a reduction of obligations.

### The Preparation That Reduces the Fear

Fear of income loss is reduced more effectively by preparation than by reassurance, and the preparation is concrete. Four items address the majority of the anxiety.

**A written statement of the household's essential monthly requirement.** The distinction between the household's essential requirement — food, housing, utilities, transport to work, school fees, medical costs, and debt instalments — and its current total expenditure is usually substantial. A man who knows his essential figure has a far more accurate picture of the risk attached to income loss than one who knows only his total spending.

**A named duration of the reserve.** A man should be able to say: at the present rate of essential expenditure, the household can sustain X months with no income. Most men cannot answer, and the inability is itself a source of the fear, because an unknown quantity of danger is always experienced as larger than a known one. Appendix D provides the calculation and the building plan.

**A defined set of response steps.** What will be done, in what order, if income stops: which expenditures stop first, which obligations are renegotiated, which relatives and institutions are approached, who else in the household can earn, which assets are available. A man who has written these steps has already made the decisions in a calm state, and will not have to make them in a frightened one.

**A reduced sensitivity through diversification.** Where a household's income derives entirely from one source, the risk is concentrated and the fear is proportionate. Where a secondary source exists — a partner's earnings, a small side activity, a skill that can be sold, an asset that produces income — the fear is materially reduced, not because the sums are large but because there is an alternative. Chapter 23 addresses the practical construction of a second source.

### The Fear of Being Seen to Have Declined

A subset of the fear of losing income concerns not the money but the appearance: the vehicle, the housing, the contributions, and the school attended by the children. Where a man's obligations have been maintained to preserve appearance, he is not merely worried about income; he is worried about exposure. Two observations are useful.

First, exposure is generally survivable. Men who have reduced their standard visibly report that the social consequences were less severe and shorter-lived than anticipated, that the number of people who remarked on it was small, and that the remarks were less damaging than the anticipation. Second, exposure is not avoided by concealment; it is deferred and enlarged, in the manner described throughout Part Three.

## 12.3 When Rest Feels Like Irresponsibility

Men in this pattern do not, in most cases, fail to rest because they do not want rest. They fail to rest because rest has acquired a moral meaning. The purpose of this section is to describe that meaning, trace it, and set out how a man can recover the capacity to rest.

### The Moral Structure of Rest

For many men, rest is not neutral. It carries a verdict. Four elements compose the structure.

**Rest as evidence of insufficiency.** If the obligations require continuous work, then a man who rests is not meeting them, and the shortfall is his. Rest therefore becomes a demonstration of inadequacy rather than a requirement of the body.

**Rest as vulnerability.** A man who is resting is not producing, and a man who is not producing is, in his own assessment, replaceable. This element is particularly strong in employment and in competitive business, and it is frequently supported by the actual behaviour of organisations, which reward availability and punish absence.

**Rest as betrayal of others.** Where a man's work supports a household and an extended network, a day of rest has a cost that is borne by somebody else. Men describe this with precision: *If I rest today, someone's fees will not be paid on Friday.* The reasoning is usually inaccurate in the specific case and accurate as a general description of a structural problem, which is precisely why it is so difficult to argue against.

**Rest as the beginning of collapse.** The fourth element is the most significant and the least articulated. Men who have been holding a system together by continuous effort frequently fear that if they stop, they will not start again. The fear is not of laziness but of discovering how tired they are. This is a real and important fear, and it deserves to be answered rather than dismissed: the experience of most men who rest after prolonged overwork is that they sleep for an unusual amount of time, feel considerably worse for two or three days, and then recover a level of capacity they had forgotten was available.

### Where the Structure Comes From

Three sources account for most of it. The first is direct training: many of the men reading this book were raised in households where idleness was identified with moral failure, and where an adult man who rested during working hours was spoken about in terms of criticism. The second is economic reality: for a man whose income depends on presence and output, resting is a genuine reduction in income, and the association between rest and loss is not a belief but an experience. The third is the male prohibition on need, described in Chapter 2, which converts a physical requirement into a character question.

It is worth noting that the moral structure of rest is not universal. In many societies, and in the earlier lives of many men reading this chapter, rest was structured, expected, and communal: the day of rest, the afternoon pause, the seasonal lull, the evening spent sitting with others. What has changed is not human physiology but the arrangement of work, and the change has been absorbed by men who believe that the loss of rest is their own failing rather than a change in their conditions.

### Restoring Rest

Five practical measures have a good record. They are listed in the order in which men usually find them workable.

**Schedule rest rather than waiting for it.** Unscheduled rest does not occur. Two hours on a fixed evening, half a day on a fixed weekend, and, where possible, one full day a month should be entered into the calendar as commitments of equal standing to any appointment. The scheduling matters more than the amount.

**Define rest as maintenance in the language you use.** *I am maintaining my capacity to work* is a statement that a man can hold against the prohibition, because it is true and because it is expressed in terms he respects. Chapter 2 introduced the distinction; this is its practical application.

**Protect sleep first.** Where only one intervention is possible, sleep is the one with the largest effect. A man sleeping five hours who restores seven will recover capacity in every domain more effectively than by any other single measure. Appendix N provides the practical method.

**Make one day non-negotiable.** A single scheduled item — a weekly football match, a Saturday morning with children, attendance at a religious service, an evening walk with a friend, a fixed dinner with the family — that is not cancelled for work, and that functions as evidence that the boundary exists.

**Explain the change before making it, in outcome terms.** A man who tells his household and his colleagues what he is doing and why — *I am cutting my Saturday work because I was making mistakes and my doctor is concerned about my blood pressure* — reduces the social cost of the change substantially, and, in the author's experience, the explanation is accepted more readily than the man expects.

### When Rest Is Not Enough

A caution is required. Where a man's exhaustion has developed into a depressive episode, an anxiety disorder, a physical condition requiring treatment, or dependence on alcohol or sedatives, rest alone will not correct it, and the delay involved in attempting rest alone is costly. The criteria in Chapters 5 and 6 and in Appendix B should be applied, and professional assessment obtained where they indicate it.
