## 24.8 Practical Exercise: The Role Inventory and the Reconstruction Plan

This exercise takes about three hours across four weeks. It is written for a man who has experienced a loss of role, whether recently or years ago, and for a man who wishes to establish the protection before a loss occurs.

### Part One: The Role Inventory

| The role | What it supplies | Does it depend on a role I have lost, or may lose? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

Include every role: occupation, income, the household's decision-making, father, husband, son, brother, head of the family, community position, association membership, professional identity, mediator, and any other function you hold.

Then answer four questions.

- How many of the roles are function-based?
- What proportion of your identity is supplied by roles that could be removed by circumstances beyond your control?
- Which role would produce the largest loss, and what would remain?
- Which material in your life exists independently of every role on the list?

The fourth question is the diagnostic one, and for most men in this pattern the answer is very small. The finding is the purpose of the exercise.

### Part Two: The Functional Record

For two weeks, record the day in half-hour blocks: the activity, the purpose, and the state. Then answer:

- What proportion of the day is occupied by activity that I have chosen?
- What proportion is occupied by activity that has no purpose and has not been chosen (screen use, errands without requirement, the management of other people's affairs)?
- At what hour is the state at its worst, and what is happening at that hour?
- How many contacts did I have with people who know me, in the two weeks?

## 24.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I retired and I am fine. Does this apply to me?"**

Apply the diagnostic question from Part One: which material in your life exists independently of a role? If the answer includes an activity, a relationship, or a contribution that is yours and not attached to a function, the protection is present and the chapter is a description of what you already have. If the answer is empty, the condition has not yet appeared and the arrangements in this chapter are the prevention.

**"Is this not simply depression, and should I not just take the treatment?"**

The two frequently co-occur, and the treatment is required where the criteria in Chapter 6 are met. The treatment alone leaves the structural absence, and the accounts of men who have taken treatment without the reconstruction are consistent: the mood improves and the emptiness remains. The medical assessment, the treatment, and the reconstruction proceed together, in that order.

**"I am sixty-eight. Is it not too late to build something new?"**

The case in Section 24.7 began at sixty-three and the materials included a workshop and a monthly friendship. Men in their seventies establish arrangements of this kind, and the specific obstacle at that age is not capacity but the belief that the work is for younger men. The scale of the arrangement is what makes it possible: four items in a week, of a size that requires no permission and no capital.

**"My wife has her own routine and I am in the way."**

This is the most common domestic form of the condition, and its resolution requires two things. The man should establish arrangements that take him out of the house, which is the subject of this chapter, and the household's routine should be discussed explicitly, which is the material of Chapter 18. The specific practical measure that men report as effective is the transfer of one household function to the man in a form that the household wants transferred, rather than the assumption of functions that the household has been managing without him.

**"I lost my business and everything with it. Where do I start?"**

With the medical assessment and the functional record, because the condition after a business failure is frequently accompanied by a depressive episode and by a physical component that has gone unaddressed. Then with the grief work, which is required here in full, since the loss is a person-sized loss: the business was the man's construction and its failure is frequently carried as a verdict. The material in Chapters 16, 22, and 38 applies alongside this chapter.

**"What if I do not want a purpose? What if I want to rest?"**

Then that is the arrangement, and it should be made specific: what rest consists of, where, for how long, and with what structure. The condition described in this chapter is not produced by rest; it is produced by the absence of an account of what the man is. A man who has chosen rest, knows what it consists of, and has a structure around it is not in the empty season, and the distinction is generally visible within a fortnight.

::: summary
**Chapter 24 in brief.** A man's identity is assembled from five materials, in order of the weight most men give them: occupation, provision, the relationships within the roles, standing, and — as the smallest material for most men in this pattern — interests, capacities, and relationships outside the roles. The materials are all function-based, they are mutually dependent, they produce a life in which every hour is occupied, and for most men there is nothing outside them. The practical point is not that men should detach from their roles but that they should hold at least one material of identity that does not depend on the roles continuing.

The events that remove a man's roles are retirement, redundancy or the loss of a business, the children's departure, separation and divorce, bereavement, illness or injury, and migration or displacement. Each has a distinct signature: retirement removes the roles while leaving the capacity to occupy them; redundancy removes the role and supplies a verdict; the children's departure is ungrievable in public; separation removes the role and the standing; bereavement removes the person and the structure at once; illness removes the materials through the body and leaves no object for the anger; migration removes standing that is not transferable. Four features recur: the loss is dated; the roles disappear faster than the man's capacity; the loss is not socially recognised; and the man's own explanation is about something else.

The empty season presents without a name: the undifferentiated day, muted or absent pleasure, a loneliness that is the absence of a position rather than of company, irritability calibrated to a private account, an increase in state-managing behaviours, withdrawal, the sense of having been overtaken, and a diffuse anger with no object. The distinctions that matter are from depression, from grief, from ordinary transition, and from a physical condition — and the medical assessment should precede the reconstruction work in every case. Men make two characteristic errors in this period: the attempt to recover the lost role, and the attempt to fill the time.

Meaning cannot be supplied by achievement, for four reasons: achievement is comparative and meaning is not; achievement terminates and meaning does not; achievement is externally rewarded and meaning is not; and achievement requires capacity that reduces with age. The materials available for the reconstruction are skill transmission, organisations that require competent people, abandoned interests, neglected relationships, a practice, and the household's own arrangements.

The reconstruction proceeds in six stages: assessment, including the medical and functional assessments and the role inventory; grief, conducted in written form; the materials, selected for availability, competence, independence from the lost roles, and small scale; the arrangement, with a schedule, a place, and, where relevant, other people; the structure of the day, built from a fixed waking time, one purposeful activity, and one activity that is the man's own; and re-entry into relationship, in which three relationships are re-established with specific arrangements.

Purpose in the second half of life must be specific, the man's own, capable of being concluded, and compatible with his actual condition. It is generally composed from four domains — relational, contributive, developmental, and receptive — of which a man should hold at least two. It is converted into practice by writing it as an arrangement, reporting it to one person outside the household, and establishing a review, with the expectation that the purpose will change over a decade. What the work produces is not a restoration of the previous condition but a day with a shape, a small number of people who know the man, and an answer to the question of what the present period is for.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the role inventory. What proportion of your identity is supplied by roles that circumstances could remove? What single material exists independently of all of them?
2. Which of the events in Section 24.2 has occurred in your life, and what precisely was removed? What did you tell yourself at the time, and how much of it was accurate?
3. Which features of the empty season in Section 24.3 apply to you — the undifferentiated day, the absent pleasure, the loneliness of position, the irritability, the state-managing behaviours, the withdrawal, the sense of having been overtaken, or the diffuse anger?
4. Have you obtained a medical assessment covering the conditions in Section 24.3? If not, what is the date on which it will be booked?
5. Write the letter to the lost role, or the lost period. What did it give you, and what did it cost you? What are you taking from it?
6. From Section 24.4, which three materials are available to you now, and which have you dismissed as too small to matter?
7. Write the purpose sentence. Who will you report it to, by what date, and what will you do when the first arrangement lapses?

## Further Reading

- **On retirement and identity:** the research literature on the transition to retirement, including the consistent findings that occupation is central to men's identity and that retirement is associated with a specific risk of depression, particularly where the transition is abrupt and where the man's identity is entirely occupational.
- **On role loss and wellbeing:** the sociological and psychological literature on role loss, role strain, and the effects of the removal of significant roles on mental health, including the research on the loss of the provider role in men.
- **On meaning and purpose in later life:** the literature on meaning in life and its correlates, including the substantial evidence associating a sense of purpose with reduced mortality and with better cognitive and functional outcomes in later life.
- **On the transition to the second half of life:** the practical and academic literature on midlife and later-life transition, including structured approaches to the reconstruction of identity after a major loss.
- **On loneliness and re-engagement in later life:** the material in Chapter 25 and Appendix M, together with the research on the re-establishment of social connection after a role loss and the specific findings on men's difficulty in doing so.
- **Practical resources:** occupational health and retirement preparation programmes; counselling for life transitions; men's groups with members in comparable transitions; community and professional organisations that require competent volunteers; and the material in Appendices I and M.
