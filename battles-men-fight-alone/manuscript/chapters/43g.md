## 43.10 Questions Readers Ask

The questions below are the ones the case material records most frequently, together with the answers that the chapter's material supports.

**"I did not receive this from my father. Am I able to teach it?"**

The question is the most common in the case material and the answer is yes, with one condition. The condition is that the reader conducts the material on himself first, because the teaching is conducted by observation and a man cannot demonstrate a competence he has not begun to acquire. The sequence is not *learn it and then teach it*. It is *begin it and teach it while beginning*, which is the more honest arrangement and, according to the case material, the more effective one, since the boy observes an adult acquiring a competence rather than an adult possessing one.

The reader should note a specific finding. Men who did not receive the curriculum from their fathers frequently teach it better than men who did, for a reason the case material records: they are aware of what they are doing, they do not assume the material is obvious, and their own work is visible. The finding does not depend on the reader's history. It depends on his conduct in the present.

**"My son is already fifteen and has learned the pattern. Is it too late?"**

It is late, and the material does not pretend otherwise. The propositions of Section 43.4 are most efficiently removed before adolescence, and a boy of fifteen has conducted them for a decade.

The material's finding is that the change remains available, and that its mechanism is different at fifteen from what it is at five. At fifteen, the boy's principal audience is his peer group, and the household's instruction has less influence than the boy's observation of a man he respects. The specific practices at this age are: the father's own visible conduct, conducted without commentary; one adult outside the household who is not the father and who is not a formal authority; the setting in which the boy's competence is real; and the repair, which at fifteen is frequently the single act that changes the relationship. The case material records substantial changes in boys of sixteen and seventeen, and it records that the changes followed the father's own disclosure more reliably than any conversation the father constructed.

**"My wife says I am too soft with him. Am I?"**

The question contains the assumption that the curriculum of this chapter is a reduction of standards. The assumption is incorrect, and the reader should examine the specific concern rather than the label.

The material's position is that the curriculum is more demanding than silence, not less. It requires the boy to name what he feels, to regulate it, to state it to a party who has a right to know, to repair what he damages, and to ask for what he requires. Each of these is a discipline, and each is harder than the alternative. The boy who is permitted to express everything without regulation has not been taught the curriculum; he has been taught a different failure.

The specific distinction to put to the concern is this: **the curriculum does not remove the boy's obligations. It supplies him with the instruments for conducting them.** If the concern is that the boy is being excused from work, from responsibility, or from respect for others, the concern is legitimate and the material agrees with it. If the concern is that the boy is being permitted to have feelings, the concern is answered by Section 43.5's third competence, which is the discipline of conducting a state rather than being governed by it.

**"I am not his father. Is it my place?"**

It is your place if you are one of the adults in his observation, and Section 43.2's material establishes that a single adult outside the household is frequently decisive. The limits are specific and should be kept: you are not the boy's parent, you do not conduct the household's decisions, you do not report the boy's material to his parents without his agreement except where safety requires it, and you do not conduct the relationship as therapy.

The practice is the one Section 43.2 describes: a stated availability, in ordinary language; a reliable presence; a response that is not scandalised; and a relationship that continues after the disclosure. The case material's finding is that the party who supplies this is frequently an uncle, a teacher, a coach, an artisan, a neighbour, or a colleague of the boy's father, and that the party's formal qualifications are irrelevant to the effect.

**"He told me something serious. What do I do now?"**

The first act is to receive it well, which is Section 43.6's reception: thanks, acknowledgement, and a question. The second act is to establish what the boy requires: *what would help?* The third is to state your limits honestly, and to identify the professional route where the material exceeds them.

The material of Chapters 35 and 36 and Appendix Q applies. Four situations require an immediate professional route rather than a household arrangement: any indication of risk to the boy's life; any indication of abuse or harm by an adult; any indication of a disorder that is affecting the boy's functioning; and any disclosure that exceeds your competence. In these situations the boy should be told, in advance and in plain language, what you will do and why, and the arrangement should be conducted with him rather than to him.

The finding that matters for the reader's confidence is this: **the boy's disclosure is not made fragile by your honesty about your limits. It is made fragile by your pretence that you have none.** The adult who says *I do not know how to help with this part, and I know who does, and I will go with you* has conducted the fifth competence in the boy's observation.

**"What if the school or the community punishes him for speaking?"**

Then the arrangement must be conducted where it is safe, and the enforcement must be addressed separately. The material of Section 43.4's third location and Section 43.7's fourth difficulty applies. The boy should not be advised to disclose in a setting that will punish him, because the advice would be a repetition of the pattern the chapter describes: an adult recommending a course of conduct whose cost the adult does not bear.

The specific practices are: identify one safe party and one safe setting; conduct the disclosure there; protect the boy's material from the institution until the institution's response is known; and address the institution's enforcement through the ordinary means available to you, which are frequently slow and occasionally effective.

**"I am a teacher with a hundred and twenty boys. What can one adult do?"**

Three things, and the case material's finding is that they are not small.

The first is to be the named adult for the boys who choose you, which requires only that you state the availability and respond well once.

The second is to conduct the classroom's standard: error is information, it is corrected, and it is not punished by ridicule. The standard is conducted by your own practice, and it is measured by the boys' willingness to attempt.

The third is to conduct one repair, one disclosure, or one admission of a limit in the boys' observation each term. The acts are brief and their effect is disproportionate, because they supply the boys with the counter-example that Section 43.4's fifth practice requires.

**"Our community considers this a Western import. How do I answer that?"**

The answer is not a defence of the material's origin. It is a demonstration that the community already possesses the material, which Section 43.7's first principle requires.

The specific answer has three parts. The first is that the community's own traditions contain arrangements for shared difficulty: the gathering after a death, the delegation that visits a bereaved household, the elder who is sent to speak with a man who has lost his position, the association that contributes at a member's occasion. The second is that the material of this chapter is an account of how those arrangements work and why they are effective, stated in the language of the present. The third is that the community's own vocabulary is more effective than any imported formulation, and that the work should be conducted in that vocabulary by the community's own people.

The finding in the case material is that communities which conduct the work in their own terms produce arrangements that endure, and that communities which import a programme produce an event that ends. The distinction is the section's practical answer to the objection.

**"What if I conduct all of this and my son still does not speak to me?"**

Then the material has been conducted and the outcome is not yours to determine. The case material records three findings for this position.

The first is that the curriculum's effect is frequently delayed. Boys who received a father's disclosure at thirteen have reported its effect at twenty-five, and the father in the interval frequently believed that nothing had been transmitted.

The second is that the curriculum's effect is frequently conducted through the boy's later relationships rather than through the relationship with the father. The boy who learned that a difficulty can be stated may state it first to a wife, a friend, or a colleague, and the father may never observe the result.

The third is that the father's own conduct remains the correct conduct independently of its reception. The material of Chapter 40's permissions applies here in its household form: the father's work is his own, and its value is not computed by the boy's response. A man who conducts the curriculum in order to produce a grateful son has made the curriculum into a transaction, and the boy will detect the transaction with precision.

## 43.11 Chapter Summary

**Chapter 43 in brief.** The patterns of this book are transmitted rather than invented, and the transmission has four channels: modelling, instruction, reinforcement and punishment, and the observation of consequence. The channel of consequence is the most influential and the least addressed; the channel of reinforcement is the most rapid and the most reversible; the channel of modelling is the most durable and the slowest; and the channel of instruction is the least influential and the easiest to mistake for the whole. Where instruction contradicts conduct, conduct prevails, and the boy learns the contradiction itself.

What is transmitted is not the adult's intention but five lessons: that an unexpressed state does not exist; that expression is a request and a request is a debt; that the audience determines the permitted content; that the cost of expression is paid by the person who expresses; and that the position is the man. The five lessons are the exact inverse of the four competences of Chapter 39.4, which is why the adult's work in Parts X and XI and the boy's curriculum are the same work at different points in the life course.

The institutions that transmit are the household, the school, the workplace, the association, and the peer group, and they share five features: a vocabulary, a permission, a response, an observed consequence, and a repetition. A single adult outside the household is frequently decisive, and the finding is specific: one adult is generally sufficient, provided that he is reliably present, is not scandalised by what the boy reports, keeps the boy's confidence, and continues the relationship after the disclosure.

The five harmful propositions are that endurance is the whole of strength, that expression is weakness, that dominance is authority, that sexual conquest is proof of worth, and that violence is the resolution of insult. Each converts a legitimate obligation into a rule that requires the boy to suppress a part of himself or to reduce another person. They are enforced in the peer group, in entertainment, in language, in the institutions, and in the household's own arrangements. The practice of challenge has six forms: name the proposition rather than the person; supply the alternative at the moment of the challenge; separate the act from the identity; correct the language in the moment and without a lecture; conduct the counter-example; and protect the boy who deviates.

The six competences are the naming of the state, the location of the state in the body and the day, the regulation of the state, the statement of the state to a party who has a right to know, the repair, and the asking for a specific instrument. They are cumulative, and the curriculum begins with the vocabulary rather than with the asking. They are taught by the four-stage method of demonstration, rehearsal, feedback, and application, and the method fails most frequently at rehearsal and at application. The adult's own practice is the curriculum's first and most durable component, and the adult's repair is more valuable than the adult's perfection.

Help-seeking is produced by five conditions: a stated permission from a named person, in ordinary language; a low-cost first request received well; a response that is attentive, unhurried, and non-punitive; an action that follows; and a repetition that survives the boy's withdrawal. The conditions are supplied by individuals and by institutions, and the institution's design has four features: access, confidentiality, competence, and follow-through.

The community's work is conducted through six principles: the permission stated in the community's own language; a named person in each institution; arrangements that are routine rather than occasional; settings in which boys form peer groups of a different kind; leaders who conduct the material in public; and arrangements that survive the individual who established them. The work's measures are the boy's vocabulary, his statements, his askings, his repairs, and the adult's own conduct, which is the leading indicator.

::: summary
**The chapter's central claim.** A man's silence is not a private inheritance. It is a curriculum, conducted by identifiable institutions, through identifiable channels, over identifiable years, and it can be replaced by a different curriculum conducted by the same institutions. The replacement does not require a programme, a campaign, or a qualification. It requires an adult whose conduct is legible, a vocabulary, a permission, a response, an observed repair, and a repetition. The reader who supplies these to one boy has interrupted the transmission for that boy and, through him, for the household he will conduct.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Which of the four channels was strongest in your own upbringing, and what specifically did it teach you?
2. What did you learn about the cost of expression, and from which incident?
3. Which of the five lessons of Section 43.2 is still governing your adult conduct, and where does it appear?
4. Who was the single adult, if there was one, to whom you could speak as a boy? What did that adult do that made the arrangement possible?
5. Which of the five harmful propositions do you still enforce, without intending to, in your household or your workplace?
6. Which of the six competences are you least able to conduct in front of a boy, and what does that inability transmit?
7. Complete Part Three of the exercise. What does your conduct over the last thirty days tell a boy about what a man does with difficulty?
8. Which boy in your observation is receiving no curriculum at all, and what is the smallest arrangement you could supply within the next month?
9. What will you do when the boy discloses something that exceeds your competence? Write the sentence you will use before you need it.
10. In five years, what do you want the boys who observed you to be able to say about what a man does with what he carries?

## Further Reading

- **On the development of emotional competence:** the research literature on emotional socialisation in childhood and adolescence, including the findings on the role of parental modelling and response, on the development of emotion vocabulary and regulation, and on the longitudinal associations between early emotional competence and adult functioning.
- **On observational learning and modelling:** the established literature on imitation, modelling, and vicarious learning, including the findings on the conditions under which a model's conduct prevails over a model's instruction, and on the effects of observing the consequences of others' conduct.
- **On help-seeking:** the research literature on help-seeking behaviour, including the findings on the barriers to help-seeking among men and boys, on the role of confidentiality and of the first response, and on the design of services that young men use.
- **On gender stereotypes and their enforcement:** the research literature on gender socialisation, stereotype threat, and the enforcement of masculine norms in peer groups, schools, and workplaces, together with the practice literature on programmes that have produced measurable change in boys' attitudes and conduct.
- **On mentoring and youth development:** the research and practice literature on mentoring relationships, including the findings on the characteristics of effective mentors, on the role of a single non-parental adult, and on the risks of poorly designed mentoring arrangements.
- **On school-based and community-based prevention:** the practice literature on whole-school approaches to emotional health, on the design of named-person arrangements, and on the evaluation of community programmes in low- and middle-income settings.
- **Practical resources:** school guidance services; clinical child and adolescent psychology; teacher training and continuing professional development; apprentice and vocational training programmes; and the material in Appendices B, E, G, and S.
