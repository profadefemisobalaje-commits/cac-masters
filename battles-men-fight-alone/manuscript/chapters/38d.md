## 38.4 The Reckoning: The Full Statement of the Position

The reckoning is the written statement of everything the man has and owes, and it is the instrument on which the reconstruction's every stage depends. It is conducted over one to three sittings, and it should be conducted with the professional where the position includes legal or debt consequences.

### The Six Sections

**Section One: The assets.** Everything the man owns or has an interest in: the property, the vehicles, the equipment, the tools, the stock, the materials, the accounts, the investments, the policies, the land, the interests in any business or family property, the debts owed to him, and the items of value in his household. Each entry with its estimated value, its status — owned, encumbered, jointly held, in dispute — and its identification.

**The instruction.** This section is frequently the one in which the man discovers that his position is better than his picture of it: the interest in the family property, the policy, the vehicle's actual value, the debt owed to him that he had written off, the tools.

**Section Two: The liabilities.** Everything the man owes, in full: the facilities, the trade accounts, the credit arrangements, the guarantees — including contingent guarantees for other parties, which men most frequently omit — the tax, the rates, the maintenance, the sums owed to family and friends, and the obligations of any business in which the man is a partner or a director, in each case with the amount, the holder, the terms, the security, and the deadline.

**The instruction.** The section should be complete before any decision is taken. The practice material's finding is that the section's completion is generally the point at which the position becomes manageable, because the man's mental account is generally a small number of the obligations and the total is frequently less alarming than the man's fear of it.

**Section Three: The income and the requirements.** The income that remains, by source and by period; the household's requirements, separated into the fixed and the variable; the obligations of Category A from Section 38.2; and the gap between them. The section supplies the figure that determines the reconstruction's pace.

**Section Four: The capacities.** What the man can do: the trade, the professional qualification, the skill, the experience, the reputation, the network, the languages, the physical capacity, and the time available. The section is the one the man most frequently omits, and the practice material identifies its omission as the reason that men who are in a financial collapse conclude that they have nothing, while their actual position contains a complete set of capacities that the reconstruction will use.

**The instruction.** The capacities should be written in the form of what they can produce, since the reconstruction's first stages concern income: the work the man can do, the services he can supply, the market that exists, and the routes to it. This is the material of Chapter 25.3's record, and its preparation is the first element of the reconstruction's second stage.

**Section Five: The obligations of standing.** The man's obligations of Part IX: the relatives, the community, the family's arrangements, the association, the church or mosque's obligations, the commitments that he was carrying. This section is written separately and it is not addressed until the reconstruction's later stages, because addressing it in the first period is the mechanism of Chapter 33's collapse. The section's function is that the obligations are identified rather than denied, and that the man has a written account of what he is deferring, which he can state to the persons concerned in the form that Section 38.2's Task Three supplies.

**Section Six: What remains intact.** The section the chapter requires and the one the man does not write unless instructed: the list of what the collapse has not taken. Its categories are specific and the reader should complete each with the actual material.

**The health, where it is intact.** The body, the capacity, the sleep, the ability to work physically, the medical position.

**The capacities.** Section Four, restated in the form of what they are worth.

**The relationships that the collapse has not reached.** The household, where the collapse has not taken it; the spouse and the children; the friends who have remained; the relatives who have not required anything; the professional network; the men with whom the man's history is shared.

**The standing that survives.** The man's reputation in the settings where his conduct is known, which is the material of Chapter 25.1's countervailing observation: the communities of long duration assess conduct rather than results, and a man's conduct is generally not what has failed.

**The years.** The time available for the reconstruction, which is generally longer than the man believes and which is the resource he has not counted.

**The experience.** The knowledge of the position, the market, the people, and the failures, which is the material of the record in Chapter 25.3 and which a man who has not been through a collapse does not possess.

### The Two Sentences the Section Requires

The reckoning is completed with two written statements, and both are required.

**The first: the accurate description of the position.** One page, in figures and facts, without either minimisation or catastrophe: what is owed, what is held, what remains, what has been lost, and what the position now is. The statement is the man's own and it is the document he will use with the professional, the household, and the institutions.

**The second: the account of the failure.** The man's own account, in writing, of what occurred and of his own part in it: the decision, the judgement, the omission, the quality of the arrangement, and the measure of what was outside his control. The material is Chapter 29.1's classification conducted on the collapse: the error, the wrong, and the tragedy, each stated separately. The statement's function is the one Chapter 38.9 of the case material supplies: the man who has written the account of his own part in the failure is able to act on the position without the account's being applied to him continuously by himself.

## 38.5 The Staged Reconstruction

The reconstruction has six stages, and the order is mechanical. The reader should establish the stage he is in and should not attempt a later stage's work before the earlier stages' conditions are met.

### Stage One: The Stabilisation of the Position

**Duration:** about three to six months. **The work:** the four tasks of Section 38.2; the statement of Section 38.4; the negotiations of Category B; the arrangements of Category A; the household's disclosure and planning; and the man's safety, sleep, and health.

**The stage's condition:** the man knows the position in figures, the household knows it, the ally knows it, the professional arrangements exist, and no new obligations have been entered.

### Stage Two: The Restoration of Income

**Duration:** about six to eighteen months. **The work:** the man's income, in the immediate form available: the work he can obtain, the trade's services, the employment at the level the position permits, the small contracts, the temporary arrangement, the second or third source as Chapter 25.3's material establishes. The stage's content is Chapter 35's asking applied to work, and Chapter 25.3's record applied to the man's capacities.

**The finding that governs the stage:** the first income is generally smaller than the man's previous position and larger than his fear of it, and the stage's function is not the restoration of the man's former standard but the establishment of the flow on which every later stage depends.

**The prohibition:** no capital-consuming venture, no attempt to restore the former position's form, no borrowing for the reconstruction, until the income flow is established.

### Stage Three: The Settlement of the Obligations

**Duration:** one to five years. **The work:** the Category B negotiations concluded and conducted; the Category A obligations maintained without default; and, where the position requires it, the formal processes — the insolvency, the arrangement with creditors, the consolidation, the renegotiation, the legal process — conducted through the professional route.

**The stage's condition:** the obligations have a schedule, the schedule is being met, and the Category C obligations have been addressed with the disclosure and the arrangement of Chapter 34.6.

**The finding:** the settlement is frequently the stage at which the man's position begins to alter in the community's account, because a schedule that is being met is evidence, and the material of Chapter 25.4's third practice — the conduct within the standard — is the mechanism.

### Stage Four: The Restoration of Standing

**Duration:** two to five years. **The work:** the man's conduct in the settings where his standing is assessed: the obligations kept, the arrangements met, the work performed properly, the community's contributions made at the level available, the attendance maintained where it was previously declined, and the statement of the position delivered once and accurately, in the form of Chapter 25.8's Part Six.

**The finding:** the restoration of standing is not obtained by the demonstration that the collapse was not the man's fault; it is obtained by years of the conduct within the standard. The material of Chapter 25.6's three prohibitions applies in full, and the prohibition on arguing with the audience is the one that matters most in the collapse setting.

### Stage Five: The Restoration of the Relationships and the Household

**Duration:** continuous. **The work:** the material of Parts III, V, and VII conducted with the position's new circumstances: the household's arrangements reconstructed at the level available; the corrections of the household's account of the period; the friendships retained and, in the case material's consistent finding, the friendships resumed after a long interval; the obligations of Part IX addressed within the terms of the new position; and the arrangements of Chapters 26 and 27 restored rather than abandoned.

**The stage's condition:** the man is known by his household and by at least one person outside it, and the accounts of the collapse period have been corrected rather than left to the household's construction.

### Stage Six: The New Construction

**Duration:** years. **The work:** the man's own reconstruction, conducted on the material of Parts VI and X: the identity, which is the material of Chapter 24; the measure, which is Chapter 25.5; the dreams, which are Chapter 28's; the rest, which is Chapter 37's; the arrangements, which are Chapters 31 and 33's; and the account of the years, which will be the material of Chapter 44.

**The stage's finding:** the men in the case material who have rebuilt describe this stage in specific terms: the reconstructed position is not the former position, and it is frequently experienced as better in ways that the man could not have described in advance. The elements most frequently identified are the accuracy of the man's account of himself, the household's knowledge of him, the arrangements that did not exist before, and the reduction of the audience's authority.
