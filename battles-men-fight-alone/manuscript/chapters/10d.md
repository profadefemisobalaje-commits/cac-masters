## 10.7 Illustrative Case: The Eldest Son of Seven

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family counselling, financial advisory work, and research on extended-family obligation.

**Godwin is fifty-six, a retired secondary-school principal who now runs a small tutorial centre.** He is the eldest of seven siblings. He was the first in the family to attend university, and it was understood from his early twenties — understood by everybody, including him — that his position carried responsibilities.

Over thirty years, Godwin's obligations accumulated in a manner nobody ever audited. He paid the school fees of all six of his younger siblings through secondary school, and of four of them through tertiary education. He built a house for his mother after his father's death and maintained it. He paid for the weddings of two sisters and the burial of an uncle. He contributed to the school fees of nine nieces and nephews. He funded medical treatment for his mother over eleven years, including two prolonged hospitalisations. He contributed to the community development association at a level set by his professional position. He built his own house, educated his four children, and supported his wife's mother for the last seven years of her life.

His income was never sufficient for these obligations, and the gap was met in a consistent order: first from savings, then from a cooperative loan, then from a second loan, then from the sale of a plot of land he had bought for his own retirement, then by delaying his own medical treatment — he had a persistent hypertension problem for which he filled prescriptions irregularly — and finally by continuing to work after retirement rather than resting.

He told nobody. His wife knew that the family needed money and knew that the relatives made requests; she did not know the total, and she did not know about the second loan or the sale of the land. His siblings knew only their own arrangements. His children knew nothing at all.

The crisis arrived when Godwin had a stroke at the age of fifty-five. He recovered substantially, and he was unable to work for seven months. In that period, three facts became visible to the whole family for the first time: the total of the outstanding obligations; the absence of any provision for himself, including a pension of very modest value and no health cover; and the fact that his wife had, for four years, been quietly financing the household's shortfall from her own teaching income without knowing the reason for the shortfall.

The family's response, once the position was known, was not what Godwin had feared. Two of his brothers restructured the support to his mother between them. A sister took over the fees for two of the nieces and nephews. The community association, informed in general terms, reduced his levy without comment. And his eldest son, who had been at university, took a part-time job, which he had not previously been permitted to do because his father considered it a mark of failure that a son of his should need to work while studying.

Godwin's own account of the episode, given two years later, was as follows: *I never decided to carry all of it. I decided, one request at a time, three hundred times, and nobody ever asked me to add it up — not my wife, not my brothers, and not me. The stroke was the first time anybody added it up, and when they did, it took four people eleven days to redistribute what I had been carrying alone for twenty years.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The obligations were individually legitimate and collectively impossible.** Every item on Godwin's list would have been defended by any member of the family. The problem was never the reasonableness of any single obligation; it was the absence of a total.

**The gap was met in a predictable sequence.** Savings, then borrowing, then productive assets, then personal health, then post-retirement work. This sequence is reported consistently by men in this position, and it is worth recording because it identifies the warning points: by the time productive assets are being sold, the arrangement is already unsustainable.

**The concealment was perpetuated by consideration.** Godwin did not tell his wife because he did not want to worry her. He did not tell his brothers because the conversation would have been awkward. He did not tell his son because it would have diminished him in the boy's eyes. Every one of these reasons was considerate, and their cumulative effect was a man carrying a family's obligations alone until his body intervened.

**The redistribution was feasible.** The most important finding in the case is the one Godwin had not anticipated: once the position was visible, the obligations were redistributed within eleven days and without the disgrace he had feared. The brothers and the sister had the capacity. They simply had never been asked, and no one had known that the arrangement existed in its actual shape.

**The cost that was not recoverable was the man's own provision.** The stroke caused permanent changes; the hypertension that had gone untreated contributed to it; the land intended for retirement was gone; and the household's security was permanently reduced. The obligations were redistributed, and the man's own future could not be.

## 10.8 Practical Exercise: The Dependency Map and the Allocation Plan

This exercise has four parts and takes about two hours. It may be completed alone, and it is considerably more effective when the results are discussed with a partner.

### Part One: The Dependency Map

Draw a central circle for yourself, and place a circle for every person or body that depends on you. For each, record four items:

| Dependent | Financial | Time | Decisions | Emotional availability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spouse or partner | | | | |
| Each child | | | | |
| Each parent | | | | |
| Each sibling receiving support | | | | |
| Other relatives | | | | |
| Community or religious bodies | | | | |
| Employees, if any | | | | |

For the financial column, enter an actual monthly figure. For the remaining columns, enter hours per week, or a rating from one to five.

Most men who complete this table for the first time find two things: that the financial total is materially higher than they had estimated, and that the time and decision columns add up to more than a working week.

### Part Two: The Test of Necessity and Choice

For each obligation, mark one of four letters.

- **N** — Necessary: the obligation protects survival, health, or an irreversible commitment.
- **C** — Chosen: I have decided this deliberately and would defend it as my responsibility.
- **A** — Accumulated: this continues because it began, not because it was chosen.
- **P** — Pressure: this continues because refusing would be socially or emotionally costly, not because the need is real.

The *A* and *P* categories are the available reductions, and the total in those two columns is almost always substantial.

### Part Three: The Allocation

Write a deliberate allocation of your income across six categories: household essentials; education and children's requirements; parents; other relatives; community; and your own health, reserve, and retirement. The allocation must total one hundred per cent and must include the sixth category at a non-zero level. Then apply one rule: the allocation takes effect from a named month, and every obligation that exceeds it is reduced, renegotiated, or ended.

### Part Four: The Three Conversations

Write the opening three sentences of each of the following conversations, in your own words and in the order given.

1. **With your partner.** Disclose the total, including obligations she does not know about, and propose the allocation. Appendix F provides a structure.
2. **With the relatives whose support will change.** Use the general-arrangement form from Section 10.4. Give the figure, the date it takes effect, and what will not change.
3. **With the siblings, about a shared obligation.** Propose the redistribution: what each sibling will contribute, and how the arrangement will respond to future increases in need.

Write the sentences rather than the intention to have the conversation. Men who write them report that the act of composition removes about half of the difficulty.

## 10.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not my duty to support my family? My father did it for his family."**

It is a duty, and it is not an unlimited one. The distinction that resolves the apparent conflict is between a defined responsibility and unlimited liability. A man who supports his family within a deliberate allocation is discharging the duty; a man who carries an unlimited obligation until his health fails is discharging it in a manner that removes his capacity to continue, which serves nobody.

**"My brother has a good job but contributes nothing. How do I raise this without destroying the family?"**

By raising it as an arrangement rather than as an accusation. *This is what I have been paying. I cannot continue at this level. This is what I propose we each contribute.* The conversation is easier when the man brings figures rather than grievances, and when he proposes rather than protests.

**"If I reduce the support, will I not be disgraced in the community?"**

Some comment is likely, and the record of men who have made the change is that it is less than expected, and that it is frequently accompanied by respect for having faced the arithmetic. The reduction in community contributions is the least costly item in the whole arrangement: standing, in most communities, depends much more on a man's character and availability than on the size of his contribution in a difficult year.

**"My wife says I give too much to my family. What if she is right, and what if she is not?"**

The productive way to settle the question is the allocation in Part Three of the exercise: a figure, agreed, with a category for her family as well as yours. Men frequently discover that their partners object not to the support but to their exclusion from the decision, and that a partner who is included is more generous than the man anticipated.

**"How do I tell my mother that I cannot continue paying for her treatment?"**

Not by reducing care, but by redistributing its cost. Involve the siblings before the conversation, so that the arrangement presented to her is a coordinated plan rather than a withdrawal, and frame it in terms of what will continue rather than what will stop.

**"What if my obligations genuinely cannot be reduced?"**

Then the priority is to increase capacity rather than to reduce the obligations: an additional income stream, a renegotiation of a rate, a change to the pattern of work, or the disposal of a low-value asset in favour of a productive one. Where neither is possible in the short term, the objective becomes the protection of the essentials — sleep, medical care, and one channel of support — and Chapter 12 addresses the management of work under these conditions.

::: summary
**Chapter 10 in brief.** The expectations placed on a family provider arrive in four bands: the household's subsistence, investment, and standard; support for parents; support for siblings, their children, and the wider kin; and community contributions. Each obligation is legitimate, and no one ever adds them up. When they are added up, the total frequently exceeds the man's entire income, and the difference is met by borrowing, by the sale of assets, by the deferral of his own medical care, and by the assumption that he will work until he dies.

Supporting each band of dependants requires different practices. For parents: a plan agreed with the siblings, a separation of predictable from unpredictable costs, and the recognition that time is frequently valued more than money. For a spouse: shared decisions about transfers to relatives, and symmetry between both partners' family obligations. For children: educational commitments structured as far as possible, consistent treatment between children, and presence as a form of provision. For relatives: defined arrangements in place of repeated ad hoc payments, capacity-building in preference to consumption support, and the redistributive principle that other relatives exist.

The emotional burden of being financially indispensable is a man who cannot be ill, cannot rest without guilt, cannot take risks with his position, and cannot be replaced. The consequences are resentment, fear, isolation, and a private sense of unreality; their effects inside the household are absence, irritability at home, the transmission of the burden to the wife, and a model of manhood that children carry forward.

Provider overload presents as virtue, which is what makes it difficult to discuss. The alternative to unlimited liability is not refusal but a defined contribution: a figure, a duration, a plan, and a review. Boundaries are established by producing the total, deciding the allocation deliberately, introducing the boundary as a general arrangement rather than a series of refusals, attaching it to facts rather than feelings, and holding it while offering substitutions where possible. The conversations should begin with the partner and end with the parents.

Shared financial planning is the most reliable protection: a monthly budget meeting with a fixed agenda; a written priority order for expenditure agreed in a calm month; a written schedule of family support obligations with durations and review dates; a reserve of three to six months; a three-account structure that removes the most common source of resentment; and a deliberate allocation to the man's own retirement and health cover, which is the item most frequently omitted and the one he cannot postpone without personal consequences.

Provider burnout develops through a recognisable sequence from full engagement to break point. Its protective practices are the establishment of a total and a limit, the maintenance of one thing that is never sacrificed, the conversion of open-ended obligations into arrangements with ends, the maintenance of one channel outside the provider role, and the existence of at least one relationship in which the man is not the provider. Where that last item is absent, it is the most important finding available to the reader.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Complete the dependency map in your head before writing it down. How many people depend on you financially? What is the total monthly figure, and when did you last calculate it?
2. Which of your obligations are in categories *A* (accumulated) and *P* (pressure)? What would the total saving be if they were reduced?
3. Section 10.3 describes four consequences of indispensability: he cannot be ill, cannot rest, cannot take risks, and cannot be replaced. Which one weighs most heavily on you, and what would reduce it?
4. When did you last have a budget meeting with your partner that concerned figures rather than conduct? What prevented it, and what would make it possible this month?
5. What is your own provision — pension, health cover, reserve — and how does it compare with what you provide to others? What does the comparison suggest?
6. Which of the five protective practices in Section 10.6 do you currently maintain? If none, which one could you establish this month, at the smallest viable scale?
7. Is there one person in your life in whose company you are not the provider? If not, who could become that person, and what is the first step?

## Further Reading

- **On extended-family obligation in Africa:** the research literature on extended-family support systems, remittances, and kinship obligation in West Africa and comparable settings, including studies of the pressures placed on the highest-earning member and of the effects of urbanisation and migration on the distribution of obligation.
- **On caregiving and burden:** the established body of research on caregiver burden, including the concepts of role overload, objective versus subjective burden, and the protective factors that reduce burnout — much of which applies directly to men carrying long-term family obligations.
- **On boundaries:** the practical literature on boundary-setting and assertiveness, including scripts and the well-documented finding that boundaries framed as general arrangements are more sustainable than those framed as individual refusals.
- **On household finance:** practical material on family budgeting, the priority ordering of expenditure, emergency funds, and the structure of household accounts. Consumer protection bodies and cooperative societies in Nigeria and elsewhere publish free guidance that is adapted to local conditions.
- **On men's provider role:** the research on how the provider role affects men's identity, health, and family relationships, including studies of the psychological effects of being the sole earner and of the transition from sole provider to shared responsibility.
