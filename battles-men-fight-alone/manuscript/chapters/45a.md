# The Day a Man Realizes He Does Not Have to Fight Alone {.chapter num="45" short="The Day a Man Realizes He Does Not Have to Fight Alone"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the mechanism of self-reliance as a competent adaptation that has become a liability, and identify the point at which the transition occurs.
2. Identify the six reasons a man refuses support that is available, and answer each one in his own record.
3. Analyse the four fears that govern the acceptance of support, and distinguish acceptance from dependence.
4. Conduct a reconciliation with his own limits that is neither resignation nor denial.
5. Reconstruct his purpose and his belonging on four foundations, and state them in a form that can be acted upon.
6. Construct a working vision of manhood that retains the standard and removes the isolation.
7. Conduct the closing exercise of the book and state the terms on which he will begin the next chapter.
:::

## Introduction

This is the last chapter of the book's main argument, and it is the one that the preceding forty-four chapters were written to reach.

The book began with a man nobody really knows, and it has spent its length describing the architecture of his isolation: the script he inherited, the states he could not name, the money he could not state, the grief he could not conduct, the marriage he could not repair, the position he could not afford to lose, the friends he could not call, the help he could not ask for. Parts X and XI described the recovery and the rebuilding. Part XII has described the reconstruction of the definition, the removal of the pretence, the education of the next generation, and the extraction of wisdom from what he has survived.

This chapter addresses the single act that all of that work finally requires, and it is the act that the book's title names: the day a man realises that he does not have to fight alone.

The realisation is not an insight. The book has been careful about this distinction throughout, and it is the chapter's first proposition. **Insight is the acquisition of information; a realisation, in the sense this chapter uses the word, is the alteration of an arrangement.** A man may understand, with complete intellectual clarity, that he does not have to fight alone, and continue to fight alone for another twenty years. The case material records this frequently and without surprise: the men who have read the most about men's isolation are frequently the men who are most isolated, because reading about isolation is itself an isolating act.

The realisation is therefore a date in a man's life at which something in his arrangements changes. The telephone call that is made rather than planned. The sentence that is spoken rather than rehearsed for another year. The appointment that is attended. The party who is told the accurate position. The support that is accepted rather than declined. The help that is asked for before the collapse rather than after it. The case material's finding is that these acts are small, that they are frequently undramatic, that they are frequently conducted on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon, and that men remember them with the precision that they give to very few other days.

The chapter's approach has five commitments.

**The first is that the chapter does not propose that a man should cease to be responsible.** The book's central message is stated in the introduction and it is repeated here because it is the point at which the material is most frequently misunderstood: men can carry serious responsibilities without being expected to endure emotional suffering in isolation. The sentence contains two clauses and the chapter requires both. The responsibilities are not reduced. The isolation is not a requirement of them. A man who reads this chapter as permission to carry less has misread the book, and a man who reads it as a requirement to carry the same load in the same way has misread it more seriously, because he has retained the part that was killing him and discarded the part that was available.

**The second is that the chapter distinguishes support from rescue.** Support is the supply of an instrument that the man uses. Rescue is the conduct of the man's position by another party. The book has treated the distinction throughout — in Chapter 20.4's substitution, in Chapter 35.5's three supports, in Chapter 44.5's prohibition of the rescue — and the chapter states it again because it is the distinction on which a man's acceptance of support depends. A man who believes that accepting support means being rescued will decline it, and he will be right to decline the rescue. The chapter's task is to show that the support is available without it.

**The third is that the chapter is written for the man who has nobody, as well as the man who has somebody and has not called him.** A proportion of the readers of this book will be genuinely without a party: men whose marriages have ended, whose friendships have lapsed, whose families are distant or dead, whose work has isolated them, whose communities have been left behind by migration. The chapter addresses this reader directly in Section 45.4, and its finding is that the construction of a party is possible at any age, that it is slower than the reader expects, and that it begins with the man's own conduct rather than with the search for a person.

**The fourth is that the chapter does not require a man to become a different kind of man.** The case material's finding is that men frequently refuse the material of this book because they believe it requires them to become gregarious, emotionally expressive, and publicly vulnerable. The chapter requires none of these. The man who conducts this material may remain private, reserved, and disinclined to discuss his interior life in public. What changes is not his temperament. It is the number of parties who hold his accurate position, and the arrangements he has established so that his position is not conducted alone.

**The fifth is that the chapter has an ending, and the ending is a beginning.** The book's title is a statement about a battle, and the chapter's title is a statement about a day. The chapter's material is the conduct of that day and the arrangement that follows it. Its product is not a feeling of connection, which is unreliable, and not a resolution to be different, which is quickly broken. Its product is a written page: the parties, the instruments, the terms, and the date. Section 45.7 supplies the page, and the reader who completes it will have conducted the book's final exercise.

The chapter's sequence follows the outline. Section 45.1 addresses the burden of constant self-reliance. Section 45.2 addresses the acceptance of support. Section 45.3 addresses the reconciliation with limitation. Section 45.4 addresses purpose and belonging. Section 45.5 addresses the vision of manhood. Section 45.6 addresses the beginning. Section 45.7 supplies the programme. Section 45.8 presents the book's closing case. Section 45.9 supplies the summary, the reflection questions, and the further reading that closes the main text.

## 45.1 Releasing the Burden of Constant Self-Reliance

The section begins with an account of self-reliance that is more careful than the culture's, because the reader will otherwise hear the section as an attack on a capacity he values, and the attack would be both inaccurate and counterproductive.

### Self-Reliance as a Competence

Self-reliance is a competence, and it is among the most valuable a man can acquire. It is the capacity to conduct his own position: to earn, to decide, to repair, to endure, to recover, and to act without requiring another party's permission or participation. The man who possesses it is useful to his household, reliable in his work, and steady in a crisis. The case material of this book is full of such men, and the book has not treated their competence as a pathology.

The competence is acquired, and its acquisition follows the pattern described in Chapter 43.5's four-stage method: the boy is taught to conduct small matters alone, rehearses them, receives feedback, and applies them at increasing stakes. The competence is not given. It is built, frequently by a boy who had no alternative, and the men who possess it most completely are frequently the men whose childhoods required it earliest.

The section's argument is therefore not that self-reliance is a defect. It is that **self-reliance is a competence with a domain, and the domain has been expanded beyond its usefulness.** A capacity that is appropriate to a man's own position has been extended, in the men this book describes, to cover matters that cannot be conducted alone: the naming of his own states, the assessment of his own judgement, the correction of his own errors, the grief that requires an audience, the position that requires a second opinion, the decision that requires a party who is not invested in it, and the collapse that requires somebody to be present.

### The Expansion and Its Mechanism

The expansion has four mechanisms, and each one is recorded in the case material.

**The first mechanism is success.** Self-reliance works, and its success is the reason for its expansion. The man who conducted his household's finances alone for ten years and whose household did not fail concludes that the arrangement is sound. The man who concealed a difficulty and survived it concludes that concealment is survivable. The man who conducted a grief alone and continued to work concludes that the grief did not require an audience. The conclusion is drawn from the absence of a visible failure, and the case material's finding is that the failure is frequently invisible to the man: the marriage that continued without intimacy, the judgement that was never corrected, the grief that returned as irritability in the eighth year, the health that was not measured.

**The second mechanism is the absence of a party.** A man who has no party to call does not choose self-reliance. He conducts the only arrangement available, and the arrangement becomes his habit. By the time a party becomes available, the habit is fifteen years old and the man's capacity to use the party has atrophied. The mechanism is the material of Chapter 27's man who has nobody to call, and its finding is that the absence and the habit reinforce one another.

**The third mechanism is the script.** The material of Chapter 41.2's five clauses supplies the man with a definition in which self-sufficiency is not a competence but an identity. To accept support is not to use an instrument; it is to cease to be the thing he has understood himself to be. The mechanism is the most resistant of the four, because it operates below the level of the man's decisions.

**The fourth mechanism is the cost of the alternative.** The man has, at some point, asked for support and paid for it: the disclosure that was used against him, the request that was refused, the vulnerability that was met with ridicule, the party who made the help into a debt. The mechanism is the material of Chapter 35.2's accounting, and its finding is that a single expensive experience frequently governs twenty subsequent years.

### The Point at Which the Competence Becomes a Liability

The section now states the point at which the transition occurs, because the reader will require a criterion and because the culture supplies none.

The criterion is not the amount the man is carrying. A man may carry a great deal alone for a period and be conducting himself correctly, particularly where the load is temporary and the man has the instruments he requires. The criterion is not the man's distress, since distress is frequently appropriate to the position.

**The criterion is the man's capacity to correct himself.** Self-reliance becomes a liability at the point where the man's position is no longer subject to correction, because correction requires a party who is not the man.

The criterion is stated in five tests, and the reader should conduct them against his own position.

**Test One: The unexamined decision.** When did I last take a decision of consequence, and who examined it before I took it? Where the answer is nobody, and where the pattern extends over years, the man's judgement is unexamined, and the case material's finding is that unexamined judgement drifts.

**Test Two: The unstated figure.** When did I last state my position in figures to a party who could tell me that I was wrong? The material of Chapter 42.5's second support applies: the man whose position has never been stated to anybody is conducting a position that nobody can confirm.

**Test Three: The unregistered change.** When did a change in my state — my sleep, my drinking, my temper, my weight, my interest, my capacity for work — go unregistered by anybody except me? The material of Chapter 34.2's warning signs applies, and the test is the one that most reliably identifies a man whose position has become uncorrectable.

**Test Four: The grief without an audience.** Whose death, loss, or ending am I conducting without a party who knows? The material of Chapter 30 applies, and the test's result is frequently the first item the reader will act upon.

**Test Five: The collapse without a witness.** If I became seriously unwell, or if I lost my position, or if I could not continue, who would know, and how long would it take them to find out? The test is the one that the case material records as the most sobering, and its finding is that men frequently discover that the answer is *nobody for several days*.

The criterion's statement is completed by its corollary: **the man whose position is not subject to correction is not conducting a position. He is conducting an account of a position, and the account is being maintained by the same faculty that constructed it.**

### What Is Released, and What Is Not

The section closes with the precise statement of what the chapter proposes should be released, because the reader's interpretation of this material determines whether he conducts it.

**What is released is the requirement of exclusive conduct.** The man ceases to require that every component of his position be conducted by him alone. He establishes the parties, the instruments, and the arrangements by which his position is examined, corrected, shared where it should be shared, and supported where it requires support.

**What is not released is the responsibility.** The man remains responsible for his position, his household, his work, and his conduct. The parties who examine and support do not become responsible for him. The distinction is the material of Chapter 35.5's three supports, and its practical form is stated in one sentence: **the man accepts the instrument, and retains the obligation.**

**What is also not released is the man's standard.** The obligations of Chapter 41.6's working definition — provision, protection, competence, accountability, presence, and repair — are retained in full. What is removed is the addition described in Chapter 41.2: the exclusivity, the anonymity, the personalisation, and the silence that converted the obligations into a requirement of self-destruction.
