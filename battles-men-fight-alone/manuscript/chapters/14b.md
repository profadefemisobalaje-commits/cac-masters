## 14.3 The Cost of Being Misunderstood

The costs of this arrangement are not abstract, and they fall on four parties.

### The Cost to the Man

**The loss of the principal support that marriage is supposed to provide.** A man who cannot speak to his wife about his interior life is operating without the support that the relationship's structure implies, and he experiences a specific deprivation that he frequently cannot name. It is not the absence of conversation; it is the absence of one person who knows.

**A further reduction in the willingness to disclose.** Each failed attempt confirms the conclusion, and the conclusion becomes general: the man does not attempt disclosure with anybody. The consequence is the arrangement described in Chapter 4, in which the man has no intimate relationship at all, and the consequences of that arrangement are the material of Part One.

**The redirection of the difficulty into behaviour that damages the marriage.** Where the difficulty cannot be spoken, it is enacted, and the enactment is directed at home: withdrawal, irritability, alcohol, longer hours, and, in some cases, conduct outside the marriage. It is important to state the attribution clearly: the marriage is damaged not primarily by the man's difficulty but by the arrangement in which the difficulty is unspoken, and the arrangement has two authors.

**The progressive reduction of what the man wants from the relationship.** Men in long-standing arrangements of this kind report a change in their expectations over time: from wanting to be understood, to wanting to be left alone, to wanting nothing in particular and being surprised when the arrangement becomes intolerable to them at fifty-five. The last stage frequently arrives at a point when the children have left home and the man's work is reducing, and the arrival of the material is then attributed to a crisis of age rather than to the arrangement that produced it.

**The development of alternatives that are not chosen deliberately.** Where the need for understanding is unmet at home, it is met elsewhere, and the elsewhere is frequently not a counsellor or a friend. Chapter 16 addresses the path from deprivation to infidelity, which is significantly more common among men in this arrangement than among men whose marriages contain an intimate conversation.

### The Cost to the Partner

**She is dealing with an unexplained change.** The wife lives with a man whose mood, availability, and temper have altered without explanation, and she is required to organise her household around an unknown quantity. This is a substantial strain and it is underestimated by men who are experiencing the same change from the inside and who know its cause.

**She acquires the belief that the marriage has failed.** Where a husband's withdrawal is not explained, the wife's most common inferences are the ones identified above. A woman who believes she has been excluded, replaced, or found inadequate will conduct her marriage on that basis, and she may not mention it for years.

**She compensates, which increases her load.** In many households, when a man becomes less available, the wife absorbs the difference: additional work, additional management of the children, additional financial arrangement, and additional responsibility for the family's social obligations. Her load increases as her husband's grows, and neither has a way to say so.

**Her own difficulties are unattended.** A wife whose husband is withdrawn or irritable does not raise her own difficulties with him, because the conditions are wrong. The result is two unattended people in the same house, which is the arrangement that produces the mutual accusation, in later years, that each was unthinking of the other.

### The Cost to the Children

The costs described in Chapter 17 apply in full. Children in these households observe a family with no spoken difficulty, and they learn the same repertoire. In particular, they learn that a man in difficulty does not speak about it, that a mother and father manage separately, and that a household's problems are handled in silence. Sons reproduce the pattern with a reliability that the research on intergenerational transmission would predict; daughters frequently select partners who reproduce it.

### The Cost to the Man's Capacity

The final cost is practical and is frequently overlooked in accounts of marriage. A man who cannot discuss his difficulties operates with less information, less perspective, and fewer corrections. His wife is the person best placed to identify deterioration — the change in his sleep, the loss of interest, the short temper, the alcohol — because she is the person who knows him well enough to notice a change, and she is also best placed to test his accounts of situations at work, because she knows the history. A man who conceals from his wife therefore loses a source of reality-checking at precisely the moment when his judgement is most impaired by stress, which is one of the routes by which men in this arrangement make consequential decisions badly.

::: keypoint
A marriage in which a man's difficulties are unspoken costs both partners, and it is maintained by both. Neither the man's silence nor the wife's inadequate response is the cause; the cause is a sequence of ordinary exchanges in which each party received insufficient information and drew a conclusion.
:::

## 14.4 How to Be Understood: A Structured Approach

This section sets out a method. It is written for a man who has decided, having read the previous sections, that the arrangement is worth one serious attempt rather than another decade of silence.

### The Four Conditions for a Successful Disclosure

**Choose the purpose.** A disclosure intended to explain a change is received differently from one intended to request help, and both differ from one intended to redistribute responsibility. The purpose must be decided in advance, because a disclosure with mixed purposes is received as a complaint or a demand.

**Choose the time.** Not at the end of a working day, not in bed, not during a crisis, not in front of children, and not immediately before or after an argument. A scheduled conversation — *I would like to talk about something on Saturday morning when the children are out* — is the most reliable format, because it removes the element of ambush and gives the partner a chance to prepare.

**Choose the quantity.** One subject. Not the accumulation of twelve years. A man who intends to disclose his financial position should not disclose his financial position, his disappointment about the relationship, and his father's illness in the same conversation. The volume is what produces the emergency response described in Section 14.1.

**Choose the form.** In the register that the partner can work with. Chapter 3 established the difficulty men have in describing internal states, and this is where it has practical consequences. Three practical devices are available.

- **Use the physical as an opening.** *My blood pressure has been high and the doctor has given me three months to change something.* This is factual, verifiable, and it opens the subject without requiring the man to make an emotional claim.
- **Use the specific event.** *When the contract was cancelled in March, I lost four million naira of expected income, and I have not been able to tell you because I was ashamed.*
- **Use the sequence.** *Here is what happened, here is what I did, here is where I am, and here is what I need.* This structure provides the partner with an account she can follow, and it prevents the most common failure, in which a man presents a conclusion without the material that produced it.

### What to Ask For

An explicit request is more effective than an implicit expectation, and the request should be specific and modest. Among the requests that work:

- *I need you to know this, and I do not need you to solve it tonight.*
- *I need to be able to say when a week has been bad, without it becoming a crisis.*
- *I need one conversation a month in which I say how things actually are.*
- *I need you to come to one appointment with me.*
- *I need you to stop telling your family the things I tell you, and I am telling you that I am asking for that.*

Each of these is concrete, deliverable, and checkable. None of them requires the wife to change her personality or her load.

### Handling the Response

Four responses are possible, and each requires a different handling.

**A good response.** Recognise it and reciprocate. Where the partner responds well, the next step is not to unload everything, but to establish the practice: a regular arrangement in which the material can be raised. Most successful repairs in this domain consist of a small number of conversations held consistently rather than one large one.

**A partially adequate response.** The most common outcome. The partner is concerned about the substance and responds clumsily about the form: an instruction, a correction of detail, an immediate practical solution, or a comparison with somebody else's situation. This should be accepted rather than scored. The useful reply is a direct request: *I appreciate that. What would help me more is if you simply heard it for now.* Men frequently abandon the whole attempt because the first response was not ideal, and the correction is usually one sentence.

**A difficult response.** Minimising, blaming, turning the subject to her own difficulties, or an angry response. The appropriate handling has three elements: name what occurred without accusing (*when I told you about the money, you said that I should have known better, and I stopped talking*); hold the position that the disclosure was legitimate (*I am not saying that you have not had difficulties; I am saying that this is mine and it is real*); and propose the repeat (*I would like to try again on Saturday*). Where the response is consistently difficult across several attempts, Section 14.5 applies.

**A refusal.** The partner declines the conversation, and the refusal may be explicit or expressed through avoidance, absence, or the introduction of a crisis each time the subject approaches. This is a serious finding, and it should be recorded rather than argued. Section 14.6 addresses what follows.

### The Reciprocal Obligation

One further element is essential, and its omission is the most common cause of failure in this domain. The man must ask, and must mean it: **what are you carrying that I do not know about?** A marriage contains two interior lives. The disclosure that produces a durable change is usually reciprocal, and the man who approaches his wife with his own difficulty but no interest in hers has requested a service rather than a relationship. Chapter 15 develops the mutual practice, and it is the mechanism by which the arrangements described in this chapter are most reliably changed.
