## 30.10 Common Questions Men Ask

**"I did not cry. Does that mean I am not grieving?"**

No. The pattern in which grief is experienced and managed through thinking and doing rather than through expression is common, is more frequently reported by men, and is a style rather than a deficiency. The relevant question is not whether the man has cried but whether the material has a place in his arrangements: whether it has a date, a location, an account, a recipient, and a form of conduct. Where those are absent and the presentations in Section 30.3 are present, the material is unattended regardless of how the man has appeared to himself or to others.

**"The funeral was three years ago. Am I not too late?"**

The absence of an occasion at the time is the reason the arrangements in Section 30.6 exist, and they can be constructed at any point. Men who conduct this work years after the loss report two findings consistently: the material is still available, and the delay has not reduced what the arrangements can do. The one qualification concerns the sixth pattern in Section 30.7: where the man's functioning has been substantially impaired for a long period, an assessment should accompany the arrangements rather than being deferred until they have been attempted.

**"My wife would be hurt if I said that the loss of a friend affected me like this."**

The disclosure recommended in Section 30.6 is the fact rather than the content, and it is directed at the household's arrangements rather than at the household's feelings. The form offered — *this person died and I am not all right about it* — is a statement about the man's state, and it does not compare the loss with the household's own. The men who have conducted it report, with consistency, that the household's response was relief rather than injury, because the household had been working with an inaccurate explanation of the man's temper, quietness, and unavailability for a period of months or years.

**"I cannot visit the grave or the house. The family's arrangements make it impossible."**

Then the place is the man's own: a location he associates with the person, a route, a photograph in a particular position, a workshop, an object. Section 30.4's position applies here as it does with an unattended funeral: the mechanisms that did not operate must be constructed, and the construction can be entirely the man's.

**"The person I lost is somebody I cannot talk about in my house."**

Section 30.5 applies. The requirement is one recipient whose confidentiality is an obligation rather than a favour, a private place, the material conducted with reference to the person rather than to the secret, and the separation of the decision about the disclosure from the decision about the loss. The last of the four is the most important and the most frequently conflated: the mourning can proceed whether or not the household is ever informed.

**"How do I know whether I need help?"**

Apply the six patterns in Section 30.7. Where the material has not varied at all, has produced no reduction over a year or more, has impaired your functioning, has become the organising fact of your account of yourself, or is being discharged into drinking, risk-taking, or any thought of ending your life, obtain an assessment. The fifth item is the most urgent and the one about which men in this material most frequently delay: persistent thinking about death or about the man's own ending is a clinical emergency and should be treated as one, with immediate professional contact rather than with private deliberation.

::: summary
**Chapter 30 in brief.** Some losses are never provided with an occasion of mourning, and the absence of acknowledgement is the factor most consistently associated with prolonged grief. Nine categories of such loss are reported by men: the death of the man's own friend, the colleague or work companion, the bereavement of a marriage that ended, the child lost before birth, the mentor, the companion from the man's cohort, the loss that occurred remotely, the person whose death was never confirmed, and the animal, home, land, or country. They share four features: the loss is real, the man is expected to continue immediately, the loss is frequently not his to display, and it is unshared between the man and his own household.

Men's grieving differs from the dominant cultural account. The grief literature distinguishes an intuitive style, in which grief is expressed as feeling and shared, from an instrumental style, in which it is managed through thinking and doing; both are common, neither is a deficiency, and the second is more frequently reported by men. Its five legitimate forms are activity, solitary thinking, physical forms, the private conversation with the deceased, and the maintenance of the bond. The two errors of interpretation belong to the man, who concludes from his own non-expression that nothing is occurring, and to the household, which reads his competence as a statement and then assigns him the management of everybody's mourning.

Grief that has no place presents in other forms: irritability, the expansion of work, drinking and other numbing, risk-taking, somatic complaints, flatness, the anniversary, the delayed arrival, and the discharge of the material into the household. Four signs indicate that the material is unattended: the subject cannot be mentioned, the account has never been delivered to anybody, the loss has no place in the man's calendar, and his conduct differs in a setting he cannot explain.

An unattended funeral — whether the funeral was not attended, had already occurred, has never been confirmed, or was prevented by restriction — requires the reconstruction of the mechanisms the occasion would have supplied: a place, a visit, the written account, a witness or an assembly, and the discharge of the obligation the absence created.

Where the loss cannot be spoken of, the requirements are structural: one recipient whose confidentiality is an obligation, a private place, the material conducted with reference to the person rather than to the secret, and the separation of the decision about disclosure from the decision about the loss. The arrangements that give grief a place are six: the date, the place, the account, the recipient, the conduct in the person's name, and the disclosure of the fact to the man's household. The work does not promise closure; it promises that the material is connected to the person and is not discharged into the man's temper, household, work, body, or drinking. Where the material does not vary, does not reduce, impairs functioning, organises the man's whole account of himself, or is being discharged into drink, risk, or thoughts of dying, an assessment should be obtained, and the last of these is an emergency.
:::

## Further Reading

- **On disenfranchised grief:** the literature on grief that is not socially recognised, including the findings on which losses are disenfranchised and why, the effects of the absence of acknowledgement on the course of grief, and the practices developed for conducting such grief.
- **On men's grief styles:** the research on instrumental and intuitive grieving patterns, including the findings on the greater frequency of the instrumental style among men, the evidence that the style is not associated with worse outcomes, and the critique of the assumption of a single correct form of mourning.
- **On prolonged and complicated grief:** the literature on prolonged grief disorder, including the diagnostic criteria, the distinction between prolonged grief and depression, the risk factors, and the treatments that have established effectiveness.
- **On continuing bonds:** the research on continuing bonds with the deceased, including the findings that the maintenance of a relationship with the deceased is common and is not associated with worse adjustment, and the critique of the prescription to let go.
- **On bereavement and substance use, and bereavement and risk:** the literature on the associations between bereavement and alcohol use, substance use, and risk-taking behaviour, including the findings on the elevated risks faced by bereaved men and the implications for assessment.
- **Practical resources:** bereavement counselling and bereavement services, including services for specific categories of loss; peer support for the bereaved, including groups for men; general practice and psychological services for assessment; and the material in Appendices K, Q, and R.
