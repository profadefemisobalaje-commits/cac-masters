## 28.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is it not a waste of a man's time to grieve a plan he abandoned twenty years ago?"**

The material is being carried whether or not it is addressed, and its carrying has costs: the continuous comparison described in Section 28.3, the deficiency by which the man's actual life is measured, and the availability of the material to be triggered by an obituary or a reunion. The work in the exercise takes about three sittings, and men report that the reduction in the incidence of the material is out of proportion to the effort. The investment is therefore not the grieving of an old plan; it is the removal of an open item that has been occupying the man's attention for a period measured in decades.

**"Everyone has dreams that did not work out. Why should mine be treated as a loss?"**

Because they are losses, and the universality of a loss does not reduce it. The universality does, however, explain why nobody offers the man anything for it, and that is precisely the reason the chapter asks him to construct the four elements himself. Furthermore, the position is not uniform: the men who carry a dead dream without any process are the ones who are occupied by it at fifty-five, whereas the men who have, in whatever form, acknowledged the loss and conducted a reckoning are generally able to describe it without the accompanying event.

**"My wife and children would feel blamed if I said that I gave something up for them."**

The framing requirement addresses this directly, and it is the element of the method that most frequently fails when it is conducted without preparation. The man must establish, before he describes the loss, that the decision was correct, that he would repeat it, and that he is not asking for it to be reopened. The case in Section 28.7 illustrates the order: the disclaimer first, the account second. Where the man is unable to state the disclaimer sincerely, the disclosure should not be made; the work of Sections 28.4 and 28.6 can be conducted with a witness outside the household.

**"What if the dream is still possible and I simply have not done it?"**

Then it is pending, and the requirement is the first step within thirty days. The chapter's position on this category is deliberately strict, because this is the position that produces the largest and most prolonged damage: a live dream that is not advanced. Men in this position frequently report that they have been *meaning to* begin for a decade, and the report is generally accompanied by the whole of the material in Chapter 24. The choice is between the step and the closing; both are legitimate; the prolongation is not.

**"I have tried to let it go and it comes back."**

Which is the expected pattern, and the reason the chapter prescribes a process rather than an attitude. The material returns on triggers, and it will continue to return so long as it has not been described, accounted for, witnessed, and marked. The measures in Section 28.4 are the ones that alter the pattern in men's own reports; the resolution to stop thinking about a matter is not among them.

**"My dream was blocked by my family. Am I supposed to say that I was prevented?"**

No, and the chapter's material on the fourth process in Section 28.2 is written for this position. The facts may be stated accurately and the requirement remains the same: the accounting of what the alternative would have cost, since the alternative in this case carried not only the dream but the rupture with the people who opposed it, and that rupture has a price that the imagined life does not include. Where the man's reading of the events is that he was prevented, the useful work is the separation of the two questions: what happened, and what the man chooses to hold about it now. The first is a matter for the record; the second is a matter for the reckoning in Section 28.6.

::: summary
**Chapter 28 in brief.** Dreams fall into eight categories — vocational, educational, entrepreneurial, migratory, creative, family and legacy, place and life, and bodily — and must be distinguished from images, from obligations, and from dreams the man never held. A real dream has a plan, belongs to the man, and can be described at the moment it was decided, the person it was told to, the step that was attempted, and the point at which it stopped. Once sorted, dreams are either dead or pending, and the two require different work.

Seven processes kill a dream quietly: the substitution of duty for the dream, in which the man repeatedly chooses his obligations and would choose them again; the narrowing produced by money, in which the income covers obligations and never plans; the failure that ended the route, whose effect is produced not by the failure but by the verdict the man attaches to it; the requirement of another person, which cannot be stated without indicting somebody; the slow erosion, in which the dream is simply not advanced and there is no event to attribute; the decision made on somebody else's timetable; and the loss of the person with whom the dream was shared. All four features of the class follow: none produces an event, each involves a decision the man would repeat, each is compatible with a functioning life, and each produces a comparison that operates continuously.

The ache of such a dream is that nobody was informed, and therefore none of the ordinary functions of acknowledgement is available. The man anticipates five responses — the practical solution, the minimisation, the accusation, the retrospective verdict, and the silence — and the silence is the most common and the most discouraging. The man does not grieve the dream for four reasons: there is no event, he chose it, he is supposed to be over it, and the loss is not of a thing but of a version of himself. Two comparisons operate continuously: with the men who went, and with the version of himself who took the path. The second is the stronger and it is rigged, since the imagined life is composed of the dream's benefits and none of its costs.

The grief is conducted without an audience through four elements: the account written in the past tense, the accounting of the reasons including the alternative's costs, the witness, and the marker. A dream that dies late — on retirement, on the last child's completion, on a bereavement, on a medical event, or on an encounter — produces four characteristic errors: attempting to resume the original form, treating the discovery as a verdict about the whole life, closing it in anger, and transferring it to a son as an assignment. Its correct handling is to establish what remains available, to distinguish the dream's content from its form, to set a deadline and a first step, and to close what is genuinely closed.

The final work is the reckoning with the life that was built instead, counted in six domains: the people who exist because of the decision, the capability accumulated, the obligations discharged, the standing in the community, the competence of the man in the present, and the life lived in the interval. It is completed with two sentences that must both be present: the man set down this and would decide the same again, and what he built instead is real and counted. A closed dream is not a regret — a regret concerns a decision believed wrong, a closed dream concerns a decision believed right whose cost is being acknowledged — and the closing does not end ambition. It frees the capacity that the open dream was consuming.
:::

2. Which of the seven processes in Section 28.2 ended the dream you carry most heavily? What does the process explain about why the loss has never been named?

## Further Reading

- **On mid-life review and the sense of time remaining:** the research on mid-life psychosocial development, including the evidence on generativity, the re-evaluation that commonly occurs in the fifth and sixth decades, and the findings on how the perception of remaining time alters motivation and priorities.
- **On disenfranchised grief:** the literature on losses that are not socially recognised or publicly mourned, including the findings on ambiguous loss, the effects of the absence of social acknowledgement, and the practices that have been developed for conducting such grief.
- **On possible selves and counterfactual thinking:** the research on possible selves and on counterfactual reasoning, including the findings on the effects of comparing an actual life with the imagined alternatives to it, and the conditions under which counterfactual thinking is useful rather than corrosive.
- **On the achievement of goals and their abandonment:** the literature on goal disengagement and re-engagement, including the evidence that the capacity to abandon an unattainable goal and to identify a substitute is associated with better wellbeing than persistence in the unattainable goal.
- **On retirement and identity:** the research on retirement adjustment and identity, including the findings on the mid-life and retirement transitions, the losses that accompany the ending of an occupation, and the factors that predict a better adjustment.
- **Practical resources:** vocational and career guidance services, including those for adults; adult education and part-time qualification routes; men's groups and discussion groups in which the material can be witnessed; financial and retirement planning services; and the material in Appendices I, K, and N.
