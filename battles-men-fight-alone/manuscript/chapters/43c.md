## 43.4 Challenging Harmful Gender Stereotypes

The section's subject is the content of the transmission, and it proceeds carefully, because the phrase *gender stereotype* is used in public argument as a term of abuse and the reader's response to it is frequently determined before he has examined the material.

The section therefore begins with a distinction that the rest of the section depends upon.

**A role is a set of expectations attached to a position, and roles are necessary.** A father has a role in his household, a teacher has a role in a school, a man has a role in his community. The expectations attached to the role — provision, protection, presence, competence, accountability — are not stereotypes, and nothing in this section proposes their abandonment. The material of Chapter 41.2's five clauses established the same point: the clauses' obligations are sound, and what makes them harmful is the addition they carry.

**A stereotype is a generalisation about a category of persons that is applied to every member of the category without regard to the individual, and that is maintained independently of the evidence.** The stereotype's harm is not that it describes a difference. It is that it prescribes a conduct, forbids an alternative, and punishes the individual who does not conform.

The distinction matters because the chapter does not propose that boys should be raised without standards, without roles, or without a clear account of what is expected of them. It proposes that the specific propositions listed below should be removed from the curriculum, and that the obligations of manhood should be retained, which is the material of Chapter 41.6.

### The Five Harmful Propositions

The case material and the research literature identify five propositions that are transmitted to boys in the ordinary course of their upbringing, and each one is harmful in a specific and identifiable way. They are stated here in the form in which boys receive them.

**Proposition One: Endurance is the whole of strength.** The proposition is transmitted by the sentences *boys do not cry*, *take it like a man*, *do not be a woman about it*, and *pain is part of being a man*. Its harm is not that it values endurance. Its harm is that it identifies endurance with strength and therefore removes every other competence from the definition: the man who endures and cannot name, cannot regulate, cannot ask, and cannot accept help is, on this proposition, the strong man. The material of Chapter 39.3's false choice is the adult form, and Section 39.4's four competences are the correction.

**Proposition Two: Expression is weakness.** The proposition is transmitted by ridicule, by the withdrawal of approval, and by the equation of speech with femininity. Its harm is that it converts the boy's ordinary need for an audience into evidence of his inadequacy. The adult form is Chapter 14.1's man who cannot state a need in his marriage and Chapter 27.3's man who has nobody to call.

**Proposition Three: Dominance is authority.** The proposition is transmitted by the household's tolerance of the father's anger, by the community's respect for the man who does not tolerate contradiction, and by the equation of a man's worth with his control of his household. Its harm is that it teaches the boy that authority is conducted by the reduction of other people's position rather than by responsibility for it, which is the material of Chapters 18 and 31. The correction is Chapter 41.2's fourth clause and its addition.

**Proposition Four: Sexual conquest is proof of worth.** The proposition is transmitted in peer groups, in entertainment, in jokes, and in the ordinary conversation of young men, and its harm is that it locates a boy's value in a performance conducted before an audience of other men, at the expense of the parties involved and at the expense of the boy's capacity for intimacy. The adult forms are Chapter 24.2's man whose identity is conducted through his attachments and Chapter 13.4's inability to grieve a relationship that was conducted as a performance.

**Proposition Five: Violence is the resolution of insult.** The proposition is transmitted by the instruction to hit back, by the celebration of the man who responds to provocation with force, and by the equating of restraint with cowardice. Its harm is specific and grave: it converts a boy's ordinary shame into a physical act, and it produces the outcomes that the case material of Chapters 12 and 16 records. The correction is not pacifism and it is not the removal of a boy's capacity to defend himself or others. It is the distinction between defence and retaliation, and the supply of the regulated alternatives that Section 43.5's fourth competence describes.

The reader should note what these five propositions have in common. Each one takes a legitimate obligation — endurance, self-control, authority, desire, courage — and converts it into a rule that requires the boy to suppress a part of himself or to reduce another person. The chapter's correction is therefore not the rejection of the obligation. It is the removal of the conversion.

### Where the Propositions Are Enforced

A proposition survives because it is enforced, and the enforcement is conducted in five locations. The reader who wishes to challenge the propositions must address the locations, because a challenge conducted in the household and not in the peer group will fail within a term.

**The first location is the peer group.** The finding in the case material is that the peer group is the most effective enforcer of the five propositions and the least accessible to adult correction. Its instrument is ridicule, and its timing is the period between roughly ten and eighteen, when the boy's standing in the group is the central fact of his social life. The correction is not the removal of the peer group. It is the supply of a peer group in which the propositions are not the currency, which is the material of Section 43.7's fourth principle.

**The second location is entertainment and the media.** The boy's models are supplied in quantity by film, music, sport, and social media, and the models are selected for their dramatic value rather than their conduct. The correction is not prohibition, which generally fails and frequently increases the material's appeal. It is commentary: the adult who watches with the boy and asks a specific question — *what did that man do when he was afraid?* — converts the material into a lesson rather than allowing it to operate as a curriculum.

**The third location is language.** The propositions are carried in ordinary speech: *stop crying like a girl*, *man up*, *he is soft*, *do not behave like a woman*. The language is frequently used without any conscious endorsement of the proposition it carries, and its effect is cumulative. The correction is the household's deliberate vocabulary, which is the material of Section 43.6's first design principle.

**The fourth location is the institution.** Schools, associations, workplaces, and community bodies enforce the propositions through their arrangements: the boys' assembly that addresses conduct and the girls' assembly that addresses emotion, the sports programme that treats injury as character, the disciplinary system that punishes a boy's disclosure as attention-seeking. The correction is the audit supplied in Section 43.8.

**The fifth location is the household's own arrangements.** The household enforces the propositions through its allocation of chores, its tolerance of the boy's anger and its intolerance of his fear, its treatment of the daughter's expression and the son's, and its account of what a man is. The correction is the material of Section 43.3 and it is conducted over years.

### Challenging Without Lecturing

The section now turns to the practice, because the reader who has accepted the material will frequently conduct it badly, and the bad conduct is one of the reasons the propositions survive.

The finding in the case material, and the common experience of teachers and parents, is that a challenge delivered as a lecture produces three effects and none of them is the intended one. The boy learns the vocabulary of the challenge without its practice. He learns that the adult's position is a position rather than a conduct. And he learns to conduct the propositions outside the adult's observation.

The section therefore states six practices, each of which is a conduct rather than an argument.

**Practice One: Name the proposition, not the person.** The sentence *that is the rule that says a man cannot be afraid, and it is not true* challenges the proposition. The sentence *you are being sexist* challenges the boy, and the boy will defend himself rather than examine the rule. The distinction is the material of Chapter 41.4's separation of the standard from the man, applied to the boy.

**Practice Two: Supply the alternative at the moment of the challenge.** A challenge that removes a rule without supplying a replacement leaves the boy without a standard, and he will return to the rule because it is the only one available. The challenge therefore includes the replacement: *a man can be afraid and still do what is required; here is how that is done.* The replacement should be concrete, brief, and conducted by an example the boy can observe.

**Practice Three: Separate the act from the identity.** The boy who has hit another boy has conducted an act, and the act can be examined. The boy who is told that he is violent has been given an identity, and identities are defended. The practice is the material of Chapter 29.4's distinction between the act and the man, and it is the condition on which a boy can repair.

**Practice Four: Correct the language in the moment and without a lecture.** The sentence *we do not say that in this house, and here is why* is sufficient. It is short, it is stated once, and it is repeated every time the language appears. The finding in the case material is that the repetition matters more than the eloquence, and that the household which corrects consistently for a year changes its ordinary speech.

**Practice Five: Conduct the counter-example.** The boy's propositions are supported by models, and they are weakened by models of a different kind. The counter-example should be a real man known to the boy: the uncle who wept at his father's funeral and conducted the family's arrangements afterwards; the coach who admitted his error; the senior colleague who sought treatment and continued to work; the man who declined a fight and was not diminished by the declining. The counter-example is more effective than the argument, and it must be true, because boys detect the manufactured example with precision.

**Practice Six: Protect the boy who deviates.** The propositions are enforced by the punishment of boys who do not conform: the boy who does not fight, the boy who cries, the boy who is artistic, the boy who is gentle, the boy who is not interested in sport. The adult's protection of that boy is the most powerful challenge available, because it demonstrates in a single act that the proposition is not universal. The protection does not require a confrontation with the peer group. It requires the adult to supply the boy with a setting in which his deviation is ordinary, and to state to him, once and clearly, that the deviation is not a defect.

### The Boy Who Does Not Fit

The section closes with the boy who does not fit, because he is the chapter's most urgent case and the one most frequently failed.

The boy who does not fit is the boy whose temperament, interests, capacities, or attachments do not match the local account of what a boy is. He may be gentle, bookish, artistic, physically small, uninterested in competition, sensitive to noise, slow to speak, or attracted to what the community considers the wrong things. The propositions described above do not merely fail to describe him. They define him as deficient, and they supply his peers with a licence to enforce the definition.

The case material records three outcomes with frequency. The first is the boy who complies: he suppresses his temperament, performs the required conduct, and produces the adult of Chapters 21 and 39 who cannot locate his own preferences. The second is the boy who resists and is punished: he is excluded, ridiculed, and frequently physically harmed, and his adult position is the material of Chapter 16's rejection. The third is the boy who finds one adult or one peer group that receives him, and this outcome is the one the chapter's material is designed to produce.

The adult's task is stated in four acts.

**The first act is to state the boy's position accurately and without apology.** The sentence *you are quiet, and that is not a fault* is more valuable than any programme, because it supplies the boy with an account of himself that is not the peer group's.

**The second act is to supply a setting in which the boy's capacities are useful.** The gentle boy is frequently excellent with animals, with younger children, with the elderly, with detail, with craft. The setting converts the deviation into a competence, and the conversion is the material of Chapter 24.5's reconstruction of identity.

**The third act is to address the enforcement.** Where the boy is being harmed, the adult's task is not to advise the boy to be more resilient. It is to change the arrangement: the class, the group, the supervision, the route home. The case material is unambiguous on this point, and the material of Chapter 16.5's rules of recovery applies.

**The fourth act is to keep the door open.** The boy who does not fit frequently does not disclose his position, because he has learned that disclosure produces further enforcement. The adult's task is to keep the availability stated, in ordinary language, at intervals, and to respond well when the disclosure eventually arrives, which may be years later.

The reader should note the chapter's position on the boy who does not fit and the book's central message. The message is that men can carry serious responsibilities without being expected to endure emotional suffering in isolation. The boy who does not fit is the point at which the message is either transmitted or contradicted, because he is the person on whom the culture's account of manhood is most directly enforced. An adult who protects him is conducting the book's argument in the only form that a boy can read: an adult's conduct, in his presence, at the moment of his difficulty.
