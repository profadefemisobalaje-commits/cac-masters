## 10.3 The Emotional Burden of Being Financially Indispensable

The financial dimension of provider overload is only half of the subject. The other half is emotional, and it is this half that men find hardest to describe.

### Indispensability

A man who is financially indispensable occupies a position that has four consequences, and all four operate simultaneously.

**He cannot be ill.** A man whose income supports nine people cannot afford a period of incapacity. Acute illness is manageable; chronic illness is not, because the reduction in income coincides with the period of greatest need. Men in this position frequently describe a specific and frightening calculation: what would happen to the household if I were unable to work for three months? The calculation usually produces an answer that no insurance arrangement would cover.

**He cannot rest without cost.** Any reduction in working hours is experienced as a reduction in protection. Men describe the same pattern repeatedly: rest taken on a Saturday is compensated for by work on Sunday, so that the rest produces no reduction in strain but adds guilt to it.

**He cannot take risks with his position.** Changing employment, starting a business, refusing additional responsibility, or declining an opportunity requires a capacity to absorb a period of lower income. A man supporting nine people does not have that capacity, which means the available options in his own life narrow precisely as his obligations grow.

**He cannot be replaced.** The circumstance that produces the greatest psychological weight is not the volume of the obligation but its concentration: the household's welfare depends on one person's continued capacity to work. This is a structural fact, and it produces a continuous, low-level dread that men describe in the same terms across countries and income levels: *if I fall, we all fall.*

### The Emotional Consequences

Four emotional consequences follow from this structure, and each is systematically under-discussed.

**Resentment.** A man maintains an internal account: what he gives, what is received, what is expected of him, and what he is permitted to need. Where the account is permanently unbalanced, resentment accumulates. Its features are specific. It is rarely expressed, because expressing it would be shameful. It is frequently directed at the wrong target: a man resents his brother but is short-tempered with his wife. And it produces a hardening that is not recognised by the man and is clearly perceived by his household.

**Fear.** Continuous anticipation of the moment at which capacity fails: the illness, the lost contract, the hospital bill that exceeds what is available.

**Isolation.** The arrangement cannot be discussed, because the disclosure would either alarm those who depend on him or produce the appearance of complaint. The older the arrangement becomes, the harder the conversation becomes, which is precisely the trap described in Chapter 1.

**A private sense of unreality.** Men in this position frequently describe a curious and difficult sensation: the sense that their own life is not entirely theirs, that they are carrying a function rather than living a life, and that they do not know what they would do if the obligations disappeared. Chapter 24 addresses this in the context of identity loss, and Chapter 40 in the context of recovering a life of one's own.

### The Effects on the Household

Provider overload produces four effects inside the man's own household, and they are worth listing because the man rarely sees them.

**Absence.** The hours required to fund the network of obligations are taken from the household: from time with children, from time with a partner, and from the domestic work of running a home.

**Irritability and withdrawal at home.** Because the strain cannot be expressed at its source — a man cannot be angry with his mother's illness or his relative's request — it is discharged where the consequences are lowest.

**The transmission of the burden to the wife.** Many women in such households absorb the consequences: they take on paid work to cover the household's shortfall while aware that the husband's income is going elsewhere, they manage the daily consequences of a reduced budget, and they carry the emotional weight of a husband who is present but unavailable.

**A model of manhood for the children.** Children in these households learn a specific set of lessons: that a man gives without limit, that needs are not expressed, that strain is carried without comment, and that the family's resources belong to whoever asks for them first. The lesson is transmitted with the same effectiveness as its older version described in Part One.

### What Is Often Mistaken for Virtue

A difficulty in addressing this subject is that provider overload presents as virtue. A man who supports nine people is admired, described as responsible, and held up as an example. This admiration is genuine and it has a cost: it establishes the arrangement as a standard that the man cannot question without appearing to diminish himself, and it removes the possibility of his raising the subject in any ordinary conversation.

Two clarifications assist. First, gratitude for the man's contribution and criticism of the arrangement are not contradictory; both can be held at once. Second, the alternative to unlimited liability is not refusal. It is a defined contribution: a figure, a duration, a plan, and a review — which is what a serious institution would require before it entered a comparable obligation.

## 10.4 Setting Boundaries Without Abandoning Responsibility

The practical work of this chapter is the introduction of boundaries into an arrangement where none has existed, and the work is difficult for three reasons that must be acknowledged in advance.

**The first boundary is always the hardest.** Where no limit has previously existed, the first *no* is experienced by everyone as a rupture rather than an adjustment. It will be interpreted as a change in the man's character, a sign that he has become proud, and, in some cases, as a betrayal.

**Boundaries are easier to establish when they are general rather than particular.** *I now give each sibling a fixed amount once a month, and I am not able to do more than that* is easier to sustain than a case-by-case refusal, because it applies to everyone equally.

**Boundaries require disclosure of capacity.** A boundary that is asserted without explanation invites a challenge. A boundary accompanied by a factual statement of capacity — income, obligations, and the arithmetic — is difficult to argue with, because it is not a matter of willingness.

### The Method

The method has five steps.

**Step One: Establish the total.** Before any conversation, produce the arithmetic: total income, total obligations, and the actual allocation across the four bands described in Section 10.1. Nothing proceeds without this figure, and it should be written down.

**Step Two: Decide the allocation deliberately.** Rather than accepting the accumulated arrangement, set the allocation: what proportion of income goes to the household, to parents, to relatives, to community contributions, and — an item that men omit and which is required — to the man's own health, his retirement, and a reserve. The allocation should be a decision, with reasons, and it should be written.

**Step Three: Introduce the boundary as a general arrangement, not a series of refusals.** Two forms work well.

- *From this month I am putting family support on a fixed schedule. Here is what I can do for you. I will not be able to do more than this, and I am telling everybody the same thing.*
- *I have had to restructure my finances. I will continue to support X, and I will not be able to continue with Y from March. I am telling you early so you have time to plan.*

**Step Four: Attach the boundary to the facts rather than to your feelings.** The conversation should contain the arithmetic, stated briefly and without self-justification. Men who explain their boundaries through emotion — *I am tired; I cannot continue; nobody considers me* — find that the conversation becomes a negotiation about their attitude, whereas a conversation about figures is a negotiation about arithmetic.

**Step Five: Hold the line, and make a substitution where possible.** A boundary accompanied by an alternative is more survivable for everyone. Where a monthly subsidy is reduced, a capacity-building option, an introduction, a specific piece of assistance, or a plan to assist with a business can be offered in its place.

### The Conversations, in Order of Difficulty

Men who have made these changes report a consistent sequence, and it is worth setting out because the sequence reduces the cost.

1. **The partner.** Easiest, most important, and the foundation for everything else. The whole position is disclosed, the allocation is agreed, and a joint position is established.
2. **The community obligations.** Usually uncomplicated: the reduction of contributions is accepted, with comment, and the man's standing is essentially unaffected. Most men discover that the cost of the reduction in social terms was considerably lower than they had assumed.
3. **The distant relatives and the informal network.** Manageable with the general-arrangement formula. Some relationships reduce in warmth; in the author's experience, few are lost.
4. **Siblings and the shared obligation towards parents.** Difficult, because the conversation concerns a third party who is not present and who is not choosing.
5. **Parents.** The most difficult conversation in the sequence, and the one most carefully phrased. Two principles apply: never frame the boundary as a reduction in respect; and where the reduction concerns medical costs, involve the siblings before the conversation, so that the parent is presented with a coordinated arrangement rather than a withdrawal.

### What Not to Do

Three errors are common and avoidable.

**Do not set the first boundary in a crisis.** Boundaries introduced at the moment of a demand, when emotion is high and there is no plan, are experienced as a refusal and are frequently reversed.

**Do not set boundaries you will not hold.** A boundary abandoned under pressure is worse than no boundary, because it teaches the network that the limit is provisional.

**Do not withdraw from the community obligation entirely.** As Section 10.1 noted, the community is a form of insurance. A man who removes his participation completely removes his access to support in the moment he needs it.
