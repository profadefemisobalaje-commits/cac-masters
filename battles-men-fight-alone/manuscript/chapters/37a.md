# Learning to Rest Without Feeling Guilty {.chapter num="37" short="Learning to Rest Without Feeling Guilty"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe why rest is difficult for men, and identify the four sources of the prohibition he carries.
2. Define rest accurately, distinguish it from sleep and from the absence of work, and identify the categories of recovery that his own position requires.
3. Recognise the presentations of a man who has not rested for years, and the relationship between sustained overwork and the conditions described in this book.
4. Analyse the guilt he experiences when he rests, in the terms of the rule that produces it, and state the counter-argument in a form he can use.
5. Construct rest as an arrangement: scheduled, of the required kind, protected, and maintained through the household's and the organisation's responses.
6. Apply the exercise at the end of the chapter to determine what his own position actually requires, and to establish the arrangement with dates.
:::

## Introduction

A man who can work continuously without difficulty and who cannot stop is not demonstrating a strength. He is describing a condition, and the condition has costs that are documented, that are borne principally by his household, his body, and the people who depend on his judgement, and that are corrected by an arrangement rather than by an attitude.

This chapter concerns rest. Its subject is the specific difficulty that a large proportion of men experience with it — the inability to stop without a reason, the guilt that arrives when the work is set down, the rest that is not rest because the telephone is on the table, and the years of continuous activity that the man describes to himself and to others as dedication.

The chapter's approach has four commitments.

The first is that the difficulty is not laziness's opposite and it is not a virtue. The chapter does not treat continuous work as a defect of character; it treats it as an arrangement that most men acquire for good reasons, that is rewarded by their environments, and that has costs which the man generally does not count. The chapter's practical purpose is the accounting.

The second is that the guilt is produced by a rule rather than by a feeling. The man's guilt at resting is the operation of a specific proposition — that his worth is his output, or that he is only secure while he is producing — and the proposition is amenable to examination. Section 37.4 states the rule and Section 37.5 supplies the counter-argument, which is drawn from evidence rather than from encouragement.

The third is that rest is a component of the man's responsibilities rather than a reward for discharging them. This is the reframing on which the chapter turns, and it is not a rhetorical device: Chapter 31 established that a leader's judgement is the resource on which the people he is responsible for depend, and the research on the effects of sustained work without recovery establishes the mechanism by which that resource is degraded. A man who cannot rest is a man whose capacity, judgement, and conduct toward his household will deteriorate, and the deterioration will be observed by others before it is observed by him.

The fourth is that rest must be constructed in the man's own form and stated in his own terms. The material on men's forms of recovery in Chapter 30.2 applies: the rest that a man will maintain is generally structured, purposeful, physical, or accompanied by an activity, and the instruction that he should lie down and do nothing is generally the instruction he will not follow. The arrangements in Section 37.6 are constructed accordingly, and their requirement is that the rest be scheduled and protected rather than earned.
