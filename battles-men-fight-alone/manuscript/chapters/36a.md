# Healing the Wounded Man {.chapter num="36" short="Healing the Wounded Man"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define healing in the terms used in this book, and distinguish it from the four things it is commonly mistaken for.
2. Conduct an inventory of the wounds he carries, in the categories that research and practice identify, and identify those that are still operating.
3. Explain the four conditions that the evidence identifies as necessary for recovery, and assess which of them he has and which are absent.
4. Describe the sequence of the work — stabilisation, the account, mourning, repair, and reconnection — and the reason the sequence matters.
5. Recognise the conditions for which professional treatment is required, and describe what the available treatments consist of, in plain terms.
6. Apply the exercise at the end of the chapter to construct the arrangements for his own recovery, with dates.
:::

## Introduction

The material in the preceding chapters has identified the wounds and the positions. This chapter concerns their repair, and its first task is a definition, because the word is used loosely and because four of its common meanings are obstacles rather than aids.

**Healing, in this book, means that a wound has been given an arrangement in which it is held, and that its operation in the man's life has been reduced.** It does not mean that the wound has been removed, that its consequences have been reversed, or that the man has ceased to carry it. It means that the wound no longer determines his conduct, his relationships, his judgement, and his household's arrangements in the manner described in the preceding parts of this book, and that the man has a place to put it.

The consequences of this definition are worth setting out at the beginning.

**It is achievable.** Men who find the word *healing* difficult because it appears to promise a condition they do not believe in should note that the definition concerns operation and not removal. The standard is not the absence of the wound; it is the reduction of what the wound does.

**It is measurable.** The operation of a wound can be observed: in the man's sleep, temper, drinking, avoidance, judgement, and conduct toward the people closest to him. A reduction is therefore not a matter of the man's opinion, and the exercise at the end of the chapter supplies the measures.

**It requires arrangements rather than resolve.** The book's central mechanism applies in full: a wound that has no arrangement operates somewhere, and the four conditions of Section 36.4 are the available arrangements rather than a description of an attitude.

**It takes years and it begins in weeks.** The two statements are both true and are not in conflict. The work has an identifiable sequence whose first steps can be taken within a week; the material's full conduct occupies a period measured in years, and the men who have conducted it describe the change in decade terms: not the year in which the material stopped, but the years in which its operation reduced.

The chapter's approach has four commitments.

The first is that the material must be approached in the sequence the evidence supplies. The chapters of this book have been arranged to that end: the practical arrangements of Part IV, the household material of Part V, the identity material of Part VI, the social material of Part VII, the loss material of Part VIII, the load material of Part IX, and the safety material of the preceding chapter. A man who attempts the mourning of a loss before the safety of his position is established will frequently surface the material and then withdraw from it, which produces the second concealment described in Chapter 9.

The second is that the process is conducted in the man's own form. Chapter 30.2 established the legitimate forms of a man's grieving; the same finding applies here. The conditions of Section 36.4 can be met through arrangements as various as the written account, the physical work, the group of men, the professional relationship, the trade association, the mentorship of a younger man, the arrangements of the household, and the conduct described in Chapter 29.6.

The third is that the reader should be told plainly what requires a professional. Section 36.6 supplies the conditions and the treatments, and the chapter takes the position that a man's refusal of professional treatment for a condition that requires it is not a form of strength but an arrangement with a known cost, borne principally by the people who depend on him.

The fourth is that recovery is not a private achievement. The evidence on recovery is consistent on this point and the case material in this book is consistent with it: the men who recover are overwhelmingly the men who had arrangements — one person, one group, one professional, one household that knew the position, one thing to build. A man's recovery is frequently the work of several people, and the chapter's practical task is the construction of the arrangements rather than the improvement of the man's solitary resolve.
