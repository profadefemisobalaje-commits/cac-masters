## 1.7 Illustrative Case: Two Years in the Life of a Man Who Was Managing

::: casebox
**The following case is composite.** It draws on patterns reported repeatedly in counselling practice, occupational health, and family research. It is not the record of any individual, and the names, places, and details have been constructed for teaching purposes.

**Daniel, fifty-two, is a district manager for an agricultural inputs company.** He supervises eleven staff across four towns, has four children, two of them in tertiary institutions, and is the eldest surviving son in a family of seven siblings. His father died nine years ago; his mother lives in the family house, which he maintains. He is the person his siblings telephone when there is a hospital bill, a school fee, a land dispute, or a funeral to organise. He has never missed a month's salary for his own household, and he is, by any reasonable standard, a successful man.

**Year one.** In March, a key supplier changes its credit terms and Daniel's branch loses roughly a third of its working margin. He does not tell his wife, because he judges that there is nothing she can do and that telling her would only frighten her. In July, he borrows from a cooperative society at an interest rate he knows is punishing, and begins to service it quietly. In September, one of his children is placed on academic probation. He is furious in a way that surprises him, and afterwards he sits in his car for forty minutes. In December he attends three funerals in eleven days, including that of a cousin his own age, and he does not cry at any of them; he notices this and files it away as a fact about himself.

**Year two.** By February, Daniel is waking at half past three in the morning with his heart going hard, and is unable to get back to sleep. By April, he has stopped attending the monthly meeting of the community association that he once helped to found, offering transport problems as his reason. In June, his wife asks him, in an ordinary voice, whether he is angry with her. He says no, and is telling the truth as he understands it. In August, a young colleague, joking, says that the district manager has become "a quiet man." In September, Daniel is short-tempered with a customer in a way that is entirely out of character, and his supervisor speaks to him about it. In October, on a Tuesday afternoon, driving between two towns, he pulls off the road, sits with the engine off, and is unable to say afterwards how long he remained there.

At no point in these two years does Daniel seek help, tell his wife the truth about the finances, or speak to a friend about the four o'clock darkness that has become a permanent feature of his life. Nobody in his family, workplace, or community believes that anything significant is wrong. His supervisor's comment is the closest anyone comes, and it concerns a symptom — rudeness to a customer — rather than the situation producing it.

A reader may find it easy to diagnose this case and difficult to feel it. The diagnosis is straightforward: prolonged concealment, chronic stress, probable depressive symptoms presenting as irritability and insomnia, unresolved grief, and provider overload. But Daniel is not a man who is failing. He is a man who is doing everything he believes is required of him, and concealing the cost, because he has concluded that the cost is his to bear.

The instructive element of the case is the mechanism, which is set out in Figure 1.5. Daniel never made a decision to keep a secret. He made a series of decisions, each of which seemed at the time to be the considerate one, and which together produced a private crisis that lasted two years and involved the progressive loss of sleep, temper, social participation, pleasure, and self-recognition.

![From event to cost. Each stage in the sequence is experienced privately and justified to the man himself; none of them appears to an observer as a change in behaviour until the later stages.](/figures/fig-01-05.png){width=86%}

Four features of this pathway deserve to be fixed in the reader's mind, because they recur in every part of this book.

**The decisions were not made once, but continuously.** There was no moment at which Daniel resolved to live a double life. There were, instead, perhaps two hundred occasions on which not mentioning something was easier than mentioning it.

**Each individual decision was defensible.** To refrain from alarming a spouse who cannot change a supplier's terms is not cowardice. It is a judgement, made without the benefit of knowing that it would be repeated two hundred times and would end by excluding her from the most consequential period of their married life.

**The costs appeared in a fixed order.** Sleep first, then temper, then withdrawal, then loss of pleasure, then a kind of absence that the man himself can describe but not explain. This ordering is so common in reports of prolonged male concealment that it deserves to be treated as a warning sequence. When a man notices that he is sleeping badly and is becoming unusually short-tempered, and he has a private burden he has told nobody about, he should treat the sleep and the temper as early warning instruments rather than as unrelated annoyances.

**The system concealed him effectively.** Note that the concealment was not maintained by Daniel alone. His wife accepted his explanation about work. His supervisor addressed the symptom. His colleague made a joke. His siblings continued to make requests, because requests were always being made and were always met. Everyone's behaviour was reasonable, and the sum of all that reasonable behaviour was a man disappearing inside his own life without anyone raising an alarm.

## 1.8 Practical Exercise: The Two Accounts

This exercise is private. Do not do it in company. It takes about forty minutes, and it should be done in writing, on paper or in a document that nobody else will read. Writing matters: thinking produces conclusions, writing produces material.

### Step One: The Public Account

Draw a line down the centre of a page. On the left, write the heading **What people know about my life**. List between eight and fifteen facts that an attentive observer — a wife, a colleague, a brother — could verify. Keep to facts about your roles and your record: employment, family, responsibilities met, assistance given, obligations discharged.

### Step Two: The Private Account

On the right-hand side, under the heading **What nobody knows**, write down, in whatever order they come, the items that never enter a conversation. Do not aim for completeness, and do not worry about the order. It may help to prompt yourself with the following seven questions.

1. What worry occupies more of my attention than anything else, and who knows about it?
2. What would I not want my family to read if they opened my phone or my bank statement?
3. What have I lost in the last five years that I have never properly mourned?
4. What did somebody say to me, at some point in my life, that I have never repeated aloud?
5. What am I pretending to be confident about?
6. What would I say about my own life if I were certain that nobody would be hurt by it?
7. If my sleep, my temper, and my appetite have changed in the last year, what do I think is causing it?

Most men produce between five and twelve items. Some produce two; some produce twenty. The number is not the point.

### Step Three: The Gap

Look at the two lists side by side. Now answer three questions in writing, in at least a paragraph each.

- **Which single item in the right-hand column carries the most weight?** Not the most dramatic — the heaviest. The one that, if it were resolved or shared, would change the most.
- **What have I told myself would happen if I spoke about it?** Write the prediction in full, including what you imagine the other person would say, and what would change afterwards. Be precise; vague fears cannot be examined.
- **Whom would I tell if I decided to tell one person, and what stops me?**

### Step Four: The Test

Choose the smallest possible action from what you have written. Not the decisive conversation, and not a confession. One of the following will usually fit.

- Sending one message to one person: *Are you free to talk this week? I have something on my mind.*
- Writing the first paragraph of the item you identified in Step Three, and keeping it for a week before deciding what to do with it.
- Making an appointment with a physician, a counsellor, or a trusted professional — Appendix Q explains how — without requiring yourself to know in advance what you will say.
- Choosing one area of your life in which you will stop answering *I'm fine* and begin answering honestly: *I'm managing, but there is one thing that has been weighing on me.* Note that this last option does not require disclosing the thing itself, only signalling that it exists — a step that many men find to be the decisive one.

### Step Five: A Note on Difficulty

If this exercise produced more distress than you expected, that is informative rather than alarming. It usually indicates that at least one item in the private account is carrying a great deal of charge, and it is worth speaking to a qualified professional before proceeding further with it. Chapters 5, 6, and 34 discuss the point at which self-directed work should be supplemented by clinical support, and Appendix B provides a structured way to assess what you may be dealing with.
