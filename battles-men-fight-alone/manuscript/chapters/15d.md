## 15.8 Practical Exercises

Three exercises are set out below. They are ordered by difficulty, and the first should be completed before the second.

### Exercise One: The Arrangement Audit

Complete alone, in about forty-five minutes, and in writing.

**Part A: The distribution of load.** Across two ordinary weeks, record for each of the following: who does it, how many hours it takes, and whether the distribution was ever agreed or has simply evolved.

| Area | Who does it | Hours per week | Agreed or evolved? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Household income | | | |
| Bill payment and record-keeping | | | |
| Food: purchase and preparation | | | |
| Children: schooling, appointments, homework, transport | | | |
| Cleaning and household maintenance | | | |
| Extended-family obligations and communication | | | |
| Care of elderly relatives | | | |
| Arrangements for visits, ceremonies and travel | | | |
| One-to-one time between the couple | | | |

**Part B: The unspoken materials.** List the matters connected with the marriage that you have never raised or have raised once and abandoned. For each, note whether it is a settled feature of your life or something that is accumulating.

**Part C: The ledger.** Where you have been keeping an internal account of what you have given, write it out. Be specific and include the dates and the amounts, and include what you believe you have received in return.

**Part D: The causes.** From the eight causes of dissatisfaction in Section 15.3, mark the two that are most present in your marriage, and write one sentence under each about what the corresponding remedy would require of you.

### Exercise Two: The Structured Conversation

This is the exercise in Section 15.4, conducted in full. Choose one subject from Part B of Exercise One, prepare one proposal, arrange the time and place, and hold the conversation. Then complete the record:

- The subject, in one sentence:
- What I said, in my own account:
- What she said, in her own account, as she stated it:
- What was agreed, specifically:
- The review date:
- What I will do differently at the next conversation:

Two notes on the first attempt. It is normal for the first structured conversation to be awkward, and awkwardness is not an indication that the approach is unsuitable. And where the first attempt produces an argument rather than an agreement, the correct response is not abandonment but the application of the stopping rule and the scheduling of the continuation, which is the procedure the framework was designed to protect.

### Exercise Three: The Joint Obligation Statement

This exercise is conducted with the partner and takes about two hours. It produces the written arrangement that ended the case in Section 15.7.

**Step One.** Each party writes out, separately, the complete list of obligations, inside and outside the household: payments, commitments of time, relatives, standing arrangements, debts, and support of any kind.

**Step Two.** The lists are exchanged and read in full, without comment.

**Step Three.** Each obligation is classified by joint agreement into one of three groups.

- **Continue:** the obligation stays as it is, and its amount and period are recorded.
- **Renegotiate:** the obligation continues in a reduced form, or in the same form for a defined period, with the change to be requested from the relevant party.
- **Conclude:** the obligation will end, and the date and the method of informing the relevant party are recorded.

**Step Four.** The statement is written out in one document, in a single page of the agreed items, and both parties keep a copy. It is reviewed at a fixed date, not more than twelve months later.

The purpose of the exercise is not the reduction of the household's obligations, although that is frequently a result. It is the conversion of a set of private arrangements into a shared position, which is what the case in Section 15.7 demonstrates. Where a couple cannot complete the exercise because the obligations are in dispute, the dispute is the finding, and it identifies either the specific matter requiring mediation or the need for professional help.

## 15.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"We have not had a serious conversation in years. Where do I begin?"**

With the smallest possible unit: one subject, one conversation, one agreed change. Men frequently attempt a comprehensive review of the marriage as their first move, and the attempt confirms the conclusion that conversation is impossible. A single subject, a specific proposal, and a defined review date is the correct first step, and its purpose is not to resolve the marriage but to establish the practice.

**"Is it possible to fix a marriage after twenty years of this arrangement?"**

Improvement is possible and transformation is not usually what occurs. What men report after a year of consistent work is a marriage with a working channel in it: a weekly conversation, a reduction of accumulated grievance, a settlement of the largest unresolved matter, and remaining differences that both parties have acknowledged. On that basis, many men describe their marriages as substantially better, and the standard against which they are judging is not an ideal marriage but the arrangement they had.

**"My wife says she wants out. What do I do?"**

Take it seriously and do not respond with an attempt to persuade her that she does not mean it. The productive sequence is: obtain an account of what has produced the position, without arguing; decide what you are willing to change and state it specifically; propose counselling, either jointly or individually; and obtain legal advice about your position and your children's, quietly, so that your subsequent decisions are not made in ignorance. Chapter 13 applies where the marriage ends, and the guidance about conducting the process without damaging the children applies from the first day.

**"How do I raise a problem without it becoming a fight?"**

By using the framework in Section 15.4, and specifically by following three rules: one subject, a scheduled time, and a proposal rather than a complaint. The rule that men find most effective is the one about beginning with the man's own position, since a statement that is partly against the man's own interest changes the character of the exchange, and it is difficult for the other party to receive it as an attack.

**"Should I tell her about the money problems I have kept from her?"**

Yes, and it should be done deliberately, with preparation: the full account, in sequence, with the figures, the causes, the steps already taken, and what you are asking for. Chapter 9 and the disclosure plan in Section 9.8 provide the structure. Two cautions: the disclosure should include everything relevant, since a partial disclosure discovered later is more damaging than the original concealment; and it should be delivered at a scheduled time, not during a crisis, and not in a form that requires her to manage your shame as well as the household's finances.

**"What if the problem is that I married the wrong person?"**

The question is legitimate and it is more useful when made specific. The classification in Section 15.3 allows a man to distinguish a difference in temperament, which can be accommodated; a conflict of priorities, which can be negotiated; an injury, which can be addressed; and an incompatibility at the level of the marriage's purpose, which frequently cannot be resolved. Where the honest finding is the last of these, the decision framework in Section 14.6 applies, and the appropriate next step is individual counselling rather than a decision made alone.
