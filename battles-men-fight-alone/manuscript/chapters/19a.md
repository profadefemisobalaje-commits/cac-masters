# The Son Who Became a Man Too Early {.chapter num="19" short="The Son Who Became a Man Too Early"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the circumstances in which a boy's childhood ends prematurely, and identify the forms that premature adulthood takes.
2. Analyse the weight of early responsibility, including the specific demands it places on a child and the resources it removes.
3. Explain the pattern in which a child is required to be strong for the family, including the mechanisms by which the requirement is imposed and maintained.
4. Describe the documented long-term effects of parentification and early responsibility in men, and identify them in his own history.
5. Apply a structured method for reclaiming the developmental territory that was lost, including the specific work that can be done in adulthood.
6. Conduct the work of addressing the boy inside the man, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

There is a category of man who is recognisable within a few minutes of conversation, and he is usually the most capable person in the room. He is composed, organised, and reliable. He manages situations that other people find unmanageable. He has a solution for every problem presented to him, and he takes responsibility for outcomes that are not his. He is frequently described as having been *old for his age* since childhood.

Ask him about his childhood, and a different pattern appears. He was the eldest. His father left, died, or worked away. His mother was ill, or overwhelmed, or managing six children on her own. There was no money, and somebody had to work. A sibling required care. A family dispute required representation. A business required running. He was between eleven and seventeen when he took up the position, and he has held it ever since.

This chapter addresses the men who became adults before they were adults, and it proceeds from a finding that surprises many of them: the costs of premature adulthood are not primarily material. The man who became responsible at fourteen frequently achieves a great deal, and his competence is genuine. What he loses is developmental territory that is difficult to obtain later: the experience of being looked after, the practice of play without purpose, the capacity to be uncertain in front of adults, the permission to fail without consequence, and, most importantly, the chance to be a child in a household that contained adults.

The chapter is careful about one thing. Premature responsibility is not a pathology to be deplored; in many households it is a necessity and in many men it produced exactly the competence that the household required. What this chapter argues is narrower and more useful: that the arrangement had costs, that the costs are identifiable in adult life, and that some of them can be addressed.

## 19.1 When Childhood Ends Too Soon

### The Occasions

The transitions that end a childhood are specific and datable, and men in this position frequently remember the exact month.

**The death of a parent.** The most common occasion. Its effect depends on which parent died, on the age of the child, and on what the household's arrangements permitted. Where the father died, the eldest son frequently acquires the household's representation and frequently the expectation of eventual provision. Where the mother died, the eldest daughter frequently acquires the household's administration, and the eldest son frequently acquires responsibility for the younger children's conduct.

**The departure of a parent.** Separation, divorce, abandonment, or long-term absence for work. The child acquires the roles vacated, and the acquisition is frequently untroubled by discussion: it is simply that the work is now available and that nobody else is present to do it.

**The illness of a parent.** Physical illness, disability, or a mental health condition. The child becomes a carer, which is a distinct pattern with its own effects, described in Section 19.4.

**Poverty and the requirement of income.** The child becomes a worker and a contributor before the end of schooling: trade, apprenticeship, farm work, transport, hawking. This is the most common form globally and the least discussed in psychological writing.

**Family conflict and its consequences.** A dispute over property, an inheritance conflict, a violent household, a conflict between the extended families. The child becomes a mediator, a messenger, a protector of siblings, or an emissary.

**Displacement.** Migration, displacement by conflict or disaster, or movement between relatives' households. The child becomes the household's outsider and adapts by becoming useful.

**A parent's own immaturity or addiction.** The child becomes the parent of the parent: managing the household, the money, and the adult's arrangements.

### The Forms Premature Adulthood Takes

What a child acquires in these circumstances falls into five categories, and men usually hold more than one of them.

1. **The provision role.** Earning, contributing, and eventually supporting the household.
2. **The care role.** Feeding, bathing, supervising, and educating younger siblings, and, in many cases, caring for an ill or disabled adult.
3. **The protection role.** Representing the family in disputes, accompanying siblings, dealing with officials, defending the household's position.
4. **The emotional role.** Absorbing the household's distress, comforting the remaining parent, holding the family together, and being the person who does not add to its problems.
5. **The surrogate spouse role.** A specific and frequently unacknowledged form in which a child, usually in adolescence, occupies the emotional or practical position vacated by an absent or incapacitated parent. Its features include the management of the remaining parent's feelings, the assumption of authority within the household, and the loss of the parental relationship's protective quality.

### The Distinguishing Feature

The feature that distinguishes premature adulthood from ordinary responsibility is not the quantity of work. Many children work hard and remain children: they are given tasks, they are supervised, they are relieved, they are corrected, and they are allowed to fail at small things.

The distinguishing feature is the **absence of an adult who carries the consequence**. A child who works and whose father remains responsible for the outcome is a working child. A child who works and who is himself responsible for the outcome — for the sibling who went hungry, for the fee that was not paid, for the house that was lost — is an adult in a child's position, and the difference is the whole subject of this chapter.

::: keypoint
Premature adulthood is defined not by the volume of work a child performed but by whether the child bore the consequence. A childhood ends when a child discovers that nobody is standing behind him.
:::

## 19.2 The Weight of Early Responsibility

The weight the child carried is not primarily the labour. It is the four things listed below, and each of them is worth examining because each produces a specific adult pattern.

### The Continuous Calculation

A child in this position acquires a permanent orientation to risk. The calculation is constant: whether the money will reach, whether the sibling will be collected, whether the parent will manage, whether the situation will hold. The calculation operates in the absence of any capacity to influence most of it, which is the specific combination that produces anxiety: high vigilance coupled with low control.

In adult life the calculation persists and is applied to everything. Men in this pattern describe an inability to be at ease in any situation whose outcome they cannot observe, and a need to monitor: to call, to check, to confirm, to keep track. The pattern is frequently regarded by the man's household as excessive solicitude and by the man as ordinary responsibility.

### The Suppression of the Child's Own Requirement

The second element is the most consequential. A child who is responsible for the household's survival does not have the option of the household's attention, and the child learns not to require it.

The learning produces the adult pattern described in Chapters 2, 3, and 4: the man who does not ask, who cannot receive, and who becomes uncomfortable when looked after. The specific link between early responsibility and the male prohibition on need is direct, and it is frequently the reason that an otherwise articulate man cannot say what he wants.

### The Premature Assumption of Authority

The third element is the assumption of authority without preparation. A child of fifteen who is required to negotiate with a landlord, to represent the family at a meeting of elders, or to decide a dispute over a relative's property acquires authority without the practice, the experience, and the errors that ordinarily accompany it.

Two adult patterns follow. The first is an unusual competence in the handling of institutions and conflict, which is frequently the reason the man is valuable to his family and his community, and which is genuine. The second is a difficulty with authority over him: men in this pattern frequently find it difficult to work under supervision, to accept a correction from a senior colleague, or to occupy a subordinate position, because their experience of authority is that of having exercised it prematurely and alone.

### The Absence of a Person to Whom the Child Could Be a Child

The fourth element is structural, and it is the one that men describe with the most feeling when they identify it. In the household that produced the premature adult, there was nobody to whom the child could be a child. The remaining parent required support rather than supplying it; the siblings required care; the relatives were auditors of the family's performance; and the community assessed the household by its conduct.

The consequence is that a particular capacity was never built: the capacity to be a dependent party in a relationship in which one is safe. Its adult effects are described throughout this book, and, in the specific form, they include the difficulty of marriage to a man in this pattern: his wife may be an excellent partner and will, at some point, find that she cannot reach him, because the relationship's structure does not include a dependent position for him.

### What Was Gained

It is important to state what the arrangement produced, because men in this pattern frequently respond to this material as though their childhood were being re-classified as damaged.

The arrangement produced: competence in difficult situations; an unusual capacity for decision under pressure; reliability as a structural feature rather than as an effort; and, frequently, the material condition of the family they were responsible for. Men who became adults at fourteen frequently rescued households, funded siblings' education, and provided their parents with a functioning old age. Those outcomes are real, they are frequently the man's central achievement, and no account of the costs should be permitted to diminish them. The purpose of the analysis in this chapter is to ensure that the costs are known, so that the man can decide what to do about them — not to establish that the arrangement was wrong.
