## 41.4 The Eight Tests

Each of the reader's clauses is examined with the following eight tests, in the order given, and the clause's classification is recorded at the end. The reader should conduct the tests in writing and should conduct them on his own clauses and not on the examples supplied here.

### Test One: What Does It Require, in Acts?

**The test.** State the clause's requirement as a list of specific acts, conducted in specific circumstances.

**Why it is first.** The clause's authority depends substantially on its abstraction. *A man does not show weakness* is a statement about a category; its actual content is a list of acts — the tears held, the sentence not stated, the face arranged, the question not asked, the appearance of the medical symptom omitted, the day's exhaustion not mentioned, the fear not named. The test converts the category into the list, and the case material records the finding that readers are frequently surprised by the length of the list and by the number of items within it that produce nothing.

**The reader's record.** The clause, the acts, and, for each act, the setting and the person concerned.

### Test Two: Is It Possible?

**The test.** Whether a human being can comply. The clauses that require the absence of a state — not to feel fear, not to grieve, not to be tired, not to be uncertain, not to be moved — are impossible, and the man's compliance with them is produced by concealment rather than by the state's absence. The finding is the material of Chapter 39.2's fourth stage: the impossible clause is held, the state continues, and the man's access to it is removed, which is the clause's cost rather than its achievement.

**The reader's record.** For each clause, whether it is possible, and, where it is not, what the man has actually been conducting in its name.

### Test Three: Whom Does It Serve?

**The test.** The clause's beneficiary: the man, his household, his organisation, his community, or the audience. The question should be asked in the direct form.

**The common findings.** The provision clause generally serves the household and would serve it better if its silence and exclusivity elements were removed, since the household's material is the reduction of the risk rather than the maintenance of the arrangement. The composure clause generally serves the audience and costs the man, which is the material of Chapter 25.6. The self-sufficiency clause generally serves the audience's regard and costs the man's health, which is the material of Chapters 31.3 and 35.2. The control clause generally serves the man's position and costs the household's capacity, which is the finding of Section 41.2's fourth clause. The achievement clause serves the audience in part and the man in part, and the test's function is to establish the proportions.

**The reader's record.** The beneficiary of each clause and, where the beneficiary is the audience, the name of the specific audience.

### Test Four: What Does It Cost, and Who Pays?

**The test.** The clause's cost, in figures and in material: the money, the hours, the health, the relationships, the opportunities, and the man's own account of his life. And the party who pays.

**The method.** The reader should conduct the test with the material of his own record: the year's hours, the medical position, the household's accounts of him from Section 39.8's questionnaire, the items of Chapter 40.5's second list, and the material of the losses in Parts VI and VII. The case material records the finding that the test is the one at which the reader's examination acquires its force, because the costs are the man's own and not the chapter's.

**The reader's record.** For each clause, the cost items and the party who pays.

### Test Five: What Does It Omit?

**The test.** The clause's silence: what the definition does not contain, and what the man therefore has no instruction about.

**The seven omissions of the inherited definition, as recorded in the case material.**

1. **What a man is to do with his grief.** The clause requires the man not to display it and supplies no instruction beyond the concealment.
2. **What a man is to do with his fear.** The same, with the addition that the fear's object is frequently the household's position.
3. **What a man is to do when his load exceeds his capacity.** The clauses require the provision and the endurance and supply no instruction about the limit.
4. **What a man owes himself.** The definition's obligations are addressed to the household, the family, the community, and the position, and the man is the instrument of them rather than a party to them. The material of Chapter 40.
5. **What a man is to do when the position collapses.** The definition is written for the position's conduct and the material of Chapter 38 is its omission.
6. **What a man is to do when he is wrong.** The clauses supply the correction of others and not the man's own; the material of Chapter 29.
7. **What a man is to do with his tenderness.** The definition's clauses address the man's protection of his household and not his affection for it; the material of Chapter 39.6's register.

**The reader's record.** The omissions he identifies in his own definition and the specific circumstances of his own life that fall into them.

### Test Six: Where Did It Come From?

**The test.** The clause's source, from the eight in Section 41.1, and the occasion of its acquisition where the man can identify it.

**The finding.** The test's function is not to disqualify the clause but to establish its provenance, which is the material of Section 41.3's third mechanism. The clauses that came from the man's own experience of a specific event are the ones that require the re-examination, and the reader's record should state the event and its date.

**The reader's record.** The source and the occasion for each clause, with the event recorded where the clause is held on the basis of the man's own evidence.

### Test Seven: What Would It Require of Me If I Applied It to Myself as I Apply It to Others?

**The test.** The consistency test, and the one that produces the largest revision in most readers.

**The method.** Apply the clause to the reader's son, his daughter, his wife, his brother, his friend, and his subordinate, and then to himself.

**The findings recorded in the case material.** *A man does not ask for help* applied to a son who is failing at something → *I would tell him to ask*. *A man does not complain* applied to a daughter in a marriage that is making her ill → *I would tell her to speak*. *A man handles his own problems* applied to a friend whose position is the man's → *I would tell him he is a fool to carry it alone*. *A man does not show weakness* applied to the man's own father in the last years of his life → *I wanted him to tell me*. The test's finding is the reason it is included: **the reader generally holds a version of the definition for other people that is different from the version he holds for himself, and the version he holds for other people is the one he can state in his own voice.**

**The reader's record.** The clause, the version applied to the named others, and the difference.

### Test Eight: What Is the Evidence, and Is It Sufficient?

**The test.** The clause's evidence, and the reasoning from it.

**The method.** For each clause held on the basis of the man's own experience, record the occasion, the parties, the outcome, and the conclusions the man drew. Then record the occasions to the contrary: the disclosure that was received well; the expression that was met with support; the assistance that was given; the request that was granted; the departure that occurred for reasons other than the man's softness; the year in which the man stated his position and the position was the better for it.

**The finding, and the chapter states it as the examination's principal mechanical instruction.** The clauses that men hold on the basis of their own experience are **very frequently held on the basis of one or two occasions, generally in adolescence, and generally of a type that the reader has never retested in the forty years since.** The specific form of the reasoning: a single event at fifteen (the disclosure to a friend that was laughed at; the tears in front of a father that were punished; the request that was refused) is converted into a rule about all parties, all settings, and all ages, and the rule is then maintained by the man's own compliance, which prevents the collection of any further evidence.

**The reader's record.** The evidence, its date, its sufficiency, and the specific retest the reader will conduct — one disclosure to one party, at one occasion, with the result recorded. The retest is the chapter's principal practical instruction and the material of Section 41.6's substitution.

### The Classification

After the eight tests, each clause is classified.

**The classification's four outcomes.**

**Outcome one: maintain.** The clause's obligation is authentic, its content is stated, and the additions identified by the tests are removed. The classification applies to the majority of the clauses that follow.

**Outcome two: revise.** The clause's obligation is authentic and its form requires alteration: the wording, the setting, the beneficiary, the acts. The material of Section 41.6.

**Outcome three: replace.** The clause's content is a prohibition on a state or an act that the man requires replaced by a specific conduct. The replacement is constructed in Section 41.6 and not adopted from a list.

**Outcome four: release.** The clause is an inheritance without a function, and the reader identifies no party it serves, no cost it is worth, and no occasion on which he would require it of anybody. The case material records the finding that the released clauses are generally the ones the man laughs at, which is not a defect in the examination but its evidence.

## 41.5 The Working Definition

The section supplies the form of the man's own definition, and the reader constructs his from it in Section 41.9. The chapter's form has four parts, and it is supplied because the case material recorded the finding that readers who were given no form produced statements that were either abstract or self-justifying.

### Part One: The Obligations

**The form.** *A man is responsible for the following, and I state their content in acts.*

The material of Parts III to V, stated as the reader's actual obligations: his children, his household, his marriage or partnership, his work, his debts, his parents where they require it, the obligations he has undertaken, and the repairs he owes.

**The requirement.** Each obligation is stated with its content in specific acts, its size, and its period. The reader's instruction: **the obligations are stated accurately and not aspirationally.** The case material records that men who stated their obligations aspirationally wrote definitions they could not conduct, which is the material of Chapter 33.2 in its definitional form.

### Part Two: The Permissions

**The form.** *A man may do the following, and it is not a departure from his obligations but a condition of conducting them.*

The material of Chapter 40.4's four permissions, in the reader's own words, together with the permissions that his own tests require: the permission to state his states, to ask, to reduce a load, to be bad at a new thing, to rest, to be wrong, to be moved, and to have a life.

**The requirement.** Each permission is stated as an act rather than as a licence: not *I may rest*, but *I may take the Saturday and state the arrangement in advance.*

### Part Three: The Prohibitions

**The form.** *A man does not do the following, and these are the acts and not the states.*

**The requirement.** The prohibitions are stated as acts, which is the examination's principal achievement: not *I do not show weakness*, but *I do not discharge my anger into my household, I do not conceal a position that concerns my wife, I do not require the household's agreement before stating my own position, and I do not continue a load past the point at which I have stated its size.*

**The finding.** The reader's prohibitions will contain the material of Chapters 19, 20, 22, 26, and 29: the violence, the contempt, the discharge, the concealment, the infidelity that is the expression of everything unstated, the failure to repair.

### Part Four: The Measure

**The form.** *A man's worth, and mine, is his whole conduct: his obligations and their conduct; his household and his presence in it; his work and its products; his conduct with the people who can do nothing for him; the repairs he has made; and his own life, which is an item and not a residue.*

The material of Chapter 25.5, in the reader's own statement.

**The requirement.** The measure is stated with at least three items that the audience cannot see, which is the test of whether the measure has been constructed or recited.
