## 44.4 Turning Personal Experience into Constructive Action

The chapter now reaches its mechanical centre. The previous sections supplied the material and the capacities. This section supplies the conversion, and its argument is stated at the outset because everything in it depends on the statement.

**Experience becomes competence only when it is used.** The proposition is simple and its consequences are not. A man may possess thirty years of difficulty and no competence, and a man may possess four years of difficulty and a great deal, and the difference between them is the number of occasions on which the material was applied to a decision, an arrangement, a relationship, or another person's difficulty.

The section's subject is the application, and it proceeds in three domains, because the case material finds that the man who applies his material in only one domain constructs an unstable arrangement.

### The First Domain: His Own Life

The first domain is the man's own conduct and arrangements, and it is the domain that must be conducted first, for the reason stated in Chapter 43.4: a man cannot transmit what he has not applied.

The application in this domain has four forms.

**Form One: The alteration of an arrangement.** The review of Section 44.2 produces design faults, and the design faults are corrected by alterations. The man who discovered that he had no written record of his commitments establishes one. The man who discovered that a single customer supplied sixty per cent of his income secures a second. The man who discovered that he had no party authorised to disagree with his plans appoints one. The alteration is small, dated, and measurable, and the case material's finding is that the man who conducts one alteration per review, twice a year, will have conducted twenty alterations in a decade and will be a substantially different man.

**Form Two: The correction of a standing pattern.** The review frequently identifies a pattern rather than an event: the man agrees to requests in the moment and regrets them afterwards; the man accepts work beyond his capacity and conceals the shortfall; the man conducts his finances alone; the man does not state a difficulty until it is a crisis. The pattern is corrected by the material of Parts X and XI, and the correction is conducted over a period rather than in a resolution.

**Form Three: The revision of the man's account of himself.** The material of Chapter 41.5's substitution exercise is the instrument. The man's account of himself after a difficulty frequently contains a clause that the difficulty installed: *I am not good with money; I cannot judge people; I am not the kind of man who can lead.* The clause is examined against the material the review produced, and it is replaced where the material does not support it. The finding is that the clause is frequently supported by one event and contradicted by the man's subsequent record, and that the examination is worth conducting because the clause governs decisions that the man does not attribute to it.

**Form Four: The conduct of the man's health.** The case material records that a significant proportion of the men who conduct this chapter's material discover, in the review, that the difficulty's cost included a health cost that was not addressed: the blood pressure that was not measured, the sleep that did not recover, the drinking that increased, the weight that changed, the back that was not examined. The application in this form is the appointment, the treatment, and the alteration of the conditions that produced the cost. The material of Chapters 23, 34, and 36 applies, and the case material's finding is that this form is the one most frequently deferred and the one with the greatest effect on the man's next two decades.

### The Second Domain: His Household

The second domain is the man's household, and the application here is the material of Chapters 17 to 20 and 43 in their constructive form.

The household application has three forms.

**The first is the transmission of the lesson without the transmission of the damage.** A man who has learned, at considerable cost, that a difficulty conducted alone becomes larger has a specific asset for his household: he can state the lesson and conduct it. The conduct is the material of Chapter 43.6's conditions, and the case material's finding is that the man's own history is frequently the most effective material he possesses, provided that it is stated proportionately and used for the household's benefit rather than for his own account.

**The second is the alteration of the household's arrangements.** The review frequently identifies a household design fault: the household's expenditure that was never stated; the arrangement in which the man's position was the household's only source; the silence about a member's difficulty; the allocation of the household's labour that had not been examined since the children were small. The alteration is conducted with the household's participation, which is the material of Chapter 42.6's third step.

**The third is the conduct of the man's own difficulty in the household's observation.** The application is Chapter 43's first channel in its household form: the man conducts a difficulty, states it, repairs what he damages, and survives it, in front of the people who will otherwise learn that difficulties are not conducted in front of anybody.

### The Third Domain: Other Men

The third domain is the man's conduct toward other men who are in difficulty, and the section's treatment of it is placed here rather than in Section 44.5 because its principle belongs to the conversion and not to the mentoring relationship.

The principle is stated as a rule. **A man's experience is available to another man only in the form in which it can be used.** The rule has two applications.

**The first application is the distinction between the account and the instrument.** A man who has survived a business failure can supply another man with an account of the failure: what happened, how it felt, what he learned. The account is frequently valuable, and it is not the same as the instrument. The instrument is the specific practice that the first man acquired: the written record, the second customer, the party authorised to disagree, the call to the bank before the third month. The case material's finding is that men in difficulty ask for the account and require the instrument, and that the man who supplies only the account is frequently experienced as inspiring and unhelpful.

**The second application is the distinction between the testimony and the warning.** A man's experience may be offered as a testimony — *I went through this, and I am still here* — which supplies hope, or as a warning — *this is what I did not see, and here is how to see it* — which supplies information. Both are useful and the second is scarcer. The case material records that men in the first weeks of a difficulty require the testimony and men in the middle months require the warning, and that a man who supplies the warning to a man in the first weeks is frequently heard as a reproach.

### The Failure Mode: Suffering as Identity

The section now addresses the failure that the conversion is prone to, because it is common and because it is frequently invisible to the man conducting it.

The failure is the conversion of the suffering into an identity. The man's difficulty becomes the thing about him that is most interesting, most valuable, and most frequently presented. It appears in his account of himself at occasions, in his professional materials, in his conversation with new acquaintances, and in his household's account of him. It supplies a standing that his present conduct does not supply, and it therefore becomes something he has an interest in maintaining.

The case material records five signs of the failure, and the reader should examine his own record against them.

**The first sign is that the account does not change.** The man's account of the difficulty in the tenth year is identical to his account in the second: the same details, the same emphasis, the same conclusion. A converted experience develops: the man's understanding of what happened alters as he applies it, and the alteration is evidence that the material is being used. An account that has not altered in eight years is frequently an account that is being performed rather than used.

**The second sign is that the difficulty is presented before the man's present competence.** The man introduces himself through the difficulty, and the parties who know him principally through it do not know what he does, what he has built, or what he is capable of.

**The third sign is that the man's present difficulties are referred to the past one.** The sentence *this is like the time I lost the business* is used to explain every subsequent setback, and the explanation removes the present event's specific information. The mechanism is the material of Chapter 29.5's pattern of recurrence.

**The fourth sign is that the man resists the resolution of the difficulty's consequences.** Where the difficulty's consequences are capable of resolution — the debt that could be settled, the qualification that could be obtained, the relationship that could be repaired, the treatment that could be undertaken — the man does not conduct the resolution, and the case material's finding is that the non-conduct preserves the account. The sign is subtle and it is the most important: **a man whose difficulty has become his identity has an interest in the difficulty's continuance.**

**The fifth sign is that the man's account requires other parties' guilt.** The account is maintained by the wrong that was done to him, and the resolution of the wrong would require the account's revision. The mechanism is the material of Section 44.6's second mechanism, and its outcome is bitterness.

The correction is not the erasure of the experience. It is the subordination of the experience to the man's present conduct: the account is shortened, the instrument is stated in its place, the present competence is presented before the past difficulty, and the consequences that can be resolved are resolved. The finding in the case material is that men who conduct the correction frequently report a specific and unexpected result: the experience becomes more useful to other men, because a short account with an instrument in it is more usable than a long account with a meaning in it.

### The Test of the Conversion

The section closes with the test by which the reader may evaluate whether his own experience has been converted.

The test has four questions, and each one is answered by evidence rather than by impression.

**The first question is: what do I do differently because of this?** The answer should be a list of arrangements, decisions, or practices that did not exist before the event. Where the answer is a change of attitude, the conversion has not begun.

**The second question is: what can I now do that I could not do before?** The answer should be a competence: the negotiation from weakness, the statement of a loss, the conduct of a household on a reduced income, the correction received in public, the asking for an instrument.

**The third question is: who has used it?** The answer should be a party other than the man: a household member, a colleague, a younger man, a member of his association. Where the answer is nobody, the material has been extracted and has not been transmitted, and the finding is that transmission is frequently the stage at which the man's own understanding becomes precise.

**The fourth question is: what does it still cost me, and what am I doing about that cost?** The question is the chapter's honesty requirement. The conversion does not remove the cost, and the man who claims that it has is conducting the myth of Section 44.1. The answer should be an honest statement of the remaining cost and an honest statement of the treatment, the accommodation, or the arrangement that addresses it.

## 44.5 Mentoring Others Through Difficult Transitions

The previous section established that experience is available to another man in the form in which it can be used. This section describes the relationship in which the use is conducted.

The section's first proposition is a caution. **Mentoring is not a licence to conduct another man's life**, and the case material records more harm from well-intentioned mentorship than from its absence. The harm has a specific form: a man in difficulty is supplied with the mentor's account, the mentor's solutions, and the mentor's timetable, and the man's own position is not examined at all. The relationship then becomes an arrangement in which the man performs the mentor's advice, and its failure is attributed by both parties to the man.

The section therefore proceeds by describing the mentor's functions, the mentor's limits, and the practices that the mentor must not conduct.

### The Five Functions

The mentor's functions are five, and they are supplied in the order below.

**Function One: Presence.** The mentor is reliably available, at stated times, without requiring the man to justify the contact. The function is the material of Chapter 43.6's first condition in its adult form, and the case material's finding is that presence is the function that men in difficulty value most and that mentors most frequently underestimate, because presence produces no visible result.

The practice is ordinary: a call on the same day each week, a meeting on the first Saturday of the month, a message at an interval. The practice's value is its reliability rather than its content, and the case material records men who were sustained through a year by a weekly call whose content they cannot remember.

**Function Two: Information.** The mentor supplies what he knows: the practice that worked in a similar position, the professional who can be approached, the institution's actual procedure, the arrangement that the man has not considered, and the warning that the mentor's own experience supplies. The function is the instrument described in Section 44.4's third domain, and it is supplied on request rather than in advance.

**Function Three: Accompaniment.** The mentor goes with the man to the appointment, the institution, the meeting, or the conversation that the man cannot conduct alone. The function is the material of Chapter 35.5's third support, and the case material's finding is that accompaniment is frequently the specific act that converts an intention into a conducted step. The mentor's role at the appointment is to be present and not to speak, unless the man asks him to.

**Function Four: The limit.** The mentor states honestly what he can and cannot supply, and identifies the professional route for what exceeds his competence. The function is the material of Chapter 43.6's fourth condition, and its conduct is the sentence that the case material records as the most valuable in the whole relationship: *I do not know how to help with this part, and I know who does, and I will go with you.*

**Function Five: Continuity.** The mentor remains in the relationship after the transitions and after the failures, including the man's withdrawal, his relapse, and his refusal of the mentor's advice. The function is the one that distinguishes mentorship from consultation, and the case material's finding is that continuity is the function that produces the relationship's effect.

### The Four Practices the Mentor Must Not Conduct

The section states four practices that mentors frequently conduct and must not.

**Practice One: The prescription.** The mentor states what the man should do, in specific terms, without the man's request. The practice has three effects: the man's own judgement is not exercised; the mentor's advice is frequently wrong because the mentor's position is not the man's; and the man's failure of the advice becomes the mentor's failure or the man's fault. The correction is the question: *what are you considering?* The question supplies the man's own judgement and leaves the mentor free to supply information.

**Practice Two: The comparison.** The mentor states what he did in a similar position and implies the comparison. The implication is frequently unintended and is always heard, and its effect is to convert the relationship into an examination that the man is failing. The correction is the statement of the mentor's experience as information rather than as a standard: *this is what I did, and it may not fit your position.*

**Practice Three: The rescue.** The mentor conducts the man's problem for him: makes the call, pays the debt, speaks to the employer, supplies the solution. The practice produces a specific and damaging outcome, which is the material of Chapter 20.4's substitution: the man learns that the mentor's capacity is the operative capacity, and his own is not exercised. The correction is accompaniment: the mentor goes with the man rather than instead of him.

**Practice Four: The testimony.** The mentor conducts his own difficulty as the subject of the relationship, and the man becomes the audience. The practice is frequently a response to the mentor's own unresolved material, and its effect is the reversal of the relationship's direction. The correction is the discipline stated in Chapter 43.2: the mentor's own material is disclosed proportionately, in service of the man's position, and never as the relationship's subject.

### The Conditions of the Relationship

The section states five conditions that the case material finds in effective mentoring relationships.

**Condition One: The relationship is bounded in time and in scope.** The mentor states when he is available, how often, for what matters, and for how long. The boundaries protect the man from an arrangement that ends without notice and protect the mentor from an arrangement that exceeds his capacity.

**Condition Two: The relationship is confidential, with stated limits.** The mentor states what he will keep confidential and what he will report, in advance, in the form supplied in Chapter 43.6's script. The statement should include the limits that safety imposes and the mentor's own supervision arrangements.

**Condition Three: The relationship has an object that is not the relationship.** The effective arrangement is conducted around a task: the rebuilding plan, the professional qualification, the business's reconstruction, the marriage's repair, the return to work. The object supplies the relationship with a measure and prevents it from becoming an arrangement in which the two men meet in order to discuss the man's difficulty indefinitely.

**Condition Four: The mentor has his own support.** The mentor who conducts another man's difficulty without a party of his own is conducting an exposure that the case material records as harmful to both. The mentor's support may be a peer, a supervisor, a professional, or a group of men conducting similar work, and its absence is the most frequent cause of the mentor's own collapse.

**Condition Five: The relationship can end well.** The effective arrangement has a stated ending, or a stated review at which the ending is considered, and the ending is conducted with an acknowledgement of what was done. The finding is that men frequently continue arrangements past their usefulness out of loyalty, and that the arrangement's continuation in these conditions prevents the man's independence.

### The Transition and Its Stages

The section closes with the specific transitions for which men most frequently require a mentor, and with the mentor's role at each stage. The stages are the material of Chapter 38.5 in its mentoring form.

**The first transition is the loss of a position.** The man has lost his employment, his business, or his professional standing. The mentor's functions at this stage are presence, information about the immediate arrangements, and accompaniment to the institutions. The specific caution is the man's timetable: the case material records men who required a period of two to six weeks before they could conduct a plan, and mentors who imposed a plan in the first week and lost the relationship.

**The second transition is the end of a marriage or a significant relationship.** The mentor's functions are presence, information about the legal and financial arrangements, and accompaniment to the professional route. The specific caution is the mentor's own account of marriage, which is frequently supplied as advice and is frequently unhelpful.

**The third transition is a bereavement.** The mentor's functions are presence, the practical arrangements, and continuity through the second year, which the material of Chapter 30.4 identifies as the period in which the community's support has ended and the man's difficulty has not.

**The fourth transition is the diagnosis of an illness or a disability.** The mentor's functions are information, accompaniment to the appointments, and the conduct of the man's household arrangements. The specific caution is the mentor's optimism, which is frequently experienced as a refusal to acknowledge the position.

**The fifth transition is the return from a period of treatment, imprisonment, or withdrawal.** The mentor's functions are presence, the reconstruction of the man's ordinary arrangements, and the introduction of the man to parties who do not know his history. The specific caution is the community's account, which the mentor can influence and cannot control, and which the material of Chapter 25.5 addresses.

In each of the five transitions the mentor's task is the same in structure: presence, information, accompaniment, the limit, and continuity. The content changes and the structure does not, and the case material's finding is that mentors who hold the structure are effective in transitions they have never experienced, which is the reason the structure matters more than the mentor's history.
