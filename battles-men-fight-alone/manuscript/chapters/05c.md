## 5.5 Recognising When Distress Requires Professional Support

One of the most frequent questions men ask, when they begin to take the subject of their own psychological state seriously, is a version of the following: *How do I know whether this is ordinary difficulty or something I need help with?*

The question is a good one, and it deserves a direct answer. The answer has four parts: what distinguishes ordinary distress from a condition that requires assessment; what the warning signs of serious risk are, which are different and more urgent; what an assessment involves; and what the man should do about it in practice.

### Ordinary Distress and Clinical Conditions

Ordinary distress is a normal response to circumstances. It is proportionate to an event, it fluctuates, it responds to changes in the situation and to ordinary support, and it resolves over weeks or months. A man who is grieving a father, struggling through a business downturn, or adjusting to a separation is not ill. He is a person in difficult circumstances, and the first line of response is time, support, practical assistance, and reasonable self-care.

A clinical condition is different in four respects, and the differences are the practical criteria for seeking assessment.

| Criterion | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| **Duration** | Symptoms present on most days for more than two to four weeks without improvement, or persisting for months. |
| **Intensity** | The state is disproportionate to circumstances, or it dominates the man's attention, or it is accompanied by physical symptoms that have no medical explanation. |
| **Impairment** | Sleep, appetite, work, sexual function, or relationships are significantly affected. Ordinary functioning requires unusual effort. |
| **Compensatory behaviour** | Alcohol, drugs, gambling, or other behaviour is being used to manage the state; or the man is withdrawing from all social contact. |

Table: Practical criteria for seeking professional assessment. The presence of one criterion warrants attention; the presence of two or more warrants prompt consultation.

To these should be added three conditions that always warrant consultation, regardless of duration:

- **Any thought of suicide, self-harm, or harming another person.** This requires immediate professional attention, and Chapter 34 and Appendix Q deal with it. It is not a matter for waiting.
- **Symptoms of possible medical origin.** Chest pain, significant weight loss, unusual neurological symptoms, and similar presentations require physical assessment first, since psychological and physical conditions can produce similar signs and both require attention.
- **Crisis in the household.** A situation involving a risk to a child, a risk of violence, or an acute family crisis requires immediate specialist involvement.

### The Warning Signs of Serious Risk

The following signs indicate that a man's situation has moved from distress to danger, and they should never be treated as waiting matters. They are described fully in Chapter 34; they are listed here in brief because they must not be left to the end of the book.

- Talking or writing about death, dying, or not being there any more.
- Withdrawal from everyone, coupled with the settling of affairs: giving away possessions, making unusual arrangements, paying off debts in an out-of-character manner.
- A sudden calm after a long period of despair, which may indicate a decision has been made.
- Increased use of alcohol or drugs in a man with other warning signs.
- Statements of being a burden, of having no reason to continue, or of being unable to go on.
- Any preparation: acquiring means, writing a note, visiting a specific location.

In such circumstances the reader — whether the man concerned or somebody who cares about him — should make contact with professional or emergency services immediately, and should not attempt to manage the situation alone. Appendix Q lists the routes of contact in Nigeria, in other African countries, and internationally, and gives practical guidance on what to say when making that contact. Chapter 34 sets out how to construct a personal crisis plan while a person is well enough to do so.

### What an Assessment Involves

Men delay seeking assessment partly because they do not know what it consists of, and fear the unknown. In practice, a first consultation with a competent professional is a structured conversation of forty to ninety minutes with four components.

**The account.** The clinician asks what has been happening, how long it has been going on, and what has made it worse or better. This is where the reader's own preparation — the exercise at the end of this chapter — is useful.

**The history.** Questions about health, family, work, relationships, alcohol and substance use, sleep, and previous episodes, together with an inquiry into any history of trauma or loss.

**The assessment of risk.** A direct question about thoughts of self-harm or suicide. It is worth stating plainly why this question is asked and is not an accusation: men in severe distress frequently do not volunteer these thoughts, and asking about them is the standard and responsible way to identify a serious risk. Being asked does not mean the clinician has concluded that the man is suicidal. It means the clinician is competent.

**The formulation and plan.** An explanation of what the clinician thinks is happening — which may be a diagnosis, or may simply be a description of a difficult situation — and a plan. The plan should specify what the treatment will consist of, how long it is likely to take, what the man should expect, what to do if matters worsen, and when there will be a review.

The reader is entitled to ask questions at any point in this process, and should. Three questions improve the quality of the interaction substantially: *What do you think is happening to me? What are the options, and what is the evidence for them? What should I look for as a sign that matters are getting worse?*

### The Pathways Available

Men frequently assume that professional help means an expensive private psychiatrist and nothing else. The range of options is much wider, and Appendix Q sets out the practical detail for Nigerian and other African settings as well as international routes. In outline:

- **General practitioners and family physicians** — the most common entry point, capable of assessing, treating, and referring, and often the least intimidating first contact.
- **Counsellors and psychologists** — talking treatments for identifiable difficulties, including counselling offered by churches, mosques, schools, universities, employers, and non-governmental organisations.
- **Psychiatrists and clinical psychologists** — specialist assessment and treatment, particularly where medication or diagnostic complexity is involved.
- **Hospital and clinic psychiatric services**, including those attached to teaching hospitals, and services for specific problems such as substance use.
- **Telephone, text, and online services**, including helplines (see Appendix Q for numbers current at the time of publication) and online counselling platforms, which have widened access considerably for men in remote areas, in occupations with no flexibility, and for those who value anonymity.
- **Peer and community support** — including mutual-help groups and men's groups, which are not a substitute for treatment in severe conditions but are effective for many men, and considerably better than nothing.

The last point deserves emphasis. There is a strong and consistent finding in the research literature that people who receive *some* appropriate support early do better than those who receive none, and that the most common reason for receiving none is a decision to wait. The reader who is unsure whether his situation warrants attention should err in the direction of making contact.

## 5.6 Finding Language for Invisible Pain

The chapter closes with a practical matter that determines whether any of the preceding material produces a result. A man cannot obtain help for a difficulty he cannot describe, and the descriptions that professionals work with are of a particular kind: they are **specific**, **temporal**, and **functional**. That is, a professional needs to know what happens, when it started, how often it occurs, and what it prevents the man from doing.

The difficulty is that men arrive with a different kind of description — the description of a situation rather than a state. *Work has been difficult since March. My brother-in-law died in April. My son is not serious about his studies.* All of these are accurate and none of them is the information the clinician requires, which is something more like: *Since February I have woken at three most mornings. I am irritable with my children in a way I was not before. I have lost interest in the things I used to enjoy, and I drink four or five bottles of beer most evenings to get to sleep.*

The exercise at the end of this chapter is designed to produce exactly that latter description, in writing, before the appointment. Men who arrive with such a document consistently describe the consultation as shorter, more useful, and less humiliating than they had feared.

### Three Principles for Describing Distress

Three principles make the task easier.

**Describe behaviour and body rather than feelings.** *I cannot sleep. I have stopped playing football. I check the doors twice. I have had a permanent tightness in my chest since March.* These are easy to report, hard to dispute, and clinically informative.

**Attach everything to a date.** *Since March. For two years. Every night for a month. Three times this week.* Time is what distinguishes a passing difficulty from a condition, and most men can supply it accurately.

**Report consequences rather than causes.** *I have missed three days of work. My wife has stopped telling me things. I no longer enjoy my children's company.* Consequences are observable, and they permit the clinician to gauge severity without requiring the man to explain his emotions.

### The Written Account

The exercise below produces a written account of the kind described. Its purpose is not to produce a self-diagnosis; readers are explicitly warned against diagnosing themselves from a book. Its purpose is to convert an unnameable state into a document — and a document can be carried into a consulting room, handed over, and used as the basis of a conversation that begins with information rather than with a struggle for words.
