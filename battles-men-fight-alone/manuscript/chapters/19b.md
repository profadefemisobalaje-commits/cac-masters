## 19.3 The Child Who Had to Be Strong for the Family

This section addresses the specific dynamic in which the requirement was not merely practical but emotional: the child who was required to be strong for the family, and who has continued to be the household's strong party ever since.

### How the Requirement Is Imposed

The requirement is rarely stated. In the accounts men give, it is imposed in six ways.

**By the visible state of the parent.** A child who sees a parent weeping, exhausted, or in despair understands the position without being told. The child's conclusion is drawn immediately and permanently: *I must not add to this.*

**By comparison with the absent or inadequate adult.** *You are the man of the house now.* The sentence is short, its effect is enormous, and men who received it can date it. The instruction is not to be responsible; it is to be the household's adult, which no child can be.

**By the allocation of the household's confidence.** A child who is told the family's financial position, the details of a parent's illness, the conflict between relatives, or the truth about a father's conduct has been moved into an adult position by the sharing of the material. The movement is a form of trust and it removes the child's childhood at the same time, both effects arriving at once.

**By the absence of any response to the child's own difficulty.** Where a child's difficulty is not answered — because the parent has no capacity, or because the child's difficulty is unwelcome as an additional requirement — the child concludes that the household has no place for it, and stops presenting it.

**By the child's role in the household's reputation.** Where a family's standing rests on the child's conduct — his results, his behaviour, his achievements — the child is performing on the family's behalf, and the arrangement is frequently expressed in the family's expectation of him rather than in anything that is said.

**By the behaviour of the extended family.** Relatives frequently appoint the child to the position by treating him as the household's representative. The appointment is made by question, by expectation, and by the transfer of requests.

### The Maintenance of the Arrangement in Adult Life

The arrangement persists for a reason that is worth stating plainly: **it is required, and it is rewarded.** A man who is the household's strong party receives a full return: his competence is valued, his judgement is sought, his provision is depended upon, and his position in his family and community is built on it. To be the strong one is not a burden imposed on an unwilling man; it is a role that supplies some of the most meaningful rewards available to him.

Its costs appear in five places and each is identifiable.

**The exclusion of his own difficulty from every relationship.** Where a man holds the position of the household's strength, his difficulty is unrepresentable: it would be a change in the arrangement that everybody relies on. Its concealment is therefore not a decision but a structural consequence.

**The selection of relationships that reproduce the arrangement.** Men in this pattern frequently form relationships with people who require support, and they are frequently described as attracted to needy partners. The description is imprecise and the pattern is real. What is occurring is that the man's available position in a relationship is the supporting one, and a relationship that offers him a different position does not feel like a relationship.

**The difficulty of receiving care, in illness especially.** Illness and incapacity are the situations in which men in this pattern fare worst, because the arrangement has no procedure for it. The pattern of concealing the diagnosis, deferring treatment, and continuing to work is described in Chapters 12 and 33, and it has its origins in this section.

**The household's inability to know him.** A household that has been organised around its strong party's competence has no practice of asking him about himself. The point is not the household's fault; it is the consequence of the role's position, and it is the mechanism of Section 18.3 in its purest form.

**The delayed arrival of the material.** Men in this pattern frequently discover the costs at a specific point: the death of the parent for whom they were responsible, the completion of the last child's education, the end of the obligation — at which point the entire arrangement loses its justification and the absence beneath it becomes visible. The event is frequently a serious depression or a period of unexplained emptiness, and it is frequently misattributed to the bereavement or to ageing.

### The Sibling Position

One further element distinguishes this pattern. In most of these households, the man is the eldest, and his position carries a particular weight that is compounded in cultures where the eldest son's responsibilities are formally defined.

The features that men report are consistent. The expectation that the eldest will resolve matters, which continues into adulthood and frequently increases after a parent's death; the assignment of responsibility for younger siblings' conduct regardless of their age; the position of arbiter in disputes between siblings; and the tendency of the family to refer the difficult matters to him, including matters that are properly between other people.

The position also carries a specific exposure: the eldest is the person blamed where the family's arrangements fail, and the blame arrives without a hearing. Chapter 16's material on misjudgment applies, and the reason it lands particularly hard in this case is that the man has been performing a function he did not select since the age of twelve.

## 19.4 The Long-Term Effects of Parentification

The psychological literature on parentification — the reversal of the parent-child role — identifies a set of outcomes that recur across studies. They are set out below in the order of their frequency in the research and in men's accounts.

**The default position of responsibility.** The most common and the most obvious. Men in this pattern assume responsibility reflexively, including for matters that are not theirs: a colleague's failure, a friend's debt, an adult sibling's difficulty, a community's problem, an accident witnessed in the street. The reflex is not a decision and the man is frequently unaware of it. Its costs are in Chapter 10.

**Difficulty in identifying and expressing needs.** Men in this pattern frequently cannot answer the question of what they want, and the inability is not modesty. The capacity was not developed, because its development requires an environment in which the expression of need produces a response.

**Hyper-responsibility combined with anxiety.** A specific combination repeatedly observed: the man assumes responsibility for outcomes beyond his control and experiences continuous anxiety about them. The combination is a description of the continuous calculation in Section 19.2, maintained for forty years.

**Difficulty in receiving, including in receiving affection.** Described above. Its marital effects are significant, and the material in Chapters 14 and 15 applies: a wife who attempts to care for a man who cannot receive care will eventually stop attempting, and the cessation will be experienced by the man as further confirmation of his position.

**A tendency towards overwork as a stabiliser.** Work supplies a structure in which the man's competence is rewarded and in which his position is clear. Men in this pattern frequently describe work as the only domain in which they know who they are — the observation of Chapter 12 in its most specific form.

**Difficulty with play, rest, and unstructured time.** The capacity was not developed, and its absence is frequently experienced by the man as a characterological seriousness that he regards as a virtue. In practice it restricts the man's relationships with his children, whose world is conducted in play.

**An elevated risk of depression and anxiety in mid-life and later life.** The research on parentification identifies elevated rates of both, and the mechanism is generally understood in the terms set out above: the arrangement's collapse when its justification ends, the absence of a developed capacity for self-care, and the accumulated effect of decades of suppression.

**Difficulty in the parental relationship itself.** Where the parent for whom the man was responsible remains alive, the adult relationship frequently retains its original structure: the man continues to manage, support, and protect, and the parent continues to be managed. Where the parent dies, the man frequently experiences a grief complicated by relief, responsibility, and guilt, and the complication is frequently not nameable by him.

**A specific difficulty in relation to the man's own childhood.** The final effect, and in some accounts the most difficult: the sense of having missed something that cannot now be obtained. Men describe specific losses — the school years not completed, the sport not played, the friendship groups not joined, the time in which nothing was required — and they describe the difficulty of grieving for them, because grieving requires acknowledging that something was taken, and the arrangement, in most cases, was nobody's fault. The occasion of the ending is frequently a bereavement or an economic collapse, and neither permits an accusation.
