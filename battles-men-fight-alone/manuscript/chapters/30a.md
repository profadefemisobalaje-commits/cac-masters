# Grieving Without an Audience {.chapter num="30" short="Grieving Without an Audience"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Identify the losses for which men are given no public occasion of mourning, and explain the specific difficulty of a grief that has no witness.
2. Describe the ways in which men's grieving differs from the accounts that dominate the culture, and assess whether his own grieving is a deficiency or a different form.
3. Recognise the ways grief presents in men when it is not expressed as sorrow, including irritability, work, drink, risk-taking, somatic symptoms, and the delayed arrival of the material.
4. Conduct the work of mourning in the form available to him, including the elements that supply the transition that a public occasion would otherwise supply.
5. Recognise when a grief has become a condition requiring treatment, and distinguish between the course of ordinary grief and the patterns that require clinical attention.
6. Apply the exercise at the end of the chapter to a specific loss, and construct the arrangements that give the material a place.
:::

## Introduction

The material of this chapter is the loss that has no occasion.

In the ordinary arrangement of societies, a death and a loss are managed by an occasion: the announcement, the gathering, the attendance, the words spoken, the period observed, the return of the community to ordinary life with the mourner in a recognised position. The occasion performs the four functions from Chapter 28.3: it establishes that something has happened, it permits the mourner to speak of it, it supplies the company of those who know, and it marks the transition.

This chapter concerns the losses for which no occasion is provided, and the men who are carrying them.

There are two categories, and they are distinct.

The first is the loss that the surrounding society does not recognise as a loss at all. The man's friend of thirty years has died and his own household offers him three days of consideration; he is expected to return to work and to be steady. The man's former wife has died, and while her family mourns, his position is ambiguous. The man's colleague, the man with whom he spent eleven hours a day for eight years, has died, and his absence is a practical matter rather than a bereavement. The man has lost a child before birth. His mentor has died. The man from his unit or his training has died. In each case, the loss is real and the mourning is not authorised.

The second is the loss that the man cannot afford to have witnessed. The death of a person who is not supposed to be mourned by him; the death of somebody whose connection to the man would damage him or his household; the loss of a person the man was not permitted to love; the death of the man's own child by a manner that the community would treat as a judgement; the death of a companion in circumstances that must be concealed; and, in the material reported from treatment services, the losses of men whose lives contain a chapter, a period, or a relationship that no member of their present household knows about.

The chapter proceeds on four commitments.

The first is that these are losses and that their status is a defect of the arrangement rather than of the man. The evidence is consistent: a grief without a witness behaves differently from a grief with one, and the difference is not that the man is stronger or weaker but that the ordinary mechanisms of acknowledgement are absent.

The second is that men's grieving has its own form and should be treated as legitimate. The dominant cultural account of mourning describes a process of expression, tears, and the sharing of feeling, and men's actual grieving frequently takes a different course: activity, problem-solving, the management of the practical, thinking about the person in private, and the maintenance of a composed exterior. The research describes these as different styles rather than as adequate and inadequate mourning, and this book takes the same position. The practical consequence is that the work of this chapter is not the instruction of men to mourn in somebody else's form; it is the construction of arrangements in which their own form can operate.

The third is that grief that has no place does not stay still. It presents in other forms, and the forms are recognisable: the irritability, the collapse of patience, the work that expands, the drinking, the risk, the physical symptoms, the sudden flatness, the anniversary, and the delayed arrival of the whole material years later. Section 30.3 sets these out, and the recognition is frequently the point at which a man who has never described himself as grieving identifies what has been operating.

The fourth is that the chapter does not promise closure. Grief is not an illness with an endpoint and the losses in this chapter are not concluded. The objective is that the material has a place in the man's life, that it is connected to the person who was lost, and that it is not discharged into his household, his work, or his own body.
