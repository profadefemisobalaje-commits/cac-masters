# The Heartbreak Men Rarely Discuss {.chapter num="13" short="The Heartbreak Men Rarely Discuss"}

## Introduction

A man's marriage ends after fourteen years. Within a week he has arranged his living situation, informed his employer, spoken to a lawyer, and told his closest friend that he is "fine, all things considered." Within a month he has changed his routine, taken on additional work, and begun exercising regularly. Within six months he has been described by four people as having handled it very well.

Two years later he is sitting in a consulting room describing symptoms he did not connect to the separation: an inability to form a new attachment, occasional unexplained anger, a persistent feeling of having been found out, and a reluctance to speak about the marriage at all. Asked what happened at the time, he says that he simply got on with it. Asked how he felt, he pauses for a long time and then says: *I did not feel anything for about a year. I thought that meant I was coping.*

This chapter is about the emotional pain that follows the end of a relationship — marriage, long partnership, engagement, or a significant attachment that was never formalised — and about the peculiar situation of men who are expected, by everybody including themselves, to be less affected than they are. Its premise is simple. Men form attachments with the same intensity as anybody else; they lose them with the same pain; and they are frequently equipped with fewer means of expressing, receiving support for, or processing that pain, with the result that the grief is delayed, concealed, converted into behaviour, or carried for years without being recognised for what it is.

## 13.1 Emotional Pain After Relationship Loss

Relationship loss is one of the most stressful events in the life of an adult, and this finding is consistent across countries, measurement methods, and decades. The pain is real, the physical response is measurable, and the duration is frequently longer than the surrounding culture assumes.

### What the Pain Consists Of

The grief that follows the end of a relationship has at least six components, and men frequently recognise only one or two of them.

**The loss of the person.** The daily presence, the conversation, the physical company, the shared history. This is the component that is most readily recognised as grief, and it is the one that people expect.

**The loss of the future.** The plans, the expectations, the children's milestones, the retirement, the house that was going to be built. This component is frequently more difficult than the first, because it cannot be mourned publicly in the same way; one cannot easily hold a funeral for a life that did not happen.

**The loss of the role.** A man who was a husband, a provider-dependant pair, or a future father loses an identity as well as a relationship. Where that identity was central, the loss is experienced as a change in himself, as Chapters 11 and 24 describe.

**The loss of the household structure.** Where children are involved, the loss includes the daily presence of the children, which is frequently the most severe element and which is systematically under-recognised in accounts of separation, particularly for fathers, as Chapter 17 examines.

**The loss of standing.** Particularly in communities where marriage is a marker of adult status, separation carries an interpretive penalty: assumptions are made, explanations are required, and the man is discussed.

**The loss of the assumption of permanence.** The most subtle and the most durable component. Where a relationship has ended, the man loses the belief that a commitment, once made, will hold. This alters his expectations about the future, and it is the component most responsible for the well-documented difficulty that some men have in forming later attachments.

### Why the Pain Is Frequently More Severe Than Acknowledged

Four features of male experience make relationship loss particularly difficult.

**The relationship may have been the only outlet.** Chapter 4 established that many men have no intimate non-romantic relationship. Where this is the case, the loss of a partner is not the loss of one relationship among several; it is the removal of the man's only source of emotional intimacy, which explains the severity that men frequently report and that observers frequently find disproportionate.

**The man's role may have been entirely occupied with the relationship's maintenance and not its enjoyment.** Men who have defined themselves by provision frequently discover, at the end of a relationship, that the arrangement contained little for them personally, and this produces a particular and disorienting combination of grief and relief that men find difficult to describe.

**The support is asymmetric.** The literature on separation consistently finds that women receive more support than men at the end of a relationship, because the networks that provide support — friends, family, and confidants — are more frequently maintained by women. A man is frequently left with the practical assistance of colleagues and relatives and with no one to speak to.

**The social permission to grieve is narrower.** A man who weeps at a parent's funeral is understood. A man who weeps at the end of a marriage is frequently read as weak, as guilty of something, or as a subject of gossip. The result is a grief that must be conducted privately, which is the least effective arrangement available.

### The Physical and Behavioural Signs

The pain appears in men in the forms identified throughout this book and deserves listing in this specific context.

| Domain | Typical manifestations after relationship loss |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep | Waking early; sleeping in the partner's place or avoiding the bedroom; dreaming of the relationship |
| Body | Chest tightness; loss of appetite or weight; gastric symptoms; fatigue |
| Mood | Numbness rather than sadness; irritability; sudden anger at small provocations; shame |
| Behaviour | Increased work; increased alcohol; sexual activity without attachment; risk-taking; withdrawal from family and social events |
| Thinking | Idealising the relationship; excessive self-blame; obsessive review of the final period; thoughts of contact |
| Function | Reduced concentration; errors at work; difficulty making decisions; loss of interest in previously valued activity |

### The Course and the Duration

Four features of the course of relationship grief are worth stating, because men's expectations about it are frequently wrong in ways that cause them to misjudge their own condition.

**The pain is at its sharpest early and its later appearance is intermittent rather than constant.** Men who experience a period of relative calm four months after a separation and then a strong recurrence at eight months frequently conclude that they are going backwards. The pattern is ordinary: acute intensity declines, and what remains is a set of specific triggers — an anniversary, a place, a song, a piece of news, a child's question — each of which produces a full-strength episode for a limited period. The episodes become less frequent and less durable. The intervals, not the intensity, are the measure of progress.

**The full weight is frequently experienced at a delay.** Where a man has practical work to perform — moving house, arranging contact, managing the legal process, informing the family, absorbing the financial consequences — the grief tends to be postponed until the work is complete. Men describe this accurately: *I was fine while there was something to do.* The period immediately after the practical matters conclude is therefore frequently the hardest, and it is the period in which men are least prepared and least supported, because everybody else has concluded that the matter is finished.

**Anniversaries and symbolic dates produce predictable recurrences.** The date of the wedding, the date of the separation, the birthdays, the family occasions at which the person is absent. Knowing the dates in advance is the practical measure: a man who expects difficulty on the date manages it better than one who is surprised by it.

**Recovery is not the absence of feeling.** The end state is not indifference. Men who have recovered describe retained affection, retained regret, and, in many cases, a continuing interest in the former partner's wellbeing, without the compulsive monitoring, the self-assessment, and the intrusive replay that characterise the acute period. The presence of feeling is not evidence that the work has not been done.

::: keypoint
Relationship loss is not a lesser grief because it involves a living person rather than a death. It is frequently more complicated, because the person remains present in the world, because unfinished business cannot be closed by a funeral, and because the surrounding community may recover faster than the man does.
:::

## 13.2 Why Men Sometimes Conceal Romantic Grief

Men conceal romantic grief for six reasons, and the reasons are practical rather than emotional. Understanding them matters because each has a different remedy.

### The Six Reasons

**The audience is unsympathetic.** Men report anticipating, accurately, that disclosure will be received in specific ways: the assumption of blame (*what did you do?*), the assumption of culpability (*you should have known*), the immediate advice (*forget her; there are others*), or a practical redirection (*you need to move on*). None of these responses is intended unkindly, and each of them closes the conversation.

**The disclosure creates an obligation for the listener.** The most common reason men give for silence is that their friends have their own problems. This is frequently true and frequently is not the point: a man who will not mention his own difficulty in order to spare a friend produces two people who are not talking rather than one.

**Concealment protects the possibility of reconciliation.** Where a man wishes to restore the relationship, public grief is tactically unhelpful, and this is a real consideration for many men in the first months after a separation.

**Concealment protects standing with children.** A father concerned about being described as bitter, unstable, or unable to manage is frequently silent in front of his children, and this silence often extends to everybody else, since the children hear from relatives.

**Grief in men is read as something else.** A man who is grieving appears to his colleagues as stressed, his family as withdrawn, his friends as uninterested. His grief is not recognised, so it is not discussed, so he concludes that either it is not happening or it does not matter.

**The vocabulary is absent.** As Chapter 3 established, men frequently have no way to describe a state of longing, abandonment, or humiliation, and they therefore report its behaviours: work, exercise, sleeping badly, and a general sense of being busy.

### The Costs of Concealment

The costs of concealing romantic grief are the costs described throughout Part One, with two additions.

**The conversion of grief into behaviour.** Where grief cannot be expressed as speech, it becomes behaviour: increased work, or increased alcohol, or sexual activity without attachment, or conspicuous spending, or a hostility that is initially invisible and gradually becomes who the man is. The behaviour frequently continues beyond the point of recovery and becomes a settled pattern, which is one of the reasons that middle-aged men sometimes describe themselves as having become somebody they do not entirely recognise.

**The exclusion of the people who would help.** Where the man's closest friends and relatives do not know that he is grieving, they cannot adjust their expectations, cannot offer support, and cannot offer corrections to the man's own interpretation of events — which is frequently the most valuable thing available to a man in this position.
