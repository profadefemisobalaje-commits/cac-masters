# The Battles That Make a Man Wiser {.chapter num="44" short="The Battles That Make a Man Wiser"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Distinguish between the cost of adversity and the value that can be extracted from it, and state why the two are not the same ledger.
2. Identify the five myths that glorify suffering, and explain the specific harm each one does to a man who is in difficulty.
3. Conduct a structured review of a mistake or a setback, including the attribution of what is his and what is not.
4. Apply the four tests of proportion, and describe the six markers of emotional maturity that the material supports.
5. Convert personal experience into constructive action in three domains, without converting the suffering into his identity.
6. State the five functions of a mentor and the four practices that a mentor must not conduct.
7. Identify the anatomy of bitterness in his own record, and state the exit from it.
8. Construct a personal wisdom account: what a specific difficulty cost, what it taught, and what it has been used for.
:::

## Introduction

The previous chapter addressed the transmission of silence to the next generation. This chapter addresses the material that the reader may, with care, transmit instead: the wisdom that a man accumulates by surviving what he has survived.

The chapter's title contains a claim that requires immediate qualification, because the claim is one of the most frequently misused propositions in the literature of manhood and one of the most damaging to the men who believe it. The claim is that battles make a man wiser. The qualification is that **they do not do so automatically, and they frequently do the opposite.**

The case material of this book establishes the qualification with sufficient consistency to state it as a rule. Two men may survive the same event — a business failure, a betrayal, a bereavement, a period of unemployment, a humiliation in public — and produce opposite outcomes. The first becomes more accurate about the world, more capable in difficulty, more generous with other men who are in it, and more stable in his own estimate of himself. The second becomes more rigid, more suspicious, more isolated, and more convinced that the world is arranged against him. The difference between them is not the severity of what happened. It is what was done with it afterwards, and that is a craft, not a gift.

The chapter's subject is therefore the craft. It proceeds from a distinction that governs everything else in it and that the reader should hold from the first page: **the cost of an event and the value extracted from it are two different ledgers, and neither cancels the other.**

The cost ledger records what the event took: the money, the years, the health, the relationship, the standing, the opportunities, the version of the man's life that would have existed had the event not occurred. The cost is real, it is frequently large, and it is not reduced by anything the man learns afterwards. The case material's finding on this point is important, because the literature of resilience frequently obscures it: **a man who is told that his suffering was worth it because of what he learned has been told something false about the people he lost, the years he spent, and the damage that was done to him.** The value ledger does not repay the cost ledger. It is a separate account, and its entries are available whether or not the cost was deserved, whether or not it was fair, and whether or not anybody acknowledged it.

The value ledger records what the event supplied: information about the world, information about the man himself, the collapse of a false assumption, the discovery of a capacity, the identification of a party who can be relied on and a party who cannot, the reduction of a fear that had been larger than the event, and the acquisition of a competence that no other route would have supplied. These entries are real, they are frequently substantial, and they are not automatic. They are produced by work, and the work is the chapter's subject.

The chapter's approach has five commitments.

**The first is that the chapter does not glorify suffering.** The distinction between this chapter and the large literature that does glorify it is stated at the outset. The glorification of suffering takes three forms, and all three are addressed in Section 44.1: the claim that difficulty is necessary for character, which is false as a general proposition and cruel as a consolation; the claim that a man should seek difficulty, which is the confusion of a craft with a discipline; and the claim that a man who has suffered is entitled to a status that others lack, which produces the bitterness that Section 44.6 examines. The chapter's position is the inverse of all three: difficulty is common, frequently undeserved, frequently damaging, and occasionally instructive; the instruction is worth extracting; and the extraction is not a justification of the damage.

**The second is that the chapter's unit is the review, not the event.** A man cannot choose most of what happens to him. He can choose, to a substantial degree, what he does in the weeks and years afterwards, and the case material's finding is that the choice is exercised through a small number of repeatable practices: the structured review, the attribution, the test of proportion, the conversion of experience into use, and the conduct of the material with another man. These practices are supplied in Sections 44.2 to 44.5, and each one is small enough to conduct in an evening.

**The third is that the chapter is written for the man who is still in the difficulty as well as the man who has left it.** A great deal of the material on adversity is written retrospectively and is useless to a man who is in the middle of the event. The chapter therefore states where each practice becomes available: the review is available in part from the first week, the attribution from the first month, the proportion test at any time, the conversion after the position has stabilised, and the mentoring after a period that the case material puts at not less than two years.

**The fourth is that the chapter's standard of wisdom is practical rather than mystical.** The word wisdom is used in this chapter in a specific and testable sense: **wisdom is the capacity to act well in circumstances that are uncertain, unwelcome, and not fully within one's control, on the basis of what one has learned from previous such circumstances.** It is not serenity, it is not detachment, and it is not the absence of distress. It is a competence, it is measurable in a man's conduct, and it is acquired in the way that other competences are acquired.

**The fifth is that the chapter's outcome is not a better story about the past.** The case material records a specific failure that the reader should avoid: the man who converts his history into a narrative that he tells at occasions, that improves with each retelling, and that leaves his present conduct unchanged. The narrative is not the product. The product is the alteration of what the man does, and Section 44.4 supplies the test for distinguishing the two.

The chapter's sequence follows the outline. Section 44.1 examines adversity and the myths that surround it. Section 44.2 supplies the review of mistakes and setbacks. Section 44.3 addresses perspective and emotional maturity. Section 44.4 addresses the conversion of experience into action. Section 44.5 addresses mentoring. Section 44.6 addresses the choice between growth and bitterness. Section 44.7 presents an illustrative case. Section 44.8 supplies the programme and the exercise.

## 44.1 Understanding Adversity Without Glorifying Suffering

The section begins with an account of what adversity actually supplies, because the account determines whether the rest of the chapter is conducted honestly.

### What Adversity Supplies

Five entries are recorded consistently in the case material, and each one is stated with the condition under which it appears.

**Entry One: Accurate information about the world.** A man's ordinary arrangements rest on assumptions that are frequently untested: that his income will continue, that his health will hold, that a particular party can be relied on, that a particular institution will behave as it is supposed to, that his competence is sufficient for the demands he will meet. Difficulty tests these assumptions, and the test produces information that ordinary life does not supply. The information is frequently unwelcome and it is frequently accurate.

The condition under which this entry appears: the man must be willing to revise his account. The case material records that men who conduct a difficulty while defending their previous assumptions extract very little from it, since the event's information is treated as an anomaly rather than as evidence. The man who emerges from a business failure believing that the failure was entirely the fault of a customer, a bank, or a competitor has learned nothing about his own exposure, and the same exposure will produce the same failure.

**Entry Two: Accurate information about himself.** Difficulty supplies a measurement of the man's own capacities that ordinary circumstances do not supply: what he does under pressure, what he does when he is afraid, what he does when he is humiliated, what he does when nobody is watching, how long he can sustain an effort, and where his limit actually lies. The measurement is frequently different from the man's self-estimate in both directions: men discover capacities they did not know they had, and limits they had denied.

The condition: the man must be willing to record the measurement rather than to defend his self-estimate. The practice is supplied in Section 44.2.

**Entry Three: The identification of his actual parties.** Difficulty conducts a test of the man's relationships, and the results are frequently surprising in both directions. The party he expected to be present is absent; the party he had not counted is present; the colleague who was reliable in ordinary business is absent in difficulty; the acquaintance who had no obligation conducts himself well. The information is among the most valuable the event supplies, and it is the information that men most frequently waste, because the disappointment of the absent party occupies their attention and the presence of the unexpected party is not registered.

The condition: the man must record the parties rather than conducting the disappointment. The practice is supplied in Section 44.8's first table.

**Entry Four: The reduction of a fear that was larger than the event.** A man frequently carries a fear of an event that has not occurred: the fear of failure, of dismissal, of humiliation, of poverty, of being left. When the event occurs, the fear is frequently replaced by the event's actual demands, and the actual demands are frequently more manageable than the fear was. The finding in the case material is consistent and it is stated carefully: this entry does not make the event desirable, and it does frequently reduce the territory that the fear had occupied.

The condition: the man must distinguish between the event and the fear. A man who conducts the event as confirmation of the fear learns the opposite lesson.

**Entry Five: A competence that no other route would have supplied.** Certain competences are acquired only in difficulty: the capacity to conduct a negotiation from a position of weakness; the capacity to state a loss to a household; the capacity to work while grieving; the capacity to be corrected in public without collapse; the capacity to conduct a household on a reduced income; the capacity to ask for help. These competences are not taught in ordinary circumstances, because ordinary circumstances do not require them.

The condition: the man must conduct the competence deliberately rather than merely surviving the circumstance. The distinction is the chapter's central mechanical point and it is developed in Section 44.4.

### What Adversity Does Not Supply

The section now states what adversity does not supply, because the myths that surround it consist almost entirely of attributing these things to it.

**Adversity does not supply meaning.** An event has no meaning in itself, and the meaning a man assigns to it is a construction. The construction is necessary and it is not automatic: a man who assigns the meaning *I am finished* will conduct himself accordingly, and a man who assigns the meaning *this has cost me a great deal and I am still here* will conduct himself differently. The construction is the material of Section 44.3, and the case material's finding is that the construction is frequently made in the first weeks and is rarely revised afterwards. It is therefore worth making deliberately.

**Adversity does not supply permission.** A man who has suffered frequently believes that the suffering entitles him to something: an exemption from ordinary obligations, a licence to conduct himself badly, a claim on other people's patience, or a status that others lack. The belief is common, it is understandable, and it is destructive, because it converts the man's difficulty into a permanent claim and the claim into the bitterness of Section 44.6. The material's position is that a man who has suffered is entitled to support, treatment, time, and accommodation, and is not entitled to reduce the position of other people.

**Adversity does not supply wisdom.** This is the chapter's central correction. Wisdom is not a substance that difficulty deposits in a man. It is a set of practices that a man conducts on the material his difficulty supplied, and a man who does not conduct the practices emerges from the same difficulty with the same information and no more capacity than he had before. The case material records this with precision: men who have survived severe events and conducted no review are frequently less capable in the following decade than men who have survived moderate events and conducted the review carefully.

**Adversity does not supply community.** A man in difficulty frequently discovers that the community's support is real and that it is time-limited. The support that arrives in the first month is frequently absent by the sixth. The absence is not necessarily a betrayal; it is the ordinary limit of the arrangements that communities maintain. The finding is that the man who mistakes the first month's support for a permanent arrangement is frequently bitter by the sixth, which is the material of Section 44.6's third mechanism.

**Adversity does not supply virtue.** A man who has suffered is not thereby more honest, more generous, more humble, or more kind. He is a man who has suffered, and his conduct afterwards is determined by what he does with the material. The case material records men who emerged from severe difficulty more generous and men who emerged more cruel, and their severity of suffering did not distinguish them.

### The Five Myths

The section now examines the five myths directly, because the reader will have encountered them and will have internalised at least one.

**Myth One: What does not kill you makes you stronger.** The statement is a compression of a proposition that is partly true and is frequently false. Its truth is that some capacities are acquired in difficulty. Its falsity is that severe events frequently leave lasting damage that no subsequent learning repairs: an injury, a bereavement, a period of malnutrition, a trauma, a criminal record, a destroyed reputation, a child's lost education. The man who believes the myth will frequently decline treatment, because treatment implies that the damage is real rather than formative, and the case material records this mechanism in the delay described in Chapter 35.2.

The correction: **what does not kill you frequently weakens you, and it may also teach you something.** Both clauses are true, and the man who holds both is in a position to seek treatment and to conduct the review.

**Myth Two: A man needs a hard life to become a man.** The statement is a prescription rather than a description, and it is one of the five propositions of Chapter 43.4 in a more respectable form. Its harm is specific: it converts the accidents of a man's history into a requirement, and it therefore justifies the withholding of support from boys and young men on the grounds that the withholding is formative. The case material records the mechanism frequently: the father who declines to assist his son on the grounds that he was not assisted, and who describes the refusal as preparation.

The correction: **a man needs a standard, an example, a party, and an instrument. Difficulty supplies the instrument only when nothing else does, and it frequently supplies damage along with it.**

**Myth Three: Suffering is where the real man is revealed.** The statement is the aesthetic form of the myth, and it is the one that literature and film supply most frequently. Its harm is that it assigns a positive value to the man's collapse and thereby removes the incentive to prevent it. The finding in the case material is that the statement is frequently held by men who are conducting a position that is damaging them, and that it functions as the permission described in Chapter 12.4.

The correction: **a man's conduct under difficulty is informative. It is not a justification for the difficulty, and it is not the only thing about him that is worth knowing.**

**Myth Four: Time heals.** Time does not heal. Time passes, and what the man does in the time determines the outcome. The case material records men who conducted no review for twenty years and whose difficulty was as present in the twentieth year as in the first, and men who conducted the review in the second year and whose difficulty had become material rather than governing by the fifth.

The correction: **time supplies distance, and distance is useful. It is a condition, not an instrument.**

**Myth Five: A wise man does not need help.** The statement is the myth that this book was written against. Its harm is the harm of the whole first half of the book, and its correction is the material of Chapter 35: the man who asks is conducting a competence, the competence is learnable, and its absence is a disability rather than a virtue.

### The Rule of the Two Ledgers

The section closes with the rule that governs the chapter, stated in the form in which the reader can use it.

**Rule One: Never use the value ledger to cancel the cost ledger.** The sentence *it was worth it because I learned something* is permitted only where the man is the party who paid. It is not permitted as a consolation offered to another man, and it is never permitted in respect of a cost paid by somebody else.

**Rule Two: Never use the cost ledger to cancel the value ledger.** The fact that an event was unjust, undeserved, and damaging does not mean that nothing was learned. The man who refuses the value because the cost was unjust is conducting a comprehensible act and is depriving himself of the only return available.

**Rule Three: Record both ledgers, separately, in writing.** The practice is the first item of Section 44.8, and its purpose is to prevent the two accounts from contaminating each other. The case material's finding is that men who record both are more accurate about the event, more effective in the following years, and less prone to the bitterness of Section 44.6 than men who record one.

**Rule Four: The value ledger's entries must be evidenced.** An entry is not *I learned to be grateful*. It is *I learned that my brother-in-law will lend money and will not mention it, which I did not know in 2019, and which I have used twice.* The requirement of evidence is the distinction between the ledger and the narrative, and it is the condition on which the entries can be used.
