## 35.2 What Asking Actually Costs

The section treats the four costs honestly. Two of them are real and require an arrangement; two are anticipated and are generally inaccurate.

### Cost One: Standing — Real in Specific Settings, Anticipated in Most

**Where the cost is real.** The settings in which a man's asking is recorded against him are identifiable: the competitive environment in which the man's position depends on his apparent command; the informal setting among men in which the exchange is a competition; the regulated setting in which the disclosure of certain material is a reportable matter with a defined consequence; the small community in which the material will become general; the family arrangement in which the man's assistance has been the family's structure, and in which his request alters the arrangement described in Chapter 33.

**Where the cost is anticipated and does not occur.** The findings from the research and from practice are consistent and should be stated plainly: the household, the friend, the general practitioner, the professional, the employer who is not in a competitive setting, and the men's group do not regard the man as reduced by his request. The specific finding is that the recipient's experience of being asked is generally positive and is frequently the occasion of a relief of their own: the man who asks a friend for help generally discovers that the friend had been aware that something was wrong and had not known how to raise it.

**The distinction's practical use.** The man should identify the setting before the request and select the recipient accordingly. Where the setting is one of the categories in the first paragraph, the request should be routed through the professional or the arrangement rather than made directly; where the setting is any of the others, the anticipated cost is generally inaccurate.

### Cost Two: Dependence — Real, and Managed by the Arrangement

**The cost.** The man who asks establishes a dependence, and its two forms are different. The assistance that is required once is a transaction; the assistance that is required regularly is a dependence, and its terms matter. The men in the case material who most fear the second form are frequently the men who have provided it to others and who know what a repayment arrangement looks like from the provider's side.

**The management.** Four arrangements reduce the cost to the level the man can carry. The **professional arrangement**, in which the exchange is a service with defined terms and no ongoing obligation. The **reciprocal arrangement**, in which the man brings something to the relationship, as Chapter 27.5's third stage specifies. The **stated terms**, in which the assistance is given with a limit and a duration, as Chapter 33.4 supplies. And the **group arrangement**, in which the assistance circulates among several men rather than running in one direction between two.

**The finding that corrects the man's assessment.** The dependence that men fear is generally less permanent than the attachment that the alternative produces. A man who has received assistance and discharged the terms of it has a relationship; a man who has concealed a matter, declined the assistance, and carried it alone has an arrangement that his household is participating in without knowledge.

### Cost Three: Obligation — Real, and Frequently Smaller Than the Man Assumes

**The cost.** Assistance creates an obligation: the assistor has a claim, the man has an account, and the account may be raised. Men report this cost precisely, and it is frequently the strongest of the four in the family setting, where the assistance is remembered and quoted for decades.

**The management.** The professional route removes the obligation entirely. The stated terms convert it into a defined transaction. The reciprocal arrangement balances it. Where an obligation is genuinely incurred, the mode of its discharge should be established at the time of the assistance and not left to the assistor's account, which is the arrangement described in Chapter 33.4 and which is worth conducting even at the cost of some awkwardness.

**The finding that corrects the man's assessment.** The obligation that men fear in the family setting is frequently the obligation they have already created in the other direction. The man who has assisted eleven relatives, guaranteed four loans, and funded two households has already established a set of obligations; the request for assistance that he declines to make is the request that would balance an account that is, in most cases, heavily in his favour.

### Cost Four: Exposure — Real, and the Actual Subject of the Chapter

**The cost.** The material of the previous chapters: the disclosure of the difficulty, the failure, the addiction, the debt, the condition, the wrong, or the state. The exposure is real and it is the cost that distinguishes the request for practical assistance from the request for help with the material of this book.

**The management.** The graduated form of Chapter 34.6 and the staged method of Chapter 27.5: the smallest true version, supplied to a recipient selected for capacity and record, with the professional route available where the confidentiality must be an obligation rather than a favour. The disclosure's cost rises with delay, as Chapter 34.2 established, and the arrangement reduces the exposure to the level the man can afford at the time.

**The finding that corrects the man's assessment.** The exposure of a difficulty is generally smaller than the exposure of its consequences. A man who discloses a financial position in the first month is exposed to a conversation; a man who discloses it in the third year is exposed to the arrears, the household's reduced arrangements, and the account the household has constructed in the interval. The same comparison holds for the medical condition, the addiction, and the difficulty at work.

### The Ledger the Man Has Not Counted

The chapter's central practical argument is the comparison, and it should be written rather than reasoned.

**The costs of asking:** the standing in the settings where it is real; the dependence, managed by the arrangement; the obligation, discharged by terms; and the exposure, controlled by the graduated form and by the choice of recipient.

**The costs of not asking, over the same period:** the material's accumulation and the interest it accrues; the household's reduced arrangements and its misattributed account of the man; the man's own condition, including the presentations of Chapters 30.3, 31.4, and 34.2; the reduced conduct of Chapter 25.2: the positions not taken, the decisions deferred, the capacity not used; the arrangements not maintained; and the risk categories of Chapter 34.3, whose totals the chapter has already supplied.

The comparison is the chapter's argument, and the finding from practice is that it is decisive: the men who conduct it discover that they have been paying the larger sum in instalments and that the sum was invisible because it was not denominated in a single transaction.

## 35.3 The Map of Available Help

Men frequently conclude that help is unavailable because the form they considered is one they will not use. The following map is arranged by the kind of need rather than by the kind of service, and its purpose is the matching of the need to the source.

### The Five Sources

**1. The person.** One man, a household member, a friend, a relative, a colleague, a mentor. What this source supplies: presence, the arrangement in which the material is held, the practical assistance, and the correction that the isolated man does not have. What it does not supply: competence in the specific technical matter, confidentiality governed by an obligation, and an arrangement that is available when the person is unavailable.

**2. The professional.** The general practitioner, the psychologist or counsellor, the psychiatrist, the lawyer, the accountant, the financial counsellor, the debt adviser, the occupational health service, the employee assistance programme, the mediator, the social worker. What this source supplies: competence in the specific matter, the confidentiality obligation, the defined terms, the absence of an ongoing obligation, and the availability. What it does not supply: the two-in-the-morning company, the presence at the hospital, and the ongoing relationship that a man's arrangements require — which is the reason the professional route is an entry point rather than a destination.

**3. The institution.** The hospital, the bank's small-business or restructuring desk, the government agency, the cooperative, the credit union, the housing authority, the school, the training provider, the regulatory body, the union. What this source supplies: scale, legality, procedure, and the routes that the man cannot construct as an individual — the restructuring, the assessment, the recognition of prior learning, the instalment arrangement, the grant, the examination.

**4. The peer group.** The professional association, the trade association, the men's group, the group of people in the same situation — the bereaved men, the men who have lost businesses, the men in recovery, the men in comparable positions, the fathers' group. What this source supplies: the recognition that the condition is general, the correction of the man's account, the absence of cost, and the recognition of the man's competence by people who can assess it, which is the material of Chapter 25.3.

**5. The arrangement.** The measures the man constructs rather than sources he approaches: the written boundary of Chapter 31.5, the inventory of Chapter 33.6, the stated terms of Chapter 33.4, the safety plan of Chapter 34.5, the measures for means safety, the arrangements for the household, the written measure of Chapter 25.5. The category is included because a substantial proportion of what men require is not a service but a structure.

### The Matching

| The need | The source |
| --- | --- |
| I am carrying material and it has no recipient. | The person, the professional, the peer group. |
| I have a specific technical matter — a legal, financial, medical, or property question. | The professional, the institution. |
| My position requires me to be the man who knows and I cannot ask my own people. | The professional outside the line, the peer group of equivalent position. |
| I cannot pay for the assistance. | The institution, the cooperative, the public service, the group. |
| I need to know whether my state is a condition requiring treatment. | The general practitioner, the mental health service. |
| I need the arrangement rather than the advice. | The institution, the professional relationship, the group with a fixed time. |
| I need to be known by people who know my work. | The professional or trade association. |
| I need somebody to be present at a specific event or period. | The person, the group. |
| I need to stop and I cannot stop on my own. | The treatment service, the medical route, the group in recovery. |

### The Finding on the Form Men Actually Use

The chapter records a finding from practice that corrects a common instruction: men's first contact is frequently not with the form prescribed for their difficulty. Men enter the arrangements of this book through the general practitioner's physical presentation, the lawyer's consultation about a document, the debt adviser's appointment concerning a notice, the trade association's technical enquiry, the mediation service's enquiry about a family dispute, and the men's group brought by a friend. The route matters less than the establishment of the arrangement, and the chapter's instruction is therefore: **use the route that is available, and allow the material to arrive later.** The case material in Chapters 27.6 and 32.7 illustrates the mechanism, and Section 35.4 supplies the forms.
