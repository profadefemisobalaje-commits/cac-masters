## 16.3 Betrayal: When Trust Is Broken

Betrayal differs from rejection in its structure, and the difference explains why betrayal is frequently more difficult to recover from.

### The Anatomy of a Betrayal

Four elements compose the experience, and the second and third are the ones that cause the greatest and most durable damage.

**The act.** What was done: the money taken, the confidence disclosed, the relationship conducted, the decision made against the man's interest, the accusation made, the support withdrawn at a moment of need.

**The concealment.** The act was accompanied by its management: the lie, the omission, the story, the inclusion of others in the silence. This is the element that most distinguishes betrayal from ordinary injury. An opponent who damages a man openly has not betrayed him. A person who damages him and then presents a false account has done something to the man's relationship with reality.

**The participation of others.** Where other people knew and did not tell him, the injury extends beyond the individual. Men describe this element with particular pain: the discovery that three people knew, that one of them was a relative, that the knowledge continued for months, and that the man was conducting his life on a false account while a group of people watched.

**The moment of discovery.** A man's experience of betrayal is organised around the moment he learned of it. Its form matters: the accidental discovery that produced everything at once; the gradual accumulation of inconsistencies; the third party's disclosure; the confrontation with the person and their partial admission. Men report that the moment of discovery is as vivid, years later, as the event itself, and that its details are retained permanently. This is why disclosure is frequently experienced as worse than the underlying act, and it is the reason that Chapter 9's insistence on early, complete disclosure is not merely ethical advice but practical protection of the relationship's future.

### The Forms Betrayal Takes in a Man's Life

**By a friend.** The disclosure of a confidence; the pursuit of the man's partner or the man's opportunity; the taking of a loan or an asset with no intention of returning it; the failure to defend him when a defence was expected; the use of private information in an advantage.

**By a relative.** The diversion of family property or resources; the obstruction of an inheritance or a business; an accusation made to the family that damages the man's standing; the refusal of assistance after having received it; the pursuit of a relationship that injures the man's household; the alliance against him with his own family's council.

**By a business partner or colleague.** The transfer of assets or clients; the double contract; the misrepresentation of the man's contribution; the removal of the man from the enterprise he built; the taking of credit; the sabotage of a contract; the falsification of accounts.

**By an institution.** An employer who undertakes a commitment and abandons it; an association or cooperative that manages the members' funds for private purposes; an organisation that dismisses a long-serving member without process.

**By the community or by those who were thought to be supporters.** The spreading of a version of events that the man cannot correct; the withdrawal of support at the moment of a man's difficulty, frequently by the very people who received his support; the public judgment of a matter in which the man was not heard.

**By a partner.** The forms and treatment are in Chapter 13 and Chapter 15, and the material in this section applies with the addition of an ongoing legal and parental dimension.

### The Four Injuries

Betrayal produces four distinct injuries, and it is useful to separate them because the treatment differs.

**The material injury.** What was lost: money, an asset, a position, an opportunity. This injury is addressable, at least in part, by recovery, by legal action, and by rebuilding. Its treatment is practical.

**The relational injury.** The loss of one relationship and the alteration of others. This is where the number of people who knew becomes significant, and where the man's response determines whether a single relationship is lost or a network is.

**The injury to the man's model of reality.** The most severe. The injured man's assumptions about other people have been shown to be false, which means his ability to assess who is reliable, who is safe, and who is truthful has been demonstrated to be inadequate. The consequence is a general loss of confidence in his own judgement about people, and the hypervigilance that follows: reading every conversation twice, keeping track of who has been told what, and never fully relying on anybody. Men in this state describe themselves as having become cynical, and they generally present it as maturity.

**The injury to the man's account of himself.** Betrayal is frequently accompanied by humiliation: others knew, the man was the last to know, and he was managed. The question that recurs is not *how could he do this?* but *how could I not have seen it?* Men are less damaged by other people's conduct than by their own conclusions about their inability to judge character, and this is the injury that the recovery method in Section 16.5 must address directly.

### The Two Destructive Responses

**The hardening response.** The man decides that trust is a mistake, that people are as they are, that he will from now on deal with others only at a distance and with terms written down. The response has genuine protective value in a business context and produces a permanently reduced capacity for closeness. The men who describe this response frequently also report that they cannot be fully known by anybody, including their wives and children, and that the arrangement is lonely.

**The exposure response.** The man attempts to correct the record publicly and continuously: the court case, the account circulated to the family, the story retold to every new person, and the perpetual preparation of the defence. This response has a specific cost: it organises the man's life around the injury, and the injury therefore occupies the position the man was trying to remove it from. Men who have pursued a wrong for a decade are frequently right about the wrong and unable to describe what else they have done in the period.

## 16.4 The Pain of Being Misjudged

This section addresses a specific and particularly damaging form: the man who is judged, accused, or discredited without a hearing, and who finds that the judgment has been accepted before he learned of its existence.

### The Forms

**The false accusation.** An allegation, made to a family, a community, an employer, or an institution, of conduct the man did not commit or of conduct presented without its context. Its features: the absence of a hearing; the frequency with which the accusation comes from within the man's close circle; and the speed with which it can acquire an official character through repetition.

**The misread conduct.** The man's action was not wrong but was read as wrong without enquiry: the financial assistance withheld from a relative because the man had nothing to give; the withdrawal from an arrangement because he was ill; the silence at a funeral because of grief. The misreading is frequently less damaging in its consequences and equally damaging in its effect, because it cannot be corrected without describing matters the man does not wish to describe.

**The inherited judgment.** A judgment on the man that was formed from the conduct of somebody else: the judgment of his father; the reputation of a family; an accusation made against a deceased relative and carried forward.

**The public failure.** A business failure, an insolvency, a professional error, a divorce, a scandal, handled or reported in a public form. The man's own handling of the events may have been scrupulous and the community judgment will frequently ignore the distinction, because a judgment is constructed from the outcome.

**The judgment of the man's household.** The most painful form for many men: the assessment, carried over years, that he has not done enough, that he is not the provider his wife's family expected, that a brother or a neighbour has done better.

### The Specific Features of This Injury

**The asymmetry.** A man cannot correct an allegation that he cannot see. The accusation circulates in the form of the community's attitude rather than in the form of a statement, and a man who responds to his impression of other people's attitude is generally described as defensive. The practical consequence: the man frequently learns the full content of the allegation years later, and the interval is a period of damage without information.

**The impossibility of proof.** The structure of many allegations is such that denial does not address them. An allegation of character — that the man is selfish, that he neglects his family, that he is not a serious person — cannot be disproved, because it has not been articulated as a claim.

**The duty to respond.** Men are frequently obliged to respond publicly, and the responses available — the counter-accusation, the defence of every item, the withdrawal of participation — are each unsatisfactory. The practitioner's experience is that a small number of the available responses are effective, and they are set out in Section 16.5.

**The legal and the social dimensions.** Where the judgment carries legal consequences — the insolvency, the criminal or civil allegation — the man must obtain legal advice immediately and separate the legal task from the social one, because the methods are different and because misinformation is common: many men believe that a favourable outcome in the legal matter will restore their standing, and frequently it does not, or not without work.

### The Cost to the Man's Own Psychology

Misjudgment produces a specific spiral, which is worth setting out because recognising it allows a man to interrupt it.

1. The judgment circulates.
2. The man becomes aware of a change in behaviour toward him and infers the content of the judgment.
3. He attempts correction, and the attempt is read as confirmation.
4. He experiences shame: the judgment is held by people whose regard he needs.
5. He withdraws.
6. His withdrawal is interpreted as a confirmation of the judgment, since a man with nothing to hide would have nothing to avoid.
7. The judgment becomes established, and the man's isolation becomes its evidence.

Step to interrupt is the fifth, and the means of interrupting it is the deliberate maintenance of normal participation, at whatever level of discomfort, together with a small number of authoritative corrections: the man does not defend himself to the community, he states the position once, factually, to the most credible people, and then continues to be present. This is the most effective single measure, and it is also the one that men most reliably fail to take, because withdrawal is what the injury demands.
