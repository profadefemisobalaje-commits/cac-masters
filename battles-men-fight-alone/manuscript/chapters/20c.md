## 20.5 Learning to State Your Own Value

This section provides the method. It is addressed to a man who has decided that the arrangement of unrecognised contribution should be corrected, and it proceeds on the assumption that the man is the party who must initiate, because the household is not aware that anything is absent.

### The Four Requirements

**1. The purpose must be information, not thanks.** The disclosure is not a request for gratitude and should not be framed as one. Its content is the man's position, which the household does not currently hold. Men who conduct the disclosure on this basis report that the conversation is received entirely differently from the exchange they had anticipated, in which the household was required to acknowledge a debt.

**2. The material must be specific and dated.** Not *I have done a lot for this family* but the specific items: the years, the amounts, the decisions, the things given up. Specificity is what permits the household to know rather than to agree.

**3. The delivery must be without accusation.** The household has not been malicious; it has been uninformed. A disclosure delivered as an accusation produces a defence, and a defence ends the conversation. The form that works is a statement of the man's position rather than an assessment of the household's conduct, which is the same distinction as in Section 18.3.

**4. The follow-up must include a request.** A disclosure that supplies information and asks for nothing is informative and produces no change in the arrangement. The request should be specific, modest, and stated last: what the man wants to be different, in terms the household can supply.

### The Structure of the Conversation

The conversation has five parts. It should be scheduled, held once with the household's adults rather than with the children, and limited to about forty-five minutes.

**Part One: The opening statement of purpose.** *I want to tell you something that I have never said and that I am not saying as a complaint. I am saying it because it has been affecting how I behave and because I do not want it to continue affecting it.*

**Part Two: The record.** The specific contribution, in sequence, with amounts and dates where they exist. The man should prepare this in writing and read it. Reading is not a weakness; it prevents the record from becoming an argument, and it demonstrates that the material has been considered rather than produced in the moment.

**Part Three: The cost.** What the contribution has required of the man: the hours, the sleep, the health items deferred, the work declined, the obligation carried in secret, the years in which the household's demands and the man's capacity were mismatched. This part is the one most frequently omitted by men, and it is the part the household most needs: the record of the contribution demonstrates the household's dependence, while the cost demonstrates the man's position.

**Part Four: The absence of acknowledgement, stated once and factually.** *I have never been told that any of it was noticed.* The statement should be delivered without embellishment and should not be repeated in the conversation. Its function is to supply missing information, and one statement supplies it.

**Part Five: The request.** Two items: what the man wants to be different in the household's conduct, and what he will be changing in his own. The second should be included even where it is unwelcome, because the arrangement will not be corrected by the household alone. Examples of each:

- *I want to be told when something I have done has been useful. It does not have to be often.*
- *I want the family's decisions that concern me to be discussed with me before they are decided.*
- *I want one evening a week in which I am not managing anything.*
- *I am going to stop doing X, and I am going to tell you when I do not have the money, instead of handling it and saying nothing.*

### Handling the Responses

Four responses occur, and each has a specific handling.

**The response that receives it.** The household acknowledges the record and agrees to the request. The corrective here is to note that this is not the only outcome that constitutes success: the objective was the supply of information, and a household that has received it may take some time to act.

**The defensive response.** The household responds with its own account of contribution — *do you know what I have done?* — which is frequently entirely valid. The handling is to agree with it and continue: *I know, and what you have done is not in question. I am telling you what I have carried, because you do not know it.* Men who respond to the counter-account by withdrawing their own have lost the opportunity, which will not recur for some years.

**The response that converts the record into a score.** *So I owe you.* The handling is a single sentence and a change of subject: *I am not keeping an account and there is nothing owed. I am telling you so that you know, and I want us to continue from here rather than from there.*

**The hostile or dismissive response.** This occurs, and where it occurs consistently it is information of the highest value about the household's position. It should be recorded and not argued. Section 14.6's framework then applies to the marriage, and Chapter 10's boundary method applies to the extended family.

### The Man's Own Conduct Before and After

Three practices make the conversation's outcome durable.

**Stop concealing the cost.** From the conversation onward, the man's disclosures of what things cost should be factual and ordinary: *that month the fees took most of the income and I covered the rest from the reserve.* This is the single practice that most reliably prevents the arrangement's recurrence.

**Stop reducing acknowledgements.** Where an appreciation is offered, accept it, in one sentence: *thank you; it was not easy that year.* The reduction of acknowledgements is a habit and it is extinguished by the man's own practice.

**Ask for what is required.** A man who does not state his own requirements will be required to meet other people's. The practice is to state one requirement per month in specific terms, and to note that stating a requirement does not create a debt, which is the belief that prevents most men in this pattern from doing it.

## 20.6 Creating a Family Culture of Recognition

The final substantive section shifts the subject from the man to the household, because the durable correction is a practice rather than a conversation.

### The Premise

Households have cultures: established ways in which members speak to each other, register each other's contributions, handle disagreement, and express regard. In most of the households examined in this book, the culture has never been chosen; it has been inherited from the households in which both parties grew up and maintained by the ordinary repetition of conduct.

The principal finding relevant to this chapter is that the culture determines who is noticed. In a household where contributions are noticed, a father's work is registered; in a household where they are not, the same work is the baseline. The difference is not the amount of gratitude in the family's character; it is whether the household has a practice.

### The Practices

Six practices establish a culture of recognition. They are listed in ascending order of difficulty.

**1. Naming a specific contribution once a week.** The practice: each adult in the household names one specific thing another member did that made a difference. It should be specific, it should be delivered in the person's presence, and it should be brief. Men frequently find this practice artificial for two or three weeks and then report that it becomes natural and that its effect on the household is disproportionate to its size.

**2. Registering cost as well as outcome.** The practice: when a contribution is discussed, its cost is included. *The fees were paid; your father took two extra contracts to do it.* This practice is the one that addresses the specific deficit of Section 20.1, which is the household's ignorance of the cost.

**3. Marking the sustained contribution.** The practice: an annual occasion, on a date of the household's own choosing, at which the household reviews its year and names what each member contributed. Households that adopt the practice report two effects: the sustained contribution acquires an event, and disagreements about money and fairness reduce, because the position of each member is visible.

**4. Distributing the acknowledging role.** The practice: recognition is not only the wife's function and not only the man's. Children should be taught to notice and to name: *what did Papa do this week that made things easier?* This practice addresses the transmission described in the introduction: children who are taught to notice will notice in their own households.

**5. Reciprocity in the acknowledgement of the man's own contributions to himself.** The practice: the man's contributions to his own maintenance — the medical appointment, the rest, the reduction of an obligation — are treated by the household as contributions rather than as withdrawals. This is the practice that makes the material of Chapters 12 and 18 sustainable, and its absence is the reason that men who adopt healthier arrangements frequently abandon them: the household responds to the change as a loss.

**6. The household's account of the past.** The practice: the household tells its own story including the difficulty. Children who know that a specific year was hard, and what the parents did in it, inherit a usable account of their family, which is the material of Section 17.6, and, in the specific context of this chapter, a knowledge of what was contributed and by whom.

### What Not to Do

Three practices should be avoided, because each is common and each is counterproductive.

**Do not institute a set of formal gratefulness rituals with a recalcitrant household.** A practice that is imposed on an unwilling household produces compliance without knowledge and becomes, within weeks, another item of administration.

**Do not use the practice as a forum for accounts.** Where the weekly practice becomes a mechanism for submitting complaints, the household will discontinue it and the man will conclude that recognition is not wanted, which will confirm the ledger.

**Do not require the acknowledgement to be in the form the man prefers.** Men frequently want a specific form — spoken words, a particular occasion, a public statement — and reject other forms. Where a household's acknowledgement arrives as a cooked meal, an appreciated visit, or a change in the children's conduct toward the man, it should be received as acknowledgement.
