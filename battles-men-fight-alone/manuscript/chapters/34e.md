## 34.6 Breaking a Silence Safely

The remainder of the chapter concerns the ordinary case: a silence that has met the criteria of Section 34.1, that is not a present risk to life, and that must be opened in a form that does not produce a worse arrangement than the silence.

### The Principle

The disclosure is an arrangement rather than an act. Its purpose is that the material be held by more than one person and that the arrangements around it be constructed with the information that they require. The form therefore follows the principle established in Chapter 27.5: the disclosure is graduated, it is proportionate to the recipient's demonstrated record, and its first instance is small.

### The Four Decisions

Before the disclosure, four decisions should be made in writing, because they determine the form.

**Decision One: What is the purpose of the disclosure?**

The possible purposes, and the man should select one: to supply the household with the information its arrangements require; to obtain an arrangement in which the material is held; to bring a specific matter into the open so that it can be addressed; to correct an account that the recipient currently holds; or to establish a boundary that the man cannot establish without the disclosure. The purpose determines what is delivered.

**Decision Two: Who is the recipient, and how much can this person hold?**

The recipient's capacity must be assessed. The categories: the household member who is entitled to the information because their arrangements depend upon it; the person who can hold the material without being damaged by it; the person who cannot hold it, and to whom the delivery would transfer the load — the elderly parent, the child, the person in their own crisis, the person whose position would require them to act on it. The second and third categories are distinguished by a single test: whether the recipient will be required to carry the material indefinitely, or whether they can place it.

**Decision Three: What is the smallest true version?**

The disclosure can be delivered at the level the man can afford, and the levels are: the fact of the material without its content (*there is a matter I have been carrying and I need you to know that it exists*); the fact with the outline; the fact with the content; and the full account, including the history of the concealment. The man should select the level and should be able to state it in one sentence. The second and third levels are legitimate and are the levels most frequently used; the requirement is that the delivery be accurate, since a partial account that misleads is a new concealment.

**Decision Four: What do I require?**

The disclosure should state what the man requires of the recipient, since the household's practical response depends on it. The forms: nothing, other than the recipient's knowledge; the recipient's participation in a specific arrangement; the recipient's silence on the matter outside the household; the recipient's assistance with a specific step.

### The Six Obstacles and Their Handling

**The recipient's distress.** The household's response to a disclosure may be substantial: the tears, the anger, the fear, the sense of betrayal where the man's conduct has been concealed, the question of why the man did not speak earlier. The man's handling: allow the response, do not manage it, do not defend the delay at the first instance, and state the position's own terms — what the material is, what he is doing about it, and what he requires.

**The recipient's response to the delay.** The specific and most common difficulty in a disclosure that follows a period of silence. The recipient's question concerns the years rather than the material. The handling: acknowledge the delay as a fact and give its reason briefly, without the explanation that converts it into a defence, and connect the delay to the disclosure: *I did not say it because I did not want to be the man who had done it, and I am saying it now because you cannot make the arrangements you need to make without knowing.*

**The recipient's own material.** The recipient's difficulty, which the disclosure may activate. The handling: recognize it, place it, and do not allow the man's disclosure to be replaced by the recipient's account of their own position. The professional arrangement is the appropriate route where the recipient is in difficulty of their own.

**The consequences in the household.** A disclosure may alter the household's arrangements: the expenditure, the schooling, the plan, the marriage, the responsibilities. The handling: the disclosure should be accompanied by the man's account of what is altered and what is not, and by the specific arrangements that follow. Men who have conducted a financial disclosure, the most common of this category, describe the finding consistently: the household's response was oriented to the plan rather than to the loss, provided a plan was supplied.

**The consequences in the man's position.** Where the material concerns work, the regulated profession, the position of trust, or the community, the disclosure carries a risk that is frequently accurate. The handling: the assessment of the risk should be conducted before the disclosure, with professional advice where the material is regulated; the professional route should be used where the exposure can be reduced by it; and the disclosure to the household should be separated from the disclosure to the employer or the institution, where the two are capable of separation.

**The consequences for other people.** The disclosure of the man's material may implicate others: a relative who participated, a friend who assisted, a person whose conduct is part of the material, a party to a dispute, a child. The handling: the man's disclosure should not deliver another person's material, and where the material is inseparable, the professional route should be established before the household's disclosure. The man should state his own position and should not distribute the accounts of those who have not consented.

### The Form of the Statement

The practice material gives a consistent form for the disclosure, and its four parts are as follows.

**Part one: the statement of the material.** One or two sentences, plain, without elaboration and without the account's full history.

**Part two: the reason for the silence, stated without defence.** *I did not say it because I was afraid of how you would look at me.* One sentence, no justification.

**Part three: what has been happening as a result.** The consequences that the recipient has already observed and attributed to something else: the temper, the withdrawal, the drinking, the quietness, the sleep. This part matters because it corrects the recipient's existing account of the man's conduct, and it is frequently the part that alters the household's arrangements most substantially.

**Part four: what happens next.** What the man is doing about the material, what he requires, what will be different, and what will not change. The part should be specific and, where possible, should have dates.

### The Subject the Man Cannot Yet Deliver

Where the man cannot deliver the material — the disclosure's content is genuinely unavailable to him, or the recipient is the person the material concerns — the chapter's position is that the professional arrangement is the required step and that the household's entitlement is limited to the fact. The form: *There is a matter I am carrying, it is being addressed with a professional, and it affects me in these specific ways.* The form is legitimate, it is accurate, and it supplies the household with what it actually requires.

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in primary care, in occupational health, and in men's counselling.

**Tunde is forty-six, a middle-ranking officer in a state agency, married to Bola for eighteen years, with three children aged sixteen, thirteen, and nine.**

The material Tunde was carrying concerned a financial position, and it began with a guarantee.

In 2019, Tunde's younger brother approached him about a business: a supply arrangement requiring working capital, for which a bank required a guarantee. Tunde gave the guarantee in the sum of about eleven months of his salary. The business operated for fourteen months and then failed. The guarantee was called in 2021, and Tunde met the sum in instalments over the following three years, using a facility from a cooperative, an advance from his employer's credit scheme, and, in the third year, the proceeds of the sale of a plot of family land that had been intended for the household's house.

The household's knowledge of the position was, at the time of the case, as follows: Bola knew of the guarantee, because Tunde had told her at the outset; she did not know that it had been called, that the instalments were being met, that the cooperative facility existed, that the credit scheme's advance was outstanding, or that the plot had been sold. The household's plan for the house had been deferred twice and Bola had been told that the delay was the agency's arrears of salary. The two eldest children attended a school at which the fees had been paid late four times in six years, on explanations that described a banking delay.

The presentations that brought the case into a service, in the third year, were four: the sleep, which had reduced to five hours with early waking; the presentation to a general practitioner with what was recorded as chest pain and palpitations, investigated with normal findings; the shortness with the children about money, in which Tunde had refused a school trip and later found himself unable to explain the refusal without raising his voice; and Bola's own statement, made in the second year, that *if there is a problem, the problem is that you have become a different man in the house.*

Tunde's own position at the point of the consultation was described by him exactly: *I had three things in my head. I could not tell her that the land is gone. The land was for the house. Her mother sold her own jewellery for the first plan in 2013 and I have not been able to look at her since the sale. And I had believed that I could recover the whole of it before anybody noticed, and at the point of the chest pain I understood the arithmetic. I have been paying the instalments out of this month's salary every month for three years. There is nothing coming.*

The work, conducted over about eight months, had five elements.

**The professional arrangement.** From the general practitioner, Tunde was referred for an assessment and then for counselling. He described the first six weeks as devoted almost entirely to arithmetic: *The counsellor made me write every obligation down. I had been keeping them in my head, which is why I could not sleep. On paper there were seven obligations and a total, and the total was three times what I had been able to think about at once. The page did not make it worse. It made it possible to look at.*

**The accounting.** With a debt counselling arrangement obtained in the second month, the position was consolidated: the obligations, the interest, the arrears, the income, and the options. The arrangement reduced the total by a third through the negotiation of one facility and the restructuring of another, and it established a schedule with dates.

**The disclosure.** Tunde made the disclosure to Bola over a period of two sittings, in the four-part form of this section, and, in the second sitting, with the accounting document present. His account of the first sitting: *I said the material in two sentences, I said why I had not said it in one, and then I said that the fees had been late four times and that the delay in the house was not arrears of salary. She was quiet for about a minute. Then she asked me for the numbers. I was prepared for something else. She asked me for the numbers and I did not have them, because I had not brought the page.*

**The household's arrangements.** In the third month, Tunde and Bola conducted the household meeting with the three children, at which the position was stated in the terms of Section 34.6's Part Four: what had happened, what the household's position was, what would change, and what would not. Tunde described the meeting: *The sixteen-year-old asked me whether he was leaving the school. I said no, and I was able to say it because I had the schedule. He asked me how much was owed and I told him, and after that he stopped asking me for the things he had been asking for, which I had not understood was connected.*

**The arrangements for himself.** Tunde joined a men's group in the fourth month and continued the counselling arrangement for a further year. He described one element of it as the most important: *I had been the one who covered. My brother, my brother-in-law, two colleagues — I had given guarantees for four people in eleven years, and I had not counted them until the group asked me to count. The group was the first place in my life where I said that I was afraid and where somebody was not relying on me for something. The man next to me had done the same thing with his own brother and had lost a house. We have both stopped giving guarantees. There is nobody else in my life who would have told me that sentence without blaming me.*

Tunde's summary, three years after the disclosure, was: *I am still paying and the schedule has about two years remaining. The house has not been built and we have a revised plan that the whole household knows. My brother has not repaid anything and Bola knows that as well, and I am not required to defend him in my own house any more, which was the part I had not expected. What I had believed was that the money was the weight. The weight was the arithmetic I was holding alone in the dark, at five in the morning, for three years, with no page.*

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The silence was maintained by the man's management of the arithmetic.** The seven obligations held in the head rather than on a page, and the total, which the man could not consider at once. This is the cognitive load of Section 34.2 in its practical form, and the case records the counsellor's intervention accurately: the page did not make the position worse; it made the position available to be looked at, which is the mechanism by which a position can be addressed.

**The household had constructed its own account and the account was worse than the material.** The arrears of salary, the banking delay, the different man in the house. The case is included because this is the most consistently reported consequence of a man's financial concealment: the household supplies an explanation, the explanation is inaccurate, and the arrangements are made on the basis of it.

**The disclosure was conducted in the four-part form and the recipient's response was to the plan rather than to the loss.** Bola's request for the numbers. The case is included because the outcome is general and because it corrects the man's central prediction about the disclosure: what the household required was the information with which to make its arrangements, and the man's assumption that the household's response would be directed at him was inaccurate.

**The household meeting with the children was conducted with the plan.** What would change and what would not, with the schedule present. The case records the son's question and the change in his requests, and the finding is worth noting: the household's members adjust their own conduct to the position once it is stated, and the adjustment is generally constructive.

**The man's own position was diagnosed through the guarantees and the group.** Four guarantees in eleven years, uncounted, and the man's own account of the group's function: the first place where he had said he was afraid and where nobody was relying on him for anything. This is the arrangement of Chapter 27.5 in operation, and the case is included because the material of the chapter is a silence and the correction is an arrangement rather than a disclosure: the disclosure supplied the household with the information, and the group supplied the man with a place in which the material was held.
