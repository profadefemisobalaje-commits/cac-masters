## 19.7 Illustrative Case: The Eldest of Nine

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in family practice, counselling, and the research literature on parentification and early caregiving.

**Musa is fifty-eight, a retired customs officer, the eldest of nine children, married for thirty-one years with four children of his own.**

The events that ended his childhood occurred in 1981, when Musa was fifteen. His father, a produce buyer, died suddenly of a stroke in Kano, leaving a household of eleven — his mother, nine children, and an elderly aunt — with a small house, a plot of farmland in the village, a modest savings account that proved to be considerably smaller than the family had assumed, and a dispute with his father's elder brother over the farmland.

Musa's account of the transition is precise and short: *In September I was in Form Two. In January I was in my father's place on the farmland, learning the trade from a man who had bought from my father for twenty years, and by the following planting season I was responsible for the household. Nobody sat me down. There was nobody to sit me down. My mother was in a state that I did not understand at the time, and my father's brother was making a claim on the land. I understood that I was the only person who could do anything.*

Over the following nine years, Musa: withdrew from secondary school in Form Three and completed his education privately to school certificate level in 1988; supported the household through produce buying, transport, and later a period of employment in a clearing agent's office; reconciled the land dispute in 1991 through the intervention of a community elder, with the loss of a portion of the farmland; funded two brothers' secondary education and one sister's nursing training; and managed his mother's affairs until her death in 2014.

He married in 1994 and joined the customs service in 1996, rising to a senior position before retirement. He has four children, three of whom are graduates. His own description of his adult life is: *I have never had a year in which I was not responsible for somebody. I do not know what that would look like.*

The material that produced the case emerged after his retirement, in the form of a depressive episode that he initially attributed to his mother's death four years earlier and to his loss of structure after retirement. The features he described: a persistent sense of emptiness; the loss of interest in matters he had previously managed; a difficulty in the morning that he described as a reluctance to begin; and one specific and recurrent thought, which he eventually described as: *There is nobody left to be responsible for.*

The assessment identified four elements of the pattern described in this chapter.

First, the instruction set had operated without revision for forty years. Musa's operative sentences, which he wrote during the assessment, were: *If I do not do it, nobody will.* *I cannot tell them what I am carrying.* *They will not manage without me.* Each was true in 1981 and each was, by 2016, either false or negotiable, and none had been examined.

Second, the requirement that had never been presented was specific and datable. Musa had wanted, at seventeen, to continue in secondary school. The decision to leave had been made by him, alone, in a period of two weeks, in the interests of the household, and it had never been discussed with his mother or with anybody else. He had carried the decision for forty-one years.

Third, the position of the family's strong party had been maintained by nine siblings and by his own household. He was the person who resolved disputes, funded emergencies, mediated between siblings, and represented the family. In the five years before his retirement, three of his siblings had brought disputes to him for resolution, all of which he had managed, and none of which he had declined.

Fourth, the capacity for rest and play had never developed. Musa's account of his leisure, at the age of fifty-eight, was that he did not have any: his week was organised by obligations, and his relaxation consisted of watching news programmes, which he described as his one interest.

The intervention ran over two years and consisted of four elements.

The clinical element came first. The depressive episode was treated, which Musa described as the precondition of everything else: *I could not have thought about any of this while I was in that state, and I would have read the whole thing as a further account of my own failure.*

The requirement was then presented. Musa wrote to his mother's surviving brother — his only surviving member of the parental generation — and, later, spoke to four of his siblings, in each case with the same statement: what had happened when he was fifteen, what he had given up, and what it had been like to carry the household without being allowed to be young. The statement was delivered as information rather than as a complaint, and the responses varied: two siblings wept, one recounted that he had never known about the withdrawn school enrolment, and one responded with a comparison to his own difficulties, at which point Musa discontinued the conversation.

The revision of the instruction set was conducted in writing with the counsellor, and its practical effect was specific. Musa, over a period of about a year, transferred: the family's dispute-resolution function, which passed to a younger brother with a professional background in law; the management of his mother's property, which was transferred to a joint arrangement between three siblings; and the arrangement by which he provided for two nephews' fees, which he concluded with a defined date and a statement to the families concerned, both of which were accepted without difficulty.

The fourth element was the recovery of the missing capacities, and it took an unusual form. Musa, at fifty-nine, took up two things: the secondary school education he had not completed, in the form of a part-time programme he enrolled in and completed in eighteen months, and swimming lessons, which he had never had. He described the second as the more consequential, on the grounds that he had been unable to swim his whole life and had never told anybody.

His summary, at the age of sixty, was: *Forty years ago I did the right thing and I have never regretted it and I would do it again. What I did not know was that I was also deciding, at fifteen, that I would never need anything from anybody. That is the part I am now undoing, and it is slow work at this age. The swimming took six months and I would have paid a great deal for it at any point in the last forty years, and it never occurred to me to ask anybody, which is the whole of the problem and the whole of the cure.*

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The role was acquired without discussion and without a date.** The transition is dated by events — September to January — and there was no moment at which an adult said: this is now yours. The absence of any formal transfer is typical, and it is why the position has no terminus: a role acquired without an agreement is not released by one.

**The instruction set survived its circumstances by four decades.** The sentences formed at fifteen were operating in 2016 on a household that had ceased to exist decades earlier. This is the case's central teaching, and it is the reason Step One of Section 19.6 is conducted in writing: the instructions are invisible until they are written down and dated.

**The unaddressed requirement was a school enrolment.** Not an abstraction about being loved, but a specific decision at seventeen with a specific cost, carried for forty-one years. The specificity matters: requirements of this kind are presented in a sentence, whereas the general requirement of having been cared for cannot be presented at all.

**The depressive episode arrived when the arrangement's justification ended.** The thought *there is nobody left to be responsible for* is the mechanism in its exact form, and its arrival after retirement and bereavement is the most commonly reported timing. The clinical treatment preceded the reflective work, in that order, and Musa's own assessment was that the reverse order would have failed.

**The recovery was conducted at the level of two concrete items.** A part-time qualification and swimming lessons. The material recovered is frequently this specific, and it is worth noting that the man's description of the second item — that he had never told anybody in sixty years that he could not swim — is the entire chapter compressed into one sentence.
