## 22.5 Practical Strategies for Change

This section sets out the methods, in the sequence in which they should be applied. The order matters: the environmental measures come before the behavioural ones, and the behavioural measures before the psychological ones, because each stage makes the next possible.

### Stage One: The Environment

**Remove the cue from the environment.** The substance from the house; the application from the phone; the route that passes the betting shop changed; the television removed from the bedroom; the specific room avoided at the specific hour. The measure is mechanical, it is decided once, and it operates continuously thereafter. Its effectiveness is disproportionate to its apparent simplicity, and men who attempt the behavioural work without it frequently report that the environmental change alone produced a substantial reduction.

**Introduce friction.** The behavioural work of the last decade has established a reliable principle: the effort required to perform the behaviour predicts the rate of the behaviour. Every step added between the cue and the act — the distance, the lock, the requirement to ask, the requirement to go somewhere, the delay of thirty minutes — reduces its frequency.

**Alter the situation that surrounds the cue.** Where the behaviour occurs at a specific hour, the hour should be occupied. Where it occurs in a specific company, the occasion should be restructured rather than the friendship ended. Where it occurs after an argument, the argument's handling is the intervention.

**Recruit the household where the household is affected.** Not as supervisors, which produces resentment, but as participants in the arrangement: the removal of alcohol from the house, the change of an evening routine, the substitution of a shared activity. Chapter 18's material on arrangements applies.

### Stage Two: The Substitute

**Identify the function from the trigger map.** Rest, sleep, company, relief from anxiety, the discharge of anger, excitement, competence.

**Choose the substitute to fit the function, not the virtue.** The most frequent error in this work is the selection of a substitute on grounds of healthfulness: the man who substitutes running for late-night screen use has chosen an activity that performs a different function, and it will not hold. The substitute should be tested against the function identified in Section 22.4, and it should be available at the hour and in the place in which the behaviour occurred.

**Arrange the substitute in advance.** The decision must be made before the cue arrives, which is the whole of the difference between a substitute and an intention. The form that works is a scheduled arrangement rather than an available option: the gym appointment, the standing call, the specific task, the fixed activity with another person.

**Accept the substitute's imperfection.** Men frequently abandon a substitute because it does not produce the relief the weakness produced. The standard is comparison with the alternative, which is the weakness and its consequences, rather than comparison with the weakness's reward.

### Stage Three: The Behavioural Method

**Measure rather than prohibit.** The record in Section 22.4 continues. Its effects are three: it produces the data on which the plan is based; it interrupts the automaticity of the behaviour, because recording requires awareness; and it converts the matter from a moral question into a practical one, which is the change of register that men report as the most important element of the whole process.

**Reduce the target rather than eliminating it, where reduction is the objective.** A defined reduction — from daily to three days, from four units to two — with a review point, is more durable than an absolute prohibition, and its failures are smaller and recoverable. Where the behaviour is a dependence, the elimination is the objective and the route is Chapter 23's.

**Set the review at a defined interval.** Two weeks for the first review, then monthly. The review's four questions are those used elsewhere in this book: what occurred, what the pattern shows, what caused the failures, and what changes in the arrangement for the coming period.

**Plan for the relapse.** The situations in which the behaviour is most likely to recur, identified in advance, with a specific arrangement for each. This is the same danger-point method as in Section 23.6, applied to a weakness rather than a dependence, and its purpose is to prevent the single episode from terminating the work.

### Stage Four: The Psychological Work

**Address the belief.** A weakness that has persisted for years is frequently supported by a belief: that the behaviour is required for sleep, for social acceptability, for the discharge of tension, for the management of an unbearable state. The belief should be written out and tested against the record. In a substantial proportion of cases the belief is contradicted by the man's own data, and the contradiction is more persuasive than any argument supplied by somebody else.

**Address the situation that produced the weakness.** As Section 22.4 establishes: the period, the loss, the role, the undisclosed material. Where the weakness began in a specific period, its treatment includes that period's business, and the relevant material is in Chapters 11, 13, and 19.

**Address the shame.** The material in Chapter 21's graduated disclosure applies. The specific finding that men report in this context is worth repeating: the disclosure of a weakness to one safe person reduces its hold substantially, and the reduction is not a matter of comfort but of evidence, since the disclosure demonstrates that the material can be known without the catastrophe the man has predicted.

### Stage Five: What to Do When It Fails

Four rules govern the handling of a failure, and they are the material that determines whether the work continues.

**Analyse, do not sentence.** The questions are: what was the cue, what was the state, what was the arrangement, and what failed? Not: what does this say about me?

**Shorten the interval between the failure and the resumption.** The critical variable in every account of behaviour change. A man who resumes within a day has lost a day; a man who resumes after a month has lost a month and has also had a month in which to conclude that the work is futile.

**Change one element of the arrangement.** The lesson of a failure is generally information about the environment or the substitute. The change should be specific: a different substitute, a different hour, a different arrangement with the person involved.

**Report the failure to the accountable person.** Described in Section 22.6. The report is the mechanism by which the failure does not become the material of shame, and the men who maintain change for years are overwhelmingly those who have somebody to whom the failures are reported.

## 22.6 Accountability and Honest Self-Examination

The final substantive section addresses the arrangement that makes the others durable, and it begins with a finding that men find counterintuitive: the private management of a personal weakness is the arrangement with the poorest record, and confidentiality is not the same as secrecy.

### The Distinction Between Confidentiality and Secrecy

**Confidentiality** is a property of an arrangement in which a small number of specified people know the situation and do not disclose it. It is the arrangement of professional practice, it is what enables men to obtain treatment, and it is compatible with accountability.

**Secrecy** is an arrangement in which nobody knows. It produces the costs described in Section 22.2 and it is the arrangement in which the behaviour is not measured, not discussed, and not corrected.

The whole of this section concerns the conversion of secrecy into confidentiality.

### The Accountable Person

**The selection.** One person, at least, and the criteria are specific: they should have no stake in the material that would compromise their judgement; they should have a record of keeping confidences; they should be able to state an assessment to the man's face; and they should be willing to hold the arrangement over time rather than to hear the matter once.

**The categories available.** A professional — counsellor, psychologist, physician, or a religious adviser where the man's framework includes one — whose function is confidential by definition and who requires no prior relationship. A peer — a friend, a sibling, or a member of a group of men in comparable positions. A group, which for many men is the most effective arrangement because the position is symmetrical: everyone present has a weakness, and the man's material is not unusual.

**What the arrangement consists of.** Not the disclosure of every occurrence, which is neither necessary nor sustainable. The arrangement is: the man reports at a defined interval, states whether the plan is being followed, states the failures, and receives an assessment. The interval matters more than its frequency; a monthly report that occurs is worth more than a weekly arrangement that lapses.

### The Four Practices of Honest Self-Examination

**1. The record.** The written measurement described in Section 22.4, maintained continuously. Men report that the record's principal value appears after several months, when it demonstrates a change or the absence of one, and that it is the only reliable protection against the man's own account of himself, which is systematically either too favourable or too harsh.

**2. The defined review.** Monthly, with the four questions, and annually, against the specification described in Section 21.6. The review converts the work into an arrangement with a schedule rather than an intention with an indefinite term.

**3. The assessment from somebody else.** The practice of asking, at intervals, for an assessment from a person who knows the man: how has he been in the last month, what has changed, and what has the person noticed. This practice supplies the correction that the man's own account cannot supply, and it is the practice most frequently omitted.

**4. The stated standard.** The written statement of what the man intends, which is the specification from Section 21.6. Its function in this context is to distinguish between the standard and the shame: the standard is specific and is met or not met on particular days, while the shame is general and is met or not met in the man's assessment of himself.

### The Four Prohibitions

**Do not conduct the arrangement with somebody who has a stake in concealing the weakness.** The colleague who shares the behaviour, the relative who would be shamed by the disclosure, the person whose own conduct the weakness protects.

**Do not use the arrangement as absolution.** The arrangement's function is measurement and assessment, not relief. Men who report the behaviour and feel relieved without changing the arrangement are reported in the literature on behaviour change as a recognisable pattern, and their reports become a substitute for the work rather than a part of it.

**Do not disclose in a form that subjects the man to a general assessment.** The arrangement concerns the specific behaviour, and its scope should be defined. The temptation to convert it into a general conversation about the man's life should be resisted, at least in the early period, because general conversations are what produced the shame in the first place.

**Do not abandon the arrangement at the point of improvement.** The most common failure of this section's method, and it occurs at about six to nine months, at the point at which the man's confidence is highest and the arrangement appears unnecessary. The behaviour's return follows within a year in a substantial proportion of cases in which the arrangement was discontinued.
