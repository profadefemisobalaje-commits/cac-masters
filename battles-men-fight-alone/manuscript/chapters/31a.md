# The Battles of Leadership, Power, and Responsibility {.part num="IX" img="/images/part9.jpg" summary="The hidden pressures of carrying other people's expectations"}

# The Loneliness of Leadership {.chapter num="31" short="The Loneliness of Leadership"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the three structural effects that a position of authority has on a man's relationships, and distinguish them from personal failings.
2. Explain why the isolation of leadership is produced by the position rather than by the man's temperament, and identify the arrangements that the position makes impossible.
3. Name the four burdens that a leader carries and that are not visible in his performance, and assess which of them he is currently carrying alone.
4. Recognise the presentations of a leader who has nothing left, including the signs that his condition is affecting the people he is responsible for.
5. Construct the arrangements that allow a man to hold authority without the position consuming his relationships, his judgement, and his household.
6. Recognise the specific risk of transferring a leadership arrangement into a household, and apply the correction described in Section 31.6.
:::

## Introduction

Every arrangement of authority separates the man who holds it from the people subject to it. This is not a defect of particular men or particular institutions. It is a structural consequence of the position, and it operates in the same way in a company, a government department, a school, a hospital, a farm, a workshop, a household, a cooperative, and an association.

The man who holds authority is the person whose decisions determine what happens to other people; the person to whom information is brought after it has been selected; the person whose reactions are read by everybody in the room; the person who cannot be told the ordinary things that colleagues tell one another; and, in a substantial proportion of cases, the person who is the last to learn what the people around him already know.

This chapter concerns the cost of that arrangement, which is not usually described as a cost at all. Authority is treated as an achievement and a privilege, and the man who holds it is not expected to require anything. He is expected to be composed, to be available, to decide, to absorb the anxiety of others, and to carry whatever he is carrying without its appearing in his conduct or his performance.

The chapter's approach has four commitments.

The first is to describe the isolation as a property of the position. Men who find themselves without a peer frequently assume that the fault is theirs: that they have become arrogant, that they have failed to make friends, that they do not know how to relate to people any more. The description in Section 31.1 and Section 31.2 makes the structural element explicit, and the distinction matters because a structural problem requires a structural arrangement rather than a personal correction.

The second is that leadership imposes obligations that are real and that the chapter is not arguing against. A man who holds authority has responsibilities to the people he leads, and those responsibilities include the management of his own state. This is the reason the chapter is placed where it is. A leader whose judgement is degraded by isolation makes worse decisions, and the costs of those decisions are borne by people who had no part in them.

The third is that the material is specifically male in its form, and not in its difficulty. Men in positions of authority are frequently the men least likely to have the arrangements described in Chapter 27, and the position compounds the ordinary barriers with a further set: the disclosure would be a risk to the position, the arrangements that exist are frequently subordinate, and the man's own model of the role excludes the requirement.

The fourth is that the work is practicable. The arrangements in Section 31.5 are specific, they are used by leaders in every sector, and they do not require the man to behave in a way that conflicts with his position. A man can lead and be known; the two are not alternatives, and the arrangements exist precisely to make them compatible.
