# Becoming Emotionally Strong Without Becoming Emotionally Cold {.chapter num="39" short="Becoming Emotionally Strong Without Becoming Emotionally Cold"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the false choice between strength and warmth, and explain why men are offered it.
2. Trace the process by which a man becomes emotionally cold, and identify the point in the process at which he currently stands.
3. Analyse the five costs of coldness, including its effects on his marriage, his children, his own perception, and his judgement.
4. Define emotional strength in the terms used in this book, and distinguish it from stoicism, bluntness, and aggression.
5. Apply the six practices that build and maintain it, in the man's own form.
6. Treat warmth as a discipline of conduct rather than as a feeling to be waited for, and construct the arrangements that maintain it.
:::

## Introduction

A man who has been injured in the arrangements described in the earlier parts of this book arrives at a specific position, and it is a rational position. He has learned that the expression of what he feels is met with a cost, that the disclosure of his material is met with consequences, that his softness is used against him, and that the reliable conduct available to him is the composed exterior. He therefore constructs it, and his competence increases as the warmth decreases.

The construction produces two outcomes, and the culture has named only one of them. The first is a man who is steady, reliable, unshakeable, and available in a crisis. The second, which arrives with the first and which is generally discovered late, is a man who cannot be reached by the people who live with him, who cannot be moved by what would ordinarily move him, and who has lost the perception of his own state because he has spent years refusing to register it.

This chapter concerns the point at which strength becomes coldness and how a man acquires the first without the second.

The chapter's approach has five commitments.

The first is that the difficulty is genuine. Men are offered a false choice by their environments: the man who feels is treated as unreliable, and the man who does not feel is treated as strong. The chapter does not treat the men who took the second position as fools; they took it for reasons, and their reasons were frequently accurate. The purpose of the chapter is to establish that the choice is false and that the position between the two extremes is not a compromise but a third thing with its own competence.

The second is that coldness is a process and not a trait. Men are not cold by nature; they are cold by arrangement, and the arrangement can be described. Section 39.2 traces it, and the reader should be able to identify the point at which his own process stands, because the practices in Section 39.5 differ by stage.

The third is that the costs of coldness are borne by others and are visible to them. The chapter's material on the wife who lives with a man who is present and unreachable, on the children who learn to select their disclosures, and on the household that organises itself around the man's state is the strongest available evidence, and the reader should apply it to his own household during the reading rather than afterwards.

The fourth is that emotional strength is definable, is composed of competences, and is learnable. Section 39.4 supplies the definition and the four competences, and Section 39.5 the practices. The claim is not that the work is easy for men; it is that the work is describable in the terms this book has used throughout — an arrangement, a practice, a set of specific acts — rather than as a temperamental change.

The fifth is that warmth is a discipline. This is the chapter's practical centre, and it is the finding that men report as the most useful: warmth is not a feeling that arrives and is therefore not something to be waited for. It is a set of specific acts — the attention, the appreciation, the touch, the remembering, the presence — and the acts are available to a man in any state. The men who conducted them report that the feeling follows the conduct, which is the reverse of the arrangement they had assumed.
