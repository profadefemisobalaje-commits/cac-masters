## 18.3 When a Husband Feels Unseen

The experience of being unseen within a functioning marriage is one of the most common reports in the clinical material on men and marriage, and it is one of the least recognisable to the households in which it occurs, because nothing is visibly wrong.

### The Distinction Between Being Unseen and Being Neglected

The distinction is the key to this section, and men who are in the situation frequently cannot make it.

**Neglect** is a matter of attention and effort. A neglectful wife does not listen, does not ask, does not consider the man's needs, and does not maintain the practical administration of the relationship. Neglect is a fault, it is visible, and it is actionable.

**Being unseen** occurs in the absence of neglect. The wife is attentive to the household and to the man's requirements: she knows his schedule, his preferences, and his obligations, and she manages a substantial part of his practical life. What she does not have is a picture of him — of what he thinks, what he fears, what he wants, and what has happened to him internally. The reason is not indifference; it is that the picture has never been supplied, as Chapter 14 established, and that she has been operating on the version of him that the role presents.

The consequence is a specific and confusing experience: a man who is well looked after and does not feel known. Men describe it with precision: *she can tell you what I eat, what I earn, and who my relatives are. She cannot tell you what I am afraid of.*

### The Signs

Six signs indicate the condition, and they can be assessed without consulting anybody.

**The man's answers to a wife's questions are administrative.** How was your day, what did they say at the meeting, what time will you be back — each is answered with information rather than with content.

**The wife's account of him is a description of his conduct.** Asked to describe her husband by somebody else, a wife in this arrangement describes what he does, how he provides, and what he is like to live with. She does not describe what he wants, which she has not been told.

**The man has no recent experience of being asked a question about himself by his wife.** Not *what did you do* but *how do you feel about it*, *what do you want*, *what worries you*.

**Disclosure, where it occurs, is transactional and late.** The man explains a difficulty to his wife when he has already decided what to do, or when the consequence has arrived, which is described in Chapter 9 as deferred disclosure.

**The man's interior life is supplied by somebody else.** A friend, a colleague, a sibling, a counsellor, or, in a proportion of cases, a person outside the marriage. Where this arrangement exists without the wife's knowledge, the conditions for the situation described in Chapter 16 have been established.

**The man's own difficulty in answering the question.** This is the most important sign and the most diagnostic. Men who are unseen frequently discover, when asked what they want or what they fear, that they cannot answer, because they have not been in the practice of knowing. The absence of a picture is not only the wife's; it is frequently also the man's.

### Why the Condition Persists

**The man does not know what he would say.** He has no vocabulary, no practice, and no precedent. The material of Chapter 3 applies in full, and its practical consequence is that the disclosure has to be constructed before it can be made.

**The disclosure appears to risk the marriage's equilibrium.** A wife who learns that her husband is unhappy frequently responds with anxiety, with self-examination, or with a crisis. The man's prediction of this response is frequently accurate, and the prediction is the reason he does not attempt the conversation.

**The arrangement is comfortable.** A marriage of logistics and competent management is comfortable for both parties in some respects. It has no quarrels about the substantial questions, no discussion of the future, and no requirement of either party to face anything uncomfortable. Its cost is invisible until it is high.

**The comparison with other households.** Where the man's father was silent and the marriages he observes are administered in the same manner, the arrangement appears to be what marriage is. The appearance is not a description of what marriage can be.

### The Consequence of Persisting

Four consequences follow from the arrangement's continuation, and the fourth is the one men most frequently report.

**The slow conversion of the partnership into an administration.** The marriage becomes a well-run institution: children managed, obligations met, occasions observed, and nothing else. Chapter 15 describes the end point.

**The increased weight of the man's external relationships.** Friendships, work, siblings, and organisations acquire the whole of the man's emotional requirement, and the man's availability to them increases at the expense of the household.

**The arrival of the material at an inconvenient point.** Men in this arrangement frequently carry the material until the children have finished school, the obligations have reduced, and the working life has begun to slow. At that point the arrangement's absence of intimacy becomes impossible to ignore, because there is no longer a load that explains it. Men at fifty-five describe this as an abrupt discovery, and it is not abrupt; it is the removal of the material that was concealing the arrangement.

**The man's conclusion that he is not loved.** The fourth consequence, and the most damaging. A man who has been unseen for twenty years does not merely conclude that his wife does not know him; he concludes, with increasing confidence, that she is not interested, that she married a position, and that if he did not provide, she would not care whether he was there. The conclusion is frequently inaccurate and it is almost never tested, and it produces the resignation described in Chapter 14 as the family's settled arrangement.
