## 39.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"If I start feeling everything, how will I carry the position?"**

The concern is the strongest barrier reported by men in this material and it is addressed by the definition in Section 39.4: the competence includes regulation and holding, and the position's requirements are met by the man who registers a state, holds it, and acts. The finding from the case material is the reverse of the man's prediction: the men who conducted the work report that their capacity to carry the position increased, because the states that had been operating outside their awareness — the mounting pressure, the accumulating resentment, the unregistered exhaustion — became available to be managed rather than discharged into their households.

**"My father was like this and his father before him. It is in the family."**

The pattern is transmitted and it is not a nature. The mechanism is the fourth of Section 39.2, and its correction is the one the case in Section 39.7 illustrates: the naming of it as the father's pattern, the man's own account of the decision at which he adopted it, and the practices. The specific finding for readers who hold this position: the transmission is interrupted by conduct, and the man's children learn the pattern's interruption from his conduct rather than from his instruction.

**"I do not want to become soft. My household needs me to be the strong one."**

The chapter's definition supplies the answer: strength is the capacity to register, regulate, hold, and act, and the man who registers his grief and acts correctly in his position is stronger in the terms that matter than the man who cannot register it. The household's requirement is the man who can be relied upon and who is present, and the material of Section 39.3 establishes that the household pays a specific price for the version of strength that omits the presence.

**"I am not built for this. I am not a talker."**

The practices are constructed for readers who are not talkers: the daily sentence, which is written and takes two minutes; the physical channel; the bounded occasion; the brief repair; the one setting; and the register of acts. The chapter's finding is that men who describe themselves as unable to talk conduct the practices successfully, and that the written forms are generally the route by which the spoken forms become available.

**"My wife has been living with this for thirty years. Is there anything left to say?"**

The case in Section 39.7 supplies the finding: the disclosure conducted in the fourth month altered the household's arrangements, and the man's wife returned from her sister's house. The specific instruction is Chapter 39.7's fourth element — the disclosure in the four-part form, with the man's own account of what he had not known rather than a defence of what he had done — and the finding from the case material is that the household's response is generally available even after a long period, because the household has been waiting for the account rather than for the correction of the past.

**"What if I do the acts and the feeling never arrives?"**

Then the acts will have altered the household's arrangements, which is the first objective. The finding from the case material is that the feeling arrives in the majority of cases and that it arrives with a delay measured in months, and that the men who conduct the acts without the feeling report a specific and unanticipated outcome: the household's conduct toward them changes on the basis of the acts, and the household's warmth is itself the material that returns the feeling.

::: summary
**Chapter 39 in brief.** Men are offered a false choice: that a man may be strong or he may be warm. The offer treats the two as ends of one scale, converts a technique of conduct into an identity, and omits the trade's costs. The evidence is that the two are separate competences, that the composed exterior is a technique rather than an identity, and that the position between the extremes is a third thing rather than a compromise: a man who can register what he feels, regulate it, state it where stating it is useful, hold it where holding it is required, and remain fully available to the people entitled to him — while acting, deciding, and carrying.

A man becomes cold in six stages: his states were met with a consequence; he acquired the technique; the technique was extended into the settings where it has no function; he stopped registering the states; the states are discharged elsewhere, generally into the household; and he becomes the exterior. The mechanisms are the immediate relief of the numbing, which does not select among states and therefore removes the satisfaction and the tenderness along with the pain; the environment's reinforcement; the absence of any occasion at which the technique is reconsidered; and the example of the man's own father.

The costs are five: the marriage, which becomes administrative and in which the spouse lives with a man who is present and unreachable; the children, who select what they bring to him and who describe him in later years as a good man they did not know, with the sons reproducing the pattern; the people he leads, whose information and loyalty are altered by his temperature; his own perception, including the loss of the capacity to be moved and of the capacity to know what he wants; and his judgement, which operates without the information his states would supply.

Emotional strength is defined, in operational terms, as a set of four competences: registration, regulation, expression, and endurance. Six practices build them: the daily registration in one written sentence; the physical channel; the deliberate non-numbing; the repair after rupture, stated within the day; the arrangement in which the man is known; and warmth as a discipline. Warmth is the chapter's practical centre and it is a conduct rather than a feeling: twelve acts are supplied — the greeting, the question and the pause, the specific appreciation, the spoken appreciation, the touch that is not an approach, the presence at what matters to the other person, the remembering, the protection of another's time, the unqualified apology, the play, the statement of the man's own position, and the question that requires an answer — and two rules govern them: the acts are conducted at a stated frequency and are not conditional on the man's state, and they are stated once to the household in the man's own words. The arrangement precedes the state; the feeling follows the conduct.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Which form of the offer did you adopt, and what is the sentence about yourself that you took from it?
2. Which stage of Section 39.2 are you in, and where are your states currently discharged?
3. Complete the household's four questions and record the answers. What did you learn that you had not known?
4. Which of the five costs is most visible in your material, and what is the evidence?
5. Complete the four competences for your own case. Which is the one that is absent, and what does its absence produce in your marriage, your children, and your decisions?
6. Write the five acts and their frequencies, and deliver the statement to your household.
7. Consider a man you know whose father was cold and who is now cold himself. What would the interruption of the pattern require of him, and what would it require of you?

## Further Reading

- **On emotional awareness and regulation:** the research literature on emotional awareness, on the identification and naming of states, and on the effects of the suppression of emotional experience, including the findings on the consequences of suppression for perception, memory, relationships, and health.
- **On men and emotional expression:** the research on men's emotional socialisation and its effects, including the findings on the consequences of restricted emotional expression, on the patterns of men's relationships, and on the evidence concerning change in adult life.
- **On marriage and the man's emotional availability:** the research on marital satisfaction and stability, including the findings on the association between the husband's emotional availability and the wife's satisfaction, on the patterns of interaction that predict marital outcomes, and on the evidence concerning change in established marriages.
- **On fatherhood and children's outcomes:** the literature on fathers' involvement and its associations with children's outcomes, including the findings on the effects of the father's availability and warmth, on the transmission of patterns between generations, and on the evidence concerning the interruption of those patterns.
- **Practical resources:** marriage and relationship counselling; individual counselling and psychological services; men's groups; parenting programmes; and the material in Appendices E, F, G, and J.
