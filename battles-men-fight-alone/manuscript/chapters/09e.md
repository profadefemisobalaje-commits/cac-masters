## 9.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"My wife and I share everything except money. Why is that the one subject I cannot discuss?"**

Because it is the one subject in which a man's standing has not been revised by modern expectations. The household may be genuinely equal in decision-making, childcare, and emotional openness, and the belief that the man should be economically competent frequently survives untouched. A man who reports this difficulty should note that his household is not unusual: concealment is more common in respectful, functional marriages than in troubled ones, precisely because the man has more to lose by disappointing someone whose regard he values.

**"Is it dishonest not to tell her? I am not taking anything from the household."**

It is concealment rather than theft, and it is harmful for a different reason. Decisions about money affect the whole household: the school, the medical treatment, the food, the housing, and the plans that a partner may be making on the assumption of a particular position. A partner who has been excluded from the true position is not making decisions; she is guessing. The relevant question is not whether money was removed, but whether the household has been making decisions on the basis of an account that is materially wrong.

**"I have been keeping this from her for years. Is it too late to say anything?"**

No, and the cost of disclosing increases with each year. What is required is not the delivery of nine years of accumulated guilt but a factual account of the position, an honest statement of why the silence was maintained, and a plan. Expect the first conversations to be difficult and the process of repair to take months rather than hours.

**"Should I take a loan to cover the gap while I sort things out?"**

Only under three conditions: that the loan reduces the total cost of the problem; that there is a defined source of repayment; and that it does not postpone an adjustment that will be larger later. Borrowing to maintain a standard that income cannot sustain is the mechanism by which shortfalls become catastrophes, and it is described in Section 9.3.

**"How do I ask a relative for money without this becoming a permanent obligation to them?"**

Specify the amount, the purpose, the repayment schedule, and the fallback, and put the arrangement in writing with a witness where the sum is significant. Offer something reciprocal where you can. Be explicit that the assistance is a defined arrangement with an end, and honour the schedule exactly, because the timetable is the part that preserves the relationship.

**"What if my difficulty is that I am supporting eleven people on one salary?"**

Then the difficulty is primarily structural rather than arithmetic, and no amount of borrowing will resolve it. Chapter 10 addresses the redistribution of responsibility and the introduction of boundaries, and it is written precisely for this situation.

**"Where can I get advice at low cost?"**

Cooperative societies and credit unions affiliated to employers and professional bodies; free financial counselling offered by consumer protection agencies; pro bono schemes operated by professional accountancy and legal bodies; non-governmental organisations working on financial inclusion; and the advisory services attached to some religious and community organisations. Appendix Q lists the categories to approach and the questions to ask.

::: summary
**Chapter 9 in brief.** Concealed financial difficulty takes five recognisable forms: the edited account, in which everything said is true and the picture is materially wrong; the separate obligation the household does not know about; the deferred disclosure, renewed at each new occasion; the maintained standard funded by borrowing and arrears; and the private calculation carried continuously in the man's head.

Concealment survives even in trusting households for four specific reasons: money remains the domain in which men expect to lose standing; disclosure has immediate consequences for others; the man frequently does not know his own position precisely and therefore cannot give a coherent account; and disclosure feels like the destruction of the self he has constructed for his family. The costs fall chiefly on the household being protected: the problem grows while hidden, trust is damaged independently of the money, the man is isolated at the moment he needs support, and the family loses the options that would have existed six months earlier.

Shame is the central emotion, and it must be distinguished from dignity. Guilt concerns an act and responds to disclosure and repair; shame concerns the self and produces concealment. The test is whether a course of action requires misrepresentation — in which case it is a shame strategy and will cost more than it saves — or whether it permits an accurate statement of the position, which preserves dignity.

Living beyond one's means is driven by four pressures — the cost of belonging, the cost of the position, the cost of the promise, and the cost of concealment itself — and sustained by three mechanisms: borrowing to fund consumption, servicing creditors in order of visibility rather than cost, and selling productive assets. Each mechanism has an intervention available to a man without disclosing anything to anybody, which makes them usable as first steps.

Debt produces a symptom profile that closely resembles anxiety and depression, including broken sleep, intrusive arithmetic, irritability at home, physical tension, avoidance of calls and places, and withdrawal. It is a distinctive kind of stress because it is continuous, has a party, is quantifiable, and carries moral content. Under strain, financial decision-making deteriorates, alcohol use rises, and social withdrawal removes the very advice and assistance that would resolve the difficulty. The alternative is to treat debt as a project: obtain the figure, engage the largest creditor first, and take advice from someone with no stake in the outcome.

Asking for assistance can be done without losing dignity, by deciding whether the gap is bridgeable or structural, choosing a person with capacity, discretion, and no leverage, making a request containing the position, the amount, the plan, what is already being done, and the fallback, and offering something reciprocal including a written schedule. Refusals should be accepted without argument and without withdrawal from the relationship.

Rebuilding proceeds in four stages: the position paper, the reduction in three categories, the creditor plan prioritised by cost, and the rebuilding of capacity — restoring the productive base, reducing the cost base permanently, building a reserve of one to three months, restoring credit standing deliberately, and changing the reference group. The rebuilding should be done with the household informed and given specific tasks, because recovery conducted as a solitary administrative project reproduces the pattern that caused the difficulty.
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. Of the five forms of concealment in Section 9.1, which applies to you? If none applies, is there a man close to you for whom one of them is clearly operative, and what is the direct question you could ask him?
2. Section 9.2 asks you to distinguish dignity from shame. Describe a recent decision about money and answer whether it preserved dignity or protected the appearance. What did it cost?
3. Work through the four pressures in Section 9.3 and identify your largest annual expenditure that exists for the purpose of being seen. What would happen, concretely, if it stopped?
4. Run the three interventions on debt mechanisms: separate productive from consumptive borrowing; list creditors by cost rather than visibility; identify the productive assets you would protect. What does the exercise reveal?
5. From the symptom profile in Table 9.2, which items do you currently experience? Have you attributed any of them to a cause other than financial strain?
6. Apply Section 9.3 to your own position: is your gap bridgeable or structural? What does the answer imply about whether to borrow or to restructure?
7. Complete the Position Paper today, including the number of months the position can be sustained. What did you feel on writing the number down, and what is the first item on the reduction list?

## Further Reading

- **On the psychology of scarcity and financial decision-making:** the research literature showing that financial strain impairs cognitive capacity and decision-making, and the work on how poverty and debt affect attention and judgement. This literature is important for men in debt, because it establishes that the deterioration in a man's decisions under strain is a documented effect rather than a personal failing.
- **On debt and mental health:** the substantial body of work linking financial difficulty to depression, anxiety, and suicide risk, including studies showing that debt counselling improves mental health outcomes as well as financial ones.
- **On over-indebtedness in Africa:** studies of consumer credit, informal lending, cooperative societies, and digital lending in Nigeria and comparable markets, together with the guidance published by consumer protection bodies and central banks on borrowing and on the handling of debt distress.
- **On financial counselling:** practical materials on budgeting, debt prioritisation, negotiation with creditors, and restructuring, together with the guidance issued by consumer credit and financial counselling organisations.
- **On family finance and disclosure:** work on money in marriage and on the effects of financial secrecy between partners, including research showing that financial deception is associated with lower marital satisfaction and a higher risk of separation.
- **On shame and money:** the literature on the moralisation of debt and poverty, including historical and cross-cultural studies of how attitudes to money are transmitted, and the research on shame as a barrier to seeking financial advice.
