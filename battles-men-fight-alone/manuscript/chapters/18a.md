# The Husband Everyone Expects to Be Strong {.chapter num="18" short="The Husband Everyone Expects to Be Strong"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the expectations placed specifically on the husband's role, and identify the sources from which those expectations are received.
2. Explain the psychological pattern of the need to be needed, and assess its benefits and its costs in his own marriage.
3. Identify the experience of feeling unseen within a marriage that is functioning well by every external measure, and describe its typical features.
4. Analyse the cost of continuous responsibility within a marriage, including its effects on decisions, on desire, and on the husband's own health.
5. Apply a structured method for recovering an identity that is not reducible to his role, including interests, relationships, and a personal standard.
6. Construct the arrangements by which emotion becomes permissible inside the marriage, using the plan at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

A man marries, and within a short period he becomes a role. He is the person from whom permission is sought, the person to whom problems are brought, the person whose mood determines the atmosphere of the house, the person who is expected to have an answer, and the person whose own concerns are not brought to anybody. The transition is not the result of anybody's decision, and neither partner can be blamed for it. It is the consequence of a set of expectations that arrive with the marriage, from both sides, and that are implemented by ordinary daily conduct.

This chapter examines what the husband's role does to the man who occupies it, and it deliberately addresses a subject that Chapter 14 and Chapter 15 have already touched from different angles. Chapter 14 concerned the man whose wife does not understand his difficulties. Chapter 15 concerned the silent arrangements that develop inside a marriage. This chapter concerns the man's own experience of the role itself: the need to be needed, the experience of being unseen by a woman who loves him and knows his habits, the cost of being the household's permanent strong party, and the specific project of recovering a self that is not reducible to function.

The subject attracts two errors, and both should be set aside at the outset.

The first error is to treat the husband's complaint as a demand for gratitude. A man who says that he does not feel seen is not asking his wife to acknowledge his efforts. He is describing the absence of a relationship in which he is a person rather than a supplier, and the difference is the subject of the whole of this chapter.

The second error is to treat the wife as the author of the arrangement. In the great majority of the marriages examined in this book, the wife is implementing a script she was given as thoroughly as the husband was, and she is frequently carrying her own version of the same problem: the experience of being needed for her labour and loved for her usefulness, without being known. The most reliable route out of the arrangement is consequently joint, and it begins, as Chapter 15 established, with one of the two people naming it.

## 18.1 The Expectations Placed on a Husband

The expectations attached to the husband's role are distinct from those attached to the man's role generally, and they are worth separating because they arrive from four different sources and are frequently in conflict.

### The Four Sources

**The family of origin.** A husband's parents expect the marriage to increase the family's standing, to produce children, to maintain contact, to bring his wife into the family's arrangements, and to leave unchanged his obligations to his parents and siblings. The last of these is the most consequential, and the conflict it produces with the wife is one of the most commonly reported marital difficulties in cultures where extended-family obligations are strong.

**The wife's family.** Comparable expectations are held in reverse. The wife's family expects a husband to be financially capable, to treat their daughter well, to maintain the household's standard, and to be available to them in the manner customary in their own arrangements.

**The community.** A husband is expected to maintain a household, to settle disputes, to contribute to community obligations, to be a representative, and to be the person held accountable for the household's conduct.

**The wife's own expectations.** These are the least often stated and the most reasonably held: that her husband will be a person she can talk to, that he will know her, that he will share the work and the worry of the household, and that the marriage will be a partnership rather than an arrangement she administers.

### The Conflicts

Three specific conflicts are produced by these four sources, and each is a recognisable site of difficulty.

**Standing against partnership.** The obligations to two extended families and to the community are public, enforced by comment, and honoured in a man's standing. The obligation to know and support his wife is private, enforced by nothing, and honoured by nobody outside the household. A man who satisfies the public obligations and neglects the private one is behaving rationally in the terms of his environment, and he produces a marriage in which his wife concludes that she is not important to him.

**Loyalty against loyalty.** Where the man's family and his wife are in settled conflict, the man's loyalty is demanded by both and cannot be given to both in the form in which it is demanded.

**Provision against presence.** The hours required for the first reduce the capacity for the second, and the trade-off is resolved in practice by whichever party complains most audibly, which is generally not the wife.

### The Failure Mode Built into the Role

The role's most important feature is rarely discussed: it is defined entirely by its function and contains no clause about the man's own state.

A husband's obligations can be listed: provide, protect, decide, remain calm, be available, be dependable, maintain the household's standing, and manage the external relationships. The list contains no item concerning his own needs, and this absence is not an oversight; it is the role's design. A role defined by function produces, by construction, a person who is evaluated by function and who evaluates himself by function. And a person who evaluates himself by function will, at the first serious failure of function, find himself without other grounds.

Two practical consequences follow and are addressed later in the chapter. The first is that the husband's self-assessment becomes indistinguishable from his performance, which is the mechanism of Chapters 11 and 24. The second is that any expression of need on his part appears, to himself and to his household, as a defect of the role rather than as an ordinary requirement of a person.

::: keypoint
The husband's role is defined by what it supplies and contains no clause about what it requires. A man who occupies the role exactly as it is defined will be reliable, self-sufficient, and unknown — and he will eventually need something the role has no vocabulary for.
:::

## 18.2 The Need to Be Needed

There is a psychological pattern in men that is not a disorder, that is frequently praised, and that produces many of the difficulties described in this book: the need to be needed.

### What the Pattern Consists Of

A man in this pattern derives his standing from being the one without whom things would not work. Its features are recognisable.

**He arranges things so that he is indispensable.** He handles the household's financial management, the negotiations, the difficult relatives, the driving, the repairs, the decisions. He does this competently, and he is generally not asked; he assumes the functions because they need to be done and because doing them establishes his position.

**He resists being unnecessary.** A request for help with one of his functions produces discomfort: the colleague who assists with the accounts, the wife who takes on the school arrangements, the child who answers the telephone call. In each case, the man's response is frequently to reclaim the function rather than to accept the assistance, and his reason, if examined, is that the assistance reduces his position.

**He is uncomfortable with being the one who needs.** Where he himself requires something, he prefers to obtain it without asking, and where asking is unavoidable, he minimises the request.

**He experiences rest as a threat.** The arrangement described in Chapter 12. A man who is indispensable cannot rest, both because the work would not be done and because resting would place him in the position of the household's dependent party.

### Why It Is Sustained

The pattern is sustained because it works, and it is worth stating its genuine benefits before its costs.

It produces a household that functions. It produces a man who is trusted with real responsibility and whose competence is not in doubt. It produces a marriage that survives economic difficulty, because the man will find a way. And it produces a set of relationships in which the man is genuinely valued, because he is genuinely necessary.

The pattern's costs are hidden in four places, and they are the reason this book treats it as a subject.

**The first cost is that being needed is not the same as being loved.** The distinction is the central one in this chapter, and it is worth stating exactly. Being needed means that the household's outcomes depend on the man; being loved means that the household's regard for him does not depend on the outcomes. A man can be indispensable and unloved, and the more he arranges for his indispensability, the less information he has about which of the two he is receiving. This is why the discovery of a wife's affair, or a child's indifference, is frequently so disorienting to a man of this pattern: he had believed his position was secure because it was necessary.

**The second cost is the exhaustion of the role.** To be needed continuously is to be in continuous use, and the arrangement described in Chapter 12 follows.

**The third cost is the suppression of the man's own requirements.** A man who is valuable because he needs nothing must not need. The suppression is maintained for decades and its effects are described in Chapters 3 and 6.

**The fourth cost is the development of a household that cannot function without him.** The pattern's most damaging feature, because it is self-confirming: a man who performs every function for twenty years produces a wife and children who have not practised them, and the arrangement is therefore both his doing and, by the time he wishes to change it, a genuine structural requirement. This is what makes the reduction of the pattern difficult in mid-life, and why the process described in Section 18.5 is conducted steadily over months rather than announced.
