## 15.6 When to Seek Help

Men frequently defer marital help for years, and the deferral has a specific cause: the request for help is read, by the man and frequently by his wife, as the beginning of the end. The evidence is more encouraging than the belief. Couples who obtain help early generally do better than couples who obtain it after a decade, and the reason is not mysterious: the accumulation described in Section 15.2 is smaller, and the practice of talking about difficulty has not yet been abandoned.

### Indicators That Help Is Required

**A single injury of the kind described in item four of Section 15.3** — an infidelity, a financial concealment of significance, a public humiliation, a major unilateral decision. Such injuries do not resolve with time, and the attempt to resolve them without help usually produces the arrangement in which the injury is permanent and unmentionable.

**A pattern of the same argument recurring over more than two years.** The recurrence is the defining feature: it means that the argument is not about its subject.

**Silence of the kind described in Chapter 14, extending beyond a few months.** Particularly where the silence is now symmetrical — where neither party raises anything — because symmetrical silence is stable and does not correct itself.

**The presence of a condition in either party.** Depression, alcohol dependence, anxiety, and trauma conditions affect marriages substantially and directly, and treating the condition frequently improves the marriage more than relational work alone.

**Escalation.** Increasing frequency, intensifying language, the introduction of children, or the use of relatives as allies.

**Any consideration of a third party or of leaving on the part of either spouse.** The mention of this subject is frequently treated as a threat and as an ending, and it is more usefully treated as information: it indicates the level of the present difficulty and the urgency of the work.

**The involvement of children in the conflict.** Where children are being used as messengers, as allies, or as witnesses, the situation requires intervention for their sake independently of the marriage's outcome.

### The Options

**Couple counselling.** The most direct route, and the most effective for communication patterns, mutual understanding, and the negotiation of specific agreements. Its requirements are that both parties attend and that the practitioner is competent and appropriately matched. Its limitations are that it cannot correct a serious individual condition, resolve a fundamental incompatibility, or repair a situation in which one party has already decided to leave.

**Individual counselling for the man.** Frequently the correct first step, particularly where the man's own state — exhaustion, depression, anger — prevents him from participating in couple work. Its value is that it addresses the man's contribution and his decisions, and it is the route by which a large number of men gain enough clarity either to conduct the conversation properly or to accept the conclusion that the relationship requires a different arrangement.

**Individual treatment for the wife's condition.** Where the wife is suffering from a treatable condition and is not receiving treatment, this is frequently the single most effective intervention available to the marriage. Raising the subject is difficult and should be done with care, without diagnosis, and with practical support: *I have noticed that you are not sleeping and I am concerned; can I arrange the appointment?*

**Structured programmes.** Marriage enrichment courses, communication programmes, and group work with other couples. Their advantage is that they present the material as a general practice rather than as a response to failure, which removes the stigma that prevents many couples from obtaining help. Many religious and community organisations offer such programmes, and readers whose framework includes them should note that their effectiveness does not depend on their setting.

**Family and community mediation.** In settings where extended-family involvement in marital matters is customary, mediation by respected relatives can be effective, particularly for disputes concerning obligations, bride-price matters, and the relationship between the couple and the wider family. Two cautions apply: the mediators should include at least one person acceptable to both parties, and the matters discussed should not include the intimate material whose disclosure in a wide forum is frequently the reason men decline this route.

### What Help Requires of the Man

Three requirements are commonly named by men who have obtained help successfully.

**Willingness to be examined.** The change that marital work requires is frequently on the man's side, and the requirement is not merely to attend. Men who present themselves as participants while treating the sessions as an evaluation of their wives produce no change, and they frequently report that counselling did not work.

**Attendance and consistency.** Weekly for a defined period, with the specific expectation of about six months for a change of the kind described in this chapter.

**Willingness to make one specific change and observe its effect.** The evidence from behaviour change applies in relationships as elsewhere: one specific, repeated change produces more than a general intention to be a better husband.

## 15.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Kept the Account

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in couple counselling, family practice, and research on marital conflict.

**Adebayo is forty-six, a chartered accountant in Ibadan with a small practice, married to Yemisi for seventeen years, with three children aged fifteen, twelve, and eight.**

The case was presented by Adebayo in individual counselling for what he described as anger: for about two years he had been experiencing episodes of intense anger at home in response to small matters. His examples: a question about whether he had eaten; a request to drop a child at a lesson when he was ready to leave; and, on one occasion, his wife's remark that the family had not taken a holiday since the previous year.

The history that emerged was, in outline, a series of entirely ordinary obligations that had accumulated without any of them being disclosed in full. Over seven years, Adebayo had: paid the secondary school fees of his younger brother's two children, after his brother's business failed, which came to approximately a third of his annual income each year for four years; funded the renovation of his mother's house, which he had undertaken under family pressure and which had required a loan he had repaid over three years; provided monthly support to his widowed aunt, at a level established when his income was at its peak and never revised; and absorbed a substantial reduction in his practice's income during a downturn, which he concealed from his wife by reducing his own personal expenditure and increasing his professional borrowing.

None of this had been disclosed. Adebayo's account of why not was consistent and, in his terms, rational: the fees were his brother's children's and could not be refused; his mother's house was a matter of the family's standing, and his wife would have objected on grounds of cost and would have been right to do so; his aunt was a widow; and the practice's difficulties were temporary and he did not want to alarm the household.

Yemisi's experience, which emerged later in a joint session, was of a husband who was increasingly irritable, who had become secretive about his practice, who had refused three family occasions on the grounds of work, and who had responded to a suggestion about a holiday with a remark that she described as contemptuous. Her conclusions, formed over two years and on the basis of the information available to her, were: that her husband no longer wanted to be in the house; that he was possibly involved with somebody else; and that in any case he did not regard her or the children as a priority. She had, in the previous eleven months, reduced her own disclosures to him accordingly.

The intervention had two parts. The first was the disclosure by Adebayo, over two sessions, of the full account: the fees, the renovation, the aunt's support, the practice's finances, and the reason each had been kept separate. The disclosure took him about ninety minutes in total, spread over the two sessions, and it was, in his description, the most difficult thing he had done in twenty years. The second was the removal of the ledger: a written statement, prepared jointly, of the household's obligations — which obligations the household would continue to carry, which would be renegotiated, and which would be concluded and at what date — and a corresponding statement of what would be expected of extended family members, including Adebayo's brother, in future.

Two features of the outcome are worth recording. The first is that Yemisi's response to the disclosure of the school fees was not opposition but anger at the concealment, and her position, once stated, was more generous than Adebayo had assumed: she was willing to continue the fees for one further year to allow the children to complete the level they were in, and then to have the arrangement renegotiated with the brother directly and jointly. The assumption that his wife would have refused, which had governed seven years of concealment, was incorrect, and Adebayo identified this in the session as the most expensive error of the period.

The second is the change in the anger. The episodes diminished substantially within four months, and their disappearance is explained by the mechanism in Section 15.5. The anger had been generated by the ledger — the accumulated, undisclosed account of what Adebayo had given and what he had received — and it had been discharged at the person who held the balance, which was his wife. Once the account was known, its balance could be discussed, and its function as a source of rage ended.

Adebayo's summary, eighteen months later, was: *I thought I was protecting the house. What I was actually doing was keeping a private account and sending her the bill.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**Every obligation was legitimate.** No element of the arrangement was improper. The fees were for children; the renovation was a family obligation; the widow was a widow; the practice's difficulty was temporary. The damage arose not from the substance of the obligations but from their cumulative, undisclosed character.

**The concealment was justified by an assumption about his wife.** Adebayo predicted his wife's refusal and acted on the prediction for seven years without testing it. The prediction was wrong, and it is frequently wrong: men consistently overestimate the rigidity of their households' positions on financial obligations and underestimate their objection to being excluded from the decision.

**The anger was generated by the ledger.** The mechanism in Section 15.5 explains the disproportionate responses: a minor domestic question activated a balance of seven years' standing. Men in this position frequently seek help for anger and are surprised to find that the treatment is a disclosure.

**The wife's conclusions were rational on the available information.** Irritability, secrecy, the refusal of occasions, and contempt in a single remark are, from outside, entirely consistent with the conclusion she reached. Her withdrawal was not a failure of understanding; it was an accurate response to an incomplete picture.

**The remedy was a written joint arrangement.** Not an emotional conversation, but a documented statement of obligations with dates attached, which both parties could hold and review. This is the form in which durable marital agreements are made.
