## 2.8 Practical Exercise: The Script Audit

This exercise takes about two hours and is completed in writing. Its purpose is to convert a lifelong arrangement into an object that can be examined, and men who complete it consistently report that the first two parts are the ones that produce the change.

### Part One: What I Was Taught

Answer in writing, with specifics and names where they exist.

1. What were you told, as a boy, about how a man behaves? Write the actual sentences, in the words used.
2. Which man or men demonstrated it, and what did you observe them do?
3. What happened to boys or men who deviated? Describe at least two instances you personally witnessed.
4. What did you learn about crying, fear, and asking for help? At what age did you last do any of the three, and what happened?

### Part Two: What I Carry Now

Using the six requirements in Section 2.1, mark each with one of three positions: **I hold this deliberately**, **I hold this because it is expected**, or **I no longer hold this at all** but behave as though I do.

| Requirement | My position | What it costs me monthly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Provide | | |
| Protect | | |
| Perform | | |
| Endure | | |
| Decide | | |
| Control feeling | | |

The fourth column is the important one. A cost is money, hours, sleep, health, a relationship, an opportunity, or a state of mind. Men frequently find that the largest costs are associated with the requirement they hold least deliberately.

### Part Three: The Load Inventory

List every obligation you are currently carrying, without evaluation: financial obligations, family obligations, community positions, mediations and disputes, care responsibilities, work commitments beyond your formal role, and personal undertakings to individuals. Write the amount, the period, and who knows about it.

Then answer three questions.

- Which of these obligations did I take on, and which arrived?
- Have I ever totalled them in one place before? What did the total produce?
- Which two could be reduced or concluded within six months, and what would be the actual consequences?

### Part Four: The Test of the Load

For each of the four unrecognised capacities in Table 2.1, score yourself from one to five and write one sentence of evidence.

| Capacity | Score (1–5) | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Articulacy: can I describe my own state accurately to another person? | | |
| Help-seeking: do I obtain assistance before a difficulty becomes a crisis? | | |
| Relational maintenance: do I have relationships in which I am known rather than relied upon? | | |
| Self-maintenance: is my sleep, health, and recovery protected over the long term? | | |

### Part Five: One Commitment in Each Gap

For every capacity scored three or below, write one commitment with a date. The commitments should be small and verifiable. Examples of the form:

- *Before the end of this month I will describe my actual state to one person, in one conversation, in no more than ten sentences.*
- *I will make the appointment for the assessment I have been deferring, and the date is ____.*
- *I will contact two men I have not spoken to this year, and propose a fixed arrangement.*
- *I will set a bedtime and hold it for fourteen consecutive nights, and record the result.*

## 2.9 Common Questions Men Ask

**"Is this book telling me to stop being a man?"**

No. It is telling you that the version of manhood in current use omits four capacities that determine whether a man is still functioning at sixty-five. The revised definition in Section 2.6 keeps every traditional requirement and adds the four that make the others sustainable. A man who endures, provides, protects, and performs — and who also describes his state accurately, obtains help early, maintains relationships in which he is known, and protects his own health — is not a lesser man. He is the version of the man that the current script fails to produce.

**"If I tell my wife or my family what I am carrying, will they respect me less?"**

Some will, at first, and most will not. The consistent finding from men who have made the disclosure is that the response is closer to relief than to contempt: the household has been living with the effects — the irritability, the absence, the unexplained change — and an explanation is frequently received as the removal of a fear. Where the response is contemptuous, that is important information about the relationship, and Section 14.6 provides the framework.

**"I was raised this way and it has worked for me. Why change?"**

Because the cost is paid later and by other people. A man who carries everything alone in the current arrangement frequently pays in three observed forms: a health event in mid-life, a marriage that becomes an administrative arrangement, and children who reproduce the same script into their own households. If none of these is present and none is likely, the arrangement is working; the audit in Section 2.8 will tell you.

**"My father was harder than me and lived to be eighty-five."**

Individual outcomes vary, and they are not a guide to risk. The relevant comparison is between populations and patterns, not individuals, and the population evidence on chronic stress, untreated hypertension, sleep deprivation, and isolation is consistent. There is also a selection effect worth noting: the men of your father's generation who did not survive to eighty-five are not available to be cited.

**"Where do I begin, if I only have the capacity to do one thing?"**

With sleep, and then with one conversation. Men who improve sleep alone — a fixed bedtime, seven hours, no alcohol as a means of sleep — report improvement in irritability, decision-making, and physical symptoms within a month. The conversation is the second step, and it should be with a person who has a record of keeping confidences. Appendix N provides the sleep method, and Chapter 35 provides the structure for the conversation.

**"What if I do not have anybody to talk to?"**

Then the first task is to create one, and the available routes are in Chapter 4 and Appendix J. The three with the best record: one relationship reinstated from an earlier period of your life; one group of men with an agreed rule of honesty; and one professional relationship, which requires no prior history and which is confidential by definition.

::: summary
**Chapter 2 in brief.** Every society hands a man a script, and the script is remarkably consistent across countries: provide, protect, perform, endure, decide, and control feeling. The requirements are not caricatures — reliability, courage, endurance, and the acceptance of responsibility are genuine virtues — and what this chapter examines is the cost of the script to the man who keeps it.

The six requirements produce a state of permanent readiness: the inability to rest without justification, the experience of ordinary leisure as a debt, continuous attention to threat, and a refusal to recognise the arrangement as stress because stress would imply it was optional. Permanent readiness is physiological rather than attitudinal, and sustained over years it produces the health outcomes documented in the later chapters of this book.

The fear of being considered weak is distinct from the fear of being weak. It is a fear about standing rather than capacity, it cannot be resolved by becoming stronger, and it produces five recognisable patterns: concealment, overcorrection, the avoidance of assistance, aggression toward whoever raises the matter, and constant demonstration.

The script says nothing about support. Men receive less emotional support than women across every setting, for four reasons: their distress signals are the opposite of the signals a household looks for; they decline the support that is offered; the people around them are frequently depleted; and there is no available cultural script for supporting a man. The consequences are later recognition of serious conditions, the conversion of distress into behaviour, decisions made in isolation, a single point of failure in marriage, and the transmission of the arrangement to the next generation.

Manhood in this framework becomes a lifelong examination with criteria, examiners, no timetable, no certificate, and no appeal. Its features are the postponement of relief, constant comparison, and the extension of the examination into the household, where children are frequently assessed on their father's behalf. In mid-life and later it produces the collapse of the arrangement at retirement, the retrospective audit between fifty and sixty-five, the difficulty of a body that no longer performs, and the eventual discovery that the examiners have died and the examination was always largely internal.

The constructive work is a revision of strength, defined as the capacity to sustain responsibility. That definition requires six capacities, of which the current script recognises two: endurance and competence. The four it omits — articulacy, help-seeking, relational maintenance, and self-maintenance — are the ones that determine whether a man is still functioning at sixty-five. They are built in quarters, demonstrated in front of the people for whom it matters, and held in conversation with the sentence: *I am keeping myself able to carry what I am carrying.*
:::

## Reflection Questions

1. From the six requirements in Section 2.1, which one governs your life most completely? Which do you hold deliberately, and which because it is expected of you?
2. Complete Part One of the exercise. What were the actual sentences you were taught as a boy about how a man behaves, and who taught them?
3. Where is the largest cost in the table in Part Two — and is it attached to a requirement you hold deliberately or one that was imposed?
4. Which of the five patterns in Section 2.3 applies to you: concealment, overcorrection, avoidance of assistance, aggression toward the messenger, or constant demonstration? What has it produced in the last five years?
5. Who are the people who would notice if your health or your mood deteriorated — and how many of them have you told anything real in the last year?
6. From Table 2.1, which of the four unrecognised capacities is your weakest, and what is the first specific act that would strengthen it?
7. Ikenna reduced six obligations to two and nothing collapsed. What are the two obligations in your life that you have never tested, and what would happen if you did?

## Further Reading

- **On masculine norms and their measurement:** the research literature on masculine role norms and the instruments developed to measure conformity to them, including the work on the specific norms of self-reliance, emotional control, and the primacy of work.
- **On conformity to masculine norms and health:** the substantial body of research associating stronger conformity to traditional masculine norms with reduced help-seeking, lower utilisation of health services, higher rates of substance use, and poorer mental health outcomes.
- **On male role strain:** the sociological literature on role strain and role conflict in men, including the work on the multiple, simultaneous, and frequently incompatible expectations attached to the provider role.
- **On social support and gender:** the research on gender differences in the receipt and provision of emotional support, including the findings on network size, the reliance of men on a spouse as principal confidant, and the consequences of the loss of that confidant in later life.
- **On men's health behaviour:** the literature on men's delayed presentation to health services, including research on the reasons men give for delay and on the interventions that have improved early presentation.
- **Practical resources:** structured programmes for men addressing role strain, fatherhood, and life transitions; men's discussion and support groups; and the material throughout the later chapters of this book, particularly Chapters 12, 24, and 27.
