## 25.3 When Society Has No Place for Your Version of Success

This section addresses the specific situation in which a man's competence and effort are substantial and the environment's available categories do not recognise them. It is a distinct problem from personal failure, and it requires a distinct treatment, because the standard advice — work harder, produce results — does not apply to a mismatch between a man's capacities and the categories available.

### The Four Mismatches

**1. The structural mismatch.** The economy does not contain the work for which the man has prepared, or contains less of it than the number of men prepared for it. The graduate in an economy with a graduate surplus; the professional in a sector that has contracted; the tradesman in a market that has been displaced by imports.

**The treatment.** The man requires an accurate account of the market rather than a personal diagnosis, and the two available routes: the identification of a related competence that the market does contain, and the construction of a livelihood from more than one source. Chapter 39 provides the practical method. The specific requirement is that the man's assessment of himself be separated from the market's assessment of his qualifications, which is the work of Section 25.5.

**2. The credential mismatch.** The man's competence exceeds his formal qualifications, and the environment requires the qualification rather than the competence. This is the most common mismatch in the countries where this book will be read, and it produces a characteristic experience: the man who is technically superior to the certified practitioner and who is invisible to every institution.

**The treatment.** The correction of the credential where it is obtainable, and it is frequently more obtainable than men assume: professional bodies, examinations, professional registration routes, and adult education. Where it is not obtainable, the construction of arrangements in which competence is demonstrated rather than certified: direct client relationships, referral networks, and the accumulation of a public record of completed work. Men in this position report that the referral network is the principal route, and its construction is described in Chapter 27.

**3. The recognition mismatch.** The man's work is of high quality and low status: the artisan, the technician, the driver, the security officer, the man whose contribution is essential and whose standing is derived from a category that society treats as inferior. His competence is not in doubt and is not counted.

**The treatment.** Two things. First, the man's own measure, which is the central subject of this chapter, and the specific finding that a man who knows the quality of his own work is substantially protected from the social assessment of its category. Second, the conversion of the work into a record and a relationship: the customers who return because the work is good, the reputation built in a specific market, the mentorship of younger men. Men in trades report that the standing available within their own professional community is frequently more substantive than the standing available in the general society.

**4. The path mismatch.** The man has chosen a path that his community does not recognise as success: the artist, the musician, the researcher in a subject without commercial application, the teacher in a family of businessmen, the man who has taken a position of lower income and greater value, the man who has chosen caring work. His results, judged by the community's scale, are inadequate; judged by his own, they are not.

**The treatment.** The clear statement of the man's own standard, the acceptance that a section of his family will not recognise it, and the deliberate maintenance of at least one reference group in which the work is understood. The material is in Chapters 11 and 27, and the case in Section 25.7 illustrates the pattern.

### What the Mismatches Have in Common

Three features are shared.

**The man's effort is not the variable.** The advice to work harder assumes a market that rewards effort, and in each of the four mismatches the market does not. The consequence of receiving the advice is that the man concludes that his effort is deficient, which is inaccurate and which produces the loss of the one resource he actually has.

**The record is the decisive practical instrument.** In all four mismatches, the man's protection is a record: of work completed, skills demonstrated, clients satisfied, students taught, and results produced in a form the market recognises. The record is constructed by the man, it is at his disposal, and it is the raw material of both the livelihood and the self-assessment.

**A reference group is required.** The man's own standard will not survive an environment that applies a different one continuously. The group may be small: three men in comparable positions, a professional association, a group of practitioners, a monthly meeting. Its function is to supply recognition from people who can judge the work, which is the form of recognition that the man actually requires, and which the general society cannot supply because it cannot assess what it has not understood.

## 25.4 Dignity in a World That Values Achievement

Dignity is the chapter's central concept, and it requires a careful definition, because it is used loosely and because two of its common meanings are obstacles.

### What Dignity Is Not

**It is not recognition.** Recognition is the acknowledgement of a man by others, and it is valuable and external. Dignity does not depend upon it, which is precisely why it is useful to a man whose recognition has been withdrawn.

**It is not resignation.** Resignation accepts an assessment and stops acting. Dignity is compatible with a full effort, and it is the position from which effort becomes sustainable: a man who has dignity can attempt and fail without losing his standing in his own account, which is the condition of persistence described in Chapter 8.

**It is not the denial of the circumstances.** A man's circumstances are what they are, and dignity is not produced by describing an inadequate position as adequate. The distinction is between the assessment of the man and the assessment of his position: the first is what dignity concerns, and the second should be described accurately.

**It is not the maintenance of pride.** Pride defends the position; dignity concerns the man. The distinction is visible in behaviour: a man defending a position will conceal, exaggerate, decline the conversation, and manage the impression, whereas a man holding dignity will state his position accurately, including its inadequacy, without its being converted into a verdict about him.

### The Definition

Dignity is **the position a man holds toward himself when his conduct is consistent with his own standards and his circumstances are described accurately.**

Three elements of the definition matter.

**It is about conduct rather than outcome.** A man's circumstances are substantially determined by factors outside him; his conduct is frequently the portion he controls. A man who has discharged his obligations, kept his commitments, treated people properly, and made his effort in full holds dignity regardless of the results, and he holds it accurately, not as a comfort.

**It requires an accurate description of the circumstances.** Dignity cannot be built on a false account. A man who describes an inescapable position as a temporary difficulty has not preserved his dignity; he has deferred the work, and the deferral is the mechanism described in Chapter 9.

**It is held in a position toward himself.** Not a statement to others and not a feeling. The practical form is a written statement of the man's standard, of his conduct against it, and of his circumstances as they actually are, which is the exercise at the end of this chapter. The statement's function is to make the man's account of himself independent of the audience that judges results.

### The Four Practices of Dignity

**1. Speak the circumstances accurately, once, without elaboration.** A man who is unemployed, who has lost a business, or who is in work of low standing should be able to state his position in one sentence without apology. The practice's function is the removal of the man's dependence on the audience's interpretation: an accurate statement cannot be corrected, and it removes the material from the register of concealment.

**2. Refuse the verdict while accepting the assessment.** The distinction is precise. *The business failed, and the market has no work for my qualifications* is an assessment, and it is accepted. *I am a failure* is a verdict, and it is refused. Men should note that the refusal is not a matter of declarations but of description: the man states what occurred, states what it means practically, and does not accept the generalisation.

**3. Keep conduct within the standard.** This is the element that produces dignity, and it is the reason that the practice works. A man who maintains his conduct — obligations discharged, commitments kept, work performed properly, household treated well — has evidence, and the evidence is not susceptible to the audience's opinion. In the accounts of men who have maintained dignity through serious reversals, this is the practice they most consistently identify.

**4. Hold one relationship in which the man is not assessed.** A wife, a friend, a sibling, or a professional relationship in which the man's position is known and in which his worth is not conditional on results. Chapter 4's material applies; the specific function here is that the relationship supplies the correction to the audience's verdict.
