## 31.5 Leading Without Losing Yourself

The arrangements that allow a man to hold authority without the position consuming his judgement, his relationships, and his household are six, and they are specifically adapted to the three structural effects of Section 31.1.

### Arrangement One: The Peer Arrangement

**What it is.** A standing arrangement with one or more people who hold comparable authority and are not in the man's reporting line and not in competition with him for the same things: a group of chief executives in a sector, a principals' group, a group of heads of department from different institutions, a professional association's senior section, a business owners' group, a small standing group of three to six men in similar positions.

**What it supplies.** The only available form of disclosure that is not compromised by the position. The members are neither the man's subordinates nor his competitors for the specific matters he requires discussion of, and the arrangement's function is exactly the one described in Chapter 27.5 Stage Six: more than one man, membership, and the correction of the man's account by people whose positions differ from his.

**The rules that make it work.** A fixed time; a small number; a rule of confidentiality; a rule that nobody reports anybody's material outside the group; and a rule against advice-giving for the first period of any man's material. The rules are not a formality. Groups of this kind fail when they become occasions for presentations or for the exchange of positions, and they succeed when they are occasions at which the man can say the sentence he has a reason not to say anywhere else.

**The obstacle.** Men in authority frequently decline the arrangement on the grounds that they cannot disclose: *anything I say will be known.* The concern is legitimate and it is answered by the rules above and by the requirement of a group whose members are outside the man's own market. Where a group cannot be formed, the arrangement is supplied by a professional relationship, which is Arrangement Two.

### Arrangement Two: The Arrangement Outside the Line

**What it is.** A supervision, mentoring, counselling, coaching, or medical arrangement with a defined purpose, a defined fee or service, and a confidentiality obligation. What distinguishes it from the peer arrangement is the professional obligation and the absence of any relationship outside the arrangement.

**The three functions.** To supply a recipient for the man's own material; to supply the calibration the position removes, since the practitioner will tell the man what nobody in his organisation will; and to supply a setting in which the man is not the leader, which is its own distinct function and which is addressed in Arrangement Five.

**The frequency.** Monthly or fortnightly for a period of no less than six months. A single arrangement of three sessions is not a test of the arrangement, and the men who report the greatest benefit describe a first period of two to three months in which the sessions appeared to be of limited use.

**The obstacle.** The man's belief that the arrangement implies incapacity. Where the arrangement is a regular feature of the man's professional community, the difficulty disappears, and where it is not, the framing that men report as workable is functional: the arrangement is a component of the man's responsibilities, since his judgement is a resource that the people he leads depend upon.

### Arrangement Three: The Questions That Retrieve the Information

**What it is.** A deliberate practice for addressing the information asymmetry of Section 31.1, and its form is a set of questions the man asks as a routine rather than as an exception.

The material that the organisation will not report upward is specific, and the questions that retrieve it are correspondingly specific:

- What is the matter that nobody has raised with me this month?
- What has changed in the mood of this group, and who noticed it first?
- Which of the people in my area is carrying something that is affecting their work?
- What is the thing that would be obvious to me if I were not the leader here?
- What have I done in the last month that was unhelpful, and who saw it?

The practice requires two conditions to work. It must be asked of somebody who can answer honestly — generally a small number of people the man has identified as willing to report accurately — and the man must not defend himself against the answers. Men who operate the practice describe the first period as uncomfortable and describe the information obtained as the most useful available to them.

### Arrangement Four: The Boundary as a Written Arrangement

**What it is.** The man's own specification, in writing, of what the position may consume: the hours, the days, the travel, the availability at weekends, the household arrangements that will not be moved, and the conditions under which the specification will be revised.

**The specific requirement.** The specification must be written, must have dates, and must be given to somebody who will notice. The material on men's overwork in Chapter 18 applies; the addition here is that a man in authority has no natural constraint and must therefore supply one deliberately. Men who have constructed the specification report that its function is not the reduction of the work but the availability of a position from which to make the trade-offs: with a written boundary, a request for the man's household time is a decision with a stated cost rather than an automatic concession.

### Arrangement Five: The Place Where He Is Not the Leader

**What it is.** One setting in which the man has no authority, is not consulted, is not responsible, and is not performing: a men's group, a sports club, a class, a choir, a workshop, a volunteer role in which he is a participant, a friend's household in which he is a guest, a place of worship where he is one of the attendees.

**The function.** The setting supplies the man's own experience of not being in charge, which is the condition of his rest. Men in authority describe the specific quality of this setting accurately: it is where the man's shoulders come down. Its other function is calibration: the man who has been addressed as the person who knows for twenty years has no information about what he is like in the ordinary position, and the setting supplies it.

**The obstacle.** The man's own conduct. Men in authority frequently convert every new setting into a leadership position within a period of months, because leading is what they are competent at and because the setting's members frequently ask them to. The arrangement requires the man's deliberate refusal of office in that setting, which he should extend to the small things: no chairing, no organising, no being the person who handles the money.

### Arrangement Six: The Household Arrangement

**What it is.** The man's spouse and children know his actual position at the level of the fact: that he is under load, that he is carrying something specific, that his short temper is not about them, that he has an arrangement in which it is being addressed, and what the household requires of him.

**The case for it.** The household is generally the party that absorbs the cost of an unsupported leader, as Section 31.3 establishes, and the household's arrangements would be substantially altered by accurate information. The specific material on this arrangement is in Chapters 17 and 18.

**The form.** The disclosure of the fact rather than the content: what the load is, what the man is doing about it, and what he requires. The men who conduct it report the household's response as relief rather than as additional burden, and the finding is consistent enough to be stated as a general proposition.

## 31.6 Leading a Household as Though It Were an Organisation

The chapter's final substantive section concerns the specific error most frequently reported by the households of men in authority, and it requires a section of its own because its effects are produced inside the man's own home.

### The Transfer

A man who leads an organisation for eleven hours a day returns to a household and applies the same arrangements. The transfer typically takes four forms.

**The reporting line.** The household is arranged as a structure with the man at the top: decisions are brought to him, information is selected on the way up, and the household's members are positioned in relation to his approval, his assessment, and his availability. The arrangement is frequently not the man's own construction; it is the household's response to his position, and it becomes the household's shape over years.

**The information asymmetry.** The household's members select what they report, in exactly the way the organisation does, and for the same reason. The man therefore does not know the household's actual position, while believing that he does, which is the arrangement described in Chapter 18 and which produces the man's own late discovery of matters his household has been managing for years.

**The emotional discharge.** The household becomes the place in which the material accumulated in the day is released, because it is the only place where the man is not required to hold it. The release frequently takes the form of shortness with the people who are most available and least likely to impose a consequence: a spouse, a child, a domestic employee.

**The standard.** The man's example burden, carried all day, is continued at home: the household's conduct is required to meet the standard of the man's organisation, and small domestic arrangements are managed with the oversight appropriate to a workplace.

### The Consequences

**The household's arrangements are managed rather than lived.** The spouse manages the man's mood, the children manage their disclosures, and the household's occasions are conducted with the man's requirements as the organising principle. This is the household described in Chapter 18, and its feature is that it functions well and none of the people in it are known to the man.

**The man's authority becomes unconditional.** In an organisation, the man's authority is bounded by the law, by his own supervisor, by procedures, and by the fact that people can leave. In a household, and particularly where the man is the provider, the authority is frequently unbounded, and the report from family practice is that this is where the pattern's most serious consequences occur. The arrangements of an organisation are designed to contain authority; a household has no such design. Any man who applies authority in his household without a container should regard his own conduct as the principal risk in the arrangement, and should obtain the arrangements of Section 31.5 in the household's terms.

**The man is not known in the one place where he is most himself.** The final and most reported consequence. The man arrives home and is not a participant: he is the person whose arrival changes the room. Men describe the discovery of this position precisely and frequently late: *I realised that my children talk differently when I am not there, and that I have never heard the conversation they have when I am not there.*

### The Correction

Three measures, and their order matters.

**Separate the positions in the man's own account.** In writing: what the man's authority at work consists of, and what it entitles him to at home. The two lists are different, and the writing frequently establishes the difference for the first time.

**Renegotiate the household's arrangements in the household's terms.** The material of Chapters 18 and 20 applies, and the specific measures are those chapters': the household's meetings at which information is exchanged rather than reported; the removal of the man from the approval position in matters that the household can decide; the deliberate practice of being told about the household's actual position; the spouse's own arrangements, resources, and standing; and the children's disclosures received without assessment.

**Conduct the fifth and sixth arrangements, and supply the man's own position to the household.** The household's difficulty is not the man's authority in itself; it is the man's authority without the man. Where the household knows what the man is carrying, knows that he has an arrangement in which it is addressed, and knows what he requires of it — the forms described in Arrangements Five and Six — the household's response is substantially altered, and its members' conduct toward the man becomes the conduct of people with information.
