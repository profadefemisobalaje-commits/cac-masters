## 43.5 Teaching Boys Healthy Coping Skills

The previous section addressed the propositions that the boy must unlearn. This section addresses the competences he must acquire, and it proceeds in the form of a curriculum, because the case material's finding is that boys who are told to *cope better* without being taught a specific competence do not cope better. They conceal more effectively.

The curriculum has six competences, and they are the boy's form of the four competences of Chapter 39.4, extended for a person who is still developing.

### The Six Competences

**Competence One: The naming of the state.** The boy can identify what he is experiencing with a word that is more precise than *fine* or *angry*. The competence includes the capacity to distinguish between states that are frequently confused: disappointment and shame; fear and anger; loneliness and boredom; exhaustion and sadness; humiliation and guilt.

**Competence Two: The location of the state in the body and in the day.** The boy can state where the state is felt and what preceded it. The competence is not a technique of relaxation. It is a technique of information: the boy who can state that his chest tightened before an examination has learned something about the examination, and something about the warning his body supplies.

**Competence Three: The regulation of the state.** The boy can conduct a specific act that reduces the state's intensity sufficiently for him to think. The acts are ordinary and should be taught as ordinary: the deliberate slowing of the breath, the removal from the setting for a stated period, the physical activity, the writing of the state on paper, the conversation with a named person. The competence is not the elimination of the state. It is the boy's capacity to act while experiencing it.

**Competence Four: The statement of the state to a party who has a right to know.** The boy can say what is happening to a party who requires the information: a parent, a teacher, a friend, a colleague, a doctor. The statement has a form, and the form is taught in Section 43.6.

**Competence Five: The repair.** The boy can acknowledge what he did, state its effect, and conduct the repair. The competence is the one that the case material identifies as most predictive of adult functioning, and it is the one that households most frequently omit, because the adults conduct their own repairs privately and the boy never observes the sequence.

**Competence Six: The asking.** The boy can request a specific instrument from a specific party: *I need you to come with me to the clinic; I need you to tell my teacher; I need you to take this off my hands until Monday.* The competence is more specific than *help*, and the specificity is what makes it learnable and what makes it effective.

The reader should note the order. The competences are cumulative: the boy who cannot name a state cannot state it, and the boy who cannot state it cannot ask for an instrument. The curriculum therefore begins with the vocabulary and proceeds through the body, the regulation, the statement, the repair, and the asking, and it does not begin with the asking, which is where adults frequently begin and where the curriculum generally fails.

### The Age Structure

The curriculum's content is the same across the boy's development. Its form changes, and the changes are stated below as a working guide.

**The years from roughly five to eight.** The boy's curriculum at this age is conducted almost entirely through the adult's vocabulary and the adult's response. The practices are: naming the boy's states for him as they occur; stating the adult's own states in simple and accurate language; receiving the boy's reports without ridicule, alarm, or immediate correction; and conducting the household's repairs in the boy's presence. The boy at this age is not taught techniques. He is taught that states exist, that they can be named, and that naming them is safe.

**The years from roughly nine to twelve.** The boy's capacity to reflect increases, and the curriculum adds the body, the regulation, and the repair. The practices are: the boy's own naming, with the adult supplying precision where the boy's word is too general; the identification of the state's location and its antecedents; two or three regulation acts, taught and rehearsed when the boy is calm rather than when he is in difficulty; and the repair, which is rehearsed in ordinary household incidents rather than reserved for serious ones.

**The years from roughly thirteen to fifteen.** The boy's peer group becomes the principal audience, and the curriculum must therefore address the group as well as the boy. The practices are: the boy's statement of his state to a peer, rehearsed with an adult beforehand; the identification of the peer group's enforcement of the propositions, conducted as an analysis rather than as a prohibition; the boy's repair of an incident with a peer, conducted with the adult's support; and the boy's first asking of a specific instrument from a party outside the household.

**The years from roughly sixteen to eighteen.** The young man's curriculum is conducted increasingly by his own practice and decreasingly by the adult's instruction. The practices are: his own management of a significant difficulty, with the adult available but not directing; his repair of a difficulty that he caused; his asking for an instrument that the adult cannot supply, which requires him to identify a professional route and to use it; and his own conduct of the curriculum with a younger boy, which is the most effective consolidation available.

The reader should note the caution that applies to the whole structure. The ages are approximate, the variation between boys is substantial, and a boy who has experienced a trauma, a bereavement, a disability, or a significant disruption will frequently be conducting the curriculum of an earlier stage. The material of Chapter 36.3's staged recovery applies, and the adult's task is to conduct the curriculum at the stage the boy is in rather than the stage his age suggests.

### The Four-Stage Method

The curriculum is taught by a method, and the method is the same one by which a boy learns any competence that his household values: the method of apprenticeship.

**Stage One: Demonstration.** The adult conducts the competence in the boy's presence and names what he is doing. The sentences are short and concrete. *I am angry about the meeting, and I am going to walk to the gate and back before I speak to your mother.* *I was wrong to shout at you this morning, and I am going to say so to you now.* *I do not know how to do this, and I am going to ask Uncle for help.* The demonstration is not a lesson. It is the adult's ordinary conduct, made legible.

**Stage Two: Rehearsal.** The boy conducts the competence in a setting of low stakes. The rehearsal is explicit and short: *your sister has taken your book, and you are angry; say the sentence you would say to her.* The rehearsal is conducted when the boy is calm, and it is repeated until the sentence is available without effort. The case material's finding is that rehearsal is the stage most frequently omitted by households, and its omission is the reason boys can recite the vocabulary and cannot use it.

**Stage Three: Feedback.** The adult states what the boy did and what it produced, without evaluating the boy. *You told her you were angry and you did not shout; she gave the book back. The sentence worked.* Where the boy's conduct failed, the feedback states the failure and the next attempt: *you shouted, and she shouted back; the sentence we rehearsed was different; try it again tomorrow.* The feedback is brief, specific, and conducted soon after the event.

**Stage Four: Application.** The boy conducts the competence in a setting of real stakes, with the adult available but not present. The application is the stage at which the competence becomes the boy's, and it is the stage at which the adult must tolerate the boy's failure without intervening. The material of Chapter 20.4's distinction between support and substitution applies directly: an adult who conducts the application for the boy teaches the boy that the competence is the adult's.

### What Not to Teach

The section states five practices that are frequently taught as coping and are not.

**The first is suppression presented as discipline.** The instruction to control oneself is not the same as the instruction to feel nothing. The first is a competence; the second is the pattern this book describes. The distinction is the material of Chapter 3.3, and it should be stated to the boy explicitly, because he will otherwise receive the curriculum as a more sophisticated version of the prohibition he already knows.

**The second is distraction presented as regulation.** The boy who is taught to play a game, watch a screen, or eat whenever he is in difficulty has been taught to remove the state from his attention and not to regulate it. Distraction has its place, and it is not the curriculum's third competence.

**The third is achievement presented as coping.** The boy who is taught to respond to every difficulty by working harder has been taught the pattern of Part III: the conversion of an emotional problem into a productivity problem. The curriculum's competences are conducted in addition to the boy's work and not as a substitute for addressing the state.

**The fourth is alcohol and other substances.** The reader may believe that no adult teaches a boy to drink in response to difficulty, and the case material establishes otherwise: the practice is transmitted by the household's own conduct, by the peer group, and by the ordinary culture of many workplaces. The material of Chapter 23.2 applies, and the boy's observation of an adult's drinking is part of the curriculum whether or not the adult intends it.

**The fifth is the confession without the repair.** The adult who states his difficulty to the boy and does nothing about it teaches the boy that disclosure is a performance of honesty rather than an instrument. The curriculum's fourth competence is the statement to a party who has a right to know, and it is followed by the asking and by the action. A statement without the action is not the competence.

### The Adult's Own Practice as the Curriculum

The section closes with the point on which the whole chapter depends, and it is repeated here because the reader will otherwise treat the curriculum as a programme for the boy.

The boy learns the six competences by observing them conducted, and the adult's conduct is therefore the curriculum's first and most durable component. This has three consequences that the reader should state plainly to himself.

**The first is that the adult's own work is not a preliminary to the boy's curriculum. It is the curriculum.** A man who is conducting the material of Parts X and XI is teaching his son every day, whether or not he speaks to him about it. A man who is not conducting it is teaching the opposite, and the teaching is more effective than any sentence he supplies.

**The second is that the adult's repair is more valuable than the adult's perfection.** Boys do not require adults who never fail. They require adults whose failures are followed by repair, because the repair is the demonstration that failure is survivable, and survivability is the belief on which the boy's adult capacity depends. The reader who has conducted Chapter 39.5's sixth practice has already begun this work.

**The third is that the adult's statement of his own difficulty must be proportionate.** The boy is not the adult's therapist, his confidant in matters that belong to adults, or the audience for the adult's unrestrained material. The proportionate statement is brief, accurate, directed at what the boy can receive, and followed by the adult's own action. The finding in the case material is that the proportionate statement is frequently one or two sentences, and that its effect is out of all proportion to its length.

## 43.6 Encouraging Help-Seeking Behaviour

This section addresses the specific behaviour that the whole book has described as the missing instrument: the act of asking. Its question is not why asking is difficult, which Parts II to IX established. Its question is what conditions make asking available to a boy, and what adults and institutions can do to supply them.

The section begins with the finding that governs its material. **Boys do not generally fail to ask because they do not know that help exists. They fail to ask because they have learned what asking costs, and because the adults around them have not supplied the conditions under which the cost is bearable.**

### Why Boys Stop Asking

Four mechanisms are recorded consistently, and each one is addressable.

**The first is the lesson.** The boy has been taught, by the channels of Section 43.1, that asking is a confession of inadequacy. The lesson is not removed by reassurance. It is removed by the boy's observation of asking being conducted by a man he respects, and by his own experience of asking with a good outcome.

**The second is the response.** The boy asked once, at a formative moment, and the response was ridicule, alarm, punishment, or indifference. The response is the single most powerful determinant in the case material, and it explains why the same boy who will not ask an adult will ask a peer, or will ask a stranger, or will ask nobody for twenty years.

**The third is the ledger.** The boy has calculated that asking places him in debt, and the debt is the material of Section 43.2's second lesson. The calculation is frequently accurate about his household: a request for help in a household under pressure is a real cost, and the boy knows it. The remedy is not to deny the cost. It is to supply a route in which the cost is not borne by the household: a named adult outside it, a school service, a professional route, a peer who is not dependent on the household.

**The fourth is the audience.** The boy has learned that the audience determines the permitted content, and he has not been supplied with an audience for the content that matters most. The remedy is the named person, and the named person is the section's principal design feature.

### The Conditions That Produce Help-Seeking

Five conditions are recorded consistently in the case material, and they are cumulative.

**Condition One: A stated permission, in ordinary language, from a named person.** The permission should be specific and unremarkable: *if something is bothering you and you do not want to talk to your mother about it, you can talk to me; I will not tell her unless you and I agree that she needs to know, or unless somebody is in danger.* The permission should be stated more than once, at intervals, and it should not be attached to a crisis.

**Condition Two: A low-cost first request.** The boy's first use of the permission should be cheap: a question about a small matter, a request for advice about a peer, a report of a minor difficulty. The adult's task is to receive the cheap request well, because the cheap request is the boy's test of the arrangement. The finding in the case material is that adults frequently pass over the cheap request as unimportant, and the boy concludes that the arrangement is not real.

**Condition Three: A response that is attentive, unhurried, and non-punitive.** The response should state what the adult has heard, ask one question, and avoid three acts: the immediate solution, the immediate reassurance, and the immediate report to another adult. The material of Chapter 35.4's listening practices applies, and its central rule is that the boy's first disclosure is not the moment for the adult's agenda.

**Condition Four: An action that follows.** The boy must observe that the asking produced something. The action may be small: the adult makes the appointment, speaks to the teacher, supplies the instrument, or simply keeps the arrangement. What matters is that the boy's request was followed by a change in the world, because the absence of a change is the specific experience that teaches pointlessness, which is Section 43.1's fourth response.

**Condition Five: A repetition that survives the boy's withdrawal.** Boys frequently disclose and then withdraw, sometimes for months. The withdrawal is not a rejection of the arrangement, and the adult who treats it as one will end the arrangement at the moment the boy is testing it. The adult's task is to remain available, to state the availability again after an interval, and to make no demand for an account of the withdrawal.

### The Adult's Script

The section supplies a script, because the reader will otherwise conduct the permission in a form that the boy cannot receive.

**The opening.** *I want to say something, and then I will leave it. If you ever have something you do not want to talk to your mother about, you can talk to me. I will listen, I will not laugh, and I will not tell her unless we agree, or unless somebody is in danger.*

**The reception.** *Thank you for telling me. That sounds difficult. Tell me what happened.* The three sentences are deliberate: the thanks acknowledges the cost of the disclosure; the statement acknowledges the difficulty without evaluating it; the question supplies the boy with the opportunity to continue.

**The question that follows.** *What would help?* The question is preferable to *here is what you should do*, because it supplies the boy with the practice of the sixth competence and because the boy frequently knows what he requires.

**The limit.** *I can help with this part, and I am not the right person for that part. We will find somebody who is.* The sentence is honest, it models the limit of Section 43.1's fifth channel, and it begins the professional route when one is required.

**The follow-up.** *I will ask you about this on Thursday. You do not have to have an answer.* The follow-up is the condition that distinguishes an arrangement from a gesture.

### The Institution's Design

The conditions are supplied by institutions as well as by individuals, and the design has four features.

**The first is access.** The institution should have a named person, a stated time, and a stated place at which a boy can speak. The features should be published in ordinary language, and they should be known to every boy, including the boy who does not read the notice board.

**The second is confidentiality.** The institution should state what will be kept confidential and what will not, in advance, and it should keep the statement. The finding in the case material is that boys frequently do not disclose because they cannot predict what will be reported, and that the prediction is more important than the promise.

**The third is competence.** The named person should be trained, supervised, and supported. An untrained adult with good intentions is frequently placed in a position where a boy's disclosure exceeds his capacity, and the outcome is frequently worse than no disclosure, because the boy's experience of the arrangement is the adult's alarm.

**The fourth is the follow-through.** The institution should record what it did after a disclosure, review it, and adjust the arrangement. The feature is the one that distinguishes a service from a policy, and it is the one that the case material finds most frequently absent.
