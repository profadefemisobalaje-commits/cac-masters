# The Man Who Cannot Afford to Break Down {.chapter num="12" short="The Man Who Cannot Afford to Break Down"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the nature of physical and emotional exhaustion in men who work through it, and identify the specific signs that distinguish ordinary tiredness from a condition that requires intervention.
2. Explain the structure of the fear of losing income, and assess the real versus the anticipated consequences of a reduction in earnings in his own circumstances.
3. Analyse the belief that rest is irresponsibility, trace where it comes from, and apply a method for restoring rest without the guilt that currently accompanies it.
4. Identify the specific mechanisms of workplace stress and chronic overwork, including those produced by organisational conditions rather than by the man's own choices.
5. Apply a balancing method that protects health and relationships without requiring a reduction in ambition, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
6. Design a sustainable working life, including the practical arrangements — hours, boundaries, leave, delegation, and the physical conditions of work — that make it possible.
:::

## Introduction

There is a specific type of man whom this chapter addresses, and he is easy to recognise once the pattern is described. He works between fifty-five and eighty hours a week. He travels when required. He has not taken a full week of leave in three years, and when he did take leave, he answered the telephone. He has a persistent condition — hypertension, back pain, gastric trouble, or an untreated dental problem — for which he has an appointment he has rescheduled twice. He sleeps between five and six hours on working nights. He has become short with his children, and he is aware of it and unable to correct it. He drinks more than he used to. He cannot identify a single thing he enjoys doing that is not connected to work, and when this is pointed out to him, he agrees that it is a problem, and continues.

The phrase that structures this chapter is his own, and it is usually offered in explanation rather than complaint: *I cannot afford to break down.* The sentence is a statement of fact as the man understands it, and it has two parts. The first is financial: the household's obligations require his income, and a period of incapacity would produce consequences he cannot allow. The second is psychological: the man's role, standing, and sense of himself require him to be the person who does not fail, and breakdown is a failure of the person rather than of the body.

Both parts of the sentence deserve careful examination. The financial part is frequently at least partly true, and the psychological part is frequently the more powerful of the two, and the less acknowledged. The purpose of this chapter is to establish three things: what the pattern costs, why it is self-defeating on its own terms, and what can be done about it by a man who is not in a position to reduce his obligations dramatically or to restructure his career.

## 12.1 Working Through Physical and Emotional Exhaustion

Exhaustion is easily confused with tiredness, and the confusion is expensive. Tiredness is a normal daily fluctuation that responds to sleep, food, and rest. Exhaustion is a state that persists in the presence of adequate sleep and that reduces the man's capacity across every domain at once. The distinction matters because a man who treats exhaustion as tiredness will apply the remedy that does not work and will delay the intervention that does.

### The Signs of Exhaustion

Six features indicate that what a man is experiencing is not ordinary tiredness.

**Exhaustion that does not respond to rest.** A full weekend of sleep is followed by the same depletion on Monday. This is the single most reliable indicator.

**Sleep that has become unreliable in structure.** Waking at the same hour in the early morning; sleeping but not feeling restored; needing to sleep at unusual hours; or, in some cases, the opposite pattern of disturbing alertness at night and heaviness during the day.

**Emotional flatness or heightened reactivity.** Either a general reduction in feeling — the numbness described in Chapter 6 — or irritability out of proportion to events. Both indicate depletion of the capacity to regulate emotion.

**Physical symptoms with no medical explanation.** Tension, headaches, gastric disturbance, chest tightness, palpitations, and recurrent minor illness. Men in this state catch every infection that passes through the office.

**Reduced cognitive performance.** Forgetfulness, difficulty holding multiple items in mind, and a marked slowdown in decision-making. Men describe this as a feeling of the mind working through water, and it is frequently the first change noticed by colleagues rather than by the man.

**Loss of interest in everything, including things unrelated to work.** Hobbies, friendships, sexual interest, family activities, and, in some cases, previously significant religious or community participation.

### Why Men Work Through It

Five reasons are consistently reported, and it is worth naming them because a man who understands his reasons can act on them.

**The absence of an acceptable alternative.** A man who believes that there is no option but to continue will continue. This belief is frequently accurate for the short term and inaccurate for the medium term, since the same man will be incapacitated within a year if the pattern proceeds.

**The daily arithmetic of the household.** The obligations are specific and dated: fees due, rent due, a loan instalment. Working through exhaustion in April is a rational response to a requirement in April. The problem is that the response recurs every month and there is no moment at which the requirement is complete.

**The fear of being replaced.** Men in employment, and particularly men in competitive roles, report a specific fear that any reduction in availability or output will be read as a decline in capability, with consequences for the position.

**The meaning of the work.** For many men, the work is not merely income; it is competence, usefulness, and identity. Stopping is not rest; it is the removal of the source of self-regard.

**The prohibition on need.** Chapter 2 described the expectation that a man should not require anything. Rest requires the man to require rest, which is an admission he has been trained to avoid.

### What Exhaustion Does Over Time

The progression is predictable and well documented. Reduced cognitive capacity produces errors. Errors in a working environment produce further work, or loss, or both. Further work increases exhaustion. Health deteriorates along known routes — cardiovascular risk, immune function, metabolic changes, and psychological conditions. Relationships deteriorate through absence and irritability. And at some point, the system fails: an illness, an accident, a collapse, or a decision made in exhaustion that cannot be reversed.

The central and unpleasant point of this section is that a man who cannot afford to break down is, by the structure of his current arrangements, planning to break down. What he is choosing is the timing, and the timing he is choosing is later — which is to say, at a moment when the consequences will be larger, the household's obligations will be greater, and his capacity to recover will be less.
