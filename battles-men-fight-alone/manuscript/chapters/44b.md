## 44.2 Learning from Mistakes and Setbacks

The previous section described what difficulty supplies. This section supplies the instrument by which the supplies are extracted: a structured review.

The section's subject is deliberately narrower than the previous one. A setback is frequently not the man's fault; a mistake is. The review of a mistake requires the man to distinguish between the two, and the distinction is the section's first difficulty because men frequently conduct it in one direction only. The man who blames himself for everything conducts a review that produces shame and no information. The man who blames himself for nothing conducts a review that produces no information and no change. The section supplies a method for holding both facts at once.

### The Five Questions

The review consists of five questions, conducted in order, in writing, within a stated period of the event. The period matters: the case material finds that a review conducted in the first three days is frequently governed by the event's emotion, and a review conducted after the third month is frequently governed by the man's revised account. The period that works is between roughly the second week and the eighth week, and the early period is used for the records that the review will need.

**Question One: What happened, in sequence, without interpretation?** The first question produces a chronology: the decisions, the dates, the parties, the information available at each point, and the actions taken. The chronology must be conducted without adjectives, without the man's evaluation of himself, and without the account he has prepared for other people.

The question's purpose is the separation of the event from the story, and the case material records its value precisely. Men who conduct the chronology frequently discover that the event they have been reviewing is not the event that occurred: the failure they have attributed to a single decision was a sequence of six, of which the first was taken eighteen months earlier; the betrayal they have attributed to a party's character was a process in which the man's own arrangements supplied the opportunity; the loss they have attributed to the market was a consequence of a commitment made before the market moved.

**Question Two: What did I decide, and on what information?** The second question isolates the man's own contribution, and it requires the distinction between a decision that was wrong on the information available and a decision that was wrong in retrospect. The distinction is the material of Chapter 29.3's difference between an error of judgement and a fault, and it is the condition on which the review produces information rather than shame.

The specific form of the question is: *at the moment I decided, what did I know, what did I not know, and what did I assume?* The answer frequently establishes that the decision was reasonable on the information and that the information was insufficient, which is a matter of the man's information arrangements rather than his character. Or it establishes that the man knew enough and decided against the information, which is a matter of his conduct, and which the next question addresses.

**Question Three: What did the decision cost, and to whom?** The third question records the cost, in figures where the cost is financial and in specific terms where it is not. The question is asked without the mitigation that the man will wish to supply, because the mitigation is the subject of the fourth question and the two are frequently confused.

The question's purpose is the prevention of the vague account. *It cost me a lot* is not a record. *It cost eleven months, four hundred and twenty thousand naira, the contract with the second client, and my wife's confidence in my judgement about money* is a record, and it is a record the man can use.

**Question Four: What in my arrangements made this possible?** The fourth question is the one that produces the section's most valuable material, and it is the one that men most frequently avoid. It does not ask what the man did wrong. It asks what in his arrangements, his habits, his information, his incentives, or his household's structure made the event possible.

The answers are frequently structural rather than personal: the man had no written record of his commitments; he was the only party who held the accounts; he had no party who could tell him that the plan was unsound; he had an arrangement in which a single customer supplied sixty per cent of his income; he had a habit of agreeing to requests in the moment and reviewing them afterwards. Each answer is a design fault, and a design fault is correctable in a way that a character fault is not.

**Question Five: What will be different in the next twelve months?** The fifth question converts the review into an alteration. Its form is specific: one change to the man's arrangements, with a date and a measure. The change should be small enough to conduct and structural enough to matter.

The case material's finding on this question is that men frequently produce a list of five intentions and conduct none, and that a single structural change, conducted, is worth more than the list. The examples are ordinary: the written record of commitments, reviewed on the first of each month; the second customer, secured before the next contract; the party who is authorised to disagree with the man's plans; the rule that no request is answered in the moment.

### The Four Errors of Review

The section now states the four errors that men make in conducting the review, because each one is common and each one is identifiable.

**Error One: The review as a court.** The man conducts the review in order to establish his own guilt, and the review therefore produces a verdict rather than information. The error's signature is the review's language: the man's account consists of adjectives about himself — careless, stupid, weak, greedy — rather than descriptions of decisions and arrangements. The correction is the chronology, which cannot be conducted in adjectives, and the structural question, which cannot be answered by a verdict.

**Error Two: The review as an exoneration.** The man conducts the review in order to establish that the event was not his fault, and the review therefore excludes the decisions that were his. The error's signature is the review's cast: the account consists entirely of other parties' conduct, and the man's own decisions appear only as responses to provocation. The correction is the second question, which requires the man to state what he decided, and the fourth, which requires him to state what in his arrangements made the event possible regardless of who was at fault.

**Error Three: The review as an avoidance.** The man conducts the review formally and produces a document that contains no material: a set of general lessons — *I will be more careful*, *I will trust fewer people*, *I will not take risks* — that are not changes to arrangements and cannot be measured. The error's signature is the absence of any answer to the fifth question with a date in it. The correction is the fifth question's specific form.

**Error Four: The review as a rumination.** The man conducts the review repeatedly, without a fifth question, and each repetition produces the same material and a stronger emotion. The error's signature is the review's recurrence: the man has conducted the same review forty times in eighteen months and has not altered an arrangement. The correction is the rule that the review is conducted once, written, dated, and filed, and that subsequent attention to the event is conducted as an application of the fifth question rather than as a repetition of the first four.

### The Attribution

The section's second instrument is the attribution, and it is conducted separately from the review because it answers a different question. The review asks what happened and what the man will change. The attribution asks how much of the event belongs to the man and how much belongs to other parties, to circumstance, and to chance.

The attribution's form is a division of the event's components, stated as percentages where the man finds percentages useful and as a list where he does not. A business failure may be divided as follows: forty per cent the man's decision to accept a single large customer; twenty per cent the customer's own difficulty, which was not knowable; twenty per cent the bank's decision to withdraw a facility, which was within the bank's discretion and not within the man's; ten per cent the currency's movement, which was neither; and ten per cent the man's failure to conduct a written record, which made the first component invisible until it was too late.

The purpose of the attribution is not precision. No man can compute these percentages accurately, and the case material does not propose that he should. The purpose is the correction of the two errors that the review is prone to: the total attribution of the event to the man, which produces shame and paralysis, and the total attribution to other parties, which produces no change.

The attribution has three rules.

**Rule One: The attribution is stated in conduct rather than in character.** *I failed to keep a record* is a conduct. *I am disorganised* is a character. The first can be altered and the second cannot, and the second is a verdict in the language of a description.

**Rule Two: The attribution includes what the man could not have known.** The inclusion is not an excuse. It is an accuracy requirement, and its absence produces the man who blames himself for a currency movement or for a customer's concealed insolvency.

**Rule Three: The attribution's other-party components are recorded for information and not for action.** The man's task is not to establish other parties' fault, which is frequently impossible and rarely useful. It is to record what he now knows about those parties and to alter his arrangements accordingly. The alteration is the fifth question of the review, and the case material's finding is that men who conduct the attribution frequently make the same alteration as men who conduct the review alone: they reduce their exposure to the party whose conduct they have now measured.

### The Setback That Was Not a Mistake

The section closes with the case that the review does not fit, because it is common and because the material's honesty requires it.

A setback is frequently not a mistake. A man may be dismissed in a restructuring that had nothing to do with his performance; may lose a contract because a relation of the client's director was given the work; may be injured in an accident caused by another driver; may be defrauded by a party who had been recommended by a friend; may lose a household member to an illness that no arrangement could have prevented.

The review's questions remain useful in these cases, and their application is different.

The first question is conducted, because the chronology is always useful. The second question is frequently answered by *I decided correctly on the information available*, which is the correct answer and should be recorded, because the man will need it later when his account of the event has begun to blame him. The third question is conducted in full, because the cost is real regardless of fault. The fourth question is conducted in the modified form: *what in my arrangements made me vulnerable to this, and what would reduce the vulnerability?* — which is a question about exposure rather than about fault, and which produces the changes that a man can make in a world where other parties behave badly. The fifth question is conducted as usual.

The finding that the section records is this: **men who conduct the review on events that were not their fault frequently make the most valuable changes, because the review discloses an exposure that they had never examined.** The man who was dismissed in a restructuring discovers that his entire professional network consisted of the colleagues in his own department; the man who was defrauded discovers that he had no procedure for verifying a recommendation; the man whose contract was lost to a director's relation discovers that his commercial arrangements depended on a relationship rather than on a contract. None of these discoveries is a fault, and each is a design fault, and each is correctable.

## 44.3 Developing Perspective and Emotional Maturity

The previous section supplied the instrument for extracting information from an event. This section addresses the capacities that determine whether the information is used well, and it proceeds by describing what perspective and maturity actually consist of rather than by exhorting the reader to acquire them.

The section's first proposition is that both are competences rather than attributes. A man is not born with perspective and does not acquire it by ageing. The case material records men of sixty with no perspective and men of thirty with a great deal, and the distinction between them is the set of practices described below.

### The Four Tests of Proportion

Perspective, in this chapter's sense, is the capacity to locate an event in its actual size. The capacity is exercised through four tests, and each one is a question that the man asks of the event.

**Test One: The test of time.** The question is: *where will this be in five years?* The test is not a reassurance, and it is not always answered favourably: some events are still present in five years, and the man who assumes otherwise is conducting a denial. The test's purpose is accuracy, and its most frequent result is a reclassification. The event that has occupied the man's entire attention for six weeks is frequently classified, on the test, as a matter that will be resolved within a year and will be a paragraph in his account of his life. The event that the man has treated as minor is occasionally classified as the thing that will determine his next decade, and the reclassification is the more valuable of the two.

**Test Two: The test of the audience.** The question is: *whose estimate of this event am I currently holding?* The test addresses the mechanism of Chapter 25.3, in which a man's estimate of his own position is conducted by an imagined audience. A man who is humiliated by a professional failure frequently discovers, on the test, that the humiliation is being conducted before an audience of four people whose opinion he does not actually value, and that the parties whose opinion he does value have not altered their estimate at all.

**Test Three: The test of the alternative.** The question is: *what would I have had to do differently, and would I have done it?* The test addresses the counterfactual that governs much of the rumination described in Section 44.2's fourth error. The answer is frequently that the alternative required information the man did not have, or a decision he was not capable of making at the time, or a cost he would not have accepted. The test's result is the conversion of the counterfactual from a reproach into a description.

**Test Four: The test of the whole.** The question is: *what else is in my life at this moment?* The test is the simplest and it is the one that men in difficulty most frequently fail. A man in a financial crisis conducts his attention as though the crisis were the whole of his position, and on the test he discovers that he has a marriage that is intact, three children who are well, a competence that the market still requires, and a body that is functioning. The discovery is not a denial of the crisis. It is the restoration of the crisis to its actual proportion within a life, and the restoration is the condition on which the man can conduct the crisis without conducting it as the end of everything.

The four tests are conducted in writing, and their result is recorded. The case material's finding is that the written form is essential, because the tests conducted in the man's head are conducted by the same faculty that produced the disproportion.

### The Six Markers of Emotional Maturity

The section now states the markers, because the reader will otherwise evaluate his maturity by his age, his composure, or his capacity to endure, and none of the three is a marker.

**Marker One: The capacity to state a state without either dramatising it or denying it.** The mature man can say that he is afraid, ashamed, or grieved, in ordinary language, to a party who has a right to know, without the statement becoming a performance or a concealment. The marker is the adult form of Chapter 39.4's four competences, and it is measured by the statement's accuracy rather than its frequency.

**Marker Two: The capacity to be corrected without collapse.** The mature man can receive information that he was wrong, consider it, and alter his conduct, without either accepting every criticism in order to avoid conflict or rejecting every criticism in order to protect his standing. The marker is the one that the case material associates most strongly with professional effectiveness, and it is the marker that the propositions of Chapter 43.4 most directly attack.

**Marker Three: The capacity to hold two true things at once.** The mature man can hold that his difficulty was unjust and that he is responsible for what he does with it; that he was wrong and that his accuser was also wrong; that his father did his best and that the harm was real; that his marriage is worth continuing and that it is currently unsatisfactory. The capacity is the one that the myths of Section 44.1 most frequently remove, since each myth offers a single account.

**Marker Four: The capacity to delay the account.** The mature man can conduct a difficulty for a period without requiring an immediate explanation, a verdict, or a resolution, and without the delay becoming avoidance. The marker is the adult form of Chapter 39.4's third competence, and its absence produces the man who must resolve every uncertainty within the hour.

**Marker Five: The capacity to act without certainty.** The mature man can take a decision on incomplete information, state the uncertainty, and revise the decision when the information changes. The marker is the material of Chapter 32.4's distinction between authority and omniscience, and it is the capacity that leadership requires and that the fear of failure most directly removes.

**Marker Six: The capacity to be generous from a position of loss.** The mature man can extend something to another party while he is himself in difficulty: attention, assistance, patience, or recognition. The marker is the one that the case material identifies as the clearest evidence that a difficulty has been converted rather than merely survived, and it is the marker that Section 44.4's conversion produces.

### What Maturity Is Not

The section states four things that are frequently mistaken for maturity and are not.

**The first is composure.** A man who presents an unaltered surface in every circumstance has not necessarily acquired maturity. He may have acquired the concealment described in Chapter 42. The distinction is the marker of statement: the mature man can state a state, and the composed man's surface is maintained by not stating it.

**The second is endurance.** A man who has carried a great deal for a long time has demonstrated a capacity, and the capacity is not the same as maturity. The case material records men who endured for thirty years and whose understanding of their own position was no more accurate at sixty than at thirty.

**The third is detachment.** A man who has ceased to be affected by events has frequently ceased to be affected because he has withdrawn from the arrangements that would affect him, which is the material of Chapter 24.3's withdrawal. Maturity is not the absence of response. It is the appropriate response.

**The fourth is seniority.** Age and position supply experience, and experience supplies material. The material becomes wisdom only through the practices of this chapter, and the case material records men of high position whose conduct in difficulty was less mature than that of men half their age.

The section closes with the relationship between the two subjects. Perspective is the capacity to locate an event correctly. Maturity is the capacity to conduct oneself well in the event's actual size. The first is an estimate and the second is a conduct, and the case material's finding is that men frequently acquire the first without the second: they can state that the failure was not the end of everything, and they continue to conduct themselves as though it were.
