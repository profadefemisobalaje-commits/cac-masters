## 21.4 The Struggle to Accept Who You Are

Self-acceptance is a subject that attracts two errors and this section disposes of both before setting out what the work actually consists of.

### What Self-Acceptance Is Not

**It is not resignation.** Resignation says that the material is unacceptable and unchangeable, and it produces inertia. Acceptance is compatible with a plan; it is the precondition of one, because a man cannot change what he will not examine.

**It is not the lowering of standards.** Standards concern conduct and outcomes; acceptance concerns the man's relationship to his own history. A man may accept what he has done and hold a high standard for what he does next, and the combination is the ordinary condition of an adult.

**It is not a feeling.** Men frequently report that they cannot accept themselves because they do not feel acceptance. Acceptance is a position rather than a feeling: it consists of holding the facts about oneself without either concealing them or being destroyed by them, and it is frequently experienced as a lack of drama rather than as warmth.

### The Six Obstacles

Each obstacle below is common in men, and each is addressable.

**1. The over-developed standard.** A man whose standard for himself is global, personal, and permanent, as described in Chapter 8, cannot accept anything, because the standard is not satisfiable. The route is not the reduction of the standard but the conversion of it into specific and achievable items.

**2. The comparison mechanism.** Acceptance requires a man to hold his own account, and comparison requires him to hold a position relative to others. The two cannot be operated simultaneously. Chapter 11's work on a personal reference point is the intervention.

**3. The rejection of what the man actually is.** Where the man's own character, temperament, capacities, or interests do not match the script he was given, he frequently treats his material as a defect. The test in Chapter 2 is relevant: which elements of the script does the man hold deliberately, and which does he hold because they were applied to him?

**4. The confusion of acceptance with giving up.** Men frequently resist acceptance because they believe that the discomfort is the mechanism of their performance. The belief is not unfounded: many men have achieved what they have by the reliable operation of a hard standard. What they have not established, and what the research on motivation indicates, is that the discomfort is necessary for the standard to operate. Men who replace self-directed hostility with a specific and maintainable standard consistently report an improvement in output alongside the improvement in mood.

**5. The absence of any experience of being known.** A man cannot accept himself against nothing. Acceptance is a relational act as well as an internal one, and men in this pattern characteristically discover that the work becomes possible only after somebody has been given an accurate account. This is the connection between this chapter and Chapters 4 and 14.

**6. The shame described in Section 21.3.** The final obstacle and the largest. Its route out is the graduated disclosure described above.

### What Acceptance Actually Requires

Four items, in the order in which men generally find them attainable.

**An accurate inventory.** A written statement of what the man is and what he has done: the capacities, the failures, the periods, the conduct, the circumstances. Its purpose is the replacement of a vague and unfavourable impression with a specific record. In the author's observation, this exercise produces the single most reliable change in men whose difficulty is a general sense of inadequacy, because the specific record is almost always more defensible than the general impression.

**A distinction between what is his and what arrived.** A substantial proportion of what men hold with shame concerns circumstances, other people's conduct, and the economic and familial conditions of their lives. The distinction should be made explicitly: which items on the inventory are my conduct, and which are what happened to me?

**A distinction between what can be changed and what is concluded.** The inventory should be divided into three parts: what is an open matter, what is amendable at a cost the man is willing to pay, and what is concluded. Concluded matters are not thereby approved; they are closed, which means that they no longer occupy the position of an open item.

**One accurate account given to one person.** The measure that converts the inventory from a private document into a fact about the man's life. Its purpose is not permission and not absolution; it is the demonstration that the material can be held by somebody else.

### Where Acceptance Is Not Possible

One caution is necessary. Where a man's difficulty is a depressive condition, the attempt to accept himself will be conducted in a state that makes acceptance feel like exposure, and the work should follow treatment rather than precede it, as Chapter 6 established. Where the man's inventory contains serious conduct — violence, serious harm to another person, abuse — the work requires professional support, and the guidance in Appendix C is the correct point of entry.

## 21.5 Facing the Truth Without Self-Destruction

The risk in this chapter's material is a specific one: that a man who examines his private account, without the structure described here, will conduct the examination as the internal critic conducts it, and will reach the conclusion that the account confirms what he feared. Men who complete the work well do so with four protections in place.

### The Four Protections

**1. The examination is conducted in writing, not in thought.** Thinking about one's own failures is the activity described in Chapter 8 as rumination: it is general, repeated, and it produces no output. Writing produces an inventory, which is specific, finite, and available for classification. The difference between the two is the difference between the whole of this section's method and the process that men have already attempted unsuccessfully.

**2. The account is classified before it is assessed.** The three-way classification in Section 21.4 — open, amendable, concluded — and the two-way distinction between the man's conduct and what arrived. These are mechanical operations, they take about an hour, and they prevent the material from being held as a single undifferentiated mass, which is what makes it crushing.

**3. The examination is bounded and time-limited.** Two sittings, of about ninety minutes each, with a defined end. The material is examined in a defined period and then closed for a week. The bound is not a refusal to face the truth; it is the condition under which facing it produces work rather than despair.

**4. The examination concludes with specific items.** Every part of the inventory must produce one of three outputs: a fact that requires no action, an item with a specific action and a date, or a matter that requires professional assistance. An inventory that concludes without items has produced a verdict rather than a plan, and verdicts are the product of the critic.

### The Method

**Sitting One: The record.**

Four columns, completed without evaluation.

| What is in my private account | Whose conduct is this? | Open, amendable, or concluded? | What does it require? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | |

The third column is completed after the first, never during it. Men who attempt to classify while recording will spend the sitting producing conclusions rather than records.

**Between sittings.** Do nothing with the material except keep the document. This interval is deliberate: it allows the material's emotional weight to settle, and men frequently find that a large proportion of it is less significant than it appeared while it was being written.

**Sitting Two: The plan.**

For each row marked *open* or *amendable*, specify the action and the date. The actions fall into a small number of kinds: a disclosure, a repair, a payment, an apology, an appointment, a change of practice, a conversation, a decision. For rows marked *concluded*, write the single sentence that closes them: *that is what occurred; it is finished; I am not required to revisit it.*

### The Three Practices After the Examination

**1. The re-reading.** Read the inventory at two months, six months, and one year. Its value is specific: the re-reading demonstrates the movement of the *open* items into *concluded*, which is the evidence that the account is a changing document rather than a permanent verdict. Men frequently report that the second reading, not the first, produced the change.

**2. The counter-record.** A second document, kept alongside, of the man's genuine capacities, contributions, and conduct of which he is not ashamed. This is not an exercise in affirmation; it is a correction of the inventory's natural bias, which is that a man records failures and does not record the ordinary and substantial good that he has done and continues to do. The counter-record should be specific and it should include the material that other people would identify if asked.

**3. The disclosure of one item.** The item selected in the graduated process of Section 21.3, conducted within the six months following the examination. Men who complete the inventory and disclose nothing frequently report that the material has been reorganised without being reduced.

### The Signals That Professional Assistance Is Required

Three signals should be treated as conclusive. Where the inventory's content is connected to abuse, violence, or a traumatic event, the material requires professional treatment rather than reflective work. Where the examination produces a sustained deterioration in mood, sleep, or functioning across more than two weeks, the conditions in Chapters 5 and 6 apply and assessment should be sought. And where the man cannot conduct the examination at all — where the reading of his own account produces a state from which he cannot recover without consequence — the work is not the obstacle and the assistance is required first.
