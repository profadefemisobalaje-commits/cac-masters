# The Tears That Never Fall {.chapter num="3" short="The Tears That Never Fall"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why many men find it difficult to express pain, and identify the specific mechanisms by which this difficulty is acquired.
2. Describe what happens to a man psychologically and physically when emotion is suppressed over long periods.
3. Distinguish clearly between self-control and emotional avoidance, and locate his own habits on the scale between them.
4. Examine the social and personal meanings attached to crying, and describe what a man loses when he is unable to cry at all.
5. Recognise unprocessed grief and emotional exhaustion as distinct conditions with distinct treatments, and describe the exhaustion pattern that follows years of unrecognised feeling.
6. Use structured methods — sensation naming, a graduated scale, one-sentence writing, and borrowed language — to rebuild an emotional vocabulary, using the exercise at the end of this chapter.
:::

## Introduction

There is a photograph that many families possess without knowing it. It was taken at a funeral — perhaps of a father, a mother, a brother, or a child — and in the middle of it there is a man standing perfectly straight, looking directly at the camera, with a face that shows nothing at all. Around him, other people are weeping. He is not. He has not wept at the graveside, at the reception, or in the car. He has arranged the transport, settled the bill with the caterer, thanked the visitors, and answered every telephone call. He is the one who kept the occasion from falling apart, and he is the one about whom somebody later says, *He was very strong.*

This chapter is about that man, and about the tears that never fell — not only at that funeral, but at whatever earlier funeral or disgrace or loss first taught him that his tears were unwelcome. It is about the habit of emotional suppression: how it is acquired, what it costs, how it differs from the self-control that men often confuse it with, and how a man who has spent thirty years not crying can learn to feel again without ceasing to be the person his family depends upon.

## 3.1 Why Some Men Struggle to Express Pain

The first task is to replace a common and unhelpful explanation with a better one. The unhelpful explanation is that men are naturally less emotional than women. It is unhelpful for two reasons: it is not supported by the evidence, and it gives the man who holds it nothing to do. If the difficulty is natural, then it is permanent, and there is no reason to work on it. The better explanation is that men and women are born with the same capacity for feeling, that this capacity persists throughout life, and that what differs — enormously, and sometimes painfully — is the *permission* and the *skill* to express it. Both permission and skill are acquired, which means that both can be revised.

Four mechanisms account for most of the difficulty.

### The Early Extinction of Crying

Infants cry, of both sexes, at more or less the same rates and in response to more or less the same stimuli. Differences emerge as the child develops, and they emerge largely because of differential responses to the same behaviour. A three-year-old boy who cries is treated differently from a three-year-old girl who cries in almost every society studied, and the difference generally consists of encouragement in one case and discouragement in the other. Discouragement is rarely harsh. It consists of redirection (*Look, there's a dog!*), mild shaming (*Big boys don't cry*), physical interruption, or the substitution of anger for sadness (*Are you going to cry, or are you going to do something?*). Applied a few hundred times over a decade, it produces a man for whom crying has become a skill he does not have, rather than an act he has chosen to avoid.

### The Substitution of Anger for Sadness

The second mechanism is the most important for understanding adult male distress, and it is well documented. Where one emotion is unwelcome, the child substitutes another that is permitted. In most environments, anger is permitted to boys in a way that fear and grief are not. The result is that some men have developed a highly efficient anger channel and an almost entirely unsupplied grief channel, so that sadness is experienced — genuinely, not as a pretence — as irritation, and grief as a hard, tight, restless state that the man cannot name.

This substitution accounts for much of what families observe in men under strain: irritability about small domestic matters, a short fuse, an intolerance of noise or of children's play, a sudden flare over a misplaced object. The provocation is trivial because the provocation is not the cause. The cause is a feeling that has been routed through the only available channel.

### The Absence of a Vocabulary

The third mechanism is linguistic. Emotional experience is learned, in part, through language. A child whose environment contains precise emotional vocabulary — for disappointment, humiliation, longing, dread, reassurance, shame, forgiveness — acquires the ability to distinguish states that would otherwise be experienced as an undifferentiated heaviness. A child whose environment contains two words, *fine* and *angry*, acquires two categories.

Adult men in this position describe the difficulty vividly. They know that something is wrong. They can describe the circumstances — the debt, the diagnosis, the marriage — with precision. What they cannot do is describe the state that the circumstances produce in them, and the inability is not a refusal. It is a genuine absence of the necessary terms. This is why a great deal of work with men begins with vocabulary rather than with feeling, and why Section 3.6 of this chapter is devoted to vocabulary-building.

### The Calculation of Social Cost

The fourth mechanism is adult and rational. By mid-life, most men have accumulated a set of experiences in which the expression of emotion produced a bad outcome: a wife who became frightened; a mother who began to telephone daily; a colleague who mentioned it to others; a supervisor who treated it as evidence of unsuitability; a peer who mocked it. These experiences are usually only two or three in number, and they are sufficient. A man does not need to be punished repeatedly to learn a lesson; he needs only to be punished once, emphatically, at a moment when the stakes felt high.

Men are not less emotional than women. They are frequently less *permitted*, less *practised*, and less *supplied with vocabulary*. All three of those are acquired conditions, and acquired conditions can be changed.

## 3.2 Emotional Suppression and Its Consequences

Having established why the difficulty arises, it is necessary to be precise about what suppression is, because the term is used loosely and the looseness causes men to reject the whole discussion.

### What Suppression Is

**Emotional suppression** is the deliberate or habitual pushing of an emotional experience out of awareness or out of expression. It occurs in two forms. *Expressive suppression* means feeling the emotion fully but preventing it from being visible: the man who is devastated and whose voice does not change. *Experiential avoidance* means preventing the emotion from being experienced at all: the man who does not know he is devastated, and who will report only that he is tired, busy, or fine.

Expressive suppression is more common in men and more visible. Experiential avoidance is more consequential, and more difficult to treat, because the person experiencing it cannot report it. Much of the material in this chapter is addressed to the second form, and the reader should therefore resist the natural tendency to answer *That is not me; I know exactly what I feel.* He may. The reader who has already noticed that he is not sure what he feels is the one for whom the chapter is most directly written.

### The Documented Consequences

The research literature on suppression is extensive and consistent. Six findings matter for the reader.

**Physiological load.** Suppressing an emotional response does not reduce the body's reaction to the event that caused it; it frequently increases it. Studies of expressive suppression have found elevated cardiovascular activity during the act of suppression, and long-term patterns of suppression are associated with higher physiological stress markers.

**Memory and attention costs.** Suppression consumes attention. People who are suppressing emotion remember less of what is said around them — including, in some studies, the content of the conversations they are having. This is the mechanism behind a complaint that many men make about themselves: *I cannot remember anything from that period.*

**Impaired communication.** Because suppression interferes with attention and with natural expression, it makes a person harder to read and less responsive to the person speaking. Partners of men who habitually suppress describe a specific experience: talking to someone who is present but not there.

**Increased physiological and psychological symptoms.** Higher rates of sleep disruption, tension, and somatic complaints are reported among habitual suppressors, along with reduced well-being.

**Contagion into the household.** Suppression does not remain private. Children and partners read the emotional weather of the house with considerable accuracy. A child who grows up with a father who never expresses fear, grief, or tenderness learns an implicit rule about which feelings are permitted, and carries it forward. This is discussed in Chapter 43.

**Rebound.** Suppressed emotion does not disappear. It reappears — often at a lower threshold, in an unexpected context, and at a disproportionate intensity — which is why a man who has been calm for months may erupt over a parking space.

### The Protections Suppression Provides

A book that described only costs would be dishonest, and men know it. Suppression provides real protection, and the reader who recognises himself needs to see that recognised.

| What suppression protects | What it eventually costs |
| --- | --- |
| Calm in the household in the short term | Marital intimacy, and children's model of openness |
| Standing and credibility at work | Sleep, judgement, health, and honesty with colleagues |
| Freedom from questions in friendships | The friendships themselves, which slowly empty |
| The ability to keep functioning after a loss | Unfinished grief, which returns as numbness or anger |
| Time: difficult conversations are deferred | Accumulated charge, and conversations that must eventually be held at greater cost |

Table: The bargain of suppression. Both columns are real; the difficulty is that the left-hand column is immediate and the right-hand column is delayed.

The practical conclusion is not that suppression must be abandoned in all circumstances. There are moments when a man must hold his feelings until he is alone, and that is a competence. The conclusion is that suppression is a short-term tool with a long-term cost, and that a man who relies on it as a permanent arrangement is paying a price he has not calculated. Chapter 34 sets out the point at which prolonged suppression becomes genuinely dangerous.
