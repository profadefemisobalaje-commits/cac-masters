## 28.6 The Life You Built Instead

The final piece of the work, and the one that determines whether the closing of a dream leaves the man with a grief alone or with an accurate account of his life, is the reckoning with what was built in the dream's place.

### Why the Reckoning Is Difficult

Four reasons, and they must be handled rather than argued away.

**The comparison is conducted in the man's own mind, and it is rigged.** The imagined life contains the dream's benefits and none of its costs; the actual life contains every cost, visible in detail, and its benefits are spread across decades in the ordinary form of a functioning household, which the man does not count because he has lived inside it. The reckoning corrects the comparison and it cannot be conducted by encouragement; it must be conducted by enumeration.

**The man's household does not know there is anything to count.** The people for whose sake the dream was set down generally do not know that the setting down occurred. A wife who learns at fifty that her husband carries an unchosen alternative will frequently respond with guilt, which is the last thing the man requires and which is why the disclosure requires the framing described in Section 28.4: the man is describing a loss and is not asking for an account of the decision to be reopened.

**The reckoning is frequently greeted as complacency.** Men report a resistance to the idea that their actual life constitutes an achievement, because the culture's instruction is that a man should not console himself and that the counting of ordinary goods is a form of settling. The instruction should be identified as what it is: a requirement that the man carry the loss without counting anything, which produces the condition described in Chapter 7.

**The reckoning must not be conducted as a substitution.** The final and most important reason. The life that was built does not replace the dream. The chapter is not proposing that the man should conclude that everything worked out for the best, which would be false and which the man will not believe. The accurate position is the one stated in Section 28.2 and repeated here: the man lost something real, and the life he built in its place contains things he would not surrender. Both are true, and the work is to hold both.

### What to Count

The enumeration is specific, and the following six domains supply it. The exercise at the end of the chapter structures the counting; the domains are listed here so that the reader knows what the work involves.

**1. The people who exist because of the decision.** The children who were raised, schooled, and kept; the siblings whose education was completed; the parent whose care was provided; the younger relatives who were supported; and, in a proportion of cases, the men and women who were trained, employed, or mentored in the man's trade. The counting is conducted by name, and it is the domain that men most consistently report as decisive.

**2. The capability that was accumulated.** The skill, the trade, the professional competence, the reputation, the knowledge of a market, the understanding of a craft. The dream was not the only thing the man built; the decades produced a body of capability that frequently exceeds what the man credits, since he has never seen it described.

**3. The obligations discharged.** What was paid, maintained, funded, repaired, and kept afloat: the fees, the rent, the medical costs, the family's contributions, the repairs, the support that was extended without being recorded. This domain is addressed at length in Chapter 20 and it is conducted from the record rather than from memory.

**4. The standing in the community.** Not the rank but the position: the man who is consulted, the man whose word is taken, the man who can be relied upon to appear. The domain is frequently invisible to men who hold it, because standing acquired over decades is not accompanied by a description of itself.

**5. The competence of the man in the present.** What he can now do that he could not do at twenty-two: the judgement, the calibration of risk, the ability to manage people, the tolerance of difficulty, the knowledge of himself. This domain is the one in which the comparison with the imagined self is most decisively corrected, since the imagined self is the man at twenty-two with the dream intact and none of the man's present capacities.

**6. The life that was lived in the interval.** The marriages, friendships, places, events, meals, journeys, and occasions of which the dream would have contained none. Men frequently report, on completing this domain, that the contributions and events they have counted occurred in the years in which the dream would have been pursued, and that a substantial portion of them would not have existed otherwise. This is not a consolation; it is a fact about the actual composition of the life.

### The Two Sentences

The reckoning is completed with two sentences, and both must be written, in the man's own words, and both must be present.

**Sentence one:** *I set down this. It cost me this. I would make the same decision again.*

**Sentence two:** *What I built instead is this, and I count it.*

A man who holds only the first sentence is the man described in Chapter 24, whose account of himself consists of the role he lost. A man who holds only the second is treating his own grief as a defect, which is the position of a substantial number of the men in the case material of this chapter, who have assumed that a contented man could not carry an unwished-for alternative. Both sentences are required, and the practice is to read them together in the same document.

### The Position on Regrets of This Kind

Two clarifications, which the chapter states because they are frequently raised.

**A closed dream is not a regret in the sense of Chapter 29.** A regret concerns a decision the man believes was wrong. A closed dream concerns a decision the man believes was right and whose cost he is acknowledging. The distinction matters because regret and grief require different treatment: Chapter 29's work addresses the decision, and this chapter's work addresses the loss. Men who treat a dead dream as a regret expend years on a question — *was it right?* — to which their own answer has been known all along.

**The dream may be closed and the ambition may remain.** The closing concerns the specific form the man intended at a specific age. It does not require the man to abandon ambition, and the men who conduct this work frequently report the opposite: with the occupied mental space freed and the imagined self retired, they undertake something, small or large, that they had not attempted for years. The finding is worth recording in the chapter's own terms: a dream that has been properly closed leaves the man with the capacity to choose what to build with the years remaining, whereas a dream that stands open indefinitely consumes that capacity in an unproductive comparison.
