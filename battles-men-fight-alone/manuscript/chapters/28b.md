## 28.1 The Dreams a Man Carries

Before the processes are described, the dreams themselves must be sorted, and the sorting matters because different kinds of dream require different handling.

### The Eight Categories

**1. The vocational dream.** The man intended to do work of a particular kind: the medicine, the law, the engineering, the teaching, the research, the music, the writing, the design, the sport. This category is the most frequently reported and the one with the clearest structure, since a vocation has observable requirements — a qualification, an entry, a period of training — and its abandonment can usually be dated.

**2. The educational dream.** The degree, the certificate, the professional qualification, the second degree, the course that was to be taken *after this busy period*. This category overlaps with the first and is heavier in the man's account when the education was the mechanism of the vocation, because the loss of the one is the loss of the other.

**3. The entrepreneurial dream.** The business, the shop, the company, the practice, the farm, the workshop, the truck, the brand. This category has a characteristic feature: it is frequently not closed but repeatedly deferred, and the deferral is organised around a condition — *when I have saved, when the children finish, when the market improves, when I have a partner I can trust*. Chapter 26's material on trust is frequently connected.

**4. The migratory dream.** The country, the city, the life elsewhere. The visa that was applied for and refused, the application that was never submitted, the opportunity that was declined because of a parent's illness, the plan that was silently abandoned when a child's schooling was established. This category is frequently accompanied by a comparison with the men who went, which supplies the continuous measure described in Chapter 11.

**5. The creative dream.** The book, the album, the art, the film, the business of a particular aesthetic kind. This dream has the property of being permanently available — the man can begin at any time — which makes its deferral easy and its abandonment invisible. The category is the one in which men most frequently report a specific and painful discovery late in life: that the material they intended to produce still exists only as an intention.

**6. The family and legacy dream.** The family that was going to be different from the one the man grew up in; the marriage of a particular kind; the number of children; the house in which the children would be raised; the name he would leave. Its abandonment is quiet because the reality is gradual and because the man's own conduct participates in every stage of it, which makes the loss hard to state without self-accusation. The material of Chapters 17 and 19 is connected.

**7. The place and life dream.** The house, the land, the village, the life in a particular setting. Its abandonment is frequently financial and frequently permanent, and it is the category in which the man's plan is most frequently carried by a single object — the plot, the title, the building — which means the loss has a date and a location and is therefore, in an unhelpful way, continuously visible.

**8. The bodily dream.** The sporting achievement, the physical capacity, the standard the man held for himself as an athlete, a soldier, a dancer, a physically capable man. This dream dies quietly with age and injury, and it is the category least often recognised as a loss, which is why the material in Chapter 32 is required.

### The Three Distinctions

Three distinctions separate a dream from the other things men carry, and each is worth applying to the list the reader has just generated.

**A dream has a plan.** Not necessarily a schedule, but a set of steps the man could name. *I intended to study medicine* has a plan: sit the examination, obtain the entry, complete the training. *I intended to be a wealthy man* does not, and it is an image rather than a dream. The distinction matters because images do not die quietly — they die by being looked at — and their abandonment does not warrant the work described in this chapter.

**A dream belongs to the man.** Chapter 2's three-way test applies: is this dream mine, or is it my family's, my community's, or my father's? A substantial proportion of what men carry is a dream that was installed by somebody else and never examined. Its abandonment is frequently not a loss but a release, and the case in Section 28.7 illustrates the pattern. The work of this chapter should not be applied to a dream the man never held.

**A dream has been held against the man's own judgement.** A dream the man actually carried has a specific signature: the man can describe the moment he decided it, the person he told, the step he attempted, and the point at which he stopped. Where a man cannot supply those four items, the item is either an image, an obligation, or a regret acquired from somebody else's account. The four items are also the raw material of the exercise at the end of the chapter.

### The Two Categories of What Remains

Once the dreams have been sorted, they fall into two groups and the grouping determines the work.

**The dead dream.** The dream that cannot be resumed in its original form. The age limit, the qualification route, the market, the injury, the migration window, the person who would have done it with him. Its handling is described in Sections 28.3 to 28.5: the acknowledgement of the loss, the grief conducted without an audience, and the closing.

**The pending dream.** The dream that is genuinely available: the course that can still be taken, the qualification that has no age barrier, the business that could be started at any point, the book, the land, the trade, the way of life. Its handling is different and it is not primarily emotional: the man must either take a first step within a defined period or reclassify the dream as closed. The most damaging of all the positions described in this chapter is that of the pending dream that receives no first step and no closing, which is where the majority of the men who carry dreams appear to be. This is the position the chapter's exercise addresses directly.

::: keypoint
Dreams fall into eight categories — vocational, educational, entrepreneurial, migratory, creative, family and legacy, place and life, and bodily — and must be distinguished from images, from obligations, and from dreams that were never the man's own. A real dream has a plan, belongs to the man, and can be described at the moment it was decided, the person it was told to, the step that was attempted, and the point at which it stopped. Once sorted, dreams are either dead or pending, and the two require entirely different work.
:::

## 28.2 What Kills a Dream Quietly

A dream rarely ends in a decision. It ends through one or more of seven processes, and the processes are worth setting out because men who can identify the process operating in their case are frequently, for the first time, able to describe the loss accurately.

### The Seven Processes

**1. The substitution of duty for the dream.** The most common process by far, and the one that deserves the most careful treatment because it is also, frequently, the correct decision. The man's obligations arrive — the household, the parent's illness, the sibling's fees, the child's schooling, the family's expectations — and the dream is set down in favour of the obligation. The process has no hostile agent: the man chooses the obligation himself, repeatedly, in small decisions, and each decision is defensible. What makes the process quiet is that no single moment can be identified as the abandonment, and what makes it painful is that the man who chose his obligations with full competence does not feel entitled to grieve something he set down deliberately.

The specific finding from men's accounts is that the majority of such decisions were correct in their own terms and are not regretted in their own terms. *I would make the same decision again* is the most common sentence in the material. The loss is nonetheless real: the man regrets the dream and would repeat the decision, and both are true, and the chapter's work is to allow both to be held at once.

**2. The narrowing produced by money.** The dream survived on the assumption of a surplus that never arrived. The margin above the household's requirements remained thin for a decade, and the amount the dream required — the fees, the capital, the passage, the equipment, the unpaid year — remained just out of reach each year. The process is not poverty in the absolute sense and it is not prosperity: it is the condition of a man whose income covers his obligations and not his plans, maintained over the whole of the period in which the plan was possible.

The specific feature is that the process produces a particular kind of bitterness when it is accompanied by visible prosperity elsewhere: the man does not resent those with more than he has, he resents the absence of a margin, which is a different and more corrosive position.

**3. The failure that ended the route.** The attempt was made and it failed: the examination not passed, the business lost, the application refused, the audition unsuccessful, the injury sustained. This process is the most visible of the seven and it is frequently not the one that killed the dream, because a failed attempt ordinarily produces a second attempt. What kills the dream in this case is generally the interpretation the man attaches to the failure, which is the material of Chapters 9, 21, and 25 combined: the failure becomes a verdict about the man, and the second attempt becomes inconceivable.

The specific finding from men's accounts: in a substantial proportion of cases the man was capable of a second attempt, had one avenue open, and had the information required, and did not attempt again because the first failure had been converted into a conclusion about himself.

**4. The requirement of another person.** The dream was set down at the request, insistence, or fear of somebody else: the father who required the man to enter the family business or the profession, the wife who would not agree to the move or the risk, the mother who was frightened of a particular path, the family that would not accept the marriage that was part of the plan.

The specific feature is the difficulty of stating the loss without indicting the person, which is why the process is frequently not described at all. Men's accounts handle it in three ways, and the chapter notes them because each has a cost: the man blames the other person and carries the resentment described in Chapter 15; the man blames himself for having yielded; or the man says nothing, which is the most common and which leaves the loss unstated for decades.

**5. The slow erosion.** No obstacle, no opposition, no failure: the dream was simply not advanced. The hours were occupied by legitimate matters, the months passed, the man's skill or preparedness declined, and at a certain point the item that remained was no longer a plan but a memory of one. This process is the quietest of the seven and it is the one that produces the most shame when it is finally examined, because there is no event to which the loss can be attributed and no other party to whom any part of it can be assigned.

The finding from men's accounts: this process is very frequently accompanied by a parallel erosion in other domains, and the man's examination of it is frequently the point at which the wider material of Chapter 24 becomes visible.

**6. The decision made on somebody else's timetable.** The dream was pursued under a schedule imposed by circumstances: the age limit, the closing date, the requirement of the employer, the expectations of a community concerning when a man should be married, established, or settled. The pressure is not malicious and it is effective: the man commits to a path because the timetable requires a commitment, and the dream is set aside because it required time the timetable did not allow.

The specific feature is the man's own sense, in later years, that he made a decision he did not actually make — that he was moved by a schedule and that the schedule belonged to other people.

**7. The loss of the person with whom the dream was shared.** The dream was a shared plan: with a friend, a brother, a wife, a mentor, a father, a business partner. Its death is a bereavement as well as a loss of plan, and it is the process that most cleanly belongs to the material of Chapter 13. Men who lose a shared dream in this way describe a specific refusal, over years, to close it: *it would be a betrayal to let it go.* The chapter addresses the position directly in Section 28.4.

### What the Seven Processes Have in Common

Four features, and they explain why this class of loss behaves as it does.

**None of them produces an event.** There is no funeral, no letter, no final conversation, and no date on which a man could say *that was the day I stopped*. The absence of an event means the loss is not registered by the man's household, by his community, or by the man himself, and it is therefore never acknowledged, which the research on loss identifies as the principal factor that turns a loss into a prolonged one.

**Each involves a decision the man would make again.** This is the distinguishing feature of the class and the reason the ordinary language of loss does not fit it. The man is not the victim of a reversal; he is the author of a sequence of decisions that were, in the terms available to him, correct. The consequence is a specific kind of stuck grief that has no villain and to which the man does not feel entitled.

**Each is compatible with a functioning life.** The dream dies while the man's other arrangements continue, which is why the loss is not visible and why the man's household is frequently surprised by its significance when it is finally mentioned.

**Each produces a comparison that operates continuously.** The man's account of what happened to the dream always includes the alternative: the man who went, the colleague who completed the training, the cousin who started the business, the version of himself who would be somewhere else. The comparison is applied automatically and it operates as the deficiency measure described in Chapter 7.
