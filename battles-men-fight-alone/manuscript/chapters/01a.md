# The Man Nobody Really Knows {.chapter num="1" short="The Man Nobody Really Knows"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Distinguish between a man's public account of his life and his private experience of it, and explain why the distance between the two is usually invisible.
2. Identify the specific social, family, and occupational reasons why men conceal their deepest struggles.
3. Describe the emotional, physical, and relational costs that accumulate when a man maintains a permanently strong appearance.
4. Recognise the particular loneliness that can accompany achievement and responsibility.
5. Explain how silence functions as a survival strategy, and distinguish the situations in which it protects from those in which it damages.

## Introduction

Consider a man who is well known in his community. He is the chairman of a cooperative society, the treasurer of a school's parent association, the person a neighbour calls at eleven o'clock at night when a child has a fever and the family car will not start. He has been married for eighteen years. He has a small business that has survived two recessions. When he walks into a room the conversation turns towards him, not because he demands it, but because his presence has become associated with steadiness. If you asked ten people who know him to describe him in a sentence, nine would use words like *reliable*, *hardworking*, *respectful*, and *strong*.

Now consider that at three o'clock in the morning, for the better part of a year, he has been lying awake with his eyes open in the dark, thinking about a debt he cannot repay, a son he cannot reach, and a sentence his father said to him when he was eleven. He has never told his wife about the debt. He has never told anyone about the sentence. He believes that to describe either would be to hand someone a weapon, or to burden them with something they did not ask to carry, or — the worst possibility of all — to invite the question he does not want to answer: *So what are you going to do about it?*

This is the man this book is about. Not the man who is visibly in trouble, whom everyone can see and, sometimes, help. The other one. The one whose difficulties are structurally invisible because everything about him is arranged to keep them that way. The man nobody really knows.

It is tempting to treat this man as a special case, somebody exceptional and unfortunate, whose circumstances are unusual. The evidence points the other way. Wherever researchers have asked men directly — in health surveys, in studies of fatherhood, in occupational research, in studies of bereavement and divorce — a substantial proportion report that they have no one with whom they can discuss their fears, that they have never told anyone about their most difficult experiences, and that they would not raise a serious personal problem with family or friends. The pattern is not confined to one country, one class, or one culture. It appears among men who are poor and men who are rich, among men with university degrees and men without, among men who live in extended families where there is always somebody in the house and men who live alone.

The purpose of this chapter is to describe that pattern clearly and to begin dismantling the assumption that sustains it: the assumption that a man's difficulties are his private business in a way that makes sharing them inappropriate, unnecessary, or shameful. Later chapters will deal with the particular battles — of the mind, of money, of love, of family, of identity, and of loss. This chapter deals with the mechanism that keeps all of them hidden.

## 1.1 The Public Image and the Private Reality

Almost every adult man in a position of responsibility manages two accounts of his life. There is the **public account** — what is visible, what is discussed, what is presented — and there is the **private account** — what is actually happening, what is feared, what is concealed. The two accounts are not merely different in content. They are different in kind.

The public account is organised around function. It records responsibilities met: fees paid, salaries distributed, problems resolved, decisions taken, hospital bills covered, weddings attended, disputes settled. It is presented in the language of outcomes, and it is largely verifiable. Other people can see much of it. This is why a man's public account is credible: it is not a boast, it is a record.

| Dimension | What the public account records | What the private account contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confidence | Decisions taken without hesitation | Uncertainty, dread, and rehearsed dread |
| Marriage | Stability, shared milestones, visible respect | Loneliness inside the marriage; unspoken disappointments |
| Money | Payments made, obligations met | Debt, arrears, borrowed money, fear of exposure |
| Health | Absence from hospital, no complaint made | Poor sleep, headaches, chest tightness, drinking |
| Grief and loss | Attendance at funerals; practical support given | Unfinished mourning; dreams; things unsaid |
| Self-regard | Titles, respect, the trust of others | The sense of being a fraud; shame about the past |

The private account is organised around experience. It records what it feels like to be the man who does all of that. It contains the arithmetic he does at night — what is left after the fees, the rent, and the transport; whether the contract will be renewed; whether his wife's silences mean what he suspects they mean; whether the tightness in his chest is ordinary tiredness or something else. It contains memories and fears that may have nothing to do with the present at all: an early failure, a father's verdict, a betrayal he never resolved.

The critical fact is that the two accounts can coexist indefinitely, because they are addressed to different audiences, and one of those audiences is entirely internal. A man can maintain a functioning public account while his private account is deteriorating, and the people around him will have no basis on which to notice — not because they are inattentive, but because the information is not available to them. This is what makes the situation of such a man so dangerous. His distress is not hidden behind a lie. It is hidden behind the truth. Everything everyone sees is accurate. It is simply incomplete.

### Why the Two Accounts Are Rarely Compared

It is worth asking why nobody ever audits the gap. There are four reasons, and each of them is ordinary rather than malicious.

**First, the public account is unusually convincing.** A man who is providing, showing up, and solving problems generates continuous evidence that he is managing. The evidence is real. The conclusion drawn from it — *he is fine* — feels reasonable and is rarely questioned.

**Second, enquiry is socially expensive.** To ask a capable man whether he is all right is to imply that he may not be. Many people, including people who love him, have learned that this implication is resented, particularly when it is repeated. So they ask once, receive the standard reply — *I'm managing* — and conclude that further enquiry would be intrusive.

**Third, there is usually no occasion.** Family life and work life are full of logistics. Conversations are conducted in the intervals between tasks, and the intervals are short. Deep disclosure needs time, privacy, and an absence of agenda, and those conditions are increasingly rare in the lives of men who carry the most.

**Fourth, and most importantly, the man himself does not compare the accounts.** A person who is living a divided life does not experience it as a division. He experiences it as normal. The practice of presenting calm while feeling otherwise is so habitual, and so validated by the response it produces, that it no longer registers as concealment. When men first complete the self-assessment in Appendix A, they are frequently surprised by the size of the gap they have just written out in their own handwriting. Until that moment, they had no picture of it.

::: keypoint
The problem is not that a man lies about his life. It is that he reports only the part of it that can be verified, and everyone, including him, treats that part as the whole.
:::

## 1.2 Why Men Conceal Their Deepest Struggles

If concealment were simply a bad habit, the solution would be straightforward: decide to be more open. The reason this advice fails so often is that concealment is not a mood. It is a calculation, made repeatedly, on the basis of real incentives. Before a man can change the calculation, he needs to see what it contains. There are six principal reasons men give, and they are not irrational.

### Concealment Protects Position

In most societies a man's standing carries an implicit requirement of adequacy. The requirement may be expressed in relation to income, employment, decisiveness, physical capability, family authority, or the ability to assist others. To disclose a serious personal problem is to introduce information that may be read as evidence of inadequacy in one of these areas, and the reading is not always wrong. Men report concrete consequences from disclosure: they have lost contracts, been passed over for promotion, had their authority questioned at home, been mocked by peers, or been told that their difficulties were a sign that something was wrong with them as men. The fear of such consequences is not paranoia. It is a reasonable reading of the environment in which many men work and live.

### Concealment Protects Other People

This is the reason that is most often overlooked, because it sounds like an excuse and is usually sincere. A man does not only conceal his struggles from those he distrusts; he conceals them most carefully from those he loves. He does not want to frighten his wife, worry his mother, destabilise his children, or add to the burdens of a friend who has problems of his own. He has watched what happens when bad news enters a household: the atmosphere changes, everyone watches everyone, and the problem multiplies because it is now shared. In his assessment, silence is the kinder option.

The logic contains an error, but it is a generous error. It assumes that the people who love him would prefer to be protected rather than included, and that his difficulty is a burden rather than a fact of a shared life. Both assumptions deserve examination, and the later chapters on marriage and fatherhood examine them at length. For now it is enough to note that this is not dishonesty. It is a mistaken theory of care, held by men who are trying to be careful.

### Concealment Protects the Concealer

There is a third function, which men rarely name aloud: not speaking prevents a man from having to encounter, fully, what is happening to him. As long as a difficulty remains unnamed, it remains provisional. It can still be managed, still be temporary, still be something that will resolve itself. To describe it to another person is to make it real, public, and — in his perception — permanent. Avoidance is not merely a strategy of concealment. It is also a strategy against knowledge, and it is a common one. Many men report that the hardest part of disclosure was not the reaction of the listener but the moment of hearing their own voice say the sentence.

### Concealment Is a Learned Competence

Men are not born knowing how to appear calm when frightened. It is a skill that is trained, in small increments, over years. A boy who is laughed at for crying by older boys learns to move the crying out of sight. A young man who is told, at work, to solve the problem rather than explain it learns to present solutions rather than difficulties. A married man who discovers that his expression of fear produces a conflict in the household learns to express something else instead. Each lesson is followed by a reward, and rewarded behaviour tends to repeat. By his thirties the man is performing the skill unconsciously, at a high level of competence, and it takes deliberate effort even to notice the habit, let alone interrupt it.

### Concealment Is Enforced by Absence of a Language

Many men who wish to speak cannot, because they do not have the words. This may sound trivial. It is not. An emotional vocabulary is learned through use and through modelling, and its absence produces a specific, documented experience: knowing that something is wrong without being able to say what it is. This produces an additional layer of distress. A man in this position may attempt disclosure and fail at the first sentence — *I don't know how to explain this* — and interpret the failure as proof that nothing can be done. Chapter 3 returns to this problem in detail.

### Concealment Is Enforced by Absence of a Confidant

Finally, a significant proportion of men have nobody with whom disclosure is realistically possible. Friendships have thinned; the ones that remain are organised around activities rather than confidences; relatives are too close to the situation; colleagues are competitors; a wife may be the person from whom he most needs to protect the information, either because it concerns her or because he believes she cannot bear it. In such a situation, a man's silence may be less a personal decision than a statement about his social position. Appendix J is addressed to this problem and treats the building of friendships as a practical skill rather than a matter of luck.

::: story
**Emeka, forty-four, is a service manager at a large vehicle dealership.** He has spent six months managing a situation that no one at work or at home knows about. His mother, who lives four hours away, has a progressive illness, and the costs of her care have absorbed his savings and pushed him into a loan that will take years to repay. His supervisors regard him as unflappable; his wife has asked, twice, whether something is disturbing him, and on both occasions he said he was only tired. When a colleague suggested that he looked stressed, his response was immediate and almost automatic: *I'm fine. I just haven't been sleeping well.*

Emeka's concealment is not a single decision but a series of them, made at the kitchen table, at the bank counter, on the phone with his brother, and in the car park before he goes into the house. Each separate decision seems small and defensible: not to mention the cost to his wife tonight because she has a difficult week at work; not to ask his sister for help because her own finances are precarious; not to take the employer's assistance programme because somebody in human resources might notice. Over six months, the small decisions have produced a large and entirely invisible problem, and — Emeka has begun to realise — a marriage in which the most important ongoing event in the family's life is known to one person only.

This is a composite illustration, and it represents a widely reported pattern: the man who is managing something serious alone, not because he is indifferent to those around him, but because each individual act of concealment appears, in isolation, to be considerate.
:::
