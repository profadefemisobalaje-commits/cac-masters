## 24.5 Reconstructing a Self After Rupture

The reconstruction is conducted in six stages. The order matters, and men who attempt the later stages without the earlier ones report that the activity is undertaken without conviction and is abandoned within months.

### Stage One: The Assessment

**The medical assessment.** Before anything else. The conditions listed in Section 24.3 — thyroid function, anaemia, sleep apnoea, testosterone deficiency, the effects of medication, and depressive illness — should be assessed by a physician. The assessment is not a formality and it should not be deferred, because a substantial proportion of men in this condition have a treatable component and because the reconstruction will not hold on top of an untreated condition.

**The functional assessment.** A written record of the man's actual day for two weeks: the hours, the activities, the contacts, the states. Its value is specific: men in the empty season describe their days inaccurately, and the record frequently shows that a substantial proportion of the time is occupied by activity of no value that has not been chosen, which is a finding that produces action.

**The role inventory.** A written table of every role the man holds, with three columns: the role, what it supplies, and whether it depends on the roles that have been lost. The output identifies exactly what is missing and what remains.

### Stage Two: The Grief

The losses must be grieved, and the work is the same as in Chapter 13: the written account of what was lost, the identification of what is permanent and what is addressable, and the permission for the material to be present. Men in this condition frequently resist the stage on the grounds that the loss was voluntary or favourable — a retirement, a child's success, a business sold — and the resistance is the reason the material remains.

The practical form for this stage is a letter written to the lost role or to the period, in which the man states what it gave him, what he gave it, what it cost him, and what he would have done differently. The letter is not sent. The exercise is used widely in loss and transition work, and men report that its effect is disproportionate to its apparent simplicity.

### Stage Three: The Materials

The man identifies the materials from Section 24.4 that are available to him, and selects one activity, one relationship, and one contribution. The selection should be made on the following criteria.

- **Available now.** Not requiring resources, permission, or a change of circumstances.
- **Within the man's existing competence or his willingness to begin as a beginner.**
- **Not attached to the roles that were lost.** The activity of the retired accountant should not be an accounting practice; the activity of the man whose children have left should not be the management of their affairs from a distance.
- **Sufficiently small to begin this month.** The scale is the most common reason that the reconstruction fails: the man selects a project of the size of his former occupations.

### Stage Four: The Arrangement

Each material is converted into an arrangement with a schedule, a place, and, where relevant, other people. The properties required are those established elsewhere in this book: a fixed time, a fixed place, a defined unit of activity, and a review point.

The specific caution for this stage is that the arrangement must be the man's rather than the household's. A retired man who fills his days with the household's requirements has exchanged one role for another of the same type, and the reconstruction requires at least one arrangement in which the man is acting on his own account.

### Stage Five: The Structure of the Day

The man builds a daily structure that is his own, from the base up. The structure should include three elements, and their order in the day reflects the evidence on mood and functioning.

1. **A fixed waking time and a morning activity.** The evidence on the effect of morning light exposure, physical activity, and consistent waking time on mood and sleep is substantial, and the morning is the period in which the structure is most easily established.
2. **One scheduled activity with a purpose.** The contribution, the skill, the work, whether paid or not.
3. **One scheduled activity that is the man's own.** The interest, the practice, the relationship.
The remaining time is left unplanned, which is the element that men find most difficult and which becomes manageable after the three elements are established.

### Stage Six: The Re-Entry Into Relationship

The final stage, and the one that determines whether the reconstruction holds. The man's withdrawal during the empty season has damaged his relationships, and the repair requires that he resume participation in the settings from which he withdrew: the family gatherings, the community's occasions, the association's meetings, the friendships that were allowed to lapse.

The practical form is the material of Chapter 4 and Chapter 27: three relationships re-established, each with a specific arrangement rather than a general intention. The specific finding for men in this condition is that the re-entry is frequently welcomed more than the man expects, because his withdrawal was read as his preference rather than as a symptom, and because the people who knew him before the rupture are frequently the only people who can reflect the parts of him that the roles did not supply.

## 24.6 Finding Purpose in the Second Half of Life

The final substantive section addresses purpose, and it does so without the vocabulary that men distrust. Purpose, in the sense used here, is not an elevated concept; it is the answer to the question of what the man's remaining time is for, expressed in a form that produces a decision.

### The Four Requirements

**1. It must be specific.** *To be useful* is not a purpose. *To teach the bookkeeping element of the association's certificate course, on Tuesdays, for two years* is a purpose, and its specificity is what allows it to be conducted.

**2. It must be the man's.** Chapter 11's test applies: is this mine, or is it the expectation of my family, my community, my former profession, or my children? Purposes that are assembled from other people's expectations survive until the first difficulty and then dissolve.

**3. It must be capable of being concluded.** A purpose that cannot be completed is an obligation. The capacity to conclude is what distinguishes a purpose from a role, and it is the property that makes the second half of life manageable: the man is able to take on substantial work because he retains the capacity to put it down.

**4. It must be compatible with the man's actual condition.** A purpose requiring physical capacity that the man does not have will produce a further account of his own inadequacy. The purpose should be calibrated to what the man can do at the present time, with the expectation that the calibration changes.

### The Four Domains

A workable purpose for the second half of life is generally composed from four domains, and a man should hold at least two of them.

**The relational.** The arrangements with the household and the people who matter: the specification in Section 21.6, the obligations of contact in Section 17.6, the friendships of Chapter 4. The distinguishing property of this domain is that its value is not measurable and its effects are cumulative.

**The contributive.** What the man does for a group, a place, an institution, or a person outside his household, on terms he has set. Its function is the supply of usefulness without a role, and its scale should be deliberately modest.

**The developmental.** What the man intends to learn, make, or understand. Its function is the supply of the future: a man with a subject he is pursuing has an answer to the question of what comes next that does not depend on his capacity to engage the world economically.

**The receptive.** What the man accepts: care, company, assistance, treatment, and rest. This domain is the one this book has placed at the centre of the whole of its argument, and in this context it is the domain that protects the reconstruction, because a man who cannot receive will rebuild an identity of the same type and will fail at the next rupture.

### The Practical Instruction

Three specific measures, for the man who is starting from the position described in Section 24.3.

**Write the purpose in one sentence, in the form of an arrangement.** The date, the unit, the frequency, the review point. A purpose that cannot be written in this form is a wish.

**Report it to one person who is not a member of the household.** The disclosure performs the same function as the accountability arrangement in Chapter 22: it converts the purpose from a private intention into a fact, and it supplies the correction that a man's own assessment cannot provide.

**Establish the review, and expect that the purpose will change.** The review's questions: is this still mine; is it producing anything; what would I change; and what would I take on if I concluded this? The expectation that the purpose will change over a decade is what distinguishes this work from the roles that preceded it, and it is the reason the work is manageable after sixty.

### What to Expect

The final expectation to manage is the timing. The reconstruction does not produce the state the man had before the loss; men report, with consistency, that the second half of life is a different arrangement rather than a restoration. What they describe, when the work has been done, is not a return to a previous condition but three specific elements: a day with a shape, a small number of people who know them, and an answer to the question of what the present period is for. That answer is the whole of the objective, and it is available to a man of seventy with a failing body as readily as to a man of fifty with a failed business.
