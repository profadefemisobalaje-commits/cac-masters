## 5.3 Persistent Worry and Intrusive Thoughts

Anxiety is not only a feeling. It is a pattern of thinking, and the pattern has a structure that can be observed, described, and — in many cases — altered. This section deals with the two principal thinking patterns that men report: persistent worry, which is voluntary and repetitive, and intrusive thoughts, which are involuntary and frequently shocking.

### Persistent Worry

Worry is thinking about a possible future difficulty. In its ordinary form it is useful: it identifies risks and prompts preparation. In its persistent form it has three distinguishing features that will be immediately recognisable to any man who has lain awake at three in the morning.

**It is repetitive rather than progressive.** Useful worry produces a conclusion — *I will speak to the supplier on Monday* — and then stops. Persistent worry circles. The same scenario is rehearsed with minor variations, forty times, without producing a decision or a plan. The man is aware of this and finds it humiliating; he also cannot stop, because stopping would require him to tolerate the uncertainty, and the uncertainty is the thing he cannot tolerate.

**It addresses matters that are not presently resolvable.** Persistent worry attaches to situations that are unresolved and cannot be resolved tonight: whether the business will recover; whether a child will complete school; whether a marriage will survive; whether a diagnosis will be confirmed. Since the matter cannot be settled, the thinking does not end.

**It substitutes for action, and it prevents action.** This is the paradox at the centre of the pattern. The man believes that his worry is a form of engagement — that he is working on the problem. In practice, the worry consumes the attention that would be needed to act, and the absence of action increases the worry. The loop is closed and self-sustaining.

The domains of worry that men report are strikingly consistent across countries, ages, and occupations. Figure 5.2 presents them as they are ordinarily described.

Men who examine their own worry usually discover that one or two domains account for the majority of the attention consumed. Identifying the dominant domain is useful for two reasons: it suggests the intervention (a financial problem requires advice and a plan rather than relaxation techniques, while an unfounded fear requires a psychological approach), and it makes the pattern visible, which reduces the sense of being at the mercy of an unnameable process.

### Intrusive Thoughts

Intrusive thoughts are unwanted mental events that arrive without invitation and are frequently experienced as alien, disturbing, and shameful. They occur in the vast majority of people; what varies is how much distress they cause and how much significance the person attaches to them.

Two features of intrusive thoughts in men deserve attention.

**The content is frequently violent, sexual, blasphemous, or catastrophic, and it does not reflect the man's character or intentions.** This is the most important point in the section, and it is the point about which men suffer most unnecessarily. A father who experiences a momentary image of harm coming to his child, a man who experiences an obscene thought during a solemn occasion, a man who imagines an accident while driving: these thoughts are not evidence of hidden wickedness. They are the ordinary output of a mind that generates material continuously, most of which is discarded. What makes them stick is the meaning assigned to them — *What kind of man thinks this?* — and once that meaning is assigned, the thought becomes an event that requires checking, and checking produces more of the same thought. Chapter 22 develops this mechanism, which is the engine of several common anxiety presentations.

**The secrecy is often more damaging than the thought.** Men rarely report intrusive thoughts, because the content feels shameful. The result is that a man may spend years concealing from everyone — including, in some cases, a physician — a symptom that is ordinary, well recognised, and treatable. A single sentence to a competent professional — *I have thoughts that come into my head that frighten me* — is usually enough to begin the process, and in the majority of cases the professional response is reassuring in the literal sense: the phenomenon is described, explained, and addressed with methods that work.

### The Two Practical Consequences

Two practical consequences follow from the material in this section.

First, **worry and intrusive thoughts are not character defects, and they are not a form of weakness**. They are the products of well-understood mental processes, and they are addressed with well-established methods, including cognitive behavioural approaches that have been tested extensively with exactly these problems.

Second, **the correct first step is not to attempt to stop the thoughts by force.** Attempts at suppression are, as Chapter 3 established, unreliable, and for intrusive thoughts they frequently make matters worse by increasing the significance of the thought. The effective approaches — including acceptance-based and cognitive methods — involve changing the relationship to the thought rather than commanding its absence. Chapter 8 and Appendix H provide the practical techniques; the purpose of this present chapter is to establish that the pattern is recognised, common, and treatable, and that the man experiencing it is not what his thoughts imply.

::: keypoint
Worry is a mental activity that pretends to be work. Intrusive thoughts are mental events that pretend to be character evidence. Neither claim survives examination, and both respond to treatment.
:::

## 5.4 Shame, Guilt, and Unresolved Memories

The deepest of the internal battles is not anxiety about the future. It is the presence of the past: shame, guilt, and memories that have never been resolved. This section deals with them, and the distinction between the first two is the most useful single piece of information in the chapter.

### Guilt and Shame: A Distinction That Matters

**Guilt** concerns an action: *I did something wrong.* **Shame** concerns the self: *I am something wrong.*

| Dimension | Guilt | Shame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object | A specific act | The whole self |
| Typical inner statement | "I did a bad thing" | "I am defective" |
| Effect on behaviour | Often prompts repair, apology, restitution | Prompts concealment, withdrawal, and sometimes aggression |
| Effect on relationships | Usually increases honesty when addressed | Usually destroys honesty and intimacy |
| Physical experience | Restlessness, difficulty resting until repaired | Heat, sinking, a desire to disappear |
| Response that helps | Acknowledgment, amendment, and where possible restitution | Acceptance, compassion, and a corrected self-evaluation |

Table: Guilt and shame distinguished. The distinction is one of the most practically useful in the whole of psychology for men who carry a sense of moral failure.

The practical significance is easy to state. Guilt, addressed, produces amendment and often repair. Shame produces concealment, because the object of shame is the self, and the self cannot be amended by an apology. A man who is ashamed does not want to speak; he wants to disappear. This is why shame is the emotion most powerfully implicated in the male pattern of silence: it does not merely discourage disclosure, it makes disclosure feel dangerous to identity.

A further practical point concerns the connection between shame and anger. Because shame is intolerable, it is frequently converted into anger. The man who has been publicly corrected, humiliated, or exposed does not typically feel sad; he feels furious, and the fury is a defence against the shame. This mechanism accounts for a substantial proportion of the incidents in which men respond with disproportionate hostility to relatively minor exposures — an error pointed out in a meeting, a correction from a wife in front of visitors, a joke at their expense.

### What Shame Is Usually About

It is worth recording the subjects that men carry with the greatest shame, because nearly all of them are things that other men carry too, and their disclosure to another person is almost always accompanied by the sentence *I thought I was the only one*.

- Failure to provide, especially the failure to provide for children.
- Sexual matters: inadequacy, infidelity, shame about desire, or shame about the absence of desire.
- Failure of a business, a career, or an ambition that was publicly announced.
- Treatment of a wife or children, particularly incidents of harshness, neglect, or violence.
- Conduct in youth: criminality, betrayal, cruelty, abandonment.
- Harm done to a parent, or the failure to have made peace before a death.
- Money: debt, fraud, concealment, or the loss of family property.
- Being the victim of something: abuse in childhood, exploitation, humiliation by a superior.
- A period of collapse — a hospitalisation, a drinking problem, a lost job — that was hidden from family and community.

The last two items deserve emphasis, because they represent a distinct class of shame that men almost never disclose: shame about having been harmed, and shame about having been ill. Both involve the belief that one's own weakness caused the event or that the fact of being affected is itself a defect. Both are common. Both are made considerably worse by silence.

### Unresolved Memories

The third element of this section concerns material that men carry from the past. The clinical literature on trauma and memory is large, and it is treated properly in Chapter 36; what is needed here is a practical description of how unresolved memories behave in the lives of men who have never told anyone about them.

**They persist.** Unresolved memories are not, in general, forgotten. They are stored in a form that produces a periodic return: a dream, a sudden recollection triggered by a sound or smell, a physical reaction in a similar situation, or a recurring thought at specific times of day.

**They acquire associations.** Men report avoiding specific roads, songs, rooms, relatives, or subjects, frequently without having connected the avoidance to the memory that produced it.

**They accumulate.** Later experiences tend to become attached to earlier ones, so that the emotional reaction to a present event carries the charge of several past events at once. This explains a phenomenon that men find bewildering: an overreaction to a trivial present incident that is in fact a response to a much older matter.

**They do not resolve by decision.** A man cannot decide to be finished with a memory. He can, however, complete certain work around it: naming what happened, recognising the meaning he attached to it, understanding the context, and, in some cases, obtaining professional help. This is the work of Chapters 29 and 36, and Appendix H provides structured methods.

**They are frequently associated with shame rather than guilt.** The memory of an act can be repaired by amendment, where amendment is possible. The memory of having been harmed, or of having been in a state of weakness, generally requires a correction of the meaning rather than the act — a change from *this proves something permanent about me* to *this happened to me, and it was not my fault, and it does not define my worth*. Making that correction is the central task of the healing chapters of this book.
