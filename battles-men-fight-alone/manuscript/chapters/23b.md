## 23.2 The Forms Addiction Takes in Men

The forms are numerous and the men affected are not a distinguishable type. The following account sets out the principal forms, with the features that matter practically.

### Alcohol

The most common dependence among men in most countries, and the one with the widest range of presentation. Its features are worth stating because the popular image of the dependent drinker — the man who is intoxicated daily and visibly — does not correspond to the majority of cases among men in professional and family life.

The patterns that men most frequently describe: drinking daily at a level that is not visible to others and that has increased over years; drinking concentrated in the evenings and increasing with stress; drinking in secret — before an event, at home alone, in the vehicle; drinking with the specific purpose of managing sleep, anxiety, or mood; and the pattern in which the man's drinking is entirely under control in public and entirely uncontrolled in private.

The features that indicate dependence rather than heavy use: drinking in the morning or during working hours; the experience of withdrawal symptoms — tremor, sweating, anxiety, sleeplessness — when drinking is interrupted; drinking to relieve those symptoms; unsuccessful attempts to reduce; and drinking in the presence of clear consequences, including medical advice, a spouse's ultimatum, or a professional warning.

The medical caution is important: alcohol withdrawal in a dependent man can be dangerous, and sudden cessation without medical support is unsafe in cases of heavy long-term use. Assessment should precede the attempt.

### Gambling and Betting

The pattern among men has changed substantially with the availability of mobile betting, and its features now include the man whose gambling occurs entirely within a device, in the house, in the presence of his family, without any of the visible markers of the traditional gambling situation.

Its characteristic progression: occasional betting; betting with the intention of covering other losses; concealment of the amounts; borrowing; the sale of assets or the use of funds held for another purpose; and, at the final stage, the attempt at a single large recovery, which consolidates rather than repairs the position. The financial consequences are frequently the largest of any addiction, because the mechanism permits losses that are orders of magnitude beyond the man's income.

The signs are described in Chapter 9 as financial: the concealed obligation, the separate account, the deferred disclosure, and, characteristically, a man who is unusually knowledgeable about money and unusually secretive about his own.

### Substances Other Than Alcohol

The forms vary by region and circumstance, and their practical features are consistent: a rapid progression from occasional use to dependence; a high association with a specific social environment; and a strong association with concealment, because the substance use is illegal as well as harmful in most jurisdictions. The treatment routes are comparable to those for alcohol, with the addition of medical management where dependence is established.

### Sexual Conduct

The form that men disclose last, and, in the author's experience, the form that produces the most severe shame. It includes: the compulsive use of sexual material, which typically escalates in quantity and in the extremity of content over years; pursuit of encounters outside the man's committed relationship; and use of commercial sexual services. The features that indicate a condition rather than a matter of choice: the compulsion to use at specific times or in specific emotional states; use that continues against the man's stated intentions and values; the escalation of content or risk; and the specific pattern of use followed by shame, followed by renewed use to manage the shame.

The clinical literature treats compulsive sexual behaviour as a behavioural addiction with mechanisms comparable to gambling, and the evidence on treatment is developing. What is established is that the disclosure of the pattern to a professional produces an improvement in a substantial proportion of cases, and that concealment maintains the pattern.

### Gaming, Internet Use, and Screens

The form most commonly dismissed by the man himself and by his household. Its practical features: hours of use that displace sleep, work, and family relationships; use as the principal means of managing negative feeling; irritability when prevented from use; and a specific pattern of concealment that is simpler than that of the other forms but equally effective, because the device is in the man's hand.

The clinical literature on gaming disorder has developed in recent years, and men should note that the same principles apply: use with consequences, failed attempts to reduce, concealment, and the escalation of quantity over time.

### Work

The form that is never classified as an addiction at all, and that produces many of this book's subjects. Its features: work as the principal means of managing anxiety, mood, and relationship difficulty; the inability to reduce work without distress; the displacement of sleep, family, and health; and, characteristically, the approval of the man's environment, which is the reason it is not identified. Chapter 12 addresses the pattern from the perspective of overwork and this chapter names it as a behavioural pattern with the same structure as the others.

### Other Behaviours

Shopping and expenditure; eating; risk-taking in driving and in financial speculation; and, in a form that is increasingly recognised, the pursuit of anger and conflict. In each case the structure is the same: a behaviour engaged in to manage a state, escalating over time, producing consequences, and concealed.

### Two Features Common to All Forms

**The concealment escalates faster than the behaviour.** The man's commitment to concealment increases with the behaviour's seriousness, and the concealment is frequently the more damaging of the two.

**The purpose is managing a state.** The substance or behaviour is always doing something. Identifying what it does — providing relief from anxiety, sleep, company, oblivion, a sense of competence, or a temporary removal of self-assessment — is the first step of treatment, and men who identify it report that they experience a reduction in the behaviour's hold, because the behaviour has stopped being a characteristic of their character and become a method with a purpose.

## 23.3 Why Addiction Is Concealed

Six reasons, each of which is practical and each of which the man has considered.

**The standing.** A man's position — in his household, his work, his community — is constructed from his reliability. The disclosure of dependence is assessed by the man as a threat to the whole of it, and the assessment is not unreasonable in every case. What the assessment omits is that the behaviour is frequently already known in its consequences if not its extent, and that the concealment's cost is being paid in the interim.

**The fear of the household's reaction.** Men anticipate, and frequently correctly, that disclosure will produce a crisis: the wife's ultimatum, the children's knowledge, the family's involvement. The anticipation is one of the strongest forces maintaining concealment, and it is the reason that treatment frequently begins with the man and a professional rather than with the household.

**The belief in self-management.** The belief that the matter can be handled without assistance, which is the script described in Chapter 2 operating in its most damaging form. Men frequently attempt multiple private resolutions before any treatment is sought, and each failure reduces the man's belief in his own capacity and increases the concealment.

**The specific fear of formal entry.** The belief that treatment makes the matter permanent by making it official: that the employer will learn, that the record will exist, that the household's account of the man will be fixed at this point. The belief is inaccurate in most jurisdictions and circumstances, and its accuracy varies by profession — which is a matter to be established with specific advice rather than assumed.

**The shame.** Described in Chapters 21 and 22. Its specific dynamic in addiction is the strongest of the reasons: the shame produces distress, the distress is managed by the behaviour, and the behaviour produces the shame.

**The absence of a person to tell.** A man who has no confidant has no route to disclosure. The material of Chapter 4 is a precondition of this chapter's material, and the practical implication is direct: the first intervention for a man with a concealed dependence is frequently the establishment of one confidential relationship, not with the household but with a professional or a person outside the man's immediate circle.
