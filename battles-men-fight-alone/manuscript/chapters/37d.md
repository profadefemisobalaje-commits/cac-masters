## 37.5 The Argument Against the Guilt

The counter-argument is supplied in seven parts, in the order of the statements in Section 37.4. Each is available to the reader as a position rather than as reassurance, and each is drawn from the material of the preceding chapters.

### 1. *I cannot afford to rest.*

The statement concerns money and time, and it should be examined in figures: the actual cost of the daily and weekly measures, which is generally small, against the cost of the presentations in Section 37.3, which is generally substantial and is paid in the man's income, his health care, his household's arrangements, and his organisation's results. The specific comparison available to men in the case material: the days of work lost in a year of reduced capacity, the medical costs, and the matters that became crises because nobody had the margins to address them, against the hours that the measures would have required. The finding is consistent and it is that the man who states that he cannot afford to rest has not counted the alternative.

### 2. *If I stop, everything I have built will be at risk.*

The statement is examined in three parts. First, the work that is actually at risk from the man's rest is the work that requires his physical presence, which is a minority of what men in the case material are carrying; the majority is the administration of the position, which survives an afternoon. Second, the man's reduced judgement, reduced conduct, and reduced capacity are already risk factors for everything he has built, and they are risk factors that grow. Third, the arrangement is not the absence of work but its bound: nothing in this chapter proposes that the man set the position down; it proposes that he conduct it from a position of maintained capacity, which is the arrangement of Chapter 31.5's boundary specification.

### 3. *There is nobody else to do it.*

The statement is frequently accurate and its consequence is not the continuation of the arrangement but its correction. The findings of Chapter 33 apply: the load's concentration is a structural vulnerability of the man, the family, and the organisation alike, and the development of other people's capacity is the measure that addresses the statement rather than the man's continuous labour. The specific and decisive point: an arrangement that depends on one man's continuous presence is an arrangement that will fail when that man is ill, and the man's rest is the first and smallest test of the structure's resilience.

### 4. *I will rest when the work is finished.*

The statement is examined in the terms of Chapter 33.6's first arrangement and of the case material on retirement. The condition is never satisfied: the position's requirements expand to the capacity available, and the arrangement in which rest follows the work's completion produces the pattern of the man's whole working life and the presentation, in retirement, of the material of Chapter 24. The reconstruction of the statement is the most useful thing the chapter supplies: **the work is not going to be finished; the man's capacity must therefore be maintained while the work continues.**

### 5. *This is what the position requires.*

The statement is accurate about the position and incomplete about its requirements. The position requires the man's judgement, his availability, his decision, his conduct, and his reliability; none of those requirements is met by a man whose sleep is reduced, whose patience has gone, whose judgement is degrading, and whose household is being managed around his state. The statement should be completed: *this is what the position requires of my capacity, and my capacity requires maintenance.* This is the reframing of the chapter's third commitment, and it is the argument that the men in the case material report as decisive, because it converts the measure from an indulgence into an obligation.

### 6. *My father worked every day of his life and did not complain.*

The statement is examined in three parts. First, the father's arrangement is generally accurate in the description and incomplete in the accounting, and the reader should consider what the father's state was: the man's own memory of his father's temper, his silence, his absences, his availability, his health, and his death. Second, the father's circumstances were frequently different, and the reader should test whether they were: the number of hours, the physical nature of the work, the imposition of his own position's requirements, the difference in the demands that arrive through a telephone. Third, and most importantly, the statement is a description of what the father did and not a statement of what the reader owes him: the material of Chapter 32.1's fourth source applies, and the chapter's position is that the appropriate response to a father's years of unrelieved work is not its reproduction but the acknowledgment of what it cost him.

### 7. *The work is the only thing I am good at.*

The statement is the identity source of Section 37.1 in its most complete form, and it is addressed in full by the material of Chapters 24 and 25: the measure of worth, the reconstruction of the account of himself, the arrangements of Chapter 27, and the categories of Section 37.2's last four. The specific finding for this statement is the one that the men in the case material report: the man who can do nothing but work is generally a man who has not conducted anything else for a substantial period, and the capacity for the other things is not absent but undeveloped. The test is available and it is small: one pursuit, one group, one arrangement that requires the man and is not the position.

### The Presentation of the Argument

The seven parts are supplied as a written position, and the practice material records that the argument works in the written form rather than in argument with the guilty feeling.

The method: the man writes the seven statements as he experiences them, in his own words, and writes the response to each beneath it. The responses are then available at the moment the guilt arises, which is the reason the written form is prescribed.

The specific application for the man who does not accept the argument at all: the exercise should begin with the accounting rather than with the argument. A man who cannot accept that rest is legitimate can generally accept that the presentations of Section 37.3 have costs and that his household requires his capacity. The argument can be conducted through the material of the household, and the finding is that the position, once adopted for the household's sake, becomes the man's own.

## 37.6 Building Rest Into the Arrangement

The practical section. Its requirement is that rest be constructed rather than requested, and the arrangement has six components.

### 1. The Sleep Specification

**What it is.** The man's specification of the hours, the arrangement, and the conditions: the time of going to bed and of rising, the room's conditions, the reduction of the screens in the period before sleep, the removal of the work material from the room, the alcohol, the caffeine, and the treatment of any disturbance of which the man is aware.

**Why it is first.** Sleep is the single largest component of restoration, and its correction is generally the largest available improvement. The research on the effects of an insufficient sleep period on judgement, mood, and health is the strongest evidence in this chapter.

**The form.** Written, with times, and given to the household, since the household's arrangements are frequently involved. Where the sleep has been disturbed for months, or where the disturbance includes early waking with the material of Chapter 34.4, the general practitioner is the route.

### 2. The Daily Measure

**What it is.** One measure conducted daily, of the categories in Section 37.2, of a duration the man will actually maintain. The practice material's recommendation is physical recovery in the first instance, because its effect is the largest and its form is the easiest for men to maintain: the walk, the exercise, the cycle, the swim, conducted at a fixed time.

**The form.** Fixed rather than discretionary, and stated as part of the position's requirements. The finding on the arrangement is the same as Chapter 30.6's: the fixed time is what survives the periods in which the man does not wish to conduct it.

### 3. The Weekly Specification

**What it is.** The specification of the man's non-working period: the day or the part of it, the period's protection, and the conditions. The forms vary — the Sunday, the Saturday, the Friday evening, the two evenings in the week — and the requirement is that the specification be written, that it be given to the household and, where relevant, to the organisation, and that the man's availability during the period be defined.

**The specific difficulty.** The telephone. The chapter records the finding plainly: the man who is physically away from the work and available by telephone has not rested, and the weekly specification's practical content is generally the telephone's arrangement: the hours, the alternatives, the person who holds the position in the period, and the exceptions.

### 4. The Annual Specification

**What it is.** The leave, taken, in blocks of a length the research supports, and taken with the arrangements in place: the person who holds the position, the telephone's arrangement, the man's own measures, and the household.

**The finding men report.** The leave taken in the absence of daily and weekly measures produces little restoration, and the leave taken with them produces considerably more. The specific instruction is the one from Section 37.2: the holiday is the least effective of the measures when it stands alone.

### 5. The Boundary and the Position's Specification

**What it is.** The written boundary of Chapter 31.5's fourth arrangement, and the material on the household's arrangements in Chapters 18 and 31.6: the hours, the days, the availability, the travel, the household arrangements that will not be moved, and the conditions under which the specification is revised.

**Why it is required.** The environment will not set the man's rest; the position's requirements will expand; the household is generally reluctant to make demands on a man whose position is understood to be demanding. The boundary is the man's own obligation and its absence is his own decision.

### 6. The Stated Arrangement With the Household

**What it is.** The man's statement to his household, in the form used elsewhere in this book: what he is going to do, why, what it changes, what it does not change, and what he requires. The specific content for this chapter: the measures, the times, the telephone's arrangement, and the request that the household support the arrangement rather than respond to it.

**Why it is required.** Two findings from the case material. First, the household frequently responds to a man's rest with the requirements that his availability had previously supplied, since the household's arrangements have been constructed around his presence whenever he was present. The statement is what allows the household to plan. Second, the household's support is one of the strongest determinants of whether the arrangement holds: the man who is reminded of the arrangement by somebody else maintains it considerably more reliably than the man who relies on his own intention.

## 37.7 Illustrative Case: The Man Who Worked Every Day for Nine Years

::: casebox
**Composite case.** Constructed for teaching purposes from patterns reported in occupational health, primary care, and family practice.

**Bashir is fifty-two, a doctor in private general practice in a state capital, married to Halima for twenty-six years, with four children between the ages of fifteen and twenty-four.**

Bashir's practice had grown from a single consulting room in 2008 to a three-doctor clinic with a laboratory and a small inpatient facility. His working pattern, which he described as having developed without a decision, was as follows: consultations from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. on six days, the clinic's administration on Sunday mornings, telephone availability at all hours, and — the element he identified as the significant one — the assumption among his patients that he would attend personally to any matter of difficulty, including deliveries, emergencies, and the hospital admissions of families who had attended the clinic for years.

His account of the nine years: *I did not decide to work every day. There is a difference between deciding something and not stopping. In the first year I was building the practice. In the third year I had four staff and I was still there at ten in the evening because the patients asked for me and I could not send them to somebody else. In the ninth year my wife said to me that she had counted and that I had not been in the house for a full day since 2017, and I checked and she was right.*

The presentations by the ninth year were six, and they were the ones recorded in Section 37.3: the sleep, which was five to six hours with the telephone beside the bed; the blood pressure, for which he had been taking medication for four years and which was not controlled; the weight; the alcohol, which had become a nightly pattern of three to four units; the flatness, described by him as *I have not been interested in anything except the clinic for about three years*; and the conduct at home, described by Halima in the family consultation: *He is not a harsh man. He is a wall. He answers questions. He does not have a conversation. My daughter asked him in December about her university choices and he answered in three sentences and he was looking at his phone and she did not ask him anything again.*

Two matters brought the case into a service. The first was a medical one: Bashir attended a hospital's emergency department with chest pain at the age of fifty-two, was investigated, and was found to have no cardiac event. The consultant's own account, recorded with permission in the case: *He came in at two in the morning from the clinic, he had a raised blood pressure and a normal cardiac workup, and he told me he had last taken a full day away from his practice in 2017. He was sitting in the department at two in the morning planning to be at work by eight. I asked him what would happen to his patients if he died in the next month and he did not answer me.*

The second matter was Halima's decision, taken with the eldest two children, to present Bashir with a written statement signed by the four children and by her, which he produced in the case. Its central passage: *You are the best doctor in this area and you are not in this house. We are not asking you to close the clinic. We are asking you to be in this family for the years that are left, and you are fifty-two years old and you have not yet been present at a meal that lasted an hour.*

The arrangements Bashir constructed over the following fourteen months had six components and he identified the sequence as the reason they held.

**The medical and the boundary.** From the hospital's referral, Bashir engaged a physician and a counselling arrangement for the blood pressure, the alcohol, and his own position. The first decision, taken in the second month, was the clinic's specification: two additional doctors engaged over the following year, a defined consultation period ending at 6 p.m., a rota for out-of-hours cover, and a rule that the telephone beyond the rota went to the practice's nurse.

His account of the first three months: *I had believed that the patients would leave. About forty left and about two hundred stayed and the practice's income was down by eleven per cent in the first year. And in the fourth month I slept for seven hours for the first time since 2016 and I understood what I had been trading.*

**The sleep.** The sleep specification, written: the telephone removed from the bedroom, the hours, the alcohol reduced to occasional use, the caffeine, and the treatment of the blood pressure. He described the sleep as the change with the largest effect: *The first three weeks were the strangest. I had been working for nine years with a battery at about thirty per cent and I did not know it, because it had been thirty per cent for so long that I thought that was the level.*

**The daily measure.** Bashir took up walking, at 6.30 in the morning, for about forty minutes. He identified the measure's function as not primarily physical: *It is the only forty minutes in the day in which nobody requires anything from me and I am not available. I have not missed a morning in fourteen months, including the days I did not want to. The first morning I went out and I did not know where to walk, because I had not been anywhere in my own neighbourhood except the clinic and the house in nine years.*

**The weekly and the annual specifications.** Saturday was made a non-working day, with the clinic's rota and the nurse's telephone, and a fortnight's leave was taken with the family in the following year. Bashir described the household's response in the first weeks of the Saturday arrangement: *They did not know what to do with me. My youngest asked me whether I was sick. My wife had been organising the house for nine years around my absence and she had a routine and I was in it. It took about six weeks before the house was normal on a Saturday and about three months before the Saturday was ordinary to me.*

**The stated arrangement with the household.** Bashir conducted the household meeting in the third month, in the form of Section 37.6's sixth component, including the four children. He described the meeting: *I told them what the clinic was, what I had been doing for nine years, what the specification was, and what the Saturday was. My second son, who is twenty-one, asked me whether I was doing it for them or for myself. I said that I was doing it because I had been told by a consultant at two in the morning that I might not have a choice in the matter if I did not, and because I wanted to be in the house for the years that are left. He said, then you should say that sentence at the start of the meeting rather than the end.*

**The position's arrangements.** Bashir established two: a group of five medical practitioners which met monthly, and a period on the association's continuing education committee, which he described as *the first thing I have done in nine years that was not my clinic.* He described the group's function: *I have been the doctor for two thousand people and I have not had a doctor. In that group I am one of five men with the same practice and the same problems, and I have said things in that room that I have never said to anybody.*

Bashir's summary, fourteen months after the emergency department, was: *I went to the hospital at two in the morning with a normal heart and a blood pressure that I had not controlled for four years, and a consultant asked me what would happen to my patients if I died in the next month and I could not answer him. I could not answer him because I had not been able to think about it, and the reason I had not been able to think about it was that I had been working for nine years without a day. The practice lost eleven per cent of its income and the patients who left were the ones who wanted a doctor available at two in the morning, and what I have instead is two additional doctors, two hundred patients who stayed, a Saturday, a morning walk at half past six, and a wife who speaks to me at the table. My daughter asked me about the university in April and we talked for an hour and a half. That is the whole of the accounting.*
:::

Five features of this case deserve isolation.

**The pattern developed without a decision, and the man's own account identified the mechanism precisely.** *I did not decide to work every day. There is a difference between deciding something and not stopping.* This is Section 37.1's second form — the prohibition on stopping — and the case is included because the absence of a decision is what makes the arrangement resistant to correction: there is no moment at which the man can identify having chosen.

**The man's capacity had operated at a reduced level for long enough that the reduced level was his normal.** *A battery at about thirty per cent, and it had been thirty per cent for so long that I thought that was the level.* This is the research finding of Section 37.3 — that the affected person's assessment of their own capacity is the measure most affected — expressed in the man's own terms, and the case is included because it is the finding that most frequently changes the reader's understanding of his own position.

**The household had constructed its arrangements around the man's absence, and his presence initially disrupted them.** The house's routine, the youngest child's question about whether he was sick, the six weeks before the house was normal on a Saturday. The case is included because men anticipate that their rest will be welcomed and are unprepared for the household's response when their availability changes the arrangements that the household has built in its absence.

**The presentation was a medical one, and the practitioner's question was the entry point.** The chest pain, the uncontrolled blood pressure, the two-in-the-morning attendance, and the consultant's question about what would happen to the patients. The case is repeated from Chapter 32.7 and Chapter 35.7 because the mechanism is the same and because it is the most frequently available route for men in this position: the physical presentation is the form in which the man arrives, and the question about a third party is the form in which the position becomes visible to him.

**The arrangements were six and they were established in sequence.** The medical and the clinic's specification first, because the position's requirements had to change before the man's hours could; the sleep second, because it produced the largest and the earliest change; the daily and the weekly measures next; the household's arrangement at the third month, with the meeting's form including the man's own reason rather than the household's request; and the position's arrangements last. The sequence is the one recommended in Section 37.6, and the man's own account of it — the Saturday ordinary after three months, the walk unmissed in fourteen — is the practical evidence that the arrangement is maintainable.
