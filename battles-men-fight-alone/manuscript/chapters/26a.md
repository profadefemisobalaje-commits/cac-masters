# The Betrayal of Friends and Brothers {.chapter num="26" short="The Betrayal of Friends and Brothers"}

::: objectives
After reading this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the friendships men build in adult life, identify what they rely on, and assess the strength of his own arrangements.
2. Explain what occurs when a friend breaks faith, and identify the specific features that distinguish betrayal by a friend from other losses.
3. Analyse the betrayals that occur between brothers and within families, including their causes, their forms, and their consequences for the family system.
4. Describe the anatomy of a betrayed friendship, including the stages through which it passes and the point at which repair remains possible.
5. Draw lessons from a betrayal without converting them into a general suspicion of other men.
6. Apply a method for discerning friendship, and use the exercise at the end of this chapter to audit and rebuild his own arrangements.
:::

## Introduction

A man's friendships occupy a position in his life that is difficult to describe to anybody who does not hold them. They are not discussed in the way that marriage and fatherhood are discussed, and they are frequently not named as important even by the men who rely on them. Yet the evidence is consistent: men with close friendships report better mental health, recover faster from losses, live longer, and describe their lives in terms that the men without them do not use. And the loss of a friendship, by betrayal, by drift, or by death, produces a grief that men frequently cannot identify as grief, because the vocabulary available for it is used for other relationships.

This chapter addresses the specific form of the loss described in its title: the betrayal of friends and brothers.

The subject is distinct from the material in Chapter 16, which examined rejection and betrayal generally, and it is distinct from the material in Chapter 27, which addresses the condition of having nobody to call. This chapter concerns the middle case: a man who had friendships, who relied on them in specific and unexamined ways, and who discovered that one of them would not hold.

Its approach has three commitments, and they should be stated at the outset.

The first is that betrayal by a friend or a brother is a real loss and should be treated as one. The material is frequently dismissed — by the man, by his household, and by the wider culture — with the observation that friendships end and that a man should not be sentimental about them. The dismissal prevents the grief and the analysis, with the results described in Chapter 24.

The second is that the betrayal is frequently the product of an arrangement that both parties maintained, and not of a single person's wickedness. Men's friendships are frequently built on an unstated set of assumptions about what each party will provide, defend, and keep silent about, and those assumptions are tested only in the circumstances described in the following sections. Many betrayals are the failure of an arrangement that was never agreed.

The third is that the chapter's objective is the reconstruction of the man's capacity to rely on other men, and not the establishment of a general suspicion. A man who concludes from a betrayal that men cannot be relied upon has accepted a proposition that is both false and expensive, and he will conduct the remainder of his life on it, as Section 26.5 describes.

## 26.1 The Friendships Men Build and What They Rely On

Male friendship has an architecture that differs from female friendship in ways that are documented and that determine what a betrayal costs. Before examining the betrayal, the architecture must be described, because the betrayal is generally a failure at one of its load-bearing points.

### The Four Functions

Men's friendships supply four functions, and a man's arrangements may supply some and not others.

**1. Company and activity.** The football, the club, the drinking group, the market, the two men who repair vehicles together on Saturdays. This function is the most common, and it is the one that men most readily recognise as friendship.

**2. Practical assistance.** The contacts, the introductions, the small loans, the help with a vehicle, the place to stay, the intervention in a bureaucratic matter. This function is transactional and it is essential, and it is the one men most frequently rely upon while denying that they rely upon it.

**3. Standing and membership.** The group that recognises a man, that has known him for years, that supplies his position in a social world that is not his household or his workplace. This function is the subject of Chapter 27's material on groups.

**4. Disclosure.** The function that men most frequently lack, and the one this book has repeatedly identified: one or more men with whom the man's actual position is known, and to whom he may speak about matters that are not practical. The absence of this function is the central finding of Part I, and its presence is the strongest single predictor of men's resilience to the losses described in this book.

### The Unstated Terms

Every friendship operates on terms, and men's friendships operate on terms that are frequently never stated. Six are common, and the betrayal described later in this chapter is generally the failure of one of them.

**Confidence.** What is said between the men remains between them. This term is universal and it is the term most frequently broken.

**Non-predation.** Neither man will pursue the other's wife, business, position, or opportunity. The term is unspoken and it is absolute, and its breach is the betrayal that men rank most severely.

**Reciprocity of effort.** The arrangement is understood to be roughly balanced over time, without accounting. Its breach produces the resentment described in Chapter 20 in a friendship rather than a household.

**Defence in absence.** The man defends the other when he is not present, and does not join in the assessment of him.

**Assistance at the moment of crisis.** The term that is tested least frequently and that carries the greatest weight when it is tested. It operates in one direction at a time and it is generally assumed rather than agreed.

**Silence about the man's failures.** The term that keeps a man's difficulty out of the community's conversation. Its breach is the mechanism of the public verdict described in Chapter 25.

### What Men Rely On Without Knowing It

Three features of the reliance are worth naming because each explains the severity of a betrayal.

**Men place a large weight on a small number of relationships.** The research on men's social networks consistently finds that men maintain fewer close relationships than women and that their networks are more dependent on specific individuals, particularly the spouse and one or two male friends. The concentration means that a single betrayal removes a large proportion of a man's support, and men frequently discover the size of what they relied on only after losing it.

**The reliance is practical as well as emotional.** A man's friend is frequently the person who supplies the intervention in a difficulty, the reference for a position, the funding for a shortfall, the assessment of a business decision. The loss of a friend is therefore also the loss of the man's access to a set of the practical capacities of his network.

**The reliance is unexamined.** Men do not audit their friendships, discuss their terms, or test their assumptions. The absence of examination is what makes the betrayal so disorienting: the man's model of the relationship contained a term that the other party's model did not, and the discovery of the divergence is frequently more damaging than the act itself.

::: keypoint
Male friendship supplies company, practical assistance, standing, and — least often — disclosure, and it operates on unstated terms concerning confidence, non-predation, reciprocity, defence in absence, assistance in crisis, and silence about failure. The severity of a betrayal is determined not by the act alone but by which term was relied upon without ever being agreed.
:::

## 26.2 When a Friend Breaks Faith

This section sets out the forms that betrayal by a friend takes, and, for each, the specific loss that follows. The forms are arranged in the order of their frequency in men's accounts, not of their severity.

### The Disclosure of a Confidence

**The act.** Material that a man gave to a friend in confidence is disclosed: to the group, to the community, to the man's family, or to the man's wife.

**The specific loss.** The man's safety in the relationship, and — in a proportion of cases that is larger than most men expect — the man's standing, where the disclosed material was the kind described in Chapter 21's private account. The severity of the loss is determined by the content rather than by the act: the disclosure of an ordinary difficulty is a breach, and the disclosure of a shameful matter is an exposure.

**The distinctive feature.** The damage is frequently invisible. The man cannot identify who knows, and his conduct changes accordingly: he avoids the group, he becomes guarded with the friend, and he is unable to state what occurred. The pattern is the material of Section 16.4.

### The Pursuit of the Man's Interest

**The act.** The friend pursues the man's opportunity, position, client, contract, or — in the most severe form — his wife or partner.

**The specific loss.** The object lost, together with the man's model of his own judgement, as described in Chapter 16. The case in Section 26.7 illustrates the form.

**The distinctive feature.** The act is frequently deniable. The friend presents the pursuit as coincidence, as an opportunity that arose independently, or as a matter in which the man's claim was never established. The denial leaves the man in the position of appearing to accuse without proof, which is the reason this betrayal frequently ends the friendship silently rather than visibly.

### The Failure of Assistance in Crisis

**The act.** The man is in difficulty — a bereavement, a financial collapse, an illness, a legal matter, a divorce — and the friend who was relied upon does not appear: no call, no presence, no assistance, and, frequently, an avoidance that becomes visible over the following weeks.

**The specific loss.** The material assistance that did not arrive, and, more significantly, the man's position: he has been left visibly alone in the moment in which the presence of a friend was the term he most relied upon.

**The distinctive feature.** This form of betrayal frequently involves no decision. It is produced by the friend's own incapacity, fear, or absorption, and it is frequently accompanied by a subsequent guilt that prevents the friend from re-establishing contact, which produces a permanent loss from an act of omission. The material for the man is the finding described in Chapter 4: the reliance was unexamined, and it was never tested before the crisis, which is why the failure arrived at the worst possible moment.

### The Financial Betrayal

**The act.** A loan not repaid; a business partnership in which the funds are diverted; a joint arrangement in which the friend's portion is not contributed; a guarantee called upon; a property arrangement in which the friend takes more than the agreement provided.

**The specific loss.** The money, together with — and this is the element that men most consistently report as the larger loss — the position of having been taken advantage of by somebody who was trusted in the one domain in which trust was assumed to be unnecessary.

**The distinctive feature.** This betrayal is frequently preceded by a period in which the man made accommodations: the delayed repayment accepted without comment, the reduced contribution overlooked, the accounting not requested. The accommodations were performed in the name of the friendship and they are the mechanism by which the loss accumulates. The material in Chapter 20 on the unspoken ledger applies: the man's account of what he has not raised is frequently the substance of what he is eventually betrayed out of.

### The Failure of Defence in Absence

**The act.** The man is being assessed, criticised, or accused in a setting in which the friend is present, and the friend does not defend him: says nothing, joins the assessment, or supplies the details that make the assessment effective.

**The specific loss.** The man's standing, which is the material of Chapters 16 and 25, together with the discovery that a term he had assumed was universal was not held by the other party.

**The distinctive feature.** The act is not a betrayal in the man's own assessment at the moment it occurs, because he is not present. The information arrives later, from a third party, and it arrives accompanied by the requirement to decide what to do with it, which men frequently resolve by not confronting the friend and by reducing the relationship silently.

### The Accumulated Betrayal

**The act.** No single act; a long pattern in which the friend takes and does not give, is present for his own requirements and absent for the man's, maintains the friendship on terms that suit him, and uses the man's resources — the vehicle, the contacts, the household's hospitality, the man's willingness — as an available supply.

**The specific loss.** The years, the resources, and the man's own account of the relationship, which is revealed to have been, from the other side, an arrangement of convenience.

**The distinctive feature.** This form produces a specific and particularly difficult injury because the man cannot identify a betrayal at all. He can identify a pattern of small events, each individually dismissing, and his conclusion — that the friendship was not what he believed it to be — cannot be stated as an accusation. It is the form most frequently reported in mid-life, and the form that most frequently produces the general conclusion described in Section 26.5.
