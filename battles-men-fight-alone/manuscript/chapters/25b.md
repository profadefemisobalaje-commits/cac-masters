## 25.1 Measuring a Man by What He Produces

The measurement of men by their results is older than any particular economy and it is maintained by six mechanisms, each of which is worth describing because each is a point at which a man can establish his position.

### The Six Mechanisms

**1. The question and its answer.** *What do you do?* The question is the primary instrument, and its power is that an answer is required immediately, in public, and without qualification. Men who are not working, whose work is informal, or whose work carries no recognised title report a specific and recurring difficulty in answering, and the difficulty is compounded by the fact that a delayed answer is itself read as an answer.

**2. The visible markers.** The housing, the vehicle, the children's institutions, the clothing, the contributions. These supply evidence whether or not a question is asked, and they are read by an environment that is practised at reading them.

**3. The comparisons conducted by others.** Men are compared by relatives, neighbours, colleagues, and their own families, frequently in their presence and frequently in the form of congratulation of somebody else. Chapter 11's material applies; the mechanism belongs here as well because the comparison is not merely a personal pressure but a public practice.

**4. The institutional records.** A man's results are recorded by institutions: the employer's file, the credit reference, the tax record, the educational certificates, the property register. These records determine what is available to him next, and their effect is that an assessment made in one period governs the man's options in another. Men who have had a business fail, a period of unemployment, or a bad credit record describe this effect precisely: the record is a verdict, and it is administered by institutions rather than by opinion.

**5. Language.** The vocabulary available for men is largely evaluative. A man is described as serious, solid, or successful; or as unserious, useless, or a failure. There is little language available for a man who is competent, generous, and productive in forms the society does not recognise.

**6. The informal consequences.** The consequences that fall outside the institutions: the invitations not extended, the introductions not made, the marriage proposals not entertained for a man's daughters, the membership not offered, the loan not advanced by a cooperative, the opportunity not mentioned. These consequences are rarely experienced as decisions, because they are not decisions; they are the ordinary operation of a system of standing.

### Why the Measurement Is Applied to Everything

A man's results determine more than his position in the economy. Three extensions are characteristic and each is damaging.

**The measurement extends to his household.** A man's children's conduct, education, and appearance are read as results, and the reading places the household's outcomes in the man's account. The material of Chapter 17 applies: the fear of failing as a father is substantially a fear of this assessment.

**It extends to his character.** Results are read as evidence about the man's qualities: a man who has not succeeded is frequently assumed to lack seriousness, intelligence, or effort, and the assumption survives the knowledge of his circumstances. Men who have lost positions in a general economic contraction report the assumption precisely: the reduction is understood as personal, because the environment has no other scale.

**It extends to his family's history.** A man's results are read as the outcome of his parents' investment and of his community's support. The reading produces a specific and heavy form of pressure in families where several members' hopes have been placed on one young man, which is the material of Chapter 19 in its adult form.

### The Countervailing Observation

One observation should be recorded, because it is true and because it moderates the analysis. The system of measurement is applied with substantial variation by setting, and the variation is important for practical purposes.

**In stable communities of long duration**, the measurement is tempered by knowledge. A community that has known a man for thirty years evaluates his conduct in relations where his results are not known: his treatment of his parents, his reliability, his conduct in a dispute, his behaviour when he had nothing. Men report that the assessment of a long-standing community is frequently more accurate and more generous than the assessment of a new environment.

**In large cities and professional settings**, the measurement is applied more sharply, because standing is determined by information that can be established quickly, which is generally results.

**In families**, the measurement is applied by the members who have least information about the man's actual position, and it is generally the relatives he sees twice a year.

The practical implication is that a man's assessment by others is not a single verdict but a set of verdicts of differing quality, and that a man who is judged to have failed is frequently being judged by the least informed of the available audiences.

::: keypoint
A man is measured by his results, and the measurement is not merely psychological: it determines what institutions extend to him, what opportunities reach him, and what his family records about him. Understanding the machinery precisely is the first step, because a man who has identified the mechanism can distinguish between the assessment that is consequential and the assessment that is merely audible.
:::

## 25.2 The Price of Being Seen as a Failure

The experience of being publicly assessed as a failure has features that distinguish it from private inadequacy, and they are worth setting out, because men in this position frequently cannot name what has occurred to them.

### The Nine Features

**1. The verdict is general.** The failure of one undertaking — a business, a marriage, a career — is read as an assessment of the man. Men describe the transition precisely: *I lost the business and became a man who had lost a business, and then I became a man who cannot do anything.*

**2. The verdict is permanent until it is contradicted by results.** Because the assessment is based on results, it cannot be altered by conduct, by explanation, or by time. Only a result alters it, which imposes the requirement described in Section 25.4.

**3. The verdict is administered socially.** It appears in the manner of others, which is the material of Chapter 16.4: the man becomes aware of a change in treatment without being told its cause, and the change is frequently more damaging than an explicit judgement.

**4. The man's own account is composed with the audience in mind.** The most consequential feature. A man who has been judged a failure constructs his account of himself to answer the judgement, and the account is therefore organised by the audience. Its content is defensive, its standard is the audience's, and its effect is that the man lives inside an assessment he did not make.

**5. The withdrawal.** As Chapter 16 establishes, the man withdraws from the settings in which the verdict circulates, which is read as a confirmation of it.

**6. The reduction of the man's own conduct.** A specific and frequently unnoticed effect: a man judged a failure reduces his ambitions, declines opportunities he would have attempted, and adjusts his household's expectations downward, in order to avoid a further verdict. The reduction is rational given the assessment and its cumulative effect over a decade is substantial.

**7. The transfer to the household.** The household experiences the verdict: the wife's treatment by her circle, the children's position among their peers, the family's standing in the community. This is frequently the element the man finds most difficult, because it is the element he cannot conceal and because it produces guilt in addition to the social injury, and because the household's response — which may be support, or may be pressure, or may be reproach — becomes part of the man's circumstances.

**8. The absence of any procedure for restoration.** This is the structural defect that the chapter addresses. A man judged by his results has no means of appeal: no forum, no procedure, no record to correct, and no mechanism by which the judgement can be replaced, except the production of results, which is not always possible.

**9. The interioration.** The final feature, and the one that determines whether the condition becomes permanent. The verdict, having been applied externally for a sufficient period, becomes the man's own assessment of himself. This is the mechanism by which a public judgement becomes a private identity, and it is the point at which the material in Chapters 8 and 21 becomes necessary.

### The Groups Most Affected

Four groups carry the verdict in specific forms.

**The man whose business has failed.** His failure is public, it is recorded, it is discussed, and, in customary settings, it is frequently accompanied by a moral interpretation concerning the man's conduct. The recovery material is in Chapter 38, and the specific burden is that the failure is treated as a completed verdict at the moment it occurs, whereas businesses ordinarily fail and recover, and the same trajectory in a man's career is treated as normal.

**The man who is unemployed or underemployed for a sustained period.** The verdict is applied immediately and it compounds: employers prefer the employed, lenders prefer the settled, and families' assessments sharpen as the period extends. Chapter 11.4 addresses the condition; the societal element is that a man in this position is being assessed for something he cannot correct by the means available to him.

**The man whose results are adequate in a form that his community does not recognise.** The man in informal trade with a substantial income; the technician whose competence exceeds his credentials; the man whose work is of high quality and low status. His difficulty is that his results are real and are not receivable as results, and that he is required to explain himself continuously.

**The man with a disability or a chronic condition.** The verdict is applied to his capacity, and it is applied by institutions and by individuals who have not assessed it. The material is in Chapter 33, and the societal element is the reduction of the man's scope to a single fact about him.
