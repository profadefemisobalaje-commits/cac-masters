## 3.7 Gender, Sexuality, Disability, and Socioeconomic Context

### Gender, power, and relational roles

Gender is socially organised and personally experienced; it shapes expectations, opportunities, safety, and distributions of unpaid and paid labour. Couples may negotiate caregiving, income, emotional work, decision-making, and public identity under explicit and implicit gender norms. The therapist should assess how these arrangements function for each person, who has freedom to refuse or renegotiate, and what consequences follow. A claim of “equal contribution” should not be accepted without exploring invisible tasks such as planning, kin coordination, emotional monitoring, and care administration.

Gender diversity should be approached without pathologising identities or assuming that a person has a particular treatment goal. Ask for names and pronouns, use them consistently, and correct errors without making the client responsible for the therapist's embarrassment. In family work involving a transgender or gender-diverse member, clarify confidentiality and safety before disclosing information to relatives. A young person's identity should not be revealed to a caregiver without consent except where a clearly applicable safeguarding or legal obligation requires a different response; the therapist must check local law and policy and discuss limits of confidentiality in advance. The clinician should assess family responses, support, rejection, and risk without treating identity itself as a symptom.

Therapists should not infer relationship roles from gender presentation, legal status, or household labels. Ask how partners define their relationship, what commitments they recognise, and how they wish to be addressed. Where services or records use binary categories, explain why information is needed, offer a way to describe identity in the person's own words, and avoid collecting details that are irrelevant. MFT's focus on relational context should not become a pretext for making one person's identity a family “problem” or requiring them to educate relatives in session.

### Sexual orientation and family diversity

Couples and families include lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, asexual, and other sexual identities, as well as people who do not use a label. The therapist should avoid heteronormative assumptions about partners, parenting, fidelity, family roles, or future plans. Ask open questions about significant relationships and chosen family. A client's decision to disclose an identity to relatives is theirs; therapy should not pressure disclosure as a universal route to authenticity or family repair. Consider how stigma and legal or social conditions affect safety, access, and trust, while recognising that clients differ in how they understand and prioritise these experiences.

For families with LGBTQ+ members, clinical work may address a particular relational rupture, ordinary life-cycle transition, or external stressor. Not every problem is caused by stigma, and not every family is in conflict about identity. Avoid both erasure (“we treat everyone the same”) and overemphasis that makes identity the only relevant dimension. Tailor questions to the family's stated concern, acknowledge discriminatory conditions where relevant, and make services visibly safe through respectful intake forms, accurate referrals, and confidentiality practices.

### Disability and neurodiversity

Disability can be understood through medical, social, and relational models. A medical account may attend to impairment and treatment; a social model highlights barriers created by inaccessible environments; relational approaches consider how disability, care, identity, and family organisation interact. These perspectives can be complementary. The clinician should ask how the person understands disability and what language they prefer; some identify as disabled, others prefer person-first language, and preferences vary. Do not presume that a diagnosis defines the person's capacity, goals, or family role.

Accessibility should be designed into practice: step-free access, clear sensory conditions, communication supports, accessible written materials, flexible pacing, and remote participation where suitable. Ask about needed accommodations rather than making assumptions. For a client who uses augmentative and alternative communication, allow enough time for responses and address the client directly. Do not treat a family member's interpretation of another person's communication as definitive; check the person's preferences and consent. Neurodivergent people may have different sensory, social, or processing needs, and therapy should adapt without framing difference as lack of motivation or empathy.

Caregiving relationships deserve careful assessment. A relative may provide essential support while also holding substantial practical power; the person receiving assistance may want greater autonomy or a different allocation of tasks. The clinician should assess caregiver stress and the disabled person's own goals, rather than treating one as the sole client and the other as a resource. When capacity or supported decision-making is relevant, follow legal standards and professional consultation, and involve the person as fully as possible.

### Age, development, and intergenerational power

Children, adolescents, and older adults are not homogeneous groups. Developmental stage, communication access, family authority, and legal status affect participation. A child may have a right to a private conversation in some settings, but the scope and limits depend on jurisdiction and circumstances. The therapist should explain confidentiality in developmentally appropriate language and avoid creating promises that cannot be kept. Parents' authority and responsibilities are important, but the young person's safety, dignity, and voice must also be represented.

Older adults may be positioned as decision-makers, dependants, caregivers, or all three at different times. Ageism can lead clinicians to discount distress, presume cognitive decline, or treat a person's preferences as subordinate to adult children's convenience. Conversely, a therapist should not overlook coercion or neglect in the name of respecting family hierarchy. Assess capacity in relation to the specific decision, use communication supports, and distinguish a family's preferred arrangement from the individual's informed choice.

### Class, work, and material resources

Socioeconomic circumstances shape access to therapy and the daily options available to families. Insecure housing, transport costs, shift work, childcare, food insecurity, debt, and limited digital access can affect attendance and stress. A therapist who interprets missed sessions as resistance may overlook the service's scheduling assumptions. A treatment plan requiring private space, internet, paid time off, or a stable home may be unrealistic. Ask about practical barriers without shaming and consider whether the service can adapt timing, location, format, or referral pathways.

Class is not only income. It includes occupation, education, social capital, neighbourhood, and familiarity with professional institutions. Clinicians may unintentionally reward clients who speak in professional language, complete forms promptly, or display emotional styles that match the service's expectations. The therapist should examine how institutional convenience is being mistaken for motivation. Where material conditions are central, relational work can still help family members coordinate, but should not substitute for advocacy or practical support when those are needed.

### Culture and safety are not competing priorities

A culturally responsive therapist respects difference while maintaining clear standards against coercion, abuse, exploitation, and discriminatory treatment. The clinician must not impose their own preferred family structure under the name of safety, but must not excuse harm as cultural practice. Risk assessment should be individualised, confidential where possible, and attentive to the person's own account of danger and available resources. If joint sessions may increase risk, individual assessment, specialist consultation, or referral may be necessary. The decision should be documented, reviewed, and explained in a way that does not blame the person at risk.

## 3.8 Adaptation, Evidence, and Organisational Practice

### Adapting a model with transparency

Intervention adaptation can involve language, examples, metaphors, session format, family participation, pacing, and the degree of directiveness. Adaptation should respond to a stated need and be discussed with participants. The clinician can distinguish the therapeutic function intended by an intervention from the form used to deliver it, but should not assume that a component is culturally neutral or universally essential. A change that improves fit for one member could intensify a power imbalance for another. Consultation and ongoing feedback help identify these effects.

When using a manualised approach, clinicians should document adaptations and consider whether they alter the model's hypothesised mechanism. Some adaptations are accommodation—such as translating materials or changing session time—while others alter intervention content. Both can be appropriate, but the consequences differ. Supervisors and researchers should record what changed, why, by whom, and with what response. Fidelity should not be equated with rigid sameness, and adaptation should not be a label that excuses unexamined practice.

### Evidence and generalisability

Treatment evidence is produced in particular populations, languages, settings, and historical periods. A statistically effective intervention may not have been tested with the family before the therapist. A small number of studies in one group does not establish universal suitability, and an absence of subgroup evidence does not prove that a treatment is ineffective for that group. Researchers should report sample composition, recruitment, attrition, adaptations, language procedures, and subgroup analyses transparently. Subgroup effects require adequate power and should not be inferred from post hoc comparisons without qualification.

Culturally responsive research asks whether outcomes were selected with affected people, whether measures function comparably, whose definitions of improvement were used, and whether the intervention was accessible. Qualitative process studies can explain how participants interpret treatment; quantitative studies can estimate reach and outcomes; mixed-methods studies can connect these findings if integration is planned. Community-engaged research can broaden relevance, but partnership requires equitable decision-making, compensation, and a clear account of data ownership and dissemination.

### A practical cultural responsiveness cycle

A clinician can use a repeatable cycle: **locate** the presenting concern within relevant identities and contexts; **ask** each member what it means and what help is wanted; **formulate** interactional and structural hypotheses; **negotiate** goals, language, participation, and adaptations; **act** with safety and access in view; and **review** the effect with each participant. The cycle is not a linear checklist. New information may require returning to an earlier step. The clinician should record the rationale for significant adaptations and seek supervision when uncertainty or risk exceeds their competence.

### Hypothetical Case Illustration: unequal participation in a couple

::: casebox
**Hypothetical case — work, language, and decision-making.** A couple seeks therapy for recurring arguments about money and caregiving. One partner recently moved to the region, has limited confidence in the service's primary language, and works irregular hours; the other partner has stable employment and usually answers questions first. The therapist notices an apparent pattern of “withdrawal” by the first partner. Rather than concluding that this partner is avoidant or culturally deferential, the therapist asks about work schedules, language preference, financial access, and whether the partner wants to speak privately. A qualified interpreter is arranged for one member's preferred language; the therapist checks whether the couple wants interpretation in joint sessions or separate time. The assessment reveals that the quieter partner is managing extensive care coordination and does not feel safe disagreeing about a shared account. The therapist screens separately for coercion, discusses confidentiality limits, and postpones joint money exercises until safety is clearer. The formulation is revised from mutual miscommunication to a combination of unequal decision-making, practical strain, and a communication pattern that requires further assessment.
:::

This hypothetical example shows why a therapist should not treat equal airtime as proof of equal power or silence as a cultural trait. Language access and income affect participation, but do not determine the interpretation. Separate safety assessment is essential because “money conflict” may conceal control. The case demonstrates how culturally responsive practice modifies the pace and structure of care without assuming that a single intervention is appropriate before risks are understood.
