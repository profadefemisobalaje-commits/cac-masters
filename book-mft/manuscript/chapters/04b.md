## 4.3 Comprehensive Couple Assessment

### Multiple perspectives and the relationship timeline

A comprehensive assessment integrates each partner's account with observation of interaction, relevant history, current context, and available records or collateral information where consent and law permit. Neither a joint interview nor separate interviews alone are sufficient for every case. Joint conversation shows how partners engage each other in the present, but may inhibit disclosure. Separate conversations may improve privacy and safety, but can create challenges around information-sharing and perceived alliances. The therapist should explain the purpose and boundaries of each format, and avoid promising that a private session will remain confidential if the treatment contract does not allow it.

Start with the relationship's current concern and the path to treatment. Ask how long the difficulty has been present, what preceded the referral, what makes it better or worse, and why help is being sought now. Elicit each partner's account of key events, including where their accounts converge and diverge. The timeline may include courtship, commitment, cohabitation, marriage or other chosen milestones, parenting, illness, work transitions, relocation, caregiving, betrayal, bereavement, and previous treatment. These events do not mechanically cause current distress; they provide context for understanding the couple's meanings, expectations, and adaptations.

Assess relationship strengths as carefully as problems. Ask what the partners appreciate, how they have coped with previous adversity, what still works, and when they feel more connected or coordinated. Strengths should be described concretely rather than used to minimise harm. A history of mutual support does not negate a current safety concern, just as severe conflict does not erase a couple's capacities. Elicit the social network, extended-family relationships, community resources, and practical constraints that may support or limit change.

### Domains to assess

A systematic assessment can consider the following domains, adjusting depth to the referral question and clinical need:

- **Presenting problems and priorities:** distress, relationship satisfaction, recurring disputes, individual concerns, and each partner's preferred outcomes.
- **Interactional process:** how disagreement begins, what each partner notices, emotional and behavioural responses, escalation or withdrawal, repair attempts, and the consequences of the sequence.
- **Commitment and decision status:** hopes for change, ambivalence, possible separation, and any external pressure to attend.
- **Attachment, development, and learning history:** experiences that may shape expectations of closeness, trust, conflict, and care, explored without treating history as destiny.
- **Sexual and affectionate relationship:** only as relevant and with consent; ask about desire, intimacy, pain, safety, boundaries, and differences without presuming a normative frequency or form.
- **Parenting and caregiving:** co-parenting, division of labour, child impact, fertility or reproductive decisions where relevant, and care for older or disabled relatives.
- **Individual and health factors:** mood, anxiety, trauma, neurodevelopmental differences, medical conditions, medication, substance use, sleep, and functional impairment, with referral when assessment exceeds the therapist's scope.
- **Social and cultural context:** identity, language, discrimination, migration, economic circumstances, family structure, and institutional involvement.
- **Risk and protection:** violence, coercion, self-harm, harm to others, child or elder safety, stalking, access to weapons where clinically relevant, and protective resources.
- **Treatment history and preference:** previous therapy, what helped or harmed, desired approach, accommodations, and concerns about confidentiality.

Assessment should not become a questionnaire marathon. The therapist prioritises domains according to the couple's concerns and risk profile, explaining why sensitive questions are asked. Standardised measures can support baseline description and monitoring, but should be selected for validity, accessibility, and relevance. A measure does not replace a direct safety interview or a careful discussion of the partners' goals.

### Observing interaction without staging a test

The therapist learns from how partners speak to one another in the session, but the observation is shaped by the setting. A couple may behave differently when being watched, when one partner controls transport or finances, or when an audience is present. The therapist should not deliberately provoke an intense conflict simply to observe it. Instead, invite discussion of a manageable topic and monitor whether each partner can pause, listen, express disagreement, and respond to cues. Observations should be described in behavioural terms: interruption, topic shift, raised voice, silence, checking for agreement, or repair. Interpretations such as “contemptuous” or “avoidant” should be tested with the partners and considered alongside context.

A sequence map can focus clinical attention: what happened immediately before the conflict, what each person did, what the other inferred, what response followed, and how the interaction ended. Ask each partner what they noticed internally and what they hoped the other would understand. This creates a shared object of inquiry without asserting that both are equally responsible for the pattern. If one partner is afraid, coerced, or constrained, the interaction must be interpreted in relation to that power difference.

### Individual and relational assessment

Couple distress and individual mental-health concerns can influence one another. Depression, anxiety, trauma symptoms, chronic pain, sleep disruption, or substance use may affect communication, availability, and conflict; relationship distress may also exacerbate individual symptoms. The clinician should assess whether an individual condition requires concurrent or prior treatment, and avoid attributing it solely to relationship dynamics. When an individual assessment or diagnosis is needed, use appropriate professional procedures and make clear whether the information is part of couple treatment or a separate clinical service.

A therapist must be alert to the limits of their expertise. Screening tools can identify the need for further evaluation but are not diagnoses. Acute suicidal risk, mania, psychosis, severe substance withdrawal, cognitive impairment, or serious medical concerns may require urgent assessment by qualified professionals. The couple therapist should know local referral and emergency pathways and should not attempt to contain a crisis solely within conjoint sessions.

## 4.4 Safety, Coercion, and Suitability for Conjoint Work

### Conflict, violence, and coercive control

Not all couple conflict is equivalent. Partners may engage in mutual disagreement, high-intensity arguments, situational violence, or patterned coercive control. A clinician must distinguish these phenomena as carefully as available information allows. **Coercive control** involves a pattern of domination that restricts another person's autonomy through intimidation, surveillance, isolation, threats, economic control, or other tactics; it is not defined simply by disagreement or by a single heated exchange. Intimate partner violence can include physical, sexual, psychological, and other forms of harm, and may occur in any gender or relationship configuration.

A symmetrical “negative cycle” formulation is unsafe if it converts coercion into reciprocal miscommunication or implies equal responsibility. The person using coercive behaviour is responsible for that behaviour. The therapist should assess the pattern, impact, fear, freedom to disagree, and consequences of disclosures, rather than relying only on whether both partners report “arguments”. Direct but non-leading questions may include: “Has either of you felt afraid of the other?” “Has anyone been prevented from leaving, contacting others, or accessing money?” “What happens after a disagreement?” “Would it be safe for you to speak honestly here, and what might happen after the session?” Questions should be asked privately where possible, with an explanation of confidentiality limits.

### Screening and safety planning

A written instrument may support but cannot replace a private clinical conversation and professional judgement. The therapist should clarify whether the information will be kept in a couple record, who may access it, and what circumstances require action. Screen for immediate danger, escalation, threats, stalking, strangulation, sexual coercion, child exposure, access to lethal means where relevant, and the person's own assessment of risk. These are sensitive questions that require training; the clinician should consult current, jurisdiction-specific guidance and specialist services rather than assume a brief checklist is sufficient.

Safety planning is collaborative and should not prescribe a single path. It may include a safe contact method, emergency contacts, access to documents or medication, a plan for leaving a session safely, specialist advocacy, child protection consultation, or referral. Do not leave written information or voicemail in a way that could increase danger. The therapist should document objectively, consult as needed, and follow applicable law and safeguarding duties. The clinician should not confront a suspected perpetrator with confidential information without considering foreseeable risk.

### Deciding whether to proceed conjointly

Conjoint therapy may be inappropriate or unsafe when one partner cannot speak freely, there is active coercive control, severe violence, fear of retaliation, or a significant imbalance that the treatment setting cannot safely manage. Other factors—active psychosis, uncontrolled substance-related impairment, acute suicidality, or severe instability—may require individual stabilisation or specialised care before couple work. These are not automatic exclusions based solely on a diagnostic label; the decision depends on current severity, safety, consent, treatment focus, available expertise, and coordination with other providers.

When conjoint work is not indicated, explain the reasoning without blaming the person at risk. Offer referral to specialist services, individual treatment, advocacy, or other appropriate support. If conjoint therapy proceeds despite a history of harm, it should do so only under a defensible plan, with specialised competence, ongoing private safety assessment, clear limits, and a willingness to stop or change course. A therapist should not promise that the office is a safe space simply because it is professionally supervised.

### Safety in diverse relationships

Clinicians should not assume that a particular gender, body, or relationship role determines who is at risk or who uses violence. They should also recognise that legal protections, community resources, and social credibility may be unevenly available. A partner may fear disclosure because of prior discrimination, outing, immigration consequences, or mistrust of authorities. Ask about the client's own concerns and seek specialist consultation without treating identity as evidence of risk. Use inclusive language and attend to the interactional pattern, access to resources, and concrete behaviour.

Risk assessment must include child and dependent-person safety when relevant. Children may witness conflict or be drawn into adult disputes. The therapist should ask how children are affected, what they understand, and what protective steps are needed, while following local safeguarding duties. A child should not be used as a messenger or mediator between parents. If the service is court-involved or mandated, clarify the clinician's role and limits; couple therapy is not a substitute for forensic assessment.
