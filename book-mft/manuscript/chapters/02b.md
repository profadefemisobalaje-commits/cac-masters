## 2.3 Research Designs, Sampling, and Inference

### Design as a structure of comparison

A research design determines which observations will be made, when they will be made, under what conditions, and what comparisons can be interpreted. No design is universally strongest. A design is strong when it addresses a well-defined question while controlling or describing the most important alternative explanations within its ethical and practical limits.

Cross-sectional studies measure variables at one time. They can describe distributions and estimate associations, but temporal order is usually ambiguous: distress may contribute to conflict, conflict may contribute to distress, and both may be influenced by economic pressure or a recent transition. Longitudinal studies repeat observations and can establish temporal ordering more clearly. Repeated measurement does not, by itself, establish causality; time-varying confounding, measurement changes, attrition, and reciprocal effects remain possible. Prospective cohort studies follow a defined group over time, while retrospective studies reconstruct prior events from records or recollection and therefore face different biases.

Experimental designs manipulate or assign an intervention. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) uses random allocation to make treatment groups comparable in expectation, subject to successful implementation and follow-up. Randomisation does not guarantee balance in a small sample, correct measurement, or absence of bias after allocation. Allocation concealment, pre-specified outcomes, transparent reporting, treatment fidelity, and appropriate analysis are essential. In MFT, the trial may randomise couples or families, clinicians, clinics, or service sites; the level of allocation must match the level at which contamination and treatment are expected. If a therapist delivers both conditions, learning or allegiance effects may blur the contrast. If whole clinics are randomised, the number of clusters and intraclass correlation become central to power and analysis.

Quasi-experimental designs estimate effects without random allocation. Options include interrupted time series, regression discontinuity, matched comparison groups, difference-in-differences, and natural experiments. Each depends on assumptions that should be stated and examined. For example, a difference-in-differences interpretation assumes that, absent the intervention, the groups would have followed parallel trends; a regression-discontinuity design depends on the allocation threshold not being manipulated and on continuity around the cut-off. Quasi-experimental methods can be useful when randomisation is unavailable or inappropriate, but the name of a method does not remove the need to evaluate its assumptions.

Single-case experimental designs collect repeated observations from one person, couple, or family across phases or conditions. Designs such as A-B-A-B reversal, multiple baseline, changing criterion, or alternating treatments can help test whether change is temporally linked to an intervention. Reversal may be unsuitable where a treatment effect is irreversible or withdrawal would be harmful. A multiple-baseline design can offer stronger control without withdrawing a beneficial intervention, provided baselines and staggered intervention points are carefully planned. Single-case work is not equivalent to an anecdote: it requires systematic repeated measurement, a defensible design, and transparent analysis of level, trend, variability, immediacy, overlap, and clinical significance.

### Sampling and representativeness

Sampling begins with a target population and an accessible sampling frame. A clinic-based sample may represent people who reach that clinic, not all families experiencing the problem. Inclusion and exclusion criteria affect both safety and generalisability; researchers should state them with enough detail to allow readers to understand who was not represented and why. Probability sampling can support population estimates when the sampling frame and response processes are adequate. Convenience, purposive, snowball, and consecutive sampling are often appropriate for different applied and qualitative aims, but their limitations should be described rather than disguised.

In qualitative work, sample adequacy is argued in relation to the research purpose, analytic depth, diversity of relevant experience, and information richness. A fixed number cannot guarantee saturation or depth. In quantitative work, sample size should be justified by the primary estimand, expected effect or precision, variability, clustering, repeated measures, attrition, and multiplicity. “A large sample” is not a calculation. For a trial involving couples, an analysis that ignores dyadic dependence may make a nominally adequate sample misleading. Power calculations should identify the allocation unit, outcome scale, anticipated attrition, and assumptions; where uncertainty is substantial, sensitivity analyses are more honest than a single precise figure.

Recruitment procedures can shape the sample. Clinicians may preferentially refer families they believe will comply; families may decline because of work, transport, language, privacy, or fear of consequences. Recruitment logs should document numbers approached, eligible, consenting, declining, and lost to follow-up, with reasons where ethically and practically available. Researchers should consider compensation carefully: it should recognise time and costs without creating undue influence. Accessible formats, interpreters, childcare support, flexible scheduling, and remote options may improve inclusion, although each introduces confidentiality and data-quality considerations.

### Threats to validity and transferability

Internal validity concerns whether the observed difference can reasonably be attributed to the exposure or intervention in the study. Threats include selection, history, maturation, regression to the mean, instrumentation change, attrition, contamination, differential attention, and selective reporting. Construct validity concerns whether the manipulation and measures represent the intended constructs. Statistical conclusion validity concerns whether the analysis supports the stated inference, including assumptions, precision, and multiplicity. External validity concerns the conditions under which results may generalise. These categories are diagnostic tools, not a checklist that can certify a study.

Qualitative quality is often evaluated through transparency, credibility, reflexivity, coherence between question and method, attention to divergent cases, and sufficient contextual detail for readers to judge relevance. The term *transferability* recognises that a reader may assess the relevance of a finding to another setting, rather than assuming statistical generalisation. Member reflection, peer discussion, audit trails, triangulation, and reflexive journals can serve different purposes; none is a ritual that automatically proves truth. Researchers should say what a procedure contributed and what it could not establish.

### Hypotheses, outcomes, and preregistration

A confirmatory analysis tests hypotheses specified before the outcome data are examined; an exploratory analysis searches for patterns that may generate later hypotheses. Both are legitimate, but they should not be presented as the same kind of evidence. Preregistration records the question, design, outcomes, sample, and analysis plan before results are known. It can reduce undisclosed analytic flexibility and selective outcome reporting, though it cannot prevent poor measurement or make a weak theory sound. Protocol amendments should be dated and explained, and exploratory analyses should be labelled as such. For qualitative research, transparent protocols can specify the analytic approach and reflexive commitments without pretending that interpretation is a mechanical algorithm.

## 2.4 Measurement and the Relational Unit of Analysis

### Choosing outcomes that matter

An outcome should be relevant to the question, meaningful to affected people, sufficiently reliable for the intended use, feasible to collect, and interpretable over time. MFT studies frequently need more than one outcome domain: symptoms or distress, relational functioning, safety, quality of life, service engagement, and adverse experiences may all matter. A measure can be statistically sensitive yet clinically unimportant; conversely, a change important to a family may not be captured by a standardised scale. Researchers should distinguish primary from secondary outcomes, define time points, and explain how clinically meaningful change will be interpreted.

Self-report is efficient and captures subjective experience, but may be influenced by recall, social desirability, literacy, mood, and the relationship with the person collecting data. Clinician ratings add a professional perspective but may be affected by allegiance and knowledge of treatment allocation. Administrative outcomes such as attendance, referrals, or service use can be useful, but record completeness and the meaning of a recorded event must be checked. Observational coding can capture interaction directly, but requires a clear sampling procedure, trained raters, blinding where possible, and assessment of inter-rater agreement. Physiological measures may add information about arousal, but should not be treated as transparent indicators of a particular emotion or relational mechanism.

Measurement invariance is the question of whether a construct is measured comparably across groups or time. If a scale's items function differently by language, age, gender, or cultural context, apparent group differences may partly reflect measurement rather than the construct. Researchers may assess configural, metric, and scalar invariance in suitable samples, but statistical tests cannot replace qualitative inquiry into what questions mean to participants. For cross-cultural adaptation, a careful process can include forward and back translation, expert review, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, and psychometric evaluation; the process should be reported rather than reduced to the statement “translated into X”.

### Dyads, families, and nested data

The **actor-partner interdependence model** (APIM) is one framework for analysing dyadic data. It distinguishes actor effects — the association between a person's predictor and that same person's outcome — from partner effects — the association between one person's predictor and the partner's outcome. APIM can be extended to distinguish distinguishable dyads, such as two roles that are meaningfully different, from indistinguishable dyads where labels are arbitrary. The assumptions, coding, and interpretation should be explained. A partner effect is not automatically a causal influence; it is an association conditional on the model and data.

For family data, observations may be nested within members, members within families, and families within therapists, clinics, or communities. Multilevel models can represent variation at these levels, provided the number of units at each level is adequate and the model is specified appropriately. A family-level intervention does not imply that every outcome should be collapsed to a single family score. Disagreement between members can be clinically meaningful, while an aggregated score may conceal who changed and who did not. The design should decide whether the estimand concerns individual members, dyads, families, or a distribution of effects across these units.

Repeated measures introduce another dependence: observations from the same person or family over time are correlated. Growth models, mixed-effects models, or other longitudinal approaches may represent trajectories, but model choice depends on the question and data structure. Two or three time points usually cannot capture a complex nonlinear process with precision. Researchers should specify whether time is treated categorically or continuously and explain the meaning of the estimated trajectory. When only pre- and post-treatment measures are available, a simple change score may be transparent but can be sensitive to measurement error and baseline differences; a model adjusting for baseline outcome may answer a different but often useful question.

### Missing data and data quality

Missingness is common in clinical research and may be related to the outcome. A participant who stops attending because the intervention is distressing or because conflict has escalated is not equivalent to a participant who misses a questionnaire because of a scheduling error. Complete-case analysis can be biased and inefficient. Researchers should describe the extent, timing, and reasons for missingness; compare available characteristics of retained and lost participants; and consider methods such as multiple imputation or likelihood-based estimation when their assumptions are plausible. No statistical method recovers information that was never collected without assumptions. Sensitivity analyses should test how conclusions might change under different missing-data mechanisms.

Data cleaning should be planned, reproducible, and documented. Range checks, duplicate detection, coding rules, and a data dictionary reduce errors. Corrections should retain an audit trail. For observational coding, a proportion of material should be independently coded, with agreement assessed using a statistic appropriate to the data and design; percent agreement alone may be misleading when categories are imbalanced. Researchers should not delete inconvenient observations without a pre-specified reason. Outlier handling, transformations, and model changes need justification and reporting.
