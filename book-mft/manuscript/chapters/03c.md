## 3.5 Kinship, Migration, and Transnational Family Life

### Family structure and kinship networks

Many clinical systems presume that the relevant family consists of a co-resident couple and their dependent children. That pattern exists alongside single-parent households, multigenerational households, separated co-parents, kinship care, foster and adoptive relationships, same-gender-parent families, chosen families, and networks whose members live across regions or countries. The therapist should ask who the family considers important, who provides care, who has decision-making authority, and who should participate. Legal status and relational significance may not align: a person may be central to care without legal guardianship, or legally related but not safe to involve.

Extended kin relationships can be a source of belonging, childcare, practical help, identity, and continuity. They can also involve conflict, pressure, unequal labour, or restrictions on autonomy. The clinical task is not to encourage or discourage extended-family involvement as a general principle, but to understand what it does in the particular network and what the family wants. Mapping kinship can reveal both support and burden. A genogram should be adaptable to chosen and culturally significant relationships, and the therapist should explain symbols rather than allow a conventional chart to erase family members who do not fit its categories.

### Migration, displacement, and settlement

Migration is not one experience. Voluntary movement for work or education, forced displacement, family reunification, refugee resettlement, seasonal mobility, and internal migration involve different risks, resources, legal conditions, and meanings. A clinician should not presume trauma from the fact of migration, nor overlook the effects of violence, loss, separation, uncertainty, discrimination, or insecure status when they are present. Ask what happened, what is safe to discuss, what the family wants to make of the experience, and how current legal or economic conditions affect daily life.

Migration can reorganise family roles. A young person who learns the dominant language quickly may become an interpreter for parents; a parent may lose professional status; family members may be separated for years; remittances may create obligations; or caregiving may be distributed across borders. These shifts can produce new competence as well as strain. A child who translates at appointments may gain responsibility but also carry adult information; a parent may feel dependent on the child while still holding important authority in other domains. The therapist should identify the specific pattern and avoid the simplistic story that children “acculturate” while parents “resist”.

Settlement stress can include housing insecurity, work exploitation, racism, uncertain documentation, restricted access to services, and isolation. Such pressures may aggravate couple conflict or parent-child disagreement without being reducible to family dysfunction. Clinical recommendations should be feasible under the family's actual constraints. A weekly daytime appointment, separate transport, or a costly referral can be inaccessible. Where legal or practical expertise is needed, therapists should make appropriate referrals rather than offer unqualified advice about immigration or status.

### Acculturation and cultural change

Acculturation refers to processes of cultural and psychological change associated with sustained contact between cultural groups. It is not a single scale on which one person moves from “heritage” to “host” culture. People may adapt language, work practices, parenting styles, or public presentation at different rates and in different contexts. They may maintain some practices, revise others, and reject still others. The process is shaped by power, discrimination, opportunity, social networks, and personal choice. A family's disagreement may concern a specific behaviour—dating, clothing, school participation, care of elders—rather than a global clash between cultures.

The therapist can explore how members describe change and what each fears losing or gaining. A parent may worry that a young person's new independence will weaken connection; the young person may experience the same movement as necessary for safety or belonging. Both perspectives deserve attention, while the clinician also assesses coercion, harm, and developmental rights. “Both sides” language should not erase asymmetries in power or responsibility. The aim is to make meanings and consequences discussable and to support negotiated choices where safe.

### Transnational ties and distributed care

Family relationships may be maintained through calls, messaging, remittances, visits, and care coordination across distance. A household member in one country may participate in decisions about schooling, healthcare, or elder care from another. Time zones, connectivity, cost, surveillance, and language access influence this communication. A conventional in-room family session may not include the people who shape the family's decisions, but adding remote participants may compromise privacy or reinforce pressure. The therapist should ask what role distant relatives play and whether their involvement is desired and safe.

Transnational caregiving can be both sustaining and demanding. A family may coordinate care for an older relative across borders while managing employment and child-rearing locally. Remittances can express solidarity, but can also be a source of conflict if family members disagree about priorities. The clinician should avoid interpreting transnational obligation as pathology or as an uncomplicated virtue. The question is how the arrangement affects each person, what choices are available, and what forms of support could reduce strain.

### Culture, trauma, and the danger of deficit narratives

Exposure to violence, persecution, displacement, or discrimination can affect family communication and regulation, but clinicians should not use a trauma label to define a family. Some people prefer not to recount events; forced retelling is not a prerequisite for care. Present-focused support, practical stabilisation, and relational safety may be more appropriate. Assessment should consider ongoing threats and systemic conditions, not only past events. Resilience should be recognised without using it to minimise harm or deny access to support.

Deficit narratives emerge when clinicians describe a community mainly through risk, dysfunction, and service need. Counter this by documenting competence, mutual aid, humour, adaptation, cultural knowledge, and aspirations alongside difficulties. Strengths are not an alternative to material support: praising resilience does not remedy unsafe housing or discrimination. The formulation should capture both the family's resources and the constraints that make those resources costly to sustain.

### Hypothetical Case Illustration: a cross-generational transition

::: casebox
**Hypothetical case — a family negotiating migration and autonomy.** A 16-year-old, their caregiver, and a younger sibling attend a clinic after repeated arguments about school attendance and social activities. The young person speaks the local language more fluently and has begun interpreting for the caregiver in public services. The caregiver describes the young person's new social circle as unsafe; the young person says that restrictions are isolating and that they are tired of translating adult matters. The clinician does not assume that the family represents a particular national pattern. In separate and joint conversations, the therapist asks what the caregiver fears, what the young person wants, what information is safe to share, how interpretation responsibilities developed, and what school or community pressures are involved. The formulation includes language burden, parent-child authority, safety, and the family's own goals. A professional interpreter is arranged for future sessions. The therapist helps the family negotiate specific limits and a safer way to share public-service tasks, while keeping the young person's privacy and the caregiver's concerns visible.
:::

This hypothetical case illustrates why cultural formulation must be specific. The concern is not simply “acculturation conflict”. It includes a role reversal in public institutions, a developmental negotiation, safety concerns, and access to professional language support. The clinician should not decide in advance that either the caregiver's caution or the young person's wish for autonomy is culturally determined. Each account is explored, safety is assessed, and the intervention is reviewed for whether it reduces burden without dismissing either person's agency.

## 3.6 Race, Ethnicity, and the Effects of Racialisation

### Naming structural experience

Race is a social and political category with real consequences, not a biological explanation for family behaviour. Racialisation is the process through which people are assigned racial meanings and treated accordingly. Families may experience discrimination in healthcare, schools, housing, employment, policing, and other institutions; these experiences can shape stress, trust, vigilance, and decisions about disclosure. The therapist should be able to ask about discrimination without presuming it has occurred or requiring clients to provide proof before it can be considered.

A question such as “Have experiences of bias or unfair treatment affected how your family is approaching this problem or this service?” can open a discussion while leaving room for “no”, “not sure”, or “I do not want to discuss it”. If a client describes discrimination, the therapist should not immediately reframe the account as a cognitive distortion, communication problem, or misunderstanding. Explore what happened, its effect, what support the client wants, and whether the service itself needs to respond. The clinician should distinguish validation of lived experience from making claims about facts outside the available information; uncertainty should not become reflexive disbelief.

### Historical context and family meaning

Historical policies and collective histories can affect present-day trust, family narratives, and institutional contact. The relevance and form differ by country and community. Therapists should learn the histories that are relevant to the populations they serve, but should not treat a client as a representative of a history or assume that a particular event explains their current behaviour. Ask whether historical or community experiences matter to the family and how they understand their relevance. The clinician's task is to situate, not to impose a collective narrative.

### Avoiding racialised diagnosis and unequal standards

Clinical labels can be influenced by expectations about threat, emotional expression, parenting, and authority. A therapist should examine whether the same behaviour would be described similarly if performed by a person from another social group. Terms such as “aggressive”, “resistant”, “non-compliant”, “hostile”, and “enmeshed” may encode judgement unless linked to specific observed behaviour and context. Documentation should distinguish what was observed from inference and avoid vague characterisations that could be harmful in later institutional decisions.

The clinician should also consider how systemic models have historically described families. Some theories have universalised middle-class, Euro-American assumptions about household boundaries, gender roles, privacy, and autonomy. Their constructs can still offer useful questions, but must be evaluated rather than applied as cultural facts. A “clear boundary” may support safety in one context and sever essential care in another; closeness may be supportive, controlling, or both. The family should help define the functional meaning of a pattern, and the clinician should assess freedom, consent, safety, and consequences.

### Community knowledge without tokenism

Community consultation can improve service design, terminology, recruitment, and referral pathways. It should not depend on one community member being expected to represent all people who share an identity. Define the purpose of consultation, compensate expertise, protect confidentiality, and communicate what changed as a result. When disagreement exists within a community, it should be represented rather than flattened into a single “cultural view”. Researchers and organisations should share decision-making where possible and avoid extracting knowledge without reciprocal benefit.
