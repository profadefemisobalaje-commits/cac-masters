## 2.5 Evaluating Interventions and Causal Claims

### From change to effect

Families often improve during treatment, but improvement during treatment is not identical to improvement caused by treatment. Symptoms may change through natural development, regression to the mean, a change in circumstances, concurrent services, measurement reactivity, or the family's own efforts. A causal effect is a comparison between what happened under an intervention and what would have happened to the same unit, at the same time, under a relevant alternative. Because both counterfactual outcomes cannot be observed for one family, research design creates a comparison that approximates the missing counterfactual.

The intervention must be specified sufficiently for the contrast to be interpretable. A treatment manual may describe session structure, but delivery also includes therapist competence, responsiveness, supervision, dose, adaptations, and the surrounding service context. “Family therapy” is not a single intervention. If the study compares a named model with usual care, usual care should be described: who provides it, what services are available, whether families receive additional treatment, and how those services are recorded. Otherwise the comparison group is a label rather than a replicable condition.

Randomisation can support causal inference by balancing known and unknown baseline characteristics in expectation. The analysis should generally preserve participants in their assigned groups (intention-to-treat) to estimate the effect of assignment under the trial's conditions. Per-protocol analyses may address a different question about outcomes among those who received treatment as planned, but attendance and adherence are not randomly assigned; people who complete therapy may differ systematically from those who do not. Both analyses may be informative if clearly distinguished and appropriately qualified. Blinding therapists and participants to psychotherapy allocation is usually impossible, but outcome assessors, coders, and analysts can sometimes be blinded.

### Mechanism, mediation, and moderation

A treatment outcome asks whether an intervention changes an outcome; a mechanism question asks how change occurs. A **mediator** is a variable through which an intervention is hypothesised to influence an outcome. A **moderator** identifies conditions or characteristics associated with variation in an intervention's effect. These concepts are often invoked loosely. To support a mediation claim, the mediator should be measured at a time that precedes the outcome, the theoretical causal structure should be specified, and confounding of mediator-outcome relations must be considered. Cross-sectional associations between alliance and outcome cannot show that alliance caused improvement: early improvement may strengthen alliance, or a third factor may affect both. Moderation should also be pre-specified where possible and tested with adequate power; post hoc subgroup findings are especially vulnerable to false positives.

A plausible causal diagram can expose assumptions. For example, therapist experience may influence treatment delivery and outcome; baseline severity may influence both attendance and outcome; and social adversity may affect both engagement and follow-up. A directed acyclic graph is not proof of a causal model, but it forces researchers to state which variables they consider causes, mediators, colliders, or consequences. Adjusting for every available variable is not a safe strategy: adjustment for a collider or a post-treatment variable can introduce bias. The causal question should determine the adjustment set, not a software default.

### Fidelity, adaptation, and therapist effects

Treatment fidelity is not simply therapist compliance with a checklist. It can include adherence to core components, competence in delivery, differentiation from comparison conditions, and the quality of the therapeutic relationship. Fidelity measures should be chosen in relation to the intervention theory. In a relational treatment, rigid adherence that prevents responsive engagement may undermine the model's intended process. At the same time, unrecorded adaptation makes it difficult to know what was delivered. Studies should document planned adaptations, distinguish core functions from adaptable forms, and record deviations with reasons.

Therapists are part of the intervention and a source of variation. Therapist effects may be estimated when the number of therapists and allocation structure support such analysis, but small numbers make estimates unstable. Therapists' allegiance to a model can influence recruitment, delivery, and ratings; equipoise, training balance, supervision, and transparent reporting can help manage but not eliminate this risk. In pragmatic settings, implementation variation may be a substantive finding rather than mere noise. The researcher should decide whether the question concerns the efficacy of a model under controlled delivery, the effectiveness of a service package in routine care, or the processes through which implementation succeeds or fails.

### Clinical significance and harms

Statistical significance is not synonymous with clinical importance. Effect estimates should be accompanied by uncertainty intervals and interpreted in relation to a pre-defined meaningful difference, baseline risk, service costs, participant burden, and possible harms. Reliable change indices, minimal clinically important differences, responder definitions, and number-needed-to-treat can each be useful, but each depends on assumptions and should be selected before results are known where possible. Reporting only a p-value obscures magnitude and precision.

Harms in relational treatment may include increased conflict, disclosure-related risk, pressure to reconcile, breaches of privacy, distress from recordings, or consequences of involving a family member in a session. Monitoring should be prospective, with procedures for response and referral. A low rate of reported adverse events can reflect inadequate monitoring or a definition that excludes relational harms. Safety planning and separate opportunities for private disclosure are especially important where coercion or violence is suspected; research protocols should not assume that joint participation is safe for every couple or family.

## 2.6 Research Ethics, Safety, and Data Governance

### Consent and participation in relational research

Informed consent is a continuing process, not a signature collected once. Participants need understandable information about purpose, procedures, foreseeable risks, alternatives, confidentiality limits, data retention, recording, withdrawal, and use of quotations or images. Consent must be voluntary and free of inappropriate pressure from clinicians, employers, schools, or family members. Where minors participate, researchers must follow applicable law and ethics guidance on parental permission, young people's assent, capacity, and circumstances in which confidentiality may be limited. Procedures should explain what will happen if a young person discloses abuse, imminent risk, or another safeguarding concern.

In family research, one member's decision may affect others. A researcher should not presume that one person can consent on behalf of the entire family to the collection or disclosure of another member's data. Each participant should receive a clear explanation of what data are individual, what will be shared, who may access recordings, and whether participants can review or withdraw their own contribution. Group interviews create limits: researchers can promise that the research team will protect confidentiality, but cannot guarantee that other participants will keep information private. This limitation must be communicated before participation.

### Confidentiality and the limits of “anonymity”

Clinical and family data can be identifiable through combinations of details even after names are removed. Rare family structures, locations, occupations, migration histories, or unusual events may enable recognition. Pseudonymisation reduces direct identification but does not make data anonymous if a key or contextual clues remain. Reports should minimise unnecessary detail, alter non-essential characteristics where appropriate, and state when a composite or hypothetical case has been used. Any alteration must preserve the analytic point and should not mislead readers about the source of evidence.

Recordings and transcripts require security proportional to sensitivity. A data-management plan should identify what is collected, where it is stored, encryption, access permissions, transfer, backup, retention, destruction, and permitted secondary uses. Cloud services, transcription platforms, and generative tools may process data beyond the researcher's intended jurisdiction; they should not be used with identifiable or confidential material without institutional approval, legal review, and informed consent. Researchers should minimise collection: data that are not necessary should not be gathered simply because a platform makes them easy to obtain.

### Dual roles, power, and reciprocal obligations

The clinician-researcher may have therapeutic, evaluative, and academic authority at once. Patients may believe that declining research will affect care; students or supervisees may believe refusal will affect assessment; staff may fear that criticism will reach managers. Separate recruitment from treatment decisions where possible, use an independent recruiter, and make clear that refusal or withdrawal will not alter care or professional standing. When the therapist is also the researcher, the protocol should address access to clinical records, disclosure of research findings during treatment, and management of distress or conflict.

Research with families must attend to power within the family as well as power between institution and participant. A joint interview can silence a member or create risk after the session. Researchers need private, safe ways for individuals to express concerns, and a pre-established response pathway for coercion, abuse, suicidal risk, or danger to others. Confidentiality cannot be promised without limits, and the limits should be described in plain language. Safety procedures should be developed with local safeguarding professionals and reflect jurisdictional obligations.

### Equity and community partnership

Research samples often overrepresent people with time, transport, institutional trust, dominant-language fluency, and stable access to services. Inclusion is not achieved simply by adding a demographic table after recruitment. It requires early attention to accessibility, appropriate language, disability accommodation, compensation, cultural safety, and the distribution of research burden and benefit. Community advisors can identify problems in terminology, recruitment, outcome choice, or dissemination that academic teams may miss. Partnership must be substantive: clarify decision rights, compensation, credit, data access, and how disagreement will be handled.

Ethical review is a minimum governance process, not a substitute for ongoing judgement. A protocol may be approved before circumstances change. Researchers should report amendments, adverse events, protocol deviations, and conflicts of interest through appropriate channels. International or cross-institutional research adds questions of data transfer, local review, ownership, and fair benefit sharing. The relevant standards and law must be checked in each jurisdiction; a generic statement of compliance does not establish that a project is lawful or ethically adequate.
