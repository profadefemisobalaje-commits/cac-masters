## 2.7 Analysis, Reporting, and Evidence Synthesis

### Analysis begins before data collection

An analysis plan should state the primary question, estimand or analytic objective, outcome variables, time points, predictors, covariates, handling of clustering and repeated measures, missing-data approach, multiplicity strategy, and sensitivity analyses. The plan should be detailed enough to distinguish the intended analysis from later choices made after seeing results. It need not anticipate every contingency; unforeseen problems should be documented with reasons and their status as confirmatory or exploratory made clear.

For quantitative studies, report descriptive statistics that allow readers to understand the sample and data distribution, not only test statistics. Estimate effects in interpretable units where possible and provide confidence or credible intervals. Model assumptions should be checked and reported, including distributional form, residual structure, independence where assumed, and influence of unusual observations. If several outcomes or subgroup analyses are tested, the report should explain how multiplicity is addressed or why results are exploratory. “No statistically significant difference” is not evidence that two interventions are equivalent; equivalence or non-inferiority requires a pre-specified margin, a design appropriate to that question, and sufficient precision.

In qualitative research, analysis should make visible how the researcher moved from material to themes, categories, cases, or theoretical claims. The report can describe familiarisation, coding or interpretive practices, development and revision of analytic ideas, attention to disconfirming material, and the influence of the researcher's position. A coding framework may be useful in some approaches, while in reflexive thematic analysis the aim is not necessarily to produce an objective codebook or inter-rater reliability coefficient. Methods should fit the epistemology and analytic tradition. A quotation illustrates an interpretation; it does not independently prove that the interpretation is representative.

Mixed-methods analysis requires both component analyses and an account of integration. A table that juxtaposes outcome change with interview themes may reveal agreement, elaboration, or contradiction. Disagreement between methods is not a flaw to be hidden. Families might report improved communication while observational coders detect unchanged interruption rates; the discrepancy may reflect different contexts, different constructs, or different definitions of progress. The researcher should explore the discrepancy and state what it means for the combined inference.

### Transparent reporting and reproducibility

Reporting guidelines support completeness but do not replace sound design. Researchers should select a guideline appropriate to the study, such as CONSORT for randomised trials, TREND for non-randomised evaluations, STROBE for observational studies, COREQ or SRQR for qualitative reports, and relevant mixed-methods guidance. The guideline should be used during planning as well as manuscript preparation. Protocols, registration records, analytic code, materials, and de-identified data may be shared when ethically, legally, and practically appropriate. Family-level data are unusually sensitive; open science does not require public release of data that could expose participants. Controlled access, data-use agreements, synthetic data, and detailed codebooks may support transparency while protecting privacy.

Research integrity includes accurate authorship, acknowledgement of community contributions, disclosure of funding and conflicts, and correction of errors. Selective reporting, p-hacking, HARKing (hypothesising after results are known while presenting hypotheses as prior), and suppression of null findings distort the evidence base. A null result may be informative if the study was adequately designed and precisely reported. Researchers should avoid overstating implications in abstracts and press releases, where qualifications are often lost.

### Systematic reviews and meta-analysis

A systematic review uses explicit methods to identify, select, appraise, and synthesise relevant studies. It begins with a focused question, a reproducible search strategy, inclusion criteria, duplicate screening or verification procedures, and a plan for risk-of-bias assessment and synthesis. The review should include unpublished or grey literature where relevant and feasible, because publication bias can make an intervention appear more effective than it is. Search limits, language restrictions, and date cut-offs should be justified. A flow diagram documents study selection, while an evidence table supports assessment of setting, population, intervention, comparator, outcome, and design.

Meta-analysis combines estimates statistically when studies are sufficiently comparable and the data permit. It is not automatically appropriate merely because several studies exist. Differences in treatment content, population, comparator, outcome measure, follow-up period, and risk of bias may make a pooled estimate difficult to interpret. A random-effects model allows that true effects may vary, but does not explain why they vary and does not make incompatible studies comparable. Heterogeneity statistics should be interpreted alongside clinical and methodological differences. Publication-bias tests are unreliable with few studies and should not be used as a reassurance ritual. Certainty-of-evidence frameworks can help distinguish the magnitude of an estimate from confidence in it.

For MFT, systematic synthesis should attend to intervention format and context: whether treatment included the identified patient alone, a couple, parents, or a wider family; how many sessions were delivered; provider training and supervision; cultural and service context; and the comparator. “Family-based treatment” is too broad to function as a single category without further specification. Reviewers should also consider whether outcome measures privilege individual symptom reduction over relational or participant-defined outcomes.

## 2.8 From Evidence to Practice and Implementation

### Evidence-informed practice as a decision process

Evidence-informed clinical practice integrates the best available research with clinical expertise and the preferences, goals, circumstances, and values of clients. It is not the mechanical application of an average treatment effect to every family. A therapist should ask whether the study population resembles the family before them; whether the intervention can be delivered with adequate competence; whether the outcome is meaningful; whether risk and safety have been assessed; and whether the family has a genuine choice. Evidence may inform a decision without dictating it.

The distinction between efficacy, effectiveness, and implementation helps organise this reasoning. Efficacy asks whether an intervention can produce benefit under controlled conditions. Effectiveness asks whether benefit occurs in ordinary practice. Implementation research studies the strategies and conditions that support adoption, fidelity, adaptation, reach, and sustainment. A treatment may have efficacy evidence but poor reach because it is inaccessible; an effective local programme may not be sustainable without supervision and funding. Dissemination is not merely publishing findings: it includes communication to families, clinicians, service leaders, educators, and policymakers in usable forms.

Implementation frameworks can help specify determinants and outcomes. For example, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research organises contextual influences, while implementation outcome taxonomies distinguish acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, cost, penetration, and sustainability. These are not interchangeable outcomes. A programme can be acceptable but ineffective, effective but unaffordable, or feasible at one clinic but difficult to sustain. Researchers should select implementation outcomes in relation to the decision being made.

### Adaptation without loss of meaning

Interventions may need adaptation for language, culture, disability, family structure, service resources, or local law. Adaptation should be documented rather than treated as a deviation that is either automatically good or automatically bad. A useful distinction is between a model's core functions — the mechanisms it is intended to activate — and its forms — the particular materials, examples, or session arrangements used to activate them. The distinction is theoretical and must be tested; researchers should not declare any inconvenient element “non-core” after the fact. Community partners, clinicians, and service users can help identify adaptations, while outcome and process evaluation can assess what changed.

Implementation research should include unintended effects and equity. A digital or manualised approach might increase standardisation while excluding families with limited connectivity or literacy. A fidelity target might improve consistency while narrowing therapist responsiveness. An intervention delivered in a school or court context may be experienced as compulsory even if the research consent form is voluntary. Monitoring who is reached, who declines, who completes treatment, and who benefits is essential to equitable translation.

### Research-informed clinical humility

Evidence can discipline clinical confidence, but it cannot eliminate uncertainty. A practitioner should communicate what is known, what is not known, and how the family's own observations will be used to evaluate progress. Routine outcome monitoring and feedback can make treatment responsive, provided scores are discussed collaboratively and are not used as a substitute for listening. If outcome data suggest no improvement, the clinician can revisit goals, alliance, formulation, safety, fit, and external conditions rather than blaming the family or defending a preferred model.

Research translation should preserve the level of evidence. A small uncontrolled study may justify further investigation, not a claim that a treatment is proven. A statistically significant average effect may not mean that every family benefits. An association between therapist alliance and outcome may support attention to alliance, but does not prove that a particular alliance-building technique will cause improvement. Precision in language is part of ethical practice.

## 2.9 Constructing a Doctoral Research Proposal

A doctoral proposal should tell a coherent story: a significant problem; what is already known; what remains uncertain; the specific question; why the proposed design can answer it; how participants will be protected; how data will be analysed; and what contribution is expected. The literature review should synthesise, not merely list studies. It should compare designs and populations, identify limitations, and show how the present question follows logically from the evidence. The theoretical framework should clarify constructs and proposed relationships without being treated as a conclusion in advance.

A practical proposal can be organised around the following elements:

1. **Title and abstract:** concise, accurate, and explicit about the population, phenomenon, intervention, or design.
2. **Problem statement:** define the clinical or service problem and its significance without exaggerating prevalence or harm.
3. **Literature synthesis:** evaluate relevant theory, measures, designs, findings, and gaps; distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.
4. **Aim and questions:** state one primary aim, linked sub-questions, and hypotheses where appropriate.
5. **Conceptual model:** define constructs, specify proposed relations, and identify assumptions or rival explanations.
6. **Methods:** setting, eligibility, recruitment, intervention or phenomenon, comparator where relevant, procedures, measures, and time points.
7. **Sample and feasibility:** justify size and recruitment, including clustering, attrition, access, and inclusion strategy.
8. **Analysis plan:** specify the estimand or interpretive approach, data preparation, primary analysis, missingness, sensitivity checks, and integration if mixed methods.
9. **Ethics and governance:** consent, safety, privacy, data protection, dual roles, adverse-event response, and community partnership.
10. **Contribution and dissemination:** identify who may use the findings, what decisions may change, and how limitations will be communicated.

A proposal should include a timeline with dependencies. Ethics approval precedes recruitment; instrument permissions may take time; clinician training should precede intervention delivery; translation and accessibility work should not be left until the final month. A risk register can identify recruitment shortfall, staff turnover, incomplete records, technical failure, participant distress, or changes in service policy. Each risk should have a mitigation strategy and a threshold for revising the design. Doctoral feasibility is a scholarly criterion: an elegant question that cannot be completed responsibly is not yet a good proposal.

### Applied Exercise: design a staged study

::: exercise
**Hypothetical exercise.** A regional service wants to know whether adding a structured co-parenting intervention to usual child-focused care improves family coordination after separation. In a short proposal outline, define the target population and safety exclusions; distinguish the primary outcome from secondary outcomes; choose an initial feasibility or effectiveness design and justify the choice; identify how each caregiver's report will be analysed without assuming independence; describe how children will be included or protected; propose a plan for missing data; and state one conclusion the design could support and one it could not. Include a brief account of how families and clinicians would help interpret the findings.
:::

The purpose of the exercise is not to produce one correct design. A researcher might begin with qualitative interviews and a feasibility study because the intervention is not yet specified; another service with a stable programme and adequate infrastructure might justify a pragmatic trial. Either choice can be defensible if the question, assumptions, safeguards, and claims are aligned. The quality of the proposal lies in the reasoning made visible.
