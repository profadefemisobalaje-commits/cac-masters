## Introduction

Research methods in marriage and family therapy (MFT) are not a technical appendix to clinical work. They are the means by which the field tests its accounts of relational processes, distinguishes helpful interventions from plausible but ineffective ones, identifies who benefits and under what conditions, and detects the unintended consequences that clinical enthusiasm may overlook. A research question about a family is also a question about how the family is represented: whether the unit is an individual, a couple, a household, a kin network, or an interaction sequence; whose report is treated as authoritative; and which social conditions are included in the model. Methods therefore carry theoretical and ethical commitments.

The distinctive challenge is interdependence. A partner's report is not independent of the other partner's behaviour; a parent's account of a child may be shaped by the child's account of the parent; siblings share environments; and a therapist's actions may vary in response to the family while the family changes in response to the therapist. Statistical procedures that assume independent observations can produce misleading standard errors and false precision when applied uncritically to such data. Qualitative procedures can also miss relational structure if each participant is interviewed and interpreted as though their account were isolated. MFT research must make its unit of analysis explicit and align its design, measurement, analysis, and conclusions with that unit.

A second challenge is the distance between efficacy and routine practice. A tightly supervised trial may establish that an intervention can produce change under specified conditions; it does not automatically establish that the intervention will be acceptable, accessible, safe, or equally effective in ordinary services. Conversely, a family's improvement in routine care cannot by itself identify which intervention caused it. Doctoral researchers must be able to reason across this continuum, neither dismissing controlled evidence as artificial nor treating it as a universal guarantee.

This chapter moves from question formation to design, measurement, analysis, ethics, and application. Section 2.1 develops the logic of research questions and the alignment of question, theory, design, and claim. Section 2.2 examines research paradigms and the complementary roles of quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods inquiry. Sections 2.3 and 2.4 address sampling, measurement, and the analysis of interdependent observations. Sections 2.5 and 2.6 consider intervention evaluation, causal inference, and research ethics. Sections 2.7 and 2.8 focus on evidence synthesis, implementation, and the construction of a doctoral proposal. A hypothetical case runs through the chapter to demonstrate how a clinically important problem can be translated into a sequence of answerable research questions.

The discussion assumes a secular, evidence-oriented academic frame. It does not treat any clinical model as self-validating. Theoretical coherence can guide a study, but only transparent methods and appropriately bounded claims can support conclusions. Readers should also remember that standards, data-protection law, ethics-review procedures, and professional regulation differ by jurisdiction; the researcher is responsible for checking the currently applicable requirements before recruitment or data access.

## 2.1 From Clinical Concern to Researchable Question

### The question precedes the method

A common doctoral error is to begin with a favoured method — a survey, a randomised trial, a thematic analysis — and then search for a problem to which it can be attached. The more defensible sequence is the reverse: specify the uncertainty that matters, identify what kind of answer would reduce it, and select a method capable of producing that answer. This sequence protects the study from methodological fashion and makes the proposal intelligible to clinicians, participants, reviewers, and funders.

A researchable question has at least five properties. It identifies a population or context; it names the phenomenon or intervention; it states the outcome or experience of interest; it defines a time frame or process where relevant; and it implies an answer that could alter knowledge or practice. “Does family therapy work?” is not yet researchable: the population, intervention, comparator, outcome, context, and meaning of “work” are unspecified. “Among adolescents referred to outpatient services for recurrent school refusal, does a manualised family-based intervention, compared with usual care, improve attendance and family functioning over six months?” is more precise, although it still requires a specification of eligibility, treatment fidelity, outcome measures, and the meaning of usual care.

Research questions can be descriptive, explanatory, predictive, evaluative, or interpretive. A descriptive question estimates prevalence or characterises a process: how often do couples disengage before seeking care, or what forms of support do families report using? An explanatory question asks how or why a pattern arises, while recognising that explanation may be causal, mechanistic, or interpretive. A predictive question asks whether information available at one time forecasts a later outcome; predictive accuracy does not itself demonstrate a causal mechanism. An evaluative question estimates the consequences of an intervention, service, or policy. An interpretive question seeks to understand the meanings participants give to an experience and the social conditions in which those meanings are formed. These categories overlap, but naming the primary purpose helps prevent a design from being asked to answer a different question than the one it can support.

A useful discipline is to write three versions of the question. The clinical version preserves the concern in ordinary professional language; the scientific version makes the population, construct, and inference explicit; and the participant-facing version explains the study without jargon or pressure. If the three versions cannot be reconciled, the construct or proposed design may be confused. Involving service users and community partners early can reveal that a technically measurable outcome is not the outcome families consider meaningful.

### Theory, construct, and operational definition

MFT concepts such as alliance, differentiation, emotional safety, conflict escalation, co-parenting, family functioning, and relational satisfaction are not directly observable in the same way as a recorded appointment date. They are **constructs**: theoretical abstractions inferred from indicators. Operationalisation specifies how the construct will be represented in a study, without pretending that the measure exhausts the construct. For example, “family functioning” might be represented by scores on a validated self-report instrument, structured observations of problem-solving, or participants' descriptions of coordination during a transition. Each operationalisation captures different aspects and carries different assumptions.

Construct validity is not established by naming a familiar scale. Researchers ask whether the measure represents the intended domain, whether its structure and scores behave as expected, whether it is distinguishable from related constructs, and whether it functions comparably across the groups in which it is used. A scale validated in one language, age range, cultural setting, or service context may not have equivalent meaning elsewhere. Translation alone does not establish equivalence; cognitive interviewing, local consultation, and empirical testing may be required. Researchers should report the exact version, scoring, source, permission status, and relevant psychometric evidence rather than relying on the label “validated”.

### A framework for question alignment

For intervention questions, a structured framework such as population, intervention, comparator, outcome, and time (PICOT) can expose missing elements. For qualitative questions, frameworks such as population, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, and research type (often abbreviated PICO or SPIDER in different traditions) may help, but should not force an interpretive question into a trial template. The framework is a prompt, not a substitute for intellectual judgement.

An alignment table is a compact way to test a proposal before data collection:

| Element | Proposal question to answer | Example: hypothetical study of couple conflict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Clinical concern | What uncertainty matters to practice? | Whether brief video feedback changes escalation in routine couple care |
| Population and setting | To whom and where might the answer apply? | Adult couples referred to two community clinics; eligibility and exclusions stated |
| Construct | What is meant by the key term? | Escalation defined through coded interaction and participant reports |
| Design | What comparison or account is needed? | Pragmatic allocation to video feedback plus usual care versus usual care |
| Outcome | What change is meaningful and when? | Conflict behaviour, distress, acceptability, and adverse experiences at post-treatment and follow-up |
| Inference | What conclusion will the design permit? | An estimate of the added intervention's effect under the study conditions, not proof of a universal mechanism |

The researcher should write the intended conclusion before finalising the design. If the intended claim is “the intervention causes improvement”, a cross-sectional survey will not support it. If the intended claim is “participants describe the intervention as restoring a sense of shared understanding”, a randomised trial's outcome scores may be insufficient. Design alignment is a form of intellectual honesty: it makes explicit what the study can and cannot know.

### Feasibility and value

A scientifically interesting question can still be unfeasible or unethical. Feasibility includes recruitment rate, staff capacity, participant burden, intervention availability, language access, data quality, follow-up retention, secure storage, analytic expertise, and realistic doctoral time. A pilot study may test recruitment, procedures, or acceptability; it should not be presented as a definitive effectiveness trial when its sample is too small for that purpose. Value includes likely contribution, relevance to affected communities, and the possibility that findings could inform decisions. The strongest proposal is not necessarily the largest study; it is a study whose question, design, resources, and claims fit together.

## 2.2 Paradigms and the Choice of Method

### Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods purposes

Quantitative methods represent observations numerically to describe distributions, estimate associations, evaluate differences, test specified models, or quantify uncertainty. They are particularly useful when constructs can be measured with adequate quality and when the question requires estimates that can be compared across people, time, or conditions. Quantification does not make a study objective by itself: decisions about inclusion, measure selection, missing data, model specification, and interpretation remain human and theoretically situated.

Qualitative methods examine language, interaction, documents, and other forms of meaning-making in context. Interviews, focus groups, observation, recordings, diaries, and documents may each be appropriate, depending on the question. Approaches differ in their assumptions: reflexive thematic analysis, grounded theory, interpretative phenomenological analysis, discourse analysis, ethnography, and qualitative case study are not interchangeable labels for “reading transcripts”. The chosen approach should define what counts as data, how interpretation is developed, what role theory plays, and how the researcher will make their analytic decisions visible. A qualitative study may generate a conceptual account or a nuanced description; it does not estimate population prevalence unless paired with an appropriate sampling and measurement strategy.

Mixed methods intentionally integrate quantitative and qualitative components so that one can illuminate, extend, or challenge the other. Merely placing a survey and interviews in the same project is not sufficient. The researcher must specify the rationale for integration, the sequence (concurrent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential, or another design), the priority of components, the point at which findings are brought together, and the inference generated by the combined evidence. Integration can occur through connecting samples, building one phase on another, merging data for comparison, or embedding one method within another. A joint display can make convergence, complementarity, and discrepancy visible.

### Pragmatism and methodological pluralism

A pragmatic approach often asks which methods are best suited to the question and its consequences, without claiming that all methods share identical assumptions. This can be valuable in applied MFT research, where a service may need to know both whether outcomes changed and how families experienced the change. Pragmatism does not mean “anything goes”. The researcher remains accountable for the validity of each component and for the logic that integrates them. Nor does pluralism require every study to use multiple methods; a well-designed single-method study can be more informative than an incoherent mixed-methods project.

Paradigmatic disagreement is most useful when made explicit. A post-positivist design may treat constructs as imperfectly measured phenomena and seek estimates with uncertainty; a critical realist account may distinguish observed events from underlying mechanisms and contextual conditions; an interpretive design may treat meaning as situated and co-produced in research interaction. These positions can overlap in practice, but they make different claims about what evidence is and how conclusions should be warranted. A doctoral proposal should state its assumptions in language that shapes concrete decisions, not as a decorative paragraph.

### Case Illustration: the referral question

::: casebox
**Hypothetical case — from referral concern to staged research.** A community clinic reports that some adolescent referrals for school non-attendance involve escalating conflict between caregivers and young people. Clinicians believe that a brief family intervention may improve attendance, but their records are incomplete and families describe different priorities. A doctoral researcher should not begin by promising a randomised trial. An initial descriptive audit may establish referral characteristics and the quality of routine attendance data; confidential interviews with families may clarify what outcomes matter and identify barriers to engagement; a feasibility study may then test recruitment, acceptability, and data collection. Only after the intervention and procedures are sufficiently specified would an adequately powered comparative evaluation be justified. The staged sequence is not a weaker version of science; it is a rational response to uncertainty.
:::

In this hypothetical example, three questions arise at different levels: What patterns are present in the clinic's referrals? How do adolescents and caregivers understand the conflict and the support they want? Can the service recruit, deliver, and evaluate the proposed intervention safely? The questions require different methods. Trying to answer all three with a single symptom questionnaire would reduce the problem to what that instrument happens to measure. A staged programme can preserve clinical relevance while making each inference appropriately modest.

The next methodological decision is the unit of analysis. If attendance is measured for each young person but family interaction is measured at the household level, the design must explain how those levels relate. If two caregivers provide separate reports, a disagreement is not simply error to be averaged away; it may itself be meaningful. These issues lead directly to the sampling and relational measurement problems examined in the following sections.
