## 3.3 Culturally Responsive Assessment and Formulation

### Assessment as inquiry and negotiation

Assessment is not a neutral collection of facts. The clinician chooses what to ask, which accounts to record, what is considered relevant, and how a description becomes a diagnosis or treatment plan. A culturally responsive assessment uses structured clinical skills while remaining open to meanings that the instrument or diagnostic system may not capture. It identifies the presenting concerns from each member's perspective, the family's own account of cause and desired change, strengths and resources, safety, developmental and relational history, and the external conditions affecting the problem.

A useful sequence begins with the family's reason for seeking care. The clinician can ask what led the family to attend now, what each person hopes will be different, and what they have already tried. Ask how the concern is named at home and in other settings, whether family members use different terms, and whether a particular term feels stigmatising or inaccurate. Explore the timeline and contexts in which the concern increases or decreases. The purpose is not to replace clinical judgement with the family's first explanation, but to understand their framework before imposing one of the therapist's own.

Questions about explanatory models can be framed in ordinary language: “What do you think is contributing to this?” “What worries you most about it?” “What kind of help would make sense to you?” “What would be a sign that treatment is useful?” The clinician can explore prior experiences with health, education, legal, or social-service systems and what the family wants done differently this time. Inquiry should be proportionate: not every session requires an exhaustive cultural history. The therapist should explain why a question matters, allow a person to decline, and avoid treating disclosure as a test of readiness.

### A multidimensional formulation

A clinical formulation should distinguish observations, interpretations, and hypotheses. It might describe a recurring sequence between caregiver and adolescent; note how housing instability and school exclusion intensify it; record that family members disagree about the meaning of the adolescent's withdrawal; and identify safety questions that remain unresolved. It should not convert a social identity into a cause or a group average into a prediction about an individual. Competing hypotheses should remain visible until there is enough evidence to revise them.

A useful formulation can organise information across six domains:

1. **Presenting concern and meanings:** each member's account, preferred language, goals, and level of distress.
2. **Interactional pattern:** sequence, triggers, responses, attempted solutions, exceptions, and consequences.
3. **Developmental and relational history:** transitions, attachment and caregiving experiences, kin relationships, and prior coping without assuming a single normative family pathway.
4. **Social position and context:** language, migration, racialisation, class, disability, gender, sexuality, age, geography, and relevant historical or institutional conditions.
5. **Resources and protective factors:** supportive relationships, skills, community connections, material resources, and prior successful adaptations.
6. **Risk, constraints, and uncertainty:** violence, coercion, safeguarding, access barriers, confidentiality limits, and questions requiring further assessment.

This is not a requirement to document every identity category. It is a prompt to include what is relevant and to identify what remains unknown. Formulations are provisional working tools, not authoritative narratives about a family. They should be reviewed with the people concerned when possible and updated when new information changes the picture.

### Distinguishing distress from difference

Clinical systems can pathologise behaviour that differs from a clinician's expectations. Quietness, limited eye contact, emotional restraint, a large extended-family network, indirect disagreement, or a strong preference for privacy may have multiple explanations. None should be assigned a meaning solely from a cultural label. The clinician asks whether a behaviour causes distress, restricts chosen functioning, creates safety concerns, or conflicts with the person's own aims. At the same time, cultural relativism should not become a reason to ignore coercion or harm. Respect for difference and protection from abuse are compatible obligations.

Diagnostic frameworks may help communicate patterns and support access to services, but diagnosis should not displace a relational and contextual account. A clinician can state that diagnostic criteria are met while also documenting the role of discrimination, trauma, poverty, language barriers, or family transition where relevant. Conversely, a social explanation does not rule out a mental-health condition that merits assessment and treatment. Avoid false choices between individual suffering and structural context: both may be real, and their relation may be complex.

### Culture-bound concepts and idioms of distress

People express distress through locally meaningful language, bodily experiences, relational concerns, and metaphors. Clinical services may recognise some terms and overlook others. An idiom of distress is not necessarily a symptom category; it can communicate suffering in ways that are socially intelligible and useful to the person. The therapist should ask what the expression means to the speaker, who else uses it, and what help it implies. A clinician should not assume that a term has the same meaning across countries or even across families.

The DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) is one structured aid for eliciting a person's definition of the problem, perceived causes and supports, coping, help-seeking, and preferences. It can improve consistency of inquiry when used flexibly and with training. It does not replace a comprehensive systemic assessment, establish cultural competence by itself, or compel disclosure. The CFI and related tools should be used in the version and language appropriate to the setting, with attention to licensing and current guidance. The clinician should explain the purpose and adapt pacing to the person's needs.

### Using measures responsibly

Standardised measures can support monitoring, but their use requires attention to literacy, language, cultural validity, accessibility, and the construct being measured. If a measure has not been validated for the relevant population, the clinician should not imply that its scores have established the same meaning as in a validation sample. Interpreters should not be asked to translate proprietary instruments spontaneously without considering standardisation and permission. If no adequate translation exists, the clinician may use qualitative inquiry or another appropriate measure and document the limitation.

A score that differs from a clinician's impression is an invitation to discuss perspectives, not a reason to dismiss one. A family member may interpret an item differently, or the measure may not capture their concern. Conversely, a reassuring score does not rule out risk when there are safety indicators. The therapist should ask how participants understood the questions and what the score means to them, particularly when the result may shape access, diagnosis, or discharge.

## 3.4 Language, Communication, and Meaning

### Working with interpreters

Language access is an ethical and clinical requirement, not a courtesy. A professional interpreter can support accuracy, privacy, and participation; using a child, relative, or untrained staff member as interpreter can create coercion, role reversal, omissions, and confidentiality risks. Family members may choose to assist in some ordinary contexts, but should not be treated as the default interpreter for sensitive clinical conversations. The clinician should follow local law, organisational policy, and professional standards regarding qualified interpretation and translated consent materials.

Before the session, the therapist should brief the interpreter about the purpose, expected terminology, turn-taking, confidentiality, and any safety concerns. The therapist speaks directly to the family member in the first person, uses short clear segments, avoids idioms and layered questions, and allows time for interpretation. The interpreter is not a co-therapist or cultural representative for an entire community. Afterward, the clinician can debrief about terminology or process without asking the interpreter to make a diagnosis or disclose information outside their role. When dialect, gender, or community affiliation affects safety or comfort, ask participants about preferences where feasible.

Interpretation is relationally complex. A member may understand a language but prefer to discuss emotion in another; partners may disagree about which language should be used; and code-switching may signal intimacy, authority, or exclusion. The therapist can ask who wants what language for which parts of the meeting. Ensure that interpreters understand confidentiality and that the family understands the limits. Remote interpretation may increase access but can reduce non-verbal information and create privacy challenges in the home; these should be reviewed explicitly.

### Communication styles and clinical assumptions

Communication is culturally patterned but not reducible to national styles. Directness, silence, overlap, gaze, gesture, emotional display, and deference can vary with context, relationship, power, neurotype, stress, and individual preference. A clinician should not treat eye contact as a universal indicator of honesty, treat emotional restraint as lack of engagement, or equate verbal fluency with insight. Nor should a therapist romanticise indirect communication or assume it is shared by all members. Ask how people prefer to communicate and whether the session format is making participation easier or harder.

Turn-taking in a family session can reproduce existing hierarchies. The therapist can invite each member to speak without forcing disclosure, notice who has not had a chance to contribute, and offer different ways of participating, such as writing, drawing, interpreter-supported speech, or a private check-in. These adjustments should be offered rather than imposed. In sessions involving children, adolescents, older adults, or people who use augmentative communication, the therapist should adapt pacing and format while preserving the person's agency.

### Confidentiality and disclosure across settings

Clients may worry that disclosure will affect immigration, custody, employment, school placement, benefits, or safety. The therapist should explain what is recorded, who can access it, whether diagnoses or reports may be shared, and the circumstances in which confidentiality has legal limits. Statements such as “everything is confidential” are misleading when exceptions apply. Avoid collecting sensitive identity information unless it is clinically or administratively justified, and explain why it is requested and how it will be used.

Family therapy creates a further confidentiality issue: the clinician may receive information from one member that changes the safety or meaning of joint work. The therapist should establish a clear policy at the outset about individual communications, secrets, records, and when information may be shared. Policies differ by jurisdiction and setting; no single “no-secrets” rule is appropriate for every family or every risk. The clinician should explain the policy before private disclosures occur, seek consultation when needed, and avoid promising a level of privacy that cannot be maintained.

### Building a shared language for change

Therapist and family may use the same word differently. “Respect”, “independence”, “support”, “discipline”, “family”, and “privacy” can carry distinct meanings. A useful practice is to ask for an example: “When you say respect, what would I see happening at home?” This moves conversation from abstract values to observable interaction without implying that one definition is correct. The family can then negotiate which changes are desired and which meanings should be preserved.

When clients correct a therapist's language, the response matters. The therapist should use the requested term, clarify if uncertain, and avoid asking a client to defend an identity. A correction is information about the relationship and the clinician's impact. Repeatedly returning to an unwanted label or name can damage trust even if the clinician believes the intention was neutral. Accuracy and repair are ongoing clinical tasks.
