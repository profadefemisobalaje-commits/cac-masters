## 1.6 Attachment, Emotion, and Neurobiological Theories

### Attachment theory and its migration into couple work

John Bowlby's attachment theory proposed that the human infant is born with a species-typical need for a protective, responsive caregiver, that the quality of early care is internalised as an **internal working model** of self and other, and that disruptions of attachment produce characteristic patterns of distress and defence that persist into adult life. Mary Ainsworth's observational procedure — the Strange Situation — supplied the empirical method, identifying the organised patterns now described as secure, insecure-avoidant, and insecure-ambivalent (resistantly attached), and later the disorganised pattern described by Mary Main and Judith Solomon.

The migration of attachment theory into adult relationship research began with the argument by Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) that romantic love can be understood as an attachment process, and it was consolidated by two developments. The first was dimensional measurement. Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz distinguished four adult attachment categories — secure, dismissing, preoccupied, and fearful — along the orthogonal dimensions of model of self and model of other; Brennan, Clark, and Shaver's factor-analytic work on a large item pool produced the two-factor structure — **attachment anxiety** and **attachment avoidance** — that underlies the self-report instruments now used throughout the field, most prominently the Experiences in Close Relationships inventory. The second development was the theoretical extension by Mario Mikulincer and Phillip Shaver, who proposed that attachment-system dynamics regulate distress across the lifespan and that secure attachment functions as a resource for affect regulation, exploration, and caregiving.

Two clarifications matter for doctoral work. First, attachment patterns in adults are not simply the childhood patterns grown up. Discontinuity is common, and the distribution of attachment classifications in adulthood is not a straightforward continuation of the infant distribution; longitudinal studies find both stability and substantial change, with change associated with intervening relationship experiences, loss, therapy, and social support. Second, self-report attachment measures and interview-based measures (notably the Adult Attachment Interview and its derivatives) do not measure the same construct reliably; correlations between them are modest. Research that treats attachment as a single variable measurable by any available instrument will produce findings that cannot be interpreted with confidence.

### Emotionally focused therapy

Emotionally focused therapy (EFT) is the most theoretically integrated and empirically supported synthesis of attachment theory with systemic practice. Developed by Leslie Greenberg and Sue Johnson in the 1980s, and elaborated principally by Johnson, the model treats relationship distress as the product of a self-reinforcing **negative interaction cycle** driven by unacknowledged attachment panic: one partner, feeling that the bond is threatened, protests, criticises, and pursues; the other, overwhelmed and inadequate, withdraws and defends; each partner's behaviour confirms the other's worst expectation, and the cycle becomes the couple's enemy rather than either partner.

![The negative interaction cycle in emotionally focused therapy. The therapeutic process proceeds through three stages that are subdivided into nine steps. In **de-escalation**, the therapist tracks the cycle with the couple, accesses and deepens the underlying emotions, and reframes the problem as the cycle, so that both partners can recognise their positions as protective rather than malicious. In **restructuring**, each partner is helped to access the primary attachment need and to articulate it in the other's presence, and the other is helped to respond; incidents of **attachment injury** — betrayal, abandonment in a moment of need, infidelity — are processed in the same terms. In **consolidation**, new patterns are rehearsed, integrated into the couple's narrative, and prepared for maintenance.

The evidence base is the strongest of any couple therapy model. Multiple randomised trials report significant reductions in relationship distress and improvements in satisfaction, including trials with distressed couples, couples with a depressed partner, parents of chronically ill children, and couples in which one partner has experienced trauma, together with process research showing that in-session emotional deepening predicts outcome. Meta-analyses report large within-group effects and moderate between-group advantages over the control conditions used, with the caveat that control conditions have often been non-specific, that follow-up periods are relatively short, and that several trials were conducted by the model's own developers or trainers. The methodological point is not to dismiss the evidence but to read it as a mature literature requires: effect sizes shrink when the investigators are independent, and the field still lacks large independent multi-site trials.

EFT has been extended to families (emotionally focused family therapy), and the underlying attachment framework has been applied to individual work with adults. The extension of an attachment-based model to whole families raises a theoretical question that remains open: if the couple's cycle is the unit of change in dyadic work, what is the corresponding unit in a family with several attachment relationships, and how is the therapist to hold multiple cycles in a single treatment?

### Observational research on couple interaction

The most methodologically ambitious research programme on couple interaction is that of John Gottman and colleagues, who from the 1970s developed laboratory observation of couples' conflict discussions combined with physiological monitoring and mathematical modelling of the interaction. The programme's theoretical contributions include the identification of behavioural sequences that predict deterioration — patterns of escalating criticism, contemptuous expression, defensive response, and withdrawal (popularised as the "four horsemen"), low ratios of positive to negative interaction during conflict, and physiological arousal that interferes with empathic listening — and the modelling of relationship change in terms of attractors in an affective state space, developed with Robert Levenson as the **dynamic interactional model**.

Two features of this literature deserve the doctoral reader's attention. The first is the methodological contribution: by measuring behaviour rather than relying on report, the Gottman laboratory made it possible to test theories of couple interaction directly, and it demonstrated the extent to which self-reports of communication are reconstructions rather than records. Second, the predictive claims require care. The frequently cited figures for accuracy in predicting divorce from observational data derive from specific samples and prediction models, and their generalisability has been criticised on the grounds that the models incorporate base-rate and sample-specific information, so that the headline accuracy figures overstate what the models would achieve in new samples. Subsequent work by many independent groups has confirmed the broad finding that negative affect reciprocity and withdrawal during conflict predict deterioration, while the specific numerical claims are best treated as model-dependent.

The practical implication for the theory of change is significant: if destructive patterns are observable and measurable, then therapy can be evaluated on whether it changes them. That shift — from evaluating therapy by whether couples feel better to evaluating it by whether measured interaction changes — is one of the field's most important methodological advances, and it has been extended to family interaction in the context of adolescent and child problems.

### Affect regulation, psychophysiology, and the neurobiology of relationships

The last two decades have seen a substantial expansion of neuroscientific and psychophysiological research on close relationships, and the field's theoretical literature has absorbed its findings eagerly — sometimes more eagerly than the evidence warrants. Four lines of work are central.

The first concerns **co-regulation**. Tiffany Field's early studies of mother–infant interaction and Edward Tronick's still-face paradigm established that infants regulate affect through interaction, and that disruption of the interaction produces disorganisation within seconds. Tronick's mutual regulation model proposes that dyadic systems are continuously repaired rather than stably attuned, and that it is repair, not constant harmony, that builds regulatory capacity. This proposition is directly relevant to couple therapy, where it reframes the goal from the elimination of rupture to the improvement of repair.

The second concerns **social regulation of physiological threat response**. James Coan and colleagues demonstrated that holding a partner's hand attenuates threat-related neural responses in the hypothalamus and anterior cingulate during the anticipation of electric shock, with the magnitude of attenuation associated with marital quality; Coan's social baseline theory interprets such findings as evidence that the human brain expects access to close others as a regulatory resource. The third concerns hormonal and autonomic mechanisms, including oxytocin research. This literature is more fragile than its prominence suggests: early findings of oxytocin's effects on trust and cooperation involved small samples and specific procedures, and several have failed to replicate in larger studies, while the construct of **vagal tone** as a marker of social engagement remains empirically unsettled, and the polyvagal framework in which it has been popularised is not generally regarded as established in physiology.

The fourth line is the popular literature on "interpersonal neurobiology", which has been enormously influential in clinical training while resting chiefly on extrapolation from basic neuroscience rather than on clinical trials. The relevant warning for doctoral readers is the documented "seductive allure" effect: explanations that include neuroscientific content are rated more convincing than the same explanations without it, even when the neuroscience is irrelevant to the logic of the argument. In this field the warning has teeth, because claims about the brain are frequently used to justify clinical methods that have not been tested by clinical research. The defensible position is that neurobiological findings may suggest mechanisms and generate hypotheses, but that they do not license treatment claims, which require clinical evidence.

::: practice
**Reading a neurobiological claim.** When a text asserts that an intervention "rewires the brain" or "releases oxytocin", ask four questions. First, what is the original study, and in what population and design was it conducted? Second, has the finding replicated in independent samples? Third, is the measure — neural, hormonal, autonomic, or behavioural — a valid proxy for the psychological construct being claimed? Fourth, does the argument move from correlation to causation, or from a laboratory effect to a clinical outcome, without warrant? Claims that survive these four questions are worth building on; claims that do not should be replaced by the behavioural or relational evidence that the intervention itself must generate.
:::

## 1.7 Social Constructionist and Narrative Theories

### The constructionist turn

By the mid-1980s the field's epistemology had shifted again, from the systems metaphors of cybernetics to the linguistic and interpretive resources of social constructionism. The shift was signalled by Kenneth Gergen's argument that psychological descriptions are not discoveries about a stable inner world but products of social interchange that acquire authority within particular communities. Applied to therapy, the argument implies that "the problem" is not an entity to be mapped but a description produced and maintained in language by particular people for particular purposes. The clinical corollaries were developed by Harlene Anderson and Harry Goolishian, whose **collaborative language systems** approach proposed that therapy is a conversation in which new meanings are generated, that the appropriate stance is **not-knowing** — a disciplined ignorance in which the therapist's understanding remains always open to revision — and that the client system is not the family as such but the **problem-determined system**: the set of people who are engaged in talking about the problem, which may include professionals, relatives, and neighbours, and which dissolves when the problem ceases to be talked about.

### Narrative therapy

The most influential constructionist practice is narrative therapy, developed by Michael White and David Epston and presented in *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (1990) and elaborated by White's *Maps of Narrative Practice* (2007). Its central premise is that people make sense of their lives by telling stories, that problems arise when the stories available to a person or family are impoverished or dominated by a single negative account, and that therapy is the negotiated re-authoring of those stories. Two techniques are particularly important.

**Externalising conversation** separates the problem from the person: rather than asking a child to describe what is wrong with her, the therapist asks about the influence of "the worry" or "the anger" on her and on the family, and about the influence she has on it. The technique is more than linguistic politeness. It is designed to reduce the shame and blame that accompany identity-level descriptions, to open the way for collective action against the problem, and to make visible exceptions — occasions when the problem's influence was diminished — which provide material for a preferred story.

**Re-authoring** develops those exceptions into an alternative account: **unique outcomes** (moments that contradict the dominant story), the identification of the values and commitments that explain them, the thickening of the new account with detail and witnesses, and the performance of the new identity in the presence of others. White drew on anthropological work for the practice of **definitional ceremonies** — occasions on which an outsider-witness recounts how the person's account resonated with their own experience — and on **re-membering**, in which the role in one's identity of living and dead figures is deliberately revised rather than passively inherited.

Two critiques are standard and should be examined together. The first concerns evidence: narrative practice has a modest empirical literature, consisting mainly of case studies, qualitative research, and a small number of trials in specific applications (for example, work with children and adolescents and with families affected by chronic illness). This does not render the approach ineffective, but it means that claims for it must be made with corresponding caution. The second concern is the limits of conversation. Narrative practice developed largely in outpatient work with families presenting relationship difficulties, and its instruments are least suited to presentations involving risk — severe self-harm, violence, acute psychosis, child protection proceedings — where assessment, monitoring, and the management of legal duties cannot be replaced by re-authoring, and where an approach that externalises problems may be experienced by family members as minimising danger.

### Solution-focused brief therapy

Solution-focused brief therapy, developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, applies the constructionist premise with unusual economy. It proposes that change is better served by elaborating solutions than by diagnosing problems, and it relies on a small set of conversational instruments: the **miracle question** (an invitation to describe in detail a future in which the problem no longer exists), **scaling questions** (rating progress on a defined scale to identify concrete next steps), the identification of **exceptions** (times when the problem did not occur), and the negotiation of small, observable goals. Later developments include the practice of beginning the first session without an assessment of the problem's history, the deliberate elicitation of the client's resources, and the use of compliments as a form of feedback.

The literature on solution-focused brief therapy is larger than for narrative practice, with randomised trials in school, mental health, and substance-use settings and several meta-analyses. The overall picture is of small to moderate effects on a range of outcomes, with substantial heterogeneity, and with the recurring methodological problem of the control conditions: when the comparison is waiting list or treatment as usual, the situation is favourable to any structured intervention, and several trials measure outcomes on instruments developed within the approach itself. The theoretical questions worth pursuing in doctoral work concern specificity — whether the technique adds anything to the relationship factors examined in Section 1.8 — and durability, since short treatments are particularly exposed to the risk that measured gains decay without follow-up support.

### Power, gender, and culture: the critical traditions

The constructionist turn did not by itself address power, and the field's most significant internal criticism came from feminist writers. Rachel Hare-Mustin's *A Feminist Approach to Family Therapy* (1978) charged the field with encoding traditional gender roles as structural health — the mother as the family's emotional manager, the father as the peripheral figure whose withdrawal was treated as normal — and with systematic double standards in the description of male and female behaviour. Later work developed the critique into a programme: the invisibility of women's relational and material labour, the way in which neutrality and circularity could be used to distribute responsibility for male violence across the whole family, and the frequency with which mothers were identified as the problem in child-focused referrals. The influential collection *The Invisible Web* (Walters, Carter, Papp, and Silverstein, 1988) and, from a psychoanalytic-systemic position, the work of Virginia Goldner on abusive relationships gave the field a vocabulary for describing couples in which conflict is not symmetrical and where the language of "complementary" interaction obscures coercion.

The cultural critique developed in parallel. The argument that family systems theory had been constructed on the experience of white, middle-class, Western nuclear households was pressed by a series of foundational texts: the comparative work of Monica McGoldrick, John Pearce, and Joseph Giordano on ethnicity and family therapy (first edition, 1982), Nancy Boyd-Franklin's analyses of therapy with black families across class and structure, and Celia Falicov's account of migration, acculturation, and the transition between cultural contexts as a central dynamic of "cultural borderlands". The **cultural genogram**, introduced by Kenneth Hardy and Tracey Laszloffy (1995), adapted the intergenerational instrument to the analysis of cultural identity, pride, shame, and the internalisation of dominant-group evaluations. In the two decades since, decolonial and critical-race critiques have enlarged the field's obligations further, questioning the export of Western therapeutic models, the universalism of concepts such as differentiation and boundary, and the global asymmetry in which the discipline's theory is produced in a small number of high-income countries and applied everywhere else.

The theoretical consequences of these critiques are not confined to ethics. If power is constitutive of family relationships, then a theory that describes systems in terms of reciprocity and feedback is incomplete: it lacks a mechanism for asymmetric influence that does not depend on each person's contribution. If the family's structure is shaped by culture, migration, religion as a social identity, class, and legal status, then a universal developmental timetable — the family life cycle as a single sequence of stages — misrepresents the lives of families whose trajectory differs. If the therapist is inside the system, then the therapist's own cultural position, its privileges and blind spots, are clinical variables. Each of these propositions is now uncontroversial in the field's literature, and each remains incompletely implemented in its theories, its measures, and its research designs.
