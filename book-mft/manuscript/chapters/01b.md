## 1.2 Structural Family Theory

### The structural claim

Structural family theory, associated above all with Salvador Minuchin and his colleagues at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic, holds that the central features of a family are its **structure**: the recurrent patterns of interaction that organise who relates to whom, in what way, and with what authority. Structure, in this usage, is not a hidden entity but an observable regularity. It is revealed by who sits where, who speaks for whom, who intervenes in whose conversations, how decisions are reached, and what happens when the family is under stress. Because structure is a pattern rather than a substance, it is described in relational terms — proximity, hierarchy, alliance, coalition, boundary — and it is understood to be both stable and changeable.

Three constructs carry most of the theoretical weight. The first is the **subsystem**: a component of the family defined by function, such as the parental subsystem, the sibling subsystem, or the spousal subsystem, each with its own tasks and its own rules of participation. The second is the **boundary**: the invisible line regulating the flow of information, affect, and contact between subsystems and between the family and its environment. Minuchin distinguished **clear** boundaries, which permit appropriate contact and appropriate separation; **diffuse** boundaries, which produce enmeshment and reduce autonomy; and **rigid** boundaries, which produce disengagement and reduce support. The third is **hierarchy**: the distribution of authority and responsibility, which in healthy families places the parental subsystem in an executive position with respect to younger children and requires that generational roles not be inverted. Structural pathology, on this account, appears as an inappropriate arrangement of these elements: weak executive authority, a **cross-generational coalition** in which one parent allies with a child against the other, an inverted hierarchy in which the child's symptom regulates the marriage, or an enmeshed subsystem in which differentiation is impossible.

### Method: enactment, joining, and restructuring

Structural theory is inseparable from its clinical method, and the method is inseparable from the theory: the therapist learns about the family's structure by attempting to change it and observing what happens. Minuchin described three overlapping phases. In **joining**, the therapist accommodates to the family's style — its language, pace, humour, and hierarchy — in order to gain access to the system without triggering its defences. Joining is not a technique of manipulation but a precondition of influence: a therapist who cannot be heard by the family's most influential member cannot restructure anything. In **enactment**, the therapist invites the family to talk to one another in the session rather than to the therapist, and then intervenes in the resulting interaction — increasing intensity, blocking a familiar move, redirecting a conversation from parent to child, or requiring a disengaged member to take a position. The third phase, **restructuring**, uses directives, boundary-making moves, and the deliberate redistribution of time and attention to install a new pattern that the family can maintain without the therapist.

The theoretical significance of enactment has been under-appreciated in the literature. It is the field's clearest attempt to solve the problem of observation. Rather than asking a family to report on itself, or inferring structure from diagnostic assessment, the structural therapist generates structure in the room and intervenes on it in real time. The approach thereby treats the session as a microcosm — a move that presupposes, but does not demonstrate, that patterns displayed in the consulting room resemble patterns displayed at home. That presupposition is an empirical claim of exactly the kind later observational research on family interaction could test, and the results have been mixed: behaviour in the laboratory shows substantial stability for some variables and considerable reactivity for others.

### Enmeshment, disengagement, and the empirical record

The enmeshment–disengagement dimension became one of the most widely used constructs in the field, and one of the most criticised. The critical literature identifies three problems. First, the concepts were originally formulated as poles of a continuum with the healthy range at the middle, but the clinical writing frequently treated enmeshment as a near-synonym for maternal over-involvement, which introduced a normative and gendered evaluation into what claimed to be a structural description. Second, the dimension overlaps with, but is not identical to, the closely related construct of family **cohesion** in the circumplex model of David Olson and colleagues, whose central hypothesis — that adaptive family functioning lies in the balanced middle range of cohesion and flexibility, not at either extreme — generated an extensive measurement programme using the Family Adaptability and Cohesion Evaluation Scales and thirty years of empirical debate about whether the relationship between cohesion and outcome is curvilinear. Third, the empirical work that has examined enmeshment directly has been conducted chiefly within the literature on adolescent development, where the relevant construct is **psychological control** and the evidence is that intrusive, autonomy-constraining parenting is associated with internalising and externalising difficulties, while warm and close, but autonomy-supportive, parenting is not. The structural distinction between clear and diffuse boundaries therefore survives in revised form: what matters is not closeness but control.

The treatment evidence for structural family therapy is more substantial than the theoretical controversy might suggest. The Philadelphia programme produced controlled studies of family therapy for adolescent substance misuse and delinquent behaviour (the family-therapy condition of the studies by Minuchin and colleagues and later by Howard Liddle and colleagues on multidimensional family therapy), and testable claims about the role of family interaction in the management of somatic conditions: the studies of family interaction and diabetes and asthma conducted with Maurizio and Dora Andolfi's group in Rome, and the well-known observations that symptom intensity in some children with brittle diabetes or severe asthma varies with family interactional conflict. Contemporary structural practice has been absorbed, in much of the literature, into broader integrative and multidimensional models — multidimensional family therapy, multisystemic therapy, and brief strategic family therapy — that retain the structural focus on hierarchy, alliance, and engagement in the family's context while adding interventions aimed at individual functioning, peer networks, school, and community.

### Case illustration: structural formulation in action

::: casebox {title="Case Illustration 1.1 — The presenting symptom as an organisational requirement (hypothetical)"}
**The referral.** A family is referred by the local school following a sudden decline in the academic performance of a fourteen-year-old boy, accompanied by episodes of asthma severe enough to require two hospital admissions in six weeks. The referring paediatrician notes that medical management has been optimised and suggests that family factors be assessed.

**The family.** Both parents work. The couple report that they have not discussed the state of their marriage "for years", and each describes the other as preoccupied. The mother manages all aspects of the son's medical care and communicates with the hospital directly; the father describes himself as "unable to do anything right" in relation to the illness and withdraws when the mother and son begin to discuss symptoms or schoolwork.

**Structural formulation.** From a structural standpoint the family presents an inverted hierarchy in the medical domain: the parental subsystem has no unified executive function. The mother and son form a strong subsystem whose organising theme is illness; the father occupies a disengaged position; the spousal subsystem is weak. The son's escalation of symptoms reliably produces a degree of parental coordination that is otherwise absent, and the sequence has the effect of suspending the marital distance by providing a shared (though conflictual) task. The symptom is not "caused" by the marriage in any linear sense; it is specified by the pattern that surrounds it.

**Intervention.** The therapist joins by taking the father's experience of exclusion seriously and by acknowledging the mother's burden without framing it as over-involvement. Interventions focus on re-establishing a functioning parental subsystem with respect to health and schooling: a shared task assigned to the couple rather than to the mother alone; an enactment in which the parents negotiate a plan in the son's presence while the son is asked to comment only at the end; and the deliberate discussion of the couple's relationship, which the son is excused from early in treatment.

**Methods note.** This case is hypothetical. It illustrates how the same set of facts can be described as an individual illness trajectory, a systemic maintenance pattern, or a structural imbalance, and how the description chosen determines the treatment offered.
:::

## 1.3 Strategic, Brief, and Milan Systemic Approaches

### The strategic tradition

The strategic tradition grew from the same Palo Alto soil as the communication theory examined below, and it shares a characteristic emphasis: therapy is a planned attempt to resolve a defined problem, and techniques are evaluated by their effects rather than by their theoretical elegance. Jay Haley's *Strategies of Psychotherapy* (1963) and *Problem-Solving Therapy* (1976) defined the essentials: therapy has a social function as well as an analytical one; the therapist must establish a position of sufficient influence to direct change while remaining within the family's hierarchy; and the presenting problem is embedded in a sequence of interactions that can be interrupted. Haley distinguished the problem-maintaining sequence from its historical origins, which made possible a form of therapy that was deliberately brief and deliberately non-historical.

Cloe Madanes extended the strategic framework with an attention to metaphor and structure in symptom presentation, notably the argument that symptoms may serve as metaphors that render a family's situation comprehensible, and that interventions may be designed to shift the relational requirement the symptom satisfies. The strategic tradition's signature techniques include **directives** (tasks assigned for the interval between sessions), **reframing** (the deliberate re-description of a behaviour in terms that open a new response), **ordeals** (assigning a burdensome task that must be performed if the symptom is to continue, thereby increasing its cost), and — most controversially — **paradoxical** prescriptions, in which the therapist prescribes the symptom or its continuation on the assumption that the family will resist the prescription and thereby change.

Paradoxical techniques require extended comment because they carry consequential ethical risk. The theoretical rationale is that a system resistant to direct instruction may mobilise its own resistance against a therapeutic instruction, producing change; the empirical status of the technique is weak, with little controlled evidence; and the ethical objections are substantial where the technique involves deception, where the family has not consented, where the presentation involves violence, self-harm, or abuse — conditions in which the prescriptive stance may be actively harmful — and where the therapist lacks the skill to manage an extreme response. Contemporary practice has largely absorbed reframing and directive tasks while abandoning paradox as a routine strategy, though it survives in some brief therapy traditions. That trajectory is instructive for doctoral readers: a technically elegant intervention may be abandoned not because its theory was refuted but because its risks were recognised as disproportionate to its plausible benefits.

### MRI brief therapy

The Mental Research Institute, founded in Palo Alto in 1959 with Don Jackson as its first director, developed the most economical of the systemic models. In *Change: Principles of Problem Formation and Problem Resolution* (Watzlawick, Weakland, and Fisch, 1974), the MRI group advanced a theory of **problem formation**: difficulties of living are universal and usually resolve themselves, but a small proportion become chronic because the ways in which people attempt to solve them — more of the same of an ineffective solution, denial that a problem exists, or attempts to change a rule that cannot be changed at that level of the system — succeed only in maintaining them. Therapy, on this account, is directed at the attempted solution rather than at the original difficulty, and it is by design limited to a small number of sessions with the explicit goal of interrupting the vicious circle.

The MRI model's contribution to theory is its economy and its emphasis on the difference between the problem and the *problem about the problem*. Its contribution to practice is a set of interventions — attention to interactions around the problem rather than to the problem's content, negotiation of concrete and minimal goals, tasks framed to interrupt the attempted solution — that have been absorbed so widely that their origin is often unnoticed. Its principal vulnerability is the assumption that the presenting problem and the system's boundaries can be identified without controversy; a model configured around a single defined problem has difficulty with presentations that are diffuse, multiply defined, or constituted by legal or child-protection processes rather than by the family's own account.

### The Milan group and systemic epistemology

The Milan group — Mara Selvini Palazzoli, Luigi Boscolo, Gianfranco Cecchin, and Giuliana Prata — began in the 1960s treating severe eating and psychiatric disorders with a psychoanalytic orientation and reworked their practice in the 1970s into the most epistemologically sophisticated of the early models. *Paradox and Counterparadox* (1978) described work with families in which the identified patient's symptom was analysed as part of an ongoing "family game" characterised by obscured alliances, disqualification of messages, and covert coalitions, with the ritualised prescription of the symptom as the central intervention. In the 1980s, following internal disagreement and the group's division, the model was reformulated around **hypothesising, circularity, and neutrality** (1980) and then around a positive connotation of the family's behaviour rather than paradoxical prescription.

**Circular questioning** is the Milan contribution of greatest durability. The technique asks each family member about the relationships and beliefs of others — "how does your mother think your father feels when your sister refuses to eat?" — thereby generating information about difference, comparison, and relationship at the same time as it introduces, through the very form of the question, the idea that relationships are mutual. The intervention is thus inseparable from the assessment: the family learns a systemic view of itself in the process of being asked systemic questions. The related device of the **reflecting team**, developed by Tom Andersen in Tromsø in the late 1980s, moved the observing team's discussion into the family's presence, so that the family could hear multiple descriptions of its situation and choose among them — a practice that operationalises second-order principles by making the observing system transparent.

::: keypoint
The Milan school's most durable theoretical claim is that the instruments of assessment and the instruments of intervention are the same: a question is already a perturbation. The claim has two consequences. First, it makes it impossible to regard assessment as a neutral phase that precedes therapy, since the assessment has already changed what it purports to measure. Second, it places a burden on the therapist to ask what the question itself does — whom it recruits as an observer, whose version it elevates, and whether the difference it installs is therapeutic or destructive.
:::

### Contemporary brief and systemic practice

The strategic and MRI traditions survive in two ways. First, their techniques are embedded in most contemporary brief work: task assignment between sessions, attention to interaction around the presenting problem, explicit goal negotiation, and the deliberate use of the session's end to consolidate change. Second, their intellectual descendants include solution-focused brief therapy (Section 1.7) and the various manualised brief models used in child and adolescent services. The empirical picture is uneven. Solution-focused and brief systemic approaches have moderate evidential support for several presentations, with the strength of evidence limited by short follow-up periods, heterogeneous outcome measures, and small samples in many studies; the specific claim that brevity itself is a mechanism of change — that fewer sessions produce better outcomes through the intensification of focus — has not been established. Doctoral readers should note the general pattern: brief approaches accumulate evidence of non-inferiority with respect to longer treatments for mild and moderate presentations, and evidence of inferiority for severe, chronic, and high-risk presentations where extended work and risk management are required.
