## 4.5 Formulation: From Assessment to a Shared Map

### A systemic formulation with individual specificity

A clinical formulation organises relevant information into a provisional account of how the difficulty arose, what maintains it, what protects against it, and what might support change. It is not a diagnosis of the relationship, a summary of every fact, or a technique-selection chart. A sound formulation integrates each partner's account, the interactional sequence, life history and current transitions, individual and health factors, cultural and structural context, resources, and safety. It makes uncertainty explicit and identifies what information is still needed.

The clinician can develop the formulation through five linked questions:

1. **What is the problem as each partner defines it?** Identify shared concerns, distinct priorities, and disagreements over the meaning of events.
2. **What happens between partners when the problem appears?** Describe observable sequences, triggers, interpretations, responses, and short- and long-term consequences.
3. **What individual, developmental, and contextual factors influence the sequence?** Consider learning history, health, work, caregiving, social position, and transitions without treating any one factor as a complete cause.
4. **What resources and exceptions are available?** Identify times when the couple handles the issue differently, supportive relationships, shared values, practical capacities, and individual strengths.
5. **What risks or constraints change the treatment options?** Clarify coercion, violence, acute mental-health needs, access barriers, confidentiality, and limits of competence.

The map should capture partner-specific meanings. One person may escalate to obtain a response; the other may withdraw to reduce overload; each response can intensify the other's expectation. This cycle may be a useful account when both partners recognise it and there is no coercive pattern that makes symmetry misleading. If one partner threatens, monitors, or punishes the other, the formulation must name those behaviours and their effects rather than translating them into a mutually maintained “cycle”.

### Differentiating precipitating, perpetuating, and protective factors

A practical formulation distinguishes **precipitating factors**—events associated with the recent onset or worsening—from **perpetuating factors** that sustain the present difficulty and **protective factors** that reduce harm or support recovery. These categories are not always cleanly separable. A job loss may precipitate conflict, economic uncertainty may perpetuate it, and a supportive relative may protect the couple from isolation. The therapist should avoid presenting correlations as established causes. Formulation is a hypothesis to guide observation and intervention; its value is judged by whether it helps the couple understand the problem, identifies feasible change, and remains open to correction.

The clinician should include the couple's preferred account of meaning alongside professional concepts. If the therapist uses terms such as pursue-withdraw, attachment threat, negative attribution, or demand-avoid, explain them in accessible language and ask whether they fit. Technical language can help organise clinical reasoning, but can also make the therapist's interpretation seem incontestable. A formulation should be concise enough to guide action and specific enough to distinguish the case from a generic description of couple distress.

### Sharing a formulation without imposing it

A collaborative formulation is offered as a proposal: “I have a possible map of what happens, and I want to check it with each of you.” The therapist can state what is observed, identify the hypothesised sequence, and ask each partner what fits or does not. Disagreement can refine the account. The clinician should not insist that partners accept a formulation because it is theoretically elegant. Nor should the therapist avoid offering a clinical perspective altogether; collaboration includes the professional's responsibility to provide a clear, reasoned view.

If partners do not agree on a joint formulation, treatment may still proceed with multiple parallel hypotheses and shared behavioural goals. For example, one partner may see a conflict as a response to neglect and the other as an effort to prevent criticism. They may nevertheless agree to pause an argument when voices rise and return to the topic at a set time. A shared plan does not require identical explanations, provided that the plan is voluntary and safe.

## 4.6 Treatment Planning and Selecting an Approach

### From goals to a treatment contract

Treatment planning translates the assessment and formulation into goals, tasks, time frames, and criteria for review. Goals should be meaningful to both partners, but need not be identical. Distinguish **relationship goals** (such as improved emotional connection), **process goals** (such as discussing conflict without intimidation), and **decision goals** (such as clarifying whether to continue the relationship). Where separation is possible, state this without treating it as treatment failure. Goals should be specific enough to guide sessions and flexible enough to adapt as circumstances change.

A treatment plan can identify: the principal concerns; each partner's goals; the agreed focus of couple work; any individual or specialist services required; the initial model or intervention rationale; session frequency and likely duration; indicators of progress; safety procedures; and the date or conditions for review. The plan should also state what would prompt a change—worsening safety, declining engagement, symptom escalation, no meaningful progress, new information, or a change in the couple's decision status. The therapist should obtain informed agreement and document revisions.

A treatment plan is not a promise of a fixed number of sessions or an obligation for partners to remain together. It is a provisional agreement about how therapy will be conducted. Review the goals periodically, and check privately where appropriate whether each partner remains willing and able to participate. A partner's continued attendance does not necessarily mean consent to the process if there is pressure or fear.

### Evidence-informed model selection

Several couple-therapy traditions have credible clinical frameworks and varying degrees of empirical support, including behavioural and cognitive-behavioural approaches, integrative behavioural couple therapy, emotionally focused couple therapy, insight-oriented approaches, and systemic or integrative models. No approach should be selected solely because it is the therapist's preferred school. The clinician considers the couple's goals, presenting concerns, risk, treatment history, preferences, developmental and cultural context, access needs, and the evidence base relevant to the population and outcome. A therapist may use an integrative plan, but should be able to explain its rationale and avoid incoherent eclecticism.

Behavioural and cognitive-behavioural approaches may be appropriate where partners seek structured work on interaction, problem-solving, communication, behavioural exchange, and interpretations. Emotionally focused approaches may be relevant where partners identify recurring attachment-related disconnection and both can participate safely in emotionally engaged work. Insight-oriented work may help partners examine patterns linked to personal history and relational expectations. Systemic approaches can focus on interaction sequences, wider context, and how attempted solutions maintain difficulty. These descriptions are broad, not manual instructions; model-specific procedures require training and supervision.

Evidence-informed practice combines research, clinical expertise, and the couple's preferences and circumstances. Average treatment effects do not guarantee an outcome for a particular couple. The therapist should be transparent about what is known, discuss reasonable alternatives, and use outcome monitoring to identify whether the selected approach is helping. If the couple's situation falls outside the evidence base, the clinician should acknowledge uncertainty rather than present a model as proven for that case.

### Sequencing and dose

A common sequence is engagement and assessment, shared formulation and goal setting, active intervention, review and consolidation, and closure or transition. It is not rigid. Safety may require delaying conjoint exploration; new disclosures may return the therapist to assessment; a lack of progress may require revising the formulation; a couple may complete a decision-focused process earlier than expected. The number and spacing of sessions should match need, accessibility, service conditions, and evidence where available. Too little treatment may not allow meaningful change; an indefinite schedule without review can foster dependency or avoid difficult decisions.

Session structure can support fairness and focus. A clinician may begin with a brief check-in from both partners, identify the agreed agenda, work on one interactional issue, and end with a summary and feedback. This format is not universal, but helps prevent sessions from being taken over by the faster or more forceful speaker. The therapist can adapt use of written agendas, turns, pause signals, or separate check-ins if these improve safety and participation. Any structure should be explained and evaluated with the couple.

### Individual sessions within couple treatment

Separate meetings can support assessment, safety, or focused individual work, but they change the clinical frame. Before offering them, clarify their purpose, duration, information-sharing policy, and how the therapist will prevent a perceived alliance with one partner. If private sessions reveal information that cannot be held within the agreed policy or that makes joint work unsafe, the therapist may need to pause, renegotiate, or refer. The clinician should not invite a disclosure without a defensible plan for responding to it.

Where a partner needs individual therapy, the couple therapist may coordinate with a separate provider with appropriate consent. Dual roles can complicate confidentiality, objectivity, and treatment goals. The same clinician providing both individual treatment and couple therapy may be inappropriate in some circumstances or jurisdictions. If unavoidable, the clinician should seek supervision and use a clear written agreement, while considering whether separate practitioners better protect each person's interests.
