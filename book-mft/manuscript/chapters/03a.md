# Multicultural Issues in Family Therapy {.chapter num="3" short="Multicultural Issues in Family Therapy"}

::: objectives
After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define culture as dynamic, relational, internally diverse, and shaped by historical and material conditions rather than as a fixed list of group traits.
2. Analyse how race, ethnicity, language, migration, class, gender, sexuality, disability, age, family structure, and geography intersect in family life and in access to care.
3. Identify how therapist and institutional power shape assessment, diagnosis, alliance, confidentiality, and intervention.
4. Conduct a culturally responsive assessment that elicits each member's meanings, explanatory models, resources, risks, and preferences without stereotyping.
5. Formulate relational problems in a way that attends to both interactional processes and structural conditions, including discrimination and economic constraint.
6. Adapt systemic interventions transparently while preserving safety, client choice, and the intervention's stated therapeutic purpose.
7. Evaluate cultural responsiveness in research, supervision, service design, and outcome monitoring.
8. Develop a reflexive plan for ongoing learning, accountability, and culturally responsive clinical practice.

## Introduction

Culture is not an optional layer added to family therapy after a clinician has completed the “real” assessment. It shapes the language in which distress is described, the meanings attached to roles and obligations, the routes through which help is sought, the authority granted to clinicians, and the material conditions under which families can act on therapeutic recommendations. The therapist's own cultural location and the institution's rules also shape what can be said, what is recorded, and which forms of family life are treated as ordinary or problematic. Multicultural competence therefore concerns the whole clinical encounter, not a specialised technique reserved for clients who appear different from the practitioner.

The phrase *multicultural issues* can suggest that cultures are bounded groups, each with a predictable set of values and behaviours. That model is inadequate. People participate in multiple cultural worlds, may identify with or distance themselves from categories assigned by others, and change their affiliations across time and setting. Within every named population there is disagreement, generational change, class difference, regional variation, political conflict, and individual choice. A family may share a language but not a migration history; siblings may interpret a tradition differently; partners may negotiate gender roles that diverge from those of their relatives; and a clinician may meet people whose identities are not visible in a first session. Culture is better approached as an evolving set of meanings, practices, relationships, institutions, and power arrangements than as a fixed property of a person.

Family therapy's systemic focus offers a particular opportunity and a particular risk. It can locate difficulty within interaction and context rather than assigning blame to an individual. At the same time, systemic language can obscure unequal power if it describes coercion as a reciprocal “cycle”, or treats a family's response to discrimination as an internal dysfunction. A formulation that calls a young person's resistance “oppositional” without asking what the young person is resisting may reproduce institutional assumptions. A formulation that interprets a caregiver's caution as disengagement without considering prior experiences of surveillance or poor treatment may mistake a reasonable protective strategy for pathology. Cultural responsiveness requires curiosity about context and equal willingness to examine the therapist's hypotheses.

This chapter offers a practical and critical framework. Section 3.1 defines culture, identity, intersectionality, and social context. Section 3.2 considers cultural humility, therapist positionality, power, and alliance. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 address assessment, formulation, communication, and the role of language. Sections 3.5 to 3.7 examine clinical adaptation across racialised and ethnic identities, migration and transnational relationships, gender and sexuality, disability, age, and socioeconomic circumstances. Section 3.8 brings these questions together in supervision, research, service design, and a hypothetical case. The chapter's central proposal is that culturally responsive practice is a disciplined process of inquiry, negotiation, action, and review; it is not a certification that allows a clinician to assume mastery of another person's experience.

The terms used in this chapter are not interchangeable across countries or communities. Labels such as race, ethnicity, Indigenous identity, minority, migrant, and diaspora have different histories and legal meanings in different jurisdictions. The clinician and researcher should use the language preferred by the people concerned, explain categories used in records or research, and avoid treating a national or ethnic label as a complete clinical description. The chapter is academic and secular in approach; it addresses social, relational, and institutional dimensions of culture without devotional instruction.

## 3.1 Culture, Identity, and the Context of Family Life

### Culture as process, not catalogue

Culture includes shared and contested meanings, practices, languages, histories, forms of knowledge, and institutions through which people organise social life. It is learned and reproduced, but also revised. It is embodied in everyday interaction and in material arrangements: who is expected to provide care, whose work is paid, who can travel safely, which languages are available in a clinic, and whose account is accepted as credible. Culture is not synonymous with ethnicity, nationality, or race. Those categories can be important, but they do not specify the full cultural world of any individual or family.

An essentialist approach attributes a behaviour to a presumed group characteristic: “families from this background do not discuss emotion,” “that community expects elders to decide,” or “this culture is collectivist.” Such claims may appear respectful because they recognise difference, yet they can constrain a person's options and make the clinician less curious. They convert a possible pattern into a rule and position the therapist as an authority on a culture to which the client may belong. Even broad comparative constructs such as individualism and collectivism can obscure within-group differences and changes across situations. They may be useful as sensitising concepts, but not as shortcuts from a label to a clinical conclusion.

A process-oriented approach asks how a specific expectation operates in this family, who endorses it, who contests it, how it has changed, what resources it provides, and what costs it imposes. It treats the family member as an interpreter of their own social world without assuming that any one member speaks for all. It also recognises that people may hold contradictory commitments: autonomy and interdependence, privacy and kin involvement, continuity and change. Contradiction is not automatically evidence of confusion; it may be a realistic way of negotiating multiple obligations.

### Identity, social position, and intersectionality

Identity includes self-understandings and the categories through which institutions and other people classify individuals. Some dimensions are chosen, some inherited, some imposed, and many are negotiated. Social position refers to a person's location within systems of advantage and disadvantage, including but not limited to racialisation, ethnicity, language, class, gender, sexuality, age, disability, citizenship, migration status, and geography. These dimensions interact rather than simply accumulate. Intersectionality, developed in Black feminist scholarship and associated particularly with Kimberlé Crenshaw's analysis of how legal and social systems can fail to recognise overlapping forms of discrimination (Crenshaw, 1991), directs attention to the specific configurations of power that shape lived experience. It is not a demographic checklist and does not imply that all members of a category have the same experience.

In family work, intersectionality requires attention to differences within a household. A child may be racialised differently from a parent in a school context; a family member with a disability may depend on another member for access while also seeking privacy; a partner's immigration status may alter economic and relational power; an older relative's language preference may affect participation in a clinical meeting. The clinician should not assume that the family is a unified cultural subject. Nor should the therapist presume that visible agreement in a session means equal influence. Questions about who speaks first, who translates, who is interrupted, and whose goals become the treatment goals can reveal relational and institutional hierarchies.

### Ecological levels and structural conditions

A family's interaction occurs within nested contexts: personal history, household relationships, extended kin, neighbourhood, school, workplace, health and legal systems, media, policy, and wider historical conditions. A systemic formulation that attends only to the household may be incomplete when the reported difficulty is shaped by unstable housing, unsafe working conditions, discriminatory discipline, inaccessible services, or a legal process. These conditions do not eliminate relational agency; they change the range and cost of available choices.

**Structural competence** describes the ability to recognise how social and economic structures shape health and clinical encounters, and to respond within the scope of one's role (Metzl and Hansen, 2014). It does not require a therapist to become a policy specialist or promise resources the service does not control. It does require distinguishing an individual or family process from a structural constraint, documenting relevant context accurately, and knowing how to connect families with appropriate advocacy or material support where available. “The couple cannot attend weekly at 3 p.m.” may be a scheduling fact, not resistance. “A parent is vigilant in meetings with school staff” may be understandable in light of prior discriminatory treatment, not merely a communication deficit.

### Culture across the family life course

Cultural meanings and practices change with development and transition. A young adult's movement towards greater privacy may be experienced by parents as healthy differentiation, disrespect, or both, depending on the family's history and current circumstances. Migration, a new partnership, divorce, disability, bereavement, parenthood, retirement, or changing work can reorganise roles and expectations. Generational difference is not identical to cultural conflict: age, cohort, class, education, language exposure, and access to digital networks may all contribute to disagreement. The clinician should ask how family members understand the transition and what they want to preserve or change, rather than assigning the disagreement to “traditional” and “modern” positions by default.

### The family is not a single cultural unit

The therapist should assess both shared practices and meaningful differences. Members may use different names for the same relationship, disagree about who belongs in a family decision, or have different thresholds for privacy and disclosure. One member may want extended relatives involved while another experiences that involvement as intrusive. An adult child may value interdependence but reject an expectation that they provide unlimited care. These are negotiable relational questions, not evidence that one person is more or less culturally authentic.

The family's own terms for its structure and relationships should be documented when possible. A household, co-parenting network, kinship group, or chosen family may extend beyond legal or biological relationships. Conversely, the presence of extended kin does not imply that every relative should be invited into treatment. Consent, safety, confidentiality, and client preference remain central. Family membership is a clinical question to be negotiated, not inferred from a clinician's preferred definition.

## 3.2 Cultural Humility, Positionality, and Power

### From mastery to accountable curiosity

The language of cultural competence can imply a set of facts to be mastered about distinct populations. Competence remains useful when it refers to practical knowledge and skills, but it becomes unsafe when it suggests that a clinician can become an expert in another person's culture through a brief training or checklist. Cultural humility, articulated by Tervalon and Murray-García in health-professions education, emphasises lifelong self-evaluation, attention to power imbalances, and institutional accountability. In MFT, humility is not passivity or an excuse for ignorance. It is an active stance: learn relevant history, ask rather than presume, acknowledge limits, repair misattunement, and remain accountable for how professional authority is used.

A therapist can ask open, concrete questions: “Are there parts of your background or community that you want me to understand as I work with you?” “What words would you like me to use for your family and relationships?” “Are there expectations from relatives or institutions that are important to this decision?” “Have previous services made it easier or harder for you to seek help?” These questions should be asked with a clear clinical purpose, not as an invitation to make the client educate the therapist. If a client does not wish to discuss identity, the therapist should respect that choice while remaining alert to context relevant to safety and care.

### Positionality and reflexive practice

Positionality refers to how a practitioner's identities, experiences, institutional role, and social location shape what the practitioner notices, expects, and can do. It also concerns how clients may experience the therapist. A therapist's race or language may affect trust for some clients, but no identity guarantees a particular response. Similarity can support connection and can also create assumptions of shared experience; difference can be navigated respectfully and can also activate mistrust or concern. The clinician should not require clients to reassure the therapist or perform a role of cultural interpreter.

Reflexivity is the repeated examination of how one's assumptions and actions enter clinical work. It is more than a personal statement about identity. Useful questions include: Which behaviour did I label as avoidance, aggression, enmeshment, or disengagement, and what alternative meanings are plausible? Whose account did I find easiest to believe? Did I mistake professional fluency or eye contact for cooperation? Did I apply a standard of “healthy boundaries” that fits my own cultural expectations rather than the family's goals and safety? What did I feel when a family challenged my formulation, and did I treat that reaction as evidence about them?

A brief reflexive record after sessions can identify assumptions, moments of uncertainty, actions taken, and questions to bring to supervision. It should protect confidentiality and avoid turning clients into objects of the therapist's self-development. Reflexivity is strongest when it changes observable clinical behaviour: clarifying an assumption, checking a term, revising a goal, arranging an interpreter, addressing a power imbalance, or acknowledging an error.

### Power in the room and in the system

Therapists hold institutional power through diagnosis, record-keeping, access to services, referrals, and professional language. In some settings, a report can influence custody, immigration, education, or child protection decisions. The family may experience an invitation to therapy as compulsory even when the form says voluntary. Clinicians must explain their role, the purpose and limits of confidentiality, who can access records, and whether information may be shared outside treatment. Ambiguity around these matters undermines trust and may expose clients to risk.

Power is also present within families. A systemic emphasis on reciprocal influence should not flatten differences in capacity, coercion, or danger. In contexts of intimate partner violence, child abuse, elder abuse, exploitation, or coercive control, describing behaviour as a mutual communication cycle can obscure responsibility and increase risk. Assessment must distinguish disagreement from intimidation, and reciprocal conflict from a patterned restriction of another person's autonomy. Joint sessions are not automatically appropriate. Separate, private assessment and a safety plan may be needed before inviting members into the same room. Cultural context may shape how risk is disclosed, but must never be used to excuse violence or deny protection.

### Alliance as negotiated collaboration

The therapeutic alliance in MFT involves agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and relational connection with each participant, while the therapist also manages the alliance among members. It is possible for the therapist to have a strong alliance with one person and a weak or unsafe alliance with another. Alliance should not be equated with compliance, warmth, or shared language. A client may disagree with a formulation and still be engaged; a client may appear agreeable because disagreement feels unsafe. Routine alliance questions can be helpful when responses are reviewed openly and not used as a score of cultural fit.

The therapist can explain the systemic frame in plain language and invite correction: “I am trying to understand how the pattern affects each of you, but I do not want that to imply that responsibility is equal. How does this description fit or fail to fit your experience?” This language both offers a hypothesis and preserves room for disagreement. When a rupture occurs, the clinician should name the specific action, listen to the impact, avoid defensive explanations, and negotiate a repair. If the therapist has used a harmful term or made a stereotyped assumption, a concise acknowledgement and changed practice are generally more useful than an elaborate apology that asks the client to comfort the therapist.

### Supervision and organisational accountability

Cultural responsiveness cannot be delegated solely to an individual clinician. Supervisors and organisations shape caseloads, interpreter access, treatment protocols, documentation, referral thresholds, and the time available for care. Supervisors can use case discussion to examine the therapist's power, divergent member perspectives, structural constraints, and the assumptions embedded in measures or service rules. They should also recognise that a supervisee may fear professional penalty for raising concerns about bias. A psychologically safe supervisory process is not one in which all actions are approved; it is one where actions can be examined honestly and repaired.

Organisations can audit who is referred, accepted, discharged, retained, and reported as improving; compare these patterns across groups where lawful and appropriate; and investigate differences rather than assuming that they reflect client deficits. Data should be interpreted carefully, with attention to small cell sizes, privacy, measurement equivalence, and the histories behind categories. Accountability includes changing systems when a pattern indicates exclusion, not merely offering staff another training module.
