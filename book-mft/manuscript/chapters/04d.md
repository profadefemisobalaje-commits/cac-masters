## 4.7 Managing the Therapeutic Process

### Alliance with each partner and between partners

Alliance in couple treatment is multidimensional. The therapist needs a working relationship with each partner and a workable process between them. A strong alliance with one person can coexist with mistrust from the other; a couple may be warm toward the clinician while avoiding the agreed work. Therapists should monitor each partner's sense of being heard, the clarity of goals and tasks, and whether the session structure feels safe. Short, direct feedback questions may help: “Did today's conversation feel balanced?” “Was there a point where I moved too quickly?” “Is there something you could not say in this format?” Responses should lead to discussion, not be filed away as a score.

Therapist impartiality is not identical to treating every action as equivalent. The clinician can respect both partners while naming a specific harmful behaviour and protecting the person affected. Avoiding “taking sides” should not become a reason to avoid responsibility or safety. If there is no coercion or abuse, the therapist can help partners recognise their respective contributions to a reciprocal pattern. If there is coercion, the therapist should not use symmetry as a clinical shortcut.

### De-escalation and emotional regulation

When interaction intensifies, the clinician's immediate task may be to regulate the session rather than pursue insight. Indicators include rapid turn-taking, repeated interruption, raised voices, contemptuous language, visible shutdown, or a partner's inability to remain oriented to the conversation. The therapist can pause, name what is happening without accusation, check each person's state, and re-establish the agreed ground rules. A break is not automatically a failure; it can prevent harm and create a chance to return more deliberately.

De-escalation should not require the less powerful partner to soothe the more powerful one or accept a compromise to restore calm. The therapist should assess whether the response is fear-based, whether a pause is safe after the session, and whether joint work remains indicated. When appropriate, a structured turn-taking exercise can help partners articulate one concern at a time, but it should not be imposed when a partner cannot speak freely. Clinical techniques are always subordinate to safety and consent.

### Rupture, misattunement, and repair

Ruptures can occur when a partner feels blamed, unseen, exposed, or pressured; when the therapist aligns too quickly with one account; or when an intervention misses the couple's meaning. A rupture may be explicit—“You are taking their side”—or indirect, through withdrawal, sarcasm, reduced participation, or missed appointments. The clinician should consider whether the rupture reflects a misunderstanding, a mismatch in goals, a harmful clinical action, or a legitimate challenge to the therapist's frame.

A repair process can include noticing the shift, naming the specific moment, asking how it affected each person, taking responsibility for the therapist's contribution where appropriate, and renegotiating the next step. Defensive explanations often deepen the rupture. At the same time, the therapist need not agree with every accusation; they can acknowledge impact while clarifying intention and inviting further discussion. Repair is demonstrated by changed practice, not by the therapist's apology alone.

### Stalemate and impasse

Couples may repeat the same argument in therapy, refuse a proposed exercise, disagree about the problem, or appear to make progress and then return to an earlier pattern. The clinician should treat impasse as information, not automatically as resistance. Possible explanations include an inaccurate formulation, incompatible goals, unaddressed safety, practical barriers, a treatment model mismatch, therapist reactivity, external stress, or a pace that is too fast or too slow. The therapist can ask each partner what makes the next step difficult and what they fear would happen if change occurred.

A structured case review can help: restate the treatment goal; check whether both partners still consent; review progress and risk; identify the therapist's assumptions; consider alternative formulations; consult supervision; and decide whether to continue, adapt, pause, or refer. Consultation is especially important when the therapist feels pressured to prove a theory, rescue the relationship, punish a partner, or avoid a difficult disclosure. Self-awareness is not sufficient on its own; use supervision and objective information where possible.

### Therapist use of self, bias, and supervision

The therapist's emotional responses can be clinically informative, but they are not direct evidence about either partner. A wish to rescue one person, irritation with another, boredom during repeated conflict, or urgency to prove that the treatment model works may signal something about the interaction, the clinician's history, fatigue, or the service context. The therapist should pause before converting a reaction into a hypothesis. Ask what was happening immediately before the response, which partner's account was being heard, whether a safety issue was minimised, and what alternative explanations are possible. Use supervision to test the formulation rather than recruit a supervisor to confirm a preferred side.

Supervision is especially important when there is unequal power, risk, legal involvement, strong model allegiance, or therapist uncertainty about the confidentiality contract. A useful presentation includes the referral question, each partner's stated goals, a brief sequence map, safety assessment, the therapist's intervention and response, and the specific decision that needs consultation. Where session recording is considered, obtain informed consent from both partners, explain access and storage, and allow either person to decline without penalty where feasible. Recordings should be used only for the purpose agreed and managed under applicable data-protection rules.

Therapists should monitor their own process without making clients responsible for it. If the clinician notices a strong pull to align with one partner, the response is not to force artificial balance but to examine the basis of the pull and return to observable behaviour, impact, and risk. If the therapist has acted unfairly, repair should be specific. If the clinician cannot maintain a useful position despite consultation, transfer or referral may be preferable to continuing in a compromised role. Professional accountability includes recognising when the therapist's presence is no longer helpful.

### Rupture after a significant disclosure

A disclosure of infidelity, undisclosed debt, sexual boundary violation, or another major event can reorganise the therapeutic frame. The clinician should assess what the disclosure means to each partner, whether there is current risk, what each person wants to know or not know, and whether the existing confidentiality agreement applies. The therapist should not use a disclosure as a dramatic intervention or pressure a partner into immediate forgiveness, confrontation, or separation. If disclosure changes the feasibility of conjoint work, explain why and discuss alternatives.

Some couples seek therapy after a breach of trust. Treatment may need to address the meaning of the event, the injured partner's safety and agency, the accountable partner's responsibility, and the conditions under which rebuilding trust is possible. “Forgiveness” or reconciliation should not be imposed as a required outcome. The therapist should not present trust repair as a simple sequence that guarantees restoration. Partners may choose different outcomes, and therapy should support informed, safe decision-making.

## 4.8 Monitoring Progress, Referral, and Ending

### Multiple indicators of change

Progress is not a single score or a therapist's impression. It can include reduced distress, safer disagreement, improved repair, greater clarity, increased emotional accessibility, changed division of labour, more effective co-parenting, or a decision made with less coercion. Partners may define success differently. A clinician should identify baseline indicators with each person and review them regularly. Brief validated measures can track relationship distress or individual symptoms when appropriate, but behavioural examples and client feedback add context.

A review can ask: What has changed since we began? What is still difficult? What did each person do that contributed? Are there goals that no longer fit? Has safety changed? Is therapy helping enough to justify continuing? Measure results should be discussed in plain language and not treated as proof that the couple is or is not trying. If one partner reports improvement and the other does not, the divergence itself is clinically relevant. The clinician can explore whether goals differ, effects are uneven, or a measure misses an important domain.

Routine progress monitoring can support responsive care when it includes feedback, shared interpretation, and the option to revise treatment. It can become counterproductive if used as surveillance, a condition of service, or a substitute for dialogue. If progress is limited, revisit fit, alliance, formulation, dose, external constraints, and treatment goals before concluding that the couple is resistant or untreatable. Consider whether individual or specialist care is needed.

### Referral and coordination

Referral is indicated when the couple needs expertise or support outside the clinician's scope, when risk cannot be managed in the current setting, when individual stabilisation is needed, or when the therapist cannot maintain an appropriate therapeutic position. A referral should be specific and collaborative: explain the reason, identify an appropriate service, obtain consent for communication where required, and make a plan for continuity. If urgent risk exists, follow local emergency and safeguarding procedures.

Coordination with individual therapists, medical providers, legal professionals, or social services requires clear consent and attention to role boundaries. Couple therapy must not become an informal forensic assessment, custody evaluation, or legal opinion. If a court or employer requests information, the therapist should clarify the request, review law and policy, seek consultation, and provide only information within the scope of the role. Do not promise absolute confidentiality where legal exceptions apply.

### Ending therapy

Ending may occur because goals have been met, partners have decided to separate, the work is no longer useful, a referral is needed, the service ends, or one or both partners withdraw. Planned ending includes summarising what has changed, identifying strategies and resources the couple can use, discussing foreseeable stressors, and clarifying how to re-access support if needed. The therapist should ask what ending means to each partner and whether there are unresolved safety or practical concerns.

A couple may have mixed feelings about ending. The clinician can acknowledge these without fostering dependency or abruptly disengaging. If treatment is ending despite lack of progress, review the reasons honestly and identify alternatives. A therapist should not extend treatment merely to avoid disappointment or end prematurely because the couple's choices differ from the clinician's hopes. When partners decide to separate, therapy may focus on respectful communication, co-parenting, or transition planning if safe and within competence; otherwise refer to appropriate services.

## 4.9 Integrating the Clinical Arc

### Hypothetical Case Illustration: pursuing, withdrawing, and the need for reassessment

::: casebox
**Hypothetical case — couple distress and changing safety information.** Two partners in a long-term relationship seek therapy after repeated arguments about finances and time together. In the first joint session, one partner speaks rapidly and asks for a plan; the other is quiet and agrees to attend. The therapist initially notices a pursue-withdraw sequence: one partner presses for discussion, the other becomes silent, and the first partner escalates. During separate safety check-ins, the quieter partner reports that disagreement about money can lead to threats to restrict access to a shared account and that they fear what might happen after the session. The therapist does not proceed with a communication exercise or describe the pattern as mutually maintained. They review confidentiality and safety, consult a specialist supervisor, and arrange a safe referral and follow-up plan. The more vocal partner is not confronted with private information without a risk plan. The clinician documents the specific account, the steps taken, and the limits of the current service. Conjoint work is paused because the conditions for free participation are not established.
:::

The hypothetical case shows why formulation must remain provisional. The initial interactional hypothesis was plausible but incomplete. New information changed the clinical question from “How can the couple communicate more effectively?” to “Can each partner participate freely and safely, and what response is required?” The therapist's task is not to defend the original model but to revise practice when evidence changes. The case also demonstrates that a referral can be the appropriate clinical outcome of an assessment, not a failure to provide therapy.

### Applied Exercise: construct and review a treatment plan

::: exercise
**Hypothetical exercise.** A couple reports recurring conflict about caregiving and household labour. One partner wants more appreciation and the other wants a fairer distribution of tasks. They each report that the other interrupts, and there is no disclosure of fear or violence during the initial meeting. Draft a one-page provisional formulation that includes both accounts, one observable interaction sequence, one contextual factor, at least two strengths, and the safety questions that remain. Then write three measurable goals, a proposed initial treatment approach, one progress indicator each partner can report, and two conditions that would require reassessment or referral. State how you would check whether the session process is balanced and safe.
:::

The exercise has no single correct model. A behavioural plan may focus on observable task allocation and collaborative problem-solving; an emotion-focused plan may explore the meaning of care and appreciation; a systemic plan may examine how work schedules, kin expectations, and attempted solutions shape the cycle. A defensible plan specifies why it fits, how it will be reviewed, and what evidence would prompt a change. It does not confuse a mutually negotiated goal with a therapist's preferred outcome.
