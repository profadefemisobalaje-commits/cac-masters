# Preface {.front}

This volume is a doctoral-level introduction to the theories that constitute the intellectual foundation of marriage and family therapy. It has been written for the first year of the doctorate, where candidates are expected to acquire a secure command of the field's theoretical inheritance, together with the critical apparatus needed to evaluate it.

Theory in this discipline is not decoration. A therapist who has no explicit theory still has one: an implicit set of assumptions about what causes distress, who is responsible for it, what counts as change, and whose voice should be heard. Implicit theory is not thereby wrong, but it is unaudited, and unaudited assumptions produce predictable clinical failures — blaming a parent for a child's symptom, escalating a couple's conflict in the name of honesty, treating a cultural difference as resistance, or abandoning a family because the therapist's preferred model has stopped working. The purpose of a doctoral course in family systems theory is to make theory explicit, comparable, and testable.

The chapters in this volume are organised around the major traditions of the field: the cybernetic and systems-theoretical foundations; structural theory; strategic and systemic approaches; intergenerational and contextual theories; experiential, communication, and humanistic traditions; attachment, emotion, and neurobiological accounts; social constructionist and narrative approaches; and cognitive-behavioural and integrative frameworks. Each tradition is presented in its own terms before it is evaluated, so that the reader understands what its authors were trying to do rather than only what later critics said about them. The chapters also examine the empirical record: which theories have generated research programmes, which have demonstrated effects, and where the evidence is thin, contested, or simply absent.

Three editorial commitments shape the text. First, the argument is presented in a plain academic register, with technical terms defined when they are introduced and used consistently thereafter. Second, the material is comparative: theories are placed against one another so that their commitments, predictions, and limits become visible. Third, the text is clinically honest. It does not promise more than the evidence supports, and it identifies the points at which the field is still divided.

The volume is a text for study, not a substitute for supervised clinical training, personal therapy, or professional regulation. Nothing in it should be read as a guarantee of outcome, and no account of clinical technique in these pages relieves the practitioner of the obligations imposed by law, professional codes, ethical guidelines, and the local systems within which practice occurs.

A word on case material. Illustrative cases in this book are hypothetical unless they are attributed to a published source, and hypothetical cases are labelled. Where a case is constructed to demonstrate a theoretical point, the point is the point; the family is a teaching device. Readers should assume that any resemblance between a hypothetical family and their own clients is a coincidence of the kind that family systems theory itself predicts — the patterns are generic, the particulars are not.
