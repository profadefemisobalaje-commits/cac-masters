# Genogram Notation {.appendix letter="A"}

The genogram is the standard multigenerational diagram used in marriage and family therapy for recording family structure, relationships, and functioning across at least three generations. Its notation is largely conventional rather than theoretically derived, and it has been codified most influentially in the work of Monica McGoldrick and colleagues. The symbols below follow that convention. Where practice varies between published sources — particularly in the notation of emotional relationships, where different texts use single, double, and triple lines with differing conventions — the variation is noted, and the governing principle is stated: notation must be defined for the record in which it is used, applied consistently, and explained to the family when the diagram is constructed with them.

## A.1 Household structure

The household is drawn as an enclosing line around the persons who live together, with the household's identifying details recorded beside or beneath it. A person who has left the household is drawn inside the boundary with a diagonal line through the box, or outside the boundary with the date of departure recorded, according to the convention adopted in the record. Where members of the extended family live in the same compound or building, a dashed outer boundary may be used to indicate a wider domestic unit.

## A.2 Descent and vital events

Birth order is recorded from left to right, with the eldest child nearest the couple line. Where children have different parents, the descent lines are drawn separately from the relevant couple lines. Deaths are marked with a diagonal cross through the symbol and the age or date of death recorded above it. Multiple births are shown by lines converging from the descent point to each child. Pregnancy, miscarriage, stillbirth, and termination are recorded by standard symbols and should be included where the family reports them, since their omission distorts the generational record and is frequently experienced by families as a silencing of significant losses.

## A.3 Migration, culture, and social context

Because structure alone omits what often matters most, the record should carry information on migration and displacement (places and dates of origin, moves, separations of family members during migration, language use within the household), legal and immigration status where the family raises it and where recording is safe, occupation and socio-economic change across generations, and religious or community affiliation as a social identity. Serious caution is required: written records of legal status, ethnicity, or religion create risks for some families and must be kept in accordance with data-protection law and professional confidentiality standards, and in some jurisdictions must not be created in written form at all.

## A.4 Relationship notation and the cultural genogram

Emotional relationships are recorded along the line connecting two persons: close and harmonious; fused or enmeshed; distant; conflictual; cutoff; and combinations such as close but conflictual, or distant and conflictual. Arrows may be added to indicate the direction of an asymmetric attachment, caretaking relationship, or hostility. The **cultural genogram** applies the same instrument to cultural identity, recording the family's experience of cultural pride, shame, discrimination, assimilation, and the internalisation of dominant-group evaluations of the family's group. It is constructed with the family and used as a conversational instrument rather than an assessment completed by the clinician alone.

## A.5 Recording conventions for doctoral work

1. **State the convention.** Every genogram presented in a dissertation, article, or clinical report must be accompanied by a key naming the symbols used, including any local conventions.
2. **State the source of the information.** Record who supplied each cluster of information. Discrepant accounts from different family members are data, not errors to be resolved silently: noting that a mother and daughter disagree about whether a grandfather left or was sent away is more informative than choosing one version.
3. **Record dates and ages, not impressions.** "Left home at 17" is data; "was in a hurry to escape" is an interpretation and belongs in the narrative, not the diagram.
4. **Do not over-diagram.** A diagram with ninety relationships marked is unreadable and functions as a display of thoroughness rather than an instrument of analysis. Record the relationships that carry the hypothesis.
5. **Anonymise.** In teaching, publication, and examination material, genograms must be anonymised: initials or labels replace names, identifying occupations and locations are generalised, and composite families are used where a single family would be identifiable.
6. **Review the diagram with the family.** Families frequently correct, dispute, and enlarge a diagram, and those corrections are among the most clinically informative moments of the assessment. A genogram that has never been shown to a family is an artefact of the clinician's research, not a family's history.
