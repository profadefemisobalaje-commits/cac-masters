# Comparative Grid of Family Systems Theories {.appendix letter="B"}

The tables in this appendix are working instruments. They summarise the traditions examined in Chapter 1 on dimensions that allow systematic comparison, and they are intended to be used in seminar preparation, in the construction of formulations, and in the design of doctoral research. Two warnings accompany them.

First, any summary of a tradition compresses it. The cell entries are claims about what a tradition *characteristically* holds; individual theorists within a tradition differ, and later writings frequently revise earlier ones. Where a grid entry is contested within the tradition itself, the entry states the dominant version and the reader should consult the primary sources.

Second, the grid is not a menu. A formulation selected by matching a presentation to a cell in a table is not a formulation. The tables are aids to comparison; competence consists in using them to interrogate one's own reasoning.

## B.1 Theories compared on core analytical dimensions

| Tradition | Unit of analysis | Core construct | Account of problem maintenance | Mechanism of change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Structural | Household subsystem organisation | Boundary; hierarchy; alliance | Rigid or diffuse boundaries; weak executive authority; cross-generational coalition | Restructuring through enactment, boundary-making, realignment |
| Strategic and brief (MRI) | The interactional sequence around a defined problem | Attempted solution; feedback loop | Ineffective attempted solutions are repeated and amplified | Interrupting the attempted solution; task design; reframing |
| Milan systemic | The family's belief system and premises | Circularity; the family game; hypothesising | Covert coalitions, disqualification, and premises that exclude alternatives | Circular questioning; positive connotation; reflecting team |
| Intergenerational (Bowen) | The multigenerational emotional unit | Differentiation of self; triangles; cutoff | Anxiety management through fusion, conflict, projection, or cutoff | Coaching; detriangulation; re-engagement with the family of origin |
| Contextual | The relational ledger across generations | Fairness; legacy; invisible loyalty; entitlement | Unbalanced give-and-take; destructive entitlement; unacknowledged merit | Multidirected partiality; acknowledgement; exoneration work |
| Experiential and communication | The here-and-now encounter and the message system | Congruence; report and command; punctuation | Incongruent communication; rigid punctuation; symmetrical escalation | Sculpting; immediacy; congruent communication practice |
| Attachment and emotion-focused | The dyadic attachment bond | Attachment security; the negative interaction cycle | Pursue–withdraw cycle driven by unacknowledged attachment panic | Emotion reprocessing; cycle de-escalation; restructuring responsiveness |
| Cognitive-behavioural | Observable exchange and cognition within the dyad or parent–child system | Reinforcement ratio; attribution; standards | Low reward-to-cost ratio; negative attributions; unrealistic standards | Behaviour exchange; communication skills; cognitive restructuring; acceptance work |
| Social constructionist and narrative | The meaning system and the conversational network | Story; problem-saturated account; language | Dominant stories that totalise identity; problem talk sustained by the network | Externalising; re-authoring; unique outcomes; solution elaboration |
| Integrative (metaframeworks, common factors) | Problem-defined system across levels | Lens selection; common factors | The problem is maintained across several levels simultaneously | Sequenced interventions selected by formulation; alliance and outcome monitoring |

Table: B.1 — Core analytical dimensions of the major traditions.

## B.2 Therapist stance, session composition, and typical duration

| Tradition | Therapist stance | Who attends | Typical duration | Characteristic risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Structural | Active, directive, accommodating during joining | Whole household; subsystems seen separately as needed | Weeks to months | Imposing the therapist's map; insufficient attention to violence |
| Strategic and brief | Directive, problem-focused, influential | Those relevant to the defined problem | One to ten sessions | Manipulation; inadequate assessment; paradox misapplied |
| Milan systemic | Curious, neutral in discipline, hypothesising | Family plus reflecting team | Months | Neutrality misread as passivity or evasion in the face of harm |
| Intergenerational (Bowen) | Coach; teacher of the theory | One person, a couple, or a family; family of origin work between sessions | Months to years | Intellectualisation; underestimation of structural constraint |
| Contextual | Advocate for all parties in turn | Whoever can be present; absent and deceased members named | Variable; often medium-term | Acknowledging harm may be heard as excusing it |
| Experiential and communication | Personal, spontaneous, use of self | Family, often with co-therapy team | Variable | Dependence on therapist charisma; weak manualisation |
| Attachment and emotion-focused | Attuned, process-guiding, actively shaping emotion | Couple (or family in family versions) | Eight to twenty sessions typically | Decentring practical and structural problems; suitability with current violence |
| Cognitive-behavioural | Structured, collaborative, data-oriented | Couple, parent(s), or family depending on focus | Ten to twenty sessions typically | Rigidity; under-attention to affect and to systemic context |
| Constructionist and narrative | Curious, decentred, influential | Those who define the problem, including professionals | Variable; often brief | Limits under risk; claims exceeding the evidence base |
| Integrative | Formulation-driven; stance selected deliberately | Determined by the formulation | Planned and monitored, then revised | Eclecticism without a controlling framework |

## B.3 A checklist for evaluating a theory

1. **Unit of analysis.** What does the theory take as its system, and how are its boundaries drawn?
2. **Observables.** What would an observer measure in order to see the theory's constructs in operation?
3. **Prediction.** What does the theory predict that a rival theory does not?
4. **Disconfirmation.** What result would count as evidence against the theory?
5. **Mechanism.** By what causal pathway does change in the theory's terms produce change in the presenting problem?
6. **Evidence.** What is the state of the outcome, process, and mediation evidence, and how independent is it of the theory's proponents?
7. **Scope and limits.** For which presentations, ages, structures, and settings has the theory been tested, and for which has it not?
8. **Power and harm.** How does the theory describe relationships in which power is unequal or harm is occurring?
9. **Culture.** Whose family form does the theory implicitly assume? What happens to its constructs when applied across cultures?
10. **Utility.** What does a competent practitioner do differently because of this theory, and would clients notice?

These ten questions can be applied to any formulation, model, or published claim. A doctoral candidate who can answer them fluently about any tradition in the field has achieved what this course is designed to teach.
