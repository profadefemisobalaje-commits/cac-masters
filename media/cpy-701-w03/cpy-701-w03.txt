CPY 701 — Week 3 — Counselling Techniques and Interventions
===========================================================

[slide 01 · title · 63.5s]
Welcome to Week Three of CPY 701. Last week we studied theory, and I told you that the skills we would examine today belong to no single tradition. That was deliberate. Today we leave the conceptual world and enter the consulting room. Our subject is counselling technique, and I want to state my argument for the session plainly at the outset, because it will shape everything that follows. The argument is this: effective counselling rests on a small number of foundational skills, and the mark of a competent practitioner is not the cleverness of their interventions but the discipline of their restraint. Most of the harm that beginning counsellors do comes not from failing to say something impressive, but from saying too much, too soon, and with too little evidence. By the end of this lecture you should be able to name the core skills, recognise them in a transcript, and, most importantly, know when to stay silent.

[slide 02 · objectives · 76.7s]
Here is what you should be able to do by the end of this session. First, distinguish a counselling skill from a counselling technique, and explain why the difference matters in practice rather than only in a textbook. Second, describe the microskills hierarchy and the order in which the foundational skills are properly learned. Third, demonstrate accurate attending behaviour, including its nonverbal dimension and its cultural variation. Fourth, discriminate between open, closed, probing and confrontational questions, and predict where each of them tends to fail. Fifth, reflect content, feeling and meaning, and distinguish all three from reassurance and advice, which are the two responses that beginners reach for most often. Sixth, sequence a first session, and identify the five errors that most reliably damage one. And seventh, evaluate a short transcript and justify which response you would have made instead. I want to draw your attention to that final objective. Being able to say what you would have said instead, and why, is the core skill of supervision. If you cannot do it, your supervisor cannot help you, because there is nothing for them to examine.

[slide 03 · section · 31.1s]
Part One concerns what technique actually is, because a great deal of confusion surrounds the word, and that confusion damages beginners. When students arrive on a counselling course, most of them are looking for the impressive intervention, the well-turned interpretation, the decisive question. What they discover, usually with some disappointment, is that the field's real expertise lies elsewhere, in something much harder to show off.

[slide 04 · definition · 104.8s]
Let me lay out three levels of practice, because the words are used loosely and the looseness costs you precision. At the first level is a skill. A skill is a discrete, trainable capacity to respond. Reflecting a feeling accurately is a skill. Making eye contact appropriately is a skill. Asking a question that opens rather than closes the client's exploration is a skill. Skills are small, they are learnable, and they can be observed and counted. At the second level is a technique. A technique is an organised sequence of skills applied for a specific purpose. A two-chair dialogue from gestalt therapy is a technique. So is a thought record from cognitive therapy, and so is a genogram. Techniques are composed of skills, and they are longer and more structured. At the third level is an intervention. An intervention is the deliberate use of a technique, chosen for this particular client, at this particular moment, on the basis of a formulation. This is the level at which clinical judgement operates, and it is the level you will spend your career developing. Here is the analogy I want you to carry. Skills are the alphabet. Techniques are the words. Interventions are the sentences. You cannot write sentences without words, and you cannot write words without letters. That is why this lecture focuses on the alphabet. It is also why the course is structured the way it is: you meet theory first, then skills, then, in the later courses and in your placements, the composing of whole interventions. And notice the implication. If you attempt advanced techniques before consolidating the foundational skills, you will produce something that looks like therapy and does not function like it.

[slide 05 · bullets · 138.8s]
Now let me name five beliefs that beginning counsellors hold, because each of them is going to get in your way, and each of them is false. The first myth is that the goal is to find the brilliant intervention. Beginners search for the decisive question or the memorable interpretation, and they imagine that clinical skill consists of producing it. The reality is that the great majority of effective counselling moments are ordinary. A reflection delivered accurately and at the right moment does more work than a striking interpretation delivered too early, and it does it more reliably. The second myth is that more technique means more competence. When you compare experienced counsellors with beginners on observable behaviour, the experienced practitioners typically do less. They speak fewer words. They ask fewer questions. They offer fewer interpretations. And they spend proportionally more of the session attending to what the client has actually said. Competence looks like economy, not abundance. The third myth is that technique can be learned quickly. Skills consolidate slowly, and, critically, they decay without practice. Accurate reflecting takes months of deliberate work to become reliable under pressured conditions, and a weekend workshop does not produce it. That is why the skills laboratory in this course is where the learning actually happens. The fourth myth is that a good technique works regardless of the relationship. The same reflection lands completely differently depending on whether the client feels understood by you. If they do, the reflection deepens their exploration. If they do not, the identical words feel like being managed. Technique is delivered through the alliance, not alongside it. The fifth myth is that silence is wasted time. I want to correct this one firmly, because beginners fill silence almost compulsively. Silence is a technique. It is frequently the response that permits a client to reach something they have not yet put into words, and a counsellor who cannot tolerate silence cannot help a client find those words. We will return to silence repeatedly this session.

[slide 06 · section · 30.7s]
Part Two introduces the foundational skills themselves. There is no universally agreed list, but there is wide agreement about the core, and I am going to teach you one sequence drawn from the microskills tradition associated with Allen Ivey and Clara Hill. The sequence matters, because each skill depends on the one before it. You cannot reflect accurately what you have not heard accurately, and you cannot hear accurately unless you are genuinely attending.

[slide 07 · process · 101.5s]
Here is the hierarchy, and the reason it is a hierarchy rather than a list is that each skill depends on those below it. Attending comes first. Attending is being physically and psychologically present to this particular person, and it is not passive. It involves orienting your body, regulating your own internal noise, and bringing your attention back when it wanders, which it will. Listening comes second, and I am going to spend time on it shortly, because it is far more demanding than the word suggests. It means receiving what is said and simultaneously noticing what has not been said, which is often where the work is. Reflecting comes third. Reflecting is returning content, feeling and meaning accurately, and the word accurately is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. A reflection that is approximately right is not a service to the client, because it invites them to agree with a version of themselves that is slightly wrong. Questioning comes fourth, which surprises many students. It follows listening, and a question asked before you have listened properly is an interruption wearing a helpful face. Summarising comes fifth. Summarising draws threads together, gives the session shape, and provides direction. Notice the order of the middle three: reflect before you question. Most beginning counsellors do the reverse, questioning in order to gather information that would have arrived on its own if they had waited through one more silence. And that gives you the practical rule for this slide: when you feel the impulse to ask a question, try waiting one more beat.

[slide 08 · bullets · 138.1s]
Let us begin with attending, which is the most basic skill and, in my experience, the one most often performed automatically rather than intentionally. Attending concerns how you place your body and your attention. Start with orientation. Facing a client at a slight angle rather than directly square-on reads as less interrogative. A relaxed posture signals that there is time, and that signal matters enormously to a client who is afraid of being a burden. Stillness signals that you are not about to leave or to move on. These are the physical form of the sentence: I am here, and I am not going anywhere. Next, eye contact. This is culturally coded, and you must read it rather than assume it. In many Nigerian and West African contexts, sustained direct eye contact with an elder or an authority figure reads as insolence rather than as engagement, and a client may show deference through an averted gaze. This matters practically because a Western-trained framework will often record low eye contact as poor rapport, or as evasiveness, or as a sign of shame, when the client is in fact demonstrating respect. So when you conduct your first assessments, notice what you are inferring from gaze, and check the inference rather than trusting it. And then there is the uncomfortable part. Attention is a finite resource, and its lapses are visible. Clients detect drift within seconds. Boredom, judgement and preoccupation leak through posture long before they appear in anything you say. You may believe you are concealing your distraction. You are not. The practical implication is that managing your own state is part of the skill, not a preliminary to it. If you are preoccupied, the honest options are to notice it and return, or, if it persists, to address it, sometimes openly. I should add that most seasoned practitioners will, at some point in their careers, say something to a client like, I noticed I was somewhere else just now. That disclosure, handled well, often strengthens the alliance rather than damaging it, because it models the honesty the client is being asked to bring.

[slide 09 · bullets · 134.9s]
Now listening, which is a far more demanding activity than the word implies, and which is not the same as hearing. Hearing is passive and continuous. Listening is active and selective, and it can be done badly or well. There are four dimensions I want you to practise. First, listen to what is present and to what is absent. Notice the topic the client circles around but never enters. Notice the person who is never mentioned. Notice the feeling that goes conspicuously unlabelled. A client who describes a devastating event in flat, factual language is telling you something, and what they are telling you is not that they feel nothing. Absence is data, and in the early sessions it is often the richest data available. Second, listen at three levels at once. The content level is what happened. The feeling level is how it registered. The meaning level is what it says about the client, or about their world, or about how the world works. Take one sentence: my husband forgot our anniversary again. Content, a fact. Feeling, hurt and humiliation. Meaning, something like I am not important to him. A counsellor listening only at the content level will respond with a question; a counsellor at the meaning level has something to reflect. Third, listen without rehearsing your response. Composing your next sentence while the client is still speaking is the most common failure of attention I see in beginners, and it is invisible to the person doing it, because it feels like being prepared. It is not preparation. It is abandonment of the present moment in favour of an imaginary future one. If you notice yourself drafting a response, let the draft go and return to the client's voice. Fourth, listen without fixing. The impulse to solve arrives early and feels like competence. In my experience it is usually our own discomfort with the client's distress arriving ahead of our willingness to sit with it. When you notice yourself reaching for a solution in the first session, ask yourself honestly whose anxiety you are addressing.

[slide 10 · table · 135.2s]
Now questioning, and I want to start with a warning. Questions are a high-power intervention. They direct attention, they set the agenda, and they place you in control of the conversation. Because they are powerful, they should be used sparingly, and beginners overuse them by a wide margin. Let me give you four types, with a rule for each. The open question is the workhorse of the whole repertoire. Tell me more about that. Say more. What was that like for you. An open question invites exploration and does not require you to know the shape of the answer. This type should dominate your repertoire. Convert as many of your questions as you can into open ones. The closed question, did you tell your sister, invites a yes or a no and closes the topic. It has a legitimate use, and I want to name it precisely. Use it when you need a specific fact to keep the client safe or to decide on your next step. If you need to know whether a client is presently safe, ask a closed question, clearly. The failure mode is not the closed question itself, it is the habit. A string of closed questions turns a session into an interrogation, and clients answer fully but reveal nothing. The probing question, what happened just before you felt that, deepens a specific thread. It is legitimate when you have listened well and the client has signalled readiness to go further. It fails when asked before the alliance is established, because it then reads as intrusion. And the confrontational question, you say you want to leave, yet you stay, how do you understand that, points out a discrepancy. Confrontation has a place in skilled hands, and I want to be careful how I say this. It requires strong evidence, a solid alliance, and a client who is ready to hear it. Asked too early, which is when beginners ask it, confrontation produces shame and withdrawal, and it is the error I most often see in students' recorded sessions. Note that these four are presented in a hierarchy of risk: the safer the question, the more you should use it, and the riskier the question, the stronger your reason must be.

[slide 11 · bullets · 136.5s]
Reflecting is the skill that most distinguishes trained practice from well-intentioned conversation, so I am going to give you three levels of it. Reflecting content returns the facts accurately and without interpretation. So the restructuring was announced on Friday, and you found out from a colleague. Its function is precise. It confirms to the client that you have followed, and it slows the conversation down enough that both of you can see it. Reflecting feeling names the feeling, tentatively, and it invites correction. You sound humiliated by that, not just annoyed. Notice the tentativeness. It is not weakness. Being corrected is part of how a client gets precise about their own experience, and a client who says, no, it is more like shame than humiliation, has just done important work. So do not fear being wrong in a reflection; fear being vague. The vagueness of a reflection like that must have been difficult is the problem, not the inaccuracy. Reflecting meaning connects the feeling to a belief or to the client's sense of their world. It sounds as though the message you took was that your contribution has never really been seen. This is the most powerful of the three, and it is the easiest to get wrong, because it is the level at which your own formulation enters the room. When a meaning reflection lands, the session turns. When it misses, it lands as being told what they think, and the client withdraws. That is why the more interpretive your reflection, the more tentatively it should be offered, and the more ready you must be to abandon it. Finally, two counterfeits that I want you to eliminate from your speech deliberately. The first is reassurance. I am sure they value you really, or, you are doing better than you think. The second is advice, and you should speak to human resources about it. Both feel helpful, and both relieve your discomfort rather than the client's. Both foreclose exploration, because once you have offered a solution or a verdict, there is nothing left for the client to discover. In your recorded skills sessions, I will be listening for these two in particular.

[slide 12 · bullets · 112.5s]
Summarising and structuring come together as the fifth skill, and they are what gives a session a shape rather than a drift. A summary tells the client that you have been keeping track. Let me see if I have this. The job loss in March, the silence from your brother since then, and the sense that you have to seem fine for your mother. Three threads, named, and notice that the summary is offered in the client's own language rather than translated into clinical vocabulary. That is not a stylistic preference. When you convert a client's words into clinical terms, even accurately, you convert a shared understanding into an assessment carried out upon them, and something is lost in the conversion. Now, when to summarise. Beginners remember to summarise at the end, and that is the least useful time. Summaries at the start of a session reconnect the work to last week. Summaries at a change of topic mark the transition and prevent the hour from becoming a sequence of unrelated conversations. Summaries at the close consolidate the work and set up the next session. And a summary delivered mid-session quietly says, I have been holding all of this, and you can put some of it down. Structuring is the same skill applied to the frame of the session. We have twenty minutes left. I would like to spend them on the part about your father. Is that the right place to put them? That sentence does three things: it announces the remaining time, names the priority, and asks permission, which keeps the agenda shared. Structure prevents drift, and I want to be explicit about why drift matters more in your programme than in most. You are on a compressed twelve-month masters. If a session drifts, you lose an hour. If a series of sessions drifts, you lose the treatment. And the client, not the practitioner, pays for that.

[slide 13 · section · 22.3s]
Part Three moves from individual skills to sequence, because in practice the skills never appear alone. They appear woven together, in real time, under the pressure of a distressed person looking at you expectantly. The question this part answers is how to sequence them, and what goes wrong when you do not.

[slide 14 · process · 132.1s]
Here is a structure for a first session, and I want to be explicit that it is a structure and not a script. The proportions matter more than the wording. First, frame. Confirm the time available, the purpose of the meeting, and the limits of confidentiality. Establish a shared agenda for the hour, and note the word shared. The framing is where you reduce uncertainty, and clients who arrive uncertain talk less freely. Second, invite. Ask one open question, and then stop talking. This is where beginners fail, and they fail by continuing to talk, filling the space with explanation until the client has nothing left to enter. Let the client set the opening. Where they begin is itself information. Third, explore. Attend, reflect, summarise. Question sparingly. This is where you gather the story, and it usually occupies more than half of a first session. If you find yourself talking more than a quarter of the time in this phase, you are doing it wrong. Fourth, deepen. Once you have the story, move from content to feeling and to meaning. Reflect tentatively. Tolerate silence, and expect the silence to be uncomfortable, which is precisely why it works. The deepening phase is short and it is where the most valuable work of a first session occurs. Fifth, consolidate. Summarise the session. Agree a next step, even a small one. And check the client's state before they leave the room. That last action is not optional and it is not politeness. A client can leave a first session more distressed than they arrived, and it is your responsibility to notice. Ask directly. How are you feeling as we finish? Is there anything you need before you go? I want to note that this five-part structure also describes the shape of the whole treatment, compressed. Frame, invite, explore, deepen, consolidate. Later sessions follow the same arc within the hour, which is why the structure repays attention now.

[slide 15 · bullets · 148.1s]
Now the five errors, and I want you to treat this slide as a checklist for your own recordings, because these five account for most of the damage that beginners do, and all five are recognisable once you know what to listen for. First, filling silence. This is speaking simply because the quiet is uncomfortable. I have said silence is a technique; here is why it is hard. In ordinary conversation a silence of four or five seconds signals that something has gone wrong, and the social reflex is to repair it. In counselling the same silence is productive. Your reflex is the problem, because the silence belongs to the client. Second, questioning instead of reflecting. Using questions to gather material that the client would have offered if you had waited. Every question moves the agenda toward you, and a session composed mainly of questions becomes an interview, where the counsellor holds all the power and the client does all the work. Third, giving advice. Its appeal is obvious: advice works for simple problems. It fails here because the problems brought to counselling are rarely simple, and because the advice removes the client's agency at precisely the moment they are trying to build it. It relieves you and disempowers them. Fourth, interpreting too early. Offering a formulation before you have earned the right to it. The interpretation may be entirely accurate and still do harm, because an accurate interpretation delivered too soon lands as being told what you think, and it converts the client from a collaborator into a recipient. Earn it first, and the same words will be received completely differently. Fifth, chasing the story instead of the person. Collecting facts efficiently while the feeling goes unmentioned. The failure mode here is subtle, because the session looks productive. You finish with a complete history and a client who does not feel known. I want to dwell on that last one for a moment, because it is the error most likely to afflict diligent students. Efficiency is a virtue in many domains. In a first session it is not. A slightly incomplete history gathered in a room where the client felt understood will serve the work better than a comprehensive assessment gathered in a room where they did not.

[slide 16 · bullets · 135.8s]
Now the evidence, stated as honestly as I can state it, including the findings that complicate the picture of your own training. First, the alliance predicts outcome, and the alliance is partly built from skills. Agreement on goals, agreement on tasks and the relational bond all predict outcome across orientations, and accurate reflecting and attending are among the behaviours that build them. That is the empirical bridge between last week and this: the common factors are produced by identifiable, trainable behaviours. Second, skills training works and it transfers to practice. Trainees improve measurably on observed skills after structured training with feedback, and improvement is fastest when it involves recorded practice with specific corrective feedback rather than reading or demonstration alone. So the skills laboratory is the active ingredient of this part of your training. Third, and this is the finding that should make you uncomfortable: self-assessed competence is unreliable. Trainees typically rate their own skills higher than observers do. The gap does not close with experience. This is why your recorded sessions will be assessed rather than self-reported. This is not distrust of you personally; it is a replicated property of how practitioners perceive their own performance, and it applies to me as much as to you. Fourth, expertise is not simply accumulated time. Years of practice predict outcomes weakly. Outcome monitoring combined with deliberate practice predicts them better. Practitioners who spend twenty years doing the same thing without feedback do not become experts; they become confident. Fifth, and I find this genuinely encouraging, ruptures are survivable, and repairing them is a skill. Alliances break, including in skilled hands, through misunderstanding, through a poorly timed interpretation, through something you said without realising its weight. Clients who experience a rupture that is then repaired have outcomes at least as good as those who never experienced one. The repair is part of the treatment rather than a failure of it. Your first clinical rupture, which will come, is not the end of your career. It is a piece of work.

[slide 17 · casestudy · 195.1s]
Let us analyse a real exchange, and I want you to work through it as you would in supervision. The client, Ngozi, has been describing being passed over for a promotion for the second time. She has spoken for four minutes without pause. The counsellor then responds in one of four ways. Response A. Have you asked your manager why? Response B. I am sure they will recognise you eventually. Response C. So twice now, and each time you heard about it from someone else. You sound less angry than tired. Response D. What you are describing sounds like a pattern of self-sabotage. Do you see that? Form your own judgement before I offer mine; the answer matters less than the reasoning. Response C is the soundest, and it demonstrates two skills together. The first half is a reflection of content that is also a summary, and notice what it captures: the repetition, twice now, and the specific detail that she heard about it from someone else, which is almost certainly part of the injury. The second half is a reflection of feeling, and crucially it draws a distinction: less angry than tired. It is tentative, precise, and invites correction, which is what a good feeling reflection should do. Now the other three. Response A is a closed question. It is not a bad question in itself, and a manager conversation may matter later. But it arrives immediately after four minutes of disclosed pain and moves straight to problem solving, which converts her disclosure into a task and tells her the feeling she just exposed was not what the counsellor was listening for. That is chasing the situation instead of the person. Response B is reassurance, the most damaging of the four despite being the kindest in intention. It offers a prediction the counsellor cannot know is true, and it forecloses exploration. If Ngozi accepts it, the conversation ends. If she rejects it, she must manage the counsellor's optimism on top of her own disappointment. Response D is an interpretation delivered far too early. Self-sabotage is a serious claim. It may be accurate and worth exploring in six weeks. Offered here, and note the phrasing, do you see that, it asks her to agree with a formulation about herself that she was not helped to reach. In a Nigerian professional context, where a client may already be doubting whether she is being unfairly overlooked, it is likely to produce shame rather than insight. Then the two questions I want you to sit with. What would you say next after response C? My answer would be nothing, for several seconds. Response C is an invitation to go deeper, and the most useful thing the counsellor can do is leave room for her to accept it. And that leads to the fourth question, which is the one I actually care about. Where in this exchange would silence have been the better intervention? The answer is before all four responses. Four minutes of unbroken speech, in a client describing a second humiliation, is not a preamble to be endured. It is the work. And the counsellor moved first.

[slide 18 · takeaways · 106.4s]
Let me consolidate. Five takeaways. One. Skills are the alphabet, techniques are the words, and interventions are the sentences. Training begins with the alphabet for a reason, and a practitioner who has memorised interventions without the underlying skills can reproduce sentences but cannot compose new ones. Two. Competence looks like economy. Skilled counsellors typically speak less, question less and intervene less than beginners, and they listen to a greater proportion of what is said. If you want a single behavioural marker to aim at this semester, aim at that one. Three. Reflect before you question. When you feel the urge to ask something, wait one more beat. The client usually supplies the material unprompted, and it arrives richer for being unprompted, because it is theirs rather than yours. Four. Reassurance and advice are counterfeits of reflection. They feel like help and they function like closure. Both relieve the counsellor's discomfort, and both take the exploration away from the client. Five. Silence is a technique, and your self-assessed skill is unreliable. That second clause is not a comment on you, it is a replicated finding about practitioners generally, and it is the reason your work will be recorded and assessed rather than self-reported. I said at the beginning that the mark of a competent practitioner is the discipline of restraint rather than the cleverness of their interventions. Everything in this lecture has been an elaboration of that claim. The skill you will find hardest is not any of the five I taught. It is the skill of not speaking.

[slide 19 · reading · 92.6s]
Your reading for the coming week. Read Part Two of Clara Hill's Helping Skills, which covers the exploration stage, and Chapters Three to Five of Ivey, Ivey and Zalaquett, which cover attending, listening and observing. Those two texts are the standard references for this material and they differ usefully on sequencing, so read them against one another rather than treating either as definitive. Read Wampold's 2015 review of the common factors. It is short, and it makes the link I drew today between alliance and observable behaviour, which is worth seeing in his own words. Read Standard 2.01 of the APA Ethics Code on competence, and note that competence is defined there as bounded, meaning you are competent for particular activities with particular populations and not in general. Finally, your preparation for the skills laboratory. Before the laboratory session, record five minutes of yourself in conversation with a peer, with their permission, and listen back to it once. I want you to do something specific with it, and it is deliberately not evaluative. Simply note two moments. The first is the moment you most wanted to speak and did not. The second is the moment you spoke when silence would have been better. Do not try to fix anything yet. Just locate those two moments, and bring them to the laboratory. I will tell you now what you will almost certainly find, based on every cohort I have taught. You will find more of the second than the first.
