TITLE: Group Counselling
  on screen: The group as the agent of change, and the leader's discipline of protection

NARRATION
Welcome to Week Four of CPY 701. We have spent three weeks on the
individual: first the field itself, then the theories, then the skills of
the consulting room. Today the unit of treatment changes. Our subject is
group counselling, and I want to state the argument of the session at the
outset, because everything that follows depends on it. The argument is this:
a group is not individual counselling performed in public. The group itself
is the agent of change, and the leader's principal work is to build and
protect the conditions in which the group can do what no single counsellor
can do. If you lead a group as though it were a sequence of private
conversations with an audience, you will waste the most powerful resource in
the room, and that resource is the members themselves. The lecture has three
movements. First, what a group is and what makes it therapeutic. Second, how
a group is built and what it goes through before it can work. Third, what
you actually do as the leader in the room.

SLIDE 4 of 21   |   DEFINITION   |   00:01   |   00:03 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: Five Kinds of Group
EYEBROW: Key Concept 1
    TERM: What Makes a Group a Group

NARRATION
Let me define the terms, because they carry different obligations. A
psychoeducational group teaches a skill or a body of information through a
structured programme, such as a stress-management course or a
marriage-preparation class. Structure is high and disclosure is moderate. A
counselling group addresses personal concerns through interaction among
members, with a leader who facilitates rather than instructs. A
psychotherapy group works at greater depth with more distressed members and
a more interpretive leader. A support group is organised around a shared
condition or loss, and its authority is the lived experience of its members
rather than the training of its leader. A task group exists to accomplish a
job, and its health is measured by the work rather than by insight. Notice
what all five have in common, because this is the point of the slide: they
depend on interaction among members, not on the leader's attention rotating
around the room. That is the definition I want you to carry. A group is a
treatment in which the relations among members are the mechanism. The choice
of format is a clinical decision rather than a matter of whichever room is
free. A psychoeducational group of eight people learning to manage anxiety
is a different intervention from a counselling group of eight people
examining their relationships, and the leader's conduct should differ
accordingly.

TITLE: The Therapeutic Factors
EYEBROW: Key Concept 2
  on screen: Eleven factors, clustered into four families. Only one of them is a technique the leader performs.
    • Through what the group says and shows
      – Imparting information — members learn from instruction and from each other's experience.
      – Development of socialising techniques — how to give and receive feedback, disagree without rupture, be direct without cruelty.
    • Through being with others
      – Universality — the discovery that you are not the only person with this problem. Often the most powerful moment in a group's life.
      – Altruism — the experience of being useful to somebody else.
      – Group cohesiveness — belonging and warmth, which keeps members coming back and taking risks.
    • Through the group as a second family

NARRATION
Yalom, who has done more than anyone to describe how groups heal, named
eleven therapeutic factors. I am going to group them, because eleven items
are hard to hold in your head, and you will need them in your head at the
moment a group goes quiet and you are tempted to fill the space. First,
factors that operate through what the group says and shows. Imparting
information, where members learn from instruction and from one another's
experience. And development of socialising techniques, where members learn
how to give and receive feedback, how to disagree without rupture, and how
to be direct without cruelty. Second, factors that operate through being
with others. Universality, the discovery that you are not the only person
with this problem, which is often the single most powerful moment in a
group's life. Altruism, the experience of being useful to somebody else,
which is unavailable in individual work. And group cohesiveness, the sense
of belonging and warmth that keeps members coming back and taking risks.
Third, factors that operate through the group as a rehearsal of the family.
Corrective recapitulation of the primary family group, where members replay
early patterns and receive a different response. And fourth, factors that
require the leader's restraint: interpersonal learning, where members learn
how they affect others by seeing it happen and hearing about it; catharsis,
the release of long-held feeling; and existential confrontation with the
givens of life. Holding all of it together is instillation of hope. Now
notice something. Only one of these eleven is a technique that the leader
performs. The rest are conditions that the leader protects. That distinction
is the defining insight of group work.

SLIDE 6 of 21   |   TABLE   |   00:02   |   00:06 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: What the Leader Must Protect
EYEBROW: Key Concept 3
  on screen: Each factor fails in a characteristic way when the leader neglects it.
  columns: Factor | What it looks like | What the leader protects
    Universality | Members recognising their own experience in someone else's | That no single member is treated as the group's problem to be solved
    Altruism | Members turning to help each other without being asked | Resisting the urge to be the only helper in the room
    Cohesion | Warmth, attendance, increasing risk-taking | Safety, punctuality, and addressing absences and silences early
    Interpersonal learning | Feedback about impact, offered and received | Linking members to each other rather than to yourself
    Catharsis | The release of feeling that has been held a long time | Not manufacturing it, not interrupting it, and containing it afterwards
    Hope | Seeing another member improve | Naming change out loud, and keeping contact with how members are doing

NARRATION
This slide turns each factor into a supervisory question, because a factor
can be present in principle and absent in practice. Universality looks like
members recognising their own experience in somebody else's account, and
what the leader protects is the rule that no single member becomes the
group's designated problem. The moment a group settles on one member as the
one who is broken, universality collapses for everybody. Altruism looks like
members turning to help each other without being asked, and the leader
protects it by resisting the urge to be the only helper in the room. When
the leader answers every distress, members learn that help comes from the
front of the room. Cohesion looks like warmth, attendance and increasing
risk-taking, and it is protected by attention to the small things: who is
late, who has been absent twice, whose last contribution went unanswered.
Interpersonal learning looks like feedback about impact being offered and
received, and the leader protects it by linking members to each other rather
than to themselves. Catharsis looks like the release of feeling held for a
long time. The leader's job is not to produce it, which is impossible, and
not to interrupt it, which is common, but to contain it afterwards, because
a member who has wept in front of six people needs somebody to attend to
what that cost. And hope looks like watching somebody else get better, which
is why the leader names change out loud when it happens. One duty sits
behind that entire third column, and it is not written on the slide.
Cohesion is carried more by your conduct than by your statements: whether
you are on time, whether you remember what a member said two sessions ago,
whether you apologise when you get something wrong. Those small
reliabilities are what members read as safety.

TITLE: Does Group Counselling Work?
EYEBROW: Key Concept 4
  on screen: What the evidence supports, and the one place groups reliably underperform.
    • Outcomes are comparable to individual therapy for many conditions.
      – Meta-analytic reviews of group treatments find effect sizes broadly equivalent to individual formats, and on measures of social support the group has a structural advantage.
    • Cohesion is the group's equivalent of the therapeutic alliance.
      – It is among the most consistent predictors of outcome in the group literature, which is why slide 6 spent its time on protecting it.
    • The main threat is attrition, not ineffectiveness.
      – Drop-out is concentrated in the first sessions. Early attendance predicts the whole course; a member who misses session two rarely returns.
    • Groups are not indicated for everyone.
      – Acute crisis requiring individual care, severe reality distortion, and an inability to tolerate feedback are the classic exclusions. Screening is a clinical act, not an administrative one.

NARRATION
Before we go further, the evidence, stated as honestly as I can state it.
First, group formats produce outcomes comparable to individual therapy
across many conditions. Meta-analytic reviews of group treatment find effect
sizes broadly equivalent to individual formats, and on measures of social
support the group has a structural advantage, because the member leaves with
a network rather than a single relationship. Second, cohesion is the group's
equivalent of the therapeutic alliance, and it is among the most consistent
predictors of outcome in the group literature. That is the empirical reason
the previous slide spent its time on protecting it. If you wanted one thing
to manage in your first group, manage cohesion. Third, and this is the
finding that should shape your practice, the main threat to groups is
attrition rather than ineffectiveness. Drop-out is concentrated in the first
few sessions. Attendances in the first two sessions predict the whole
course, and a member who misses the second session rarely returns. So the
practical implication is that your job in the first hour is not to
demonstrate insight; it is to make the second hour likely. Fourth, groups
are not indicated for everyone. Acute crisis requiring individual care,
severe reality distortion, and a marked inability to tolerate feedback are
the classic exclusions. I want to add that modern practice is more inclusive
than the original lists, especially for psychoeducational groups, but the
principle stands. Screening is a clinical act, not an administrative one,
and it is where group leadership begins. One caveat about the evidence. Most
of the trials concern psychotherapy and counselling groups for adults, and
much of the meta-analytic work pools formats that differ from one another.
So the honest summary is this. Group formats work, they work for many of the
problems you will meet, and they work least well with members who cannot yet
use other people. Combining a group with occasional individual sessions is
common practice, and it is often the best arrangement available.

SLIDE 9 of 21   |   PROCESS   |   00:02   |   00:10 in

TITLE: Stages of Group Development
EYEBROW: Key Concept 5
  on screen: Tuckman's sequence, with the leader's task at each stage.
    1. Forming: Polite, anxious, dependent on the leader. Task: orient, structure, set the frame and the norms.
    2. Storming: Conflict, competition for position, dissatisfaction with the leader. Task: do not flee. Name it and work with it.
    3. Norming: Norms settle and cohesion grows. Task: reinforce what is working and notice what is not, early.
    4. Performing: The group does its work with less structure. Task: keep process commentary alive, and stay quiet more.
    5. Adjourning: Ending, and the loss that comes with it. Task: process the ending explicitly, and review what changed.

NARRATION
Here is the developmental sequence, described by Tuckman in 1965 and still
the most useful map we have. Forming is polite, anxious and dependent.
Members are watching you to find out what is allowed, and your task is to
orient them, provide structure, and set the frame and the norms. Notice that
structure is high here, and that this is legitimate rather than a failure of
democracy. Storming is the stage leaders fear most, and it is the one I most
want you to expect. Conflict appears, members compete for position, and
somebody is dissatisfied with you. A great many beginning leaders respond to
this stage by becoming more directive, or by reassuring everyone that the
group is fine, and both responses postpone the work. The task here is not to
prevent the conflict but to keep it inside the frame: name it, normalise it,
and make sure the person carrying it is not harmed or abandoned. Norming is
where norms settle and cohesion grows, and the leader's task is to reinforce
what is working and to notice what is not, early, because a bad norm is much
cheaper to address in week three than in week ten. Performing is the stage
at which the group does its work with less structure and the leader speaks
least. And adjourning is the ending, which I want to insist is a stage of
treatment rather than an administrative event. Members who have been honest
with each other are about to lose that, and the loss needs to be processed
explicitly. I should add that the sequence is not a one-way escalator. A
group can move backwards under stress: a new member, a departure, or a
painful disclosure can return a performing group briefly to storming. That
is normal, and it is not regression in the clinical sense.

TITLE: Selection, Screening and Composition
EYEBROW: Key Concept 6
  on screen: The hour in which you screen a member is the hour in which you lead the group.
    • Size: six to eight members for an interactive group.
      – Below five, the group lacks energy and every absence is felt. Above ten, it fragments into subgroups and the quiet members disappear.
    • Screen every prospective member individually, before the group starts.
      – Explain the format, the norms and the limits of confidentiality. Assess whether this person can use the group's mechanism, and whether the group can hold them.
    • Compose for cohesion first, and for difference second.
      – Enough shared ground that members recognise each other; enough variety in age, temperament and coping style that members have something to learn.
    • Agree the frame in writing: time-limited or open, how many sessions, what attendance is expected, what happens when someone misses.
      – Commitment agreed in advance is the strongest single protection against the attrition that threatens every group.

NARRATION
Selection, screening and composition. On size, six to eight members is the
classic recommendation for an interactive group. Below five the group lacks
energy, and every absence is felt disproportionately. Above ten it fragments
into subgroups and the quiet members disappear. On screening, I want to be
emphatic: screen every prospective member individually, and do it before the
group begins. The screening conversation is where you explain the format,
the norms and the limits of confidentiality, and where you assess two
things: whether this person can use the group's mechanism, and whether this
particular group can hold this particular person. Those are different
questions, and both matter. A distressed member in a group of six who cannot
be held will damage the group and be damaged by it. On composition, look for
cohesion first and difference second. You need enough shared ground that
members recognise one another, and enough variety in age, temperament and
coping style that there is something to learn. A group of six identical
people is comfortable and inert. On the frame, agree it in advance and put
it in writing: whether the group is time-limited or open, how many sessions,
what attendance is expected, and what happens when somebody misses.
Commitment agreed in advance is the strongest single protection you have
against the attrition I described earlier, and it costs you nothing but a
conversation. I want to close on the point that screening is where group
leadership starts. A leader who inherits a badly composed group spends the
whole course compensating, and the members pay for it. Two more decisions
before you start. Decide whether the group is open or closed. A closed,
time-limited group of twelve sessions gives cohesion a chance to build and
gives the ending a meaning, while an open group absorbs departures but must
re-establish its norms with every arrival. And prepare your members before
the first session: tell them what the group will be like, what will be asked
of them, and what they may find difficult. Preparation is one of the few
things you can do before a group starts that demonstrably reduces early
drop-out.

TITLE: Culture, Community and Confidentiality
EYEBROW: Key Concept 7
  on screen: Group work in a close-knit community is not the same as group work among strangers.
    • In Nigeria the counsellor-to-population ratio makes the group model a necessity, not an option.
      – One trained counsellor can serve eight people in an hour, and the members leave with a network rather than a single relationship.
    • The group is not a foreign form. The extended family, the age-grade meeting, the church cell and the cooperative already work this way.
      – Your task is to use that familiarity rather than to import a foreign etiquette wholesale.
    • Confidentiality cannot be promised, only agreed and protected.
      – Members are not bound by a code of ethics. State the limit at the outset, ask for an explicit commitment, and return to it at intervals.
    • In a small city, disclosure can travel through kinship networks.
      – When members may be known to one another, the fear of exposure shapes what is said. Ask about it openly rather than waiting for it to emerge.

NARRATION
Now the cultural and community dimension, which in our setting is not an
addendum but a determinant of whether the group works at all. Consider scale
first. In Nigeria the counsellor-to-population ratio is thin enough that the
group model is a necessity rather than a preference. One trained counsellor
can serve eight people in an hour, and the members leave with a network
rather than a single relationship. That makes group work, in my view, the
most important thing you will learn in this programme for the health system
you are actually going to work in. Second, the group is not a foreign form.
The extended family, the age-grade meeting, the church cell and the
cooperative already work in this way, and members arrive with expectations
formed by them. Your task is to use that familiarity rather than to import a
foreign etiquette wholesale. Third, and this requires care. Confidentiality
in a group cannot be promised, only agreed and protected. Members are not
bound by a code of ethics, and you cannot guarantee what they will do. State
the limit in the screening conversation and again at the first session, ask
for an explicit commitment, and return to it at intervals. In a small city,
disclosure can travel through kinship networks, and members know that better
than you do. When people in the group may be known to one another, the fear
of exposure shapes what is said, so ask about it openly rather than waiting
for it to surface as an unexplained silence. Fourth, expect the authority
dynamic and manage it deliberately. Deference to the leader, or to an older
member, can silence the younger ones. Norms that permit disagreement have to
be stated, demonstrated by you, and protected the first time somebody tests
them. One practical matter inside the room. When feeling rises, members
often switch language, into Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa or pidgin, and what is
sayable can change with the language. If you do not share the language, say
so early and agree how it will be handled. If you do share it, notice
carefully what is said in one language and not in the other.

TITLE: Leader Functions
EYEBROW: Key Concept 8
  on screen: Six things a group leader does. Notice how few of them involve information.
    • Structure — high at the start, deliberately reduced over time.
      – The leader's activity should decline as the group's competence grows. A group that still needs you in week eight has been taught dependence.
    • Linking — connecting members to each other, not to yourself.
      – "Chidi, that sounds close to what Amaka described a moment ago." The leader's most valuable sentence is often a bridge between two members.
    • Drawing out — creating space rather than demanding speech.
      – "There are three people who have not spoken yet, and there is no hurry." This invites without putting anybody on the spot.
    • Blocking — protecting the group from one member's behaviour.
      – Interrupting a monologue, stopping an interrogation, preventing a scapegoat. Blocking is done for the group, not against the person.

NARRATION
Six leader functions. First, structure, high at the start and deliberately
reduced over time. The leader's activity should decline as the group's
competence grows, and I want to name the failure mode: a group that still
needs you in week eight has been taught dependence, and that is our doing
rather than theirs. Second, linking. Connecting members to each other rather
than to yourself. Chidi, that sounds close to what Amaka described a moment
ago. The leader's most valuable sentence is frequently a bridge between two
members, and the reason is that every link you build between them
strengthens the mechanism, while every answer you supply strengthens only
you. Third, drawing out, which is creating space rather than demanding
speech. There are three people who have not spoken yet, and there is no
hurry. That sentence invites without putting anyone on the spot, and it
tells the group that you are watching the whole system rather than the last
person who spoke. Fourth, blocking. This is the function beginners find
hardest, because it feels rude, and it is the one the group most needs you
to perform. Blocking means interrupting a monologue, stopping one member
interrogating another, and preventing the group from settling on a
scapegoat. Do it for the group, and say why you are doing it. Fifth,
modelling. Your conduct is the norm, not your statements about norms. How
you receive disagreement, acknowledge an error, and handle conflict with
another person teaches the group more than any rule you announce. Sixth,
process commentary. Naming what is happening between people: I notice that
whenever we approach your father, the conversation turns to logistics. That
is a sentence no member can easily say, which is exactly why it is yours.

SLIDE 14 of 21   |   TABLE   |   00:01   |   00:20 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: Six Leader Errors
EYEBROW: Key Concept 9
  on screen: Every one of these is a well-intentioned response to discomfort, usually yours.
  columns: The error | What it does to the group | What to do instead
    Rescuing | Removes the group's opportunity to help, and teaches that help comes from the front of the room | Wait. Count to ten. Let the members work, and comment on the process afterwards
    Over-structuring | Turns the group into a class, and members into an audience for the leader | Reduce agenda as the group matures; let silence and choice exist
    Spotlighting | Forces a reluctant member to perform, and confirms that the group is unsafe for the hesitant | Create space without assignment: "no hurry" rather than "what do you think?"
    Colluding with silence | Lets avoidance become the norm, and the important topic never arrives | Name the avoidance itself, respectfully: "I notice we move away from this each time"
    Permitting scapegoating | One member is sacrificed to relieve the group's anxiety; the group learns that honesty is dangerous | Block it at the first sign, and ask the group what it is doing with that member
    Serial individual counselling | The group becomes a waiting room while you conduct eight private sessions in public | Return to the group after any individual attention: "who else recognises this?"

NARRATION
Now the six errors, and I want to say at the outset that every one of them
is a well-intentioned response to discomfort, and the discomfort is usually
the leader's. First, rescuing. Moving in as soon as the group struggles,
which removes the group's opportunity to help and teaches that help comes
from the front of the room. The remedy is almost embarrassingly simple:
wait, count to ten, let the members work, and comment on the process
afterwards. Second, over-structuring. Filling the hour with an agenda, which
turns the group into a class and the members into an audience. The remedy is
to reduce structure as the group matures and let silence and choice exist.
Third, spotlighting. Tunde, you have been quiet, what do you think? It feels
inclusive and it forces a reluctant member to perform, confirming that the
group is unsafe for the hesitant. Create space instead, without assignment.
Fourth, colluding with silence. Letting an avoidance become a norm, so that
the topic the group is organised around never arrives. Name the avoidance
itself, respectfully: I notice that we move away from this each time we come
near it. Fifth, permitting scapegoating. One member is sacrificed to relieve
the group's anxiety, and the group learns that honesty is dangerous and that
somebody will always pay for it. Block this at the first sign, and ask the
group what it is doing with that member. Sixth, serial individual
counselling. Working with one member at a time while the others wait, so the
group becomes a waiting room for eight private sessions conducted in public.
The remedy is a habit worth forming now: after any individual attention,
return to the group. Who else recognises this?

TITLE: The Here-and-Now
EYEBROW: Key Concept 10
  on screen: The group's own behaviour is the material. Use it when it is live, and not otherwise.
    • Groups drift towards content and towards the leader.
      – Members tell stories about elsewhere, and turn to you for verdicts. Both are comfortable, and both avoid the room.
    • The here-and-now means treating what happens between members as the clinical material.
      – Not the story about the workplace, but who was interrupted while telling it, and who went quiet.
    • Two tiers of process: what the group is doing, and how members feel about what it is doing.
      – The second tier is harder and more valuable. "That is the first time anyone has disagreed with you here. What was that like?"
    • Do not process everything.
      – Process commentary is indicated when the group's behaviour is itself an instance of the problem members bring. Otherwise, let the discussion be a discussion.

NARRATION
The here-and-now. Every group drifts in two directions, and both are away
from the room. It drifts towards content, so that members tell stories about
elsewhere, and it drifts towards the leader, so that members turn to you for
verdicts. Both are comfortable, and both avoid the thing that makes the
group therapeutic. The here-and-now is the corrective: treating what happens
between the members as the clinical material. Not the story about the
workplace, but who was interrupted while telling it, and who went quiet when
it was told. This is the group's version of the shift from content to
process that we discussed in Week Three, with the volume turned up, because
in a group the material is being produced live by eight people. There are
two tiers of process, and I want you to distinguish them. The first is what
the group is doing: we have spent twenty minutes on one member and the rest
of you have been listeners. The second tier is how members feel about what
the group is doing: that is the first time anyone has disagreed with you
here, Chidi. What was that like? The second tier is harder to say and
considerably more valuable, because it puts feeling and relationship into
the room rather than describing them from a distance. A caution, though, and
it is an important one. Do not process everything. A leader who comments on
every exchange becomes an interruption and the group stops speaking freely.
Process commentary is indicated when the group's own behaviour is an
instance of the problem members bring. Otherwise, let a discussion be a
discussion, and save the commentary for the moment it is live.

TITLE: Difficult Moments
EYEBROW: Key Concept 11
  on screen: Five situations you will meet in your first year of leading, and what each one is actually asking for.
    • Silence
      – Almost always anxiety about risk, not failure. Tolerate it. The leader's composure is what tells the group the silence is survivable.
    • The monopolist
      – Block kindly and publicly, for the group's sake rather than against the person. The monopolist is often the most anxious member in the room.
    • The silent member
      – Distinguish unable to speak from unwilling, and from nothing to say yet. Join them to another member's theme instead of requesting a contribution.
    • Conflict between members
      – A working group needs conflict. Protect the person, not the harmony, and do not resolve it on their behalf.

NARRATION
Five situations you will meet in your first year of leading. First, silence.
A group's silence is almost always anxiety about risk rather than evidence
of failure, and the leader's composure is what tells the members that the
silence is survivable. If you cannot sit in it, the group learns that quiet
is an emergency, and they will fill every pause for the rest of the course.
Second, the monopolist. Block kindly and publicly, for the group's sake
rather than against the person, and remember that the member who speaks
without stopping is often the most anxious person in the room. The monologue
is a way of managing fear, so your block must leave the member's dignity
intact. Third, the silent member. Distinguish three cases, because they call
for different responses: unable to speak, unwilling to speak, and nothing to
say yet. The first may be a language or safety issue, the second may be a
confidential reason you learn in supervision, and the third is normal in a
new group. In all three cases, join the member to another member's theme
rather than requesting a contribution. Fourth, conflict between members. A
working group needs conflict, and the leader's task is to protect the person
rather than the harmony. Do not resolve it on their behalf, and do not let
it run unmanaged. Fifth, a member in crisis. Triage outside the group. Do
not conduct the crisis in front of the others, and do not allow the group to
become a substitute for individual care. That is partly a clinical judgement
and partly an ethical one, and it is the subject of the next slide but one.

TITLE: First Session of a Group
EYEBROW: Case Vignette
    2 paragraphs, 5 discussion questions

NARRATION
Let us analyse a first session. Six members, first meeting. The leader has
given the frame and the confidentiality agreement and asked the group to
begin. For ten minutes, Bisi has spoken almost continuously about her
marriage, in detail. Two members, Chidi and Amaka, have begun to exchange
glances. Tunde, who is twenty-two and the youngest, has said nothing at all.
The other two, Idowu and Grace, have offered small murmurs of agreement. The
leader feels two impulses at once: to let Bisi finish, because interrupting
feels unkind, and to work individually with Tunde, because he is being left
out. I want you to notice that both impulses are the leader's discomfort
speaking, and neither is a plan. So what is the group doing? Describe it as
a process rather than as six individuals. It is organising itself around one
voice, and it has already begun to mark the person who is not speaking. If
you let it run, the pattern becomes the norm in forty minutes, and by
session three you have a leader with an audience and one member carrying the
whole group. If you ask Tunde directly whether he would like to say
something, you have spotlighted him, which tells the group that silence is a
problem to be repaired by the leader and that the youngest member must
perform on request. Now, which function would you use, and what sentence
would you say? I would block, gently, for the group. Something like: Bisi,
thank you, and I want to stop you there for a moment. Two things. Three
people have not spoken yet, and there is no hurry. And I want to ask the
group something. What has it been like to listen for ten minutes? Notice
what that sentence does. It does not criticise Bisi, it releases her, it
names the pattern without shaming anyone, and it hands the work back to the
group. And then the last question: how would you use the silence rather than
end it? After that question, say nothing. Whatever comes next from the
members is worth more than anything you could add, and your silence is the
demonstration that the room can hold the moment.

TITLE: Ethics, Safety and the Frame
EYEBROW: Key Concept 12
  on screen: What the code requires of you specifically because this is a group.
    • Describe roles and the limits of confidentiality at the outset, and mean it.
      – The APA Ethics Code addresses group work directly: in group settings you describe the roles and responsibilities of all parties and the limits of confidentiality. Ask for an explicit commitment from each member.
    • Screen for suitability, and record that you did.
      – Screening is both a clinical and a protective act. Document the criteria you used and who you did not accept.
    • Manage dual relationships actively in small communities.
      – Members may be colleagues, relatives or church members. Anticipate it in screening, name it in the group, and be prepared to refer a member on.
    • Write group notes that protect the other members.
      – Notes identify people beyond your client. Record themes, patterns and your own process, not other members' confidences.

NARRATION
Finally, the ethics, organised around what the code requires of you
specifically because this is a group rather than an individual client.
First, describe roles and the limits of confidentiality at the outset, and
mean it. The APA Ethics Code addresses group work directly: in group
settings, psychologists describe the roles and responsibilities of all
parties and the limits of confidentiality. That is a requirement to speak
about it, clearly, at the beginning, and to ask for an explicit commitment
from each member. Second, screen for suitability and record that you did.
Screening is both a clinical act and a protective one, and documentation
should show the criteria you used and who you decided not to accept. Third,
manage dual relationships actively, because in small communities you will
not avoid them. Members may be colleagues, relatives, or members of your
church, and these relationships do not disappear when the group starts.
Anticipate them in screening, name them in the group when they exist, and be
prepared to refer a member on when the overlap is too great to manage.
Fourth, write group notes that protect the other members. This is a
distinctive group problem. Your notes identify people beyond your client,
and a note that records another member's confidence has now been shared with
a third party without consent. Record themes, patterns and your own process,
not other members' disclosures. Fifth, and this is advice I give to every
new group leader: do not lead alone what you cannot hold alone. Co-leading
is the standard training model for a reason. It provides a witness, a check
on your blind spots, and a supervisor's perspective inside the room.

TITLE: Key Takeaways
EYEBROW: Summary
    1. A group is not individual counselling in public. The relations among members are the mechanism, and the leader's task is to protect the conditions in which they work.
    2. Of the therapeutic factors, only one is a technique the leader performs. The rest are conditions the leader protects, and universality and cohesion are the first two to defend.
    3. Group outcomes are comparable to individual therapy, and the main threat is attrition in the first sessions rather than ineffectiveness.
    4. The group will pass through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning. Storming is normal; the leader's job is to stay and work with it rather than to flee or impose order.
    5. Economy is the leader's discipline. Link members to each other, block for the group, use process commentary when it is live, and let silence do its work.

NARRATION
Let me consolidate. Five takeaways. One. A group is not individual
counselling performed in public. The relations among members are the
mechanism, and the leader's task is to protect the conditions in which those
relations can work. Two. Of the eleven therapeutic factors, only one is a
technique the leader performs. The rest are conditions the leader protects,
and the first two to defend are universality and cohesion, because when they
fail everything else fails with them. Three. Group outcomes are comparable
to individual therapy across many conditions, and the main threat to the
groups you will run is attrition in the first sessions rather than
ineffectiveness. So your first hour has one overriding objective: make the
second hour likely. Four. The group will pass through forming, storming,
norming, performing and adjourning, and storming is normal rather than
evidence that you have failed. The leader's job at that stage is to stay,
keep the conflict inside the frame, and work with it. And five. Economy is
the leader's discipline, just as it was the counsellor's discipline last
week. Link members to each other rather than to yourself, block for the
group, use process commentary when it is live, and let silence do the work
that only silence can do. If you retain one sentence from today, let it be
the definition we began with. The relations among members are the mechanism,
and everything a leader does is either protecting that mechanism or getting
in its way.
