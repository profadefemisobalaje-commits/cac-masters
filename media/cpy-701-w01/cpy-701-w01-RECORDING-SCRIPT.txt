SLIDE 4 of 22   |   DEFINITION   |   00:01   |   00:02 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: What Is Counseling Psychology?
EYEBROW: Key Concept 1
    TERM: Counseling Psychology

NARRATION
Here is the working definition we will use throughout this course.
Counseling psychology is a psychological specialty that facilitates personal
and interpersonal functioning across the lifespan, with attention to
emotional, social, vocational, educational, health-related, developmental,
and organisational concerns. Let me pull that apart, because every phrase in
it is doing work. First, it is a specialty within psychology. It is not a
synonym for psychology as a whole, and it is not a brand of general
counselling. Second, it facilitates functioning. Notice the verb. The
profession is oriented towards the enhancement of normal functioning, not
only towards the relief of pathology. Third, it attends to functioning
across the lifespan. That means the specialty is concerned with the child,
the adolescent, the working adult, the couple, and the older adult, and with
the transitions between those stages. Fourth, the list of domains,
emotional, social, vocational, educational, health-related, developmental
and organisational, is deliberately wide. Vocational and educational
concerns in particular have been central to this profession since its birth,
and they distinguish it from specialties that confine themselves to clinical
settings. And notice finally what the definition does not say. It does not
lead with diagnosis. It does not lead with disorder. It leads with
functioning, with context, and with strengths. Write that down, because it
will reappear in every course you take this year.

TITLE: Why This Matters: The Scale of Need
EYEBROW: Context
    1 in 8 — people worldwide live with a mental health condition
    ~85% — of those in low- and middle-income countries receive no treatment
    0.1% — of Nigeria's health budget is directed to mental health
    <1 — psychiatrist per 100,000 people in Nigeria

NARRATION
Before we go further into definition, let me show you why definition matters
in this particular region of the world. The World Health Organization
estimates that roughly one in eight people globally lives with a mental
health condition. In low income and middle income countries, the great
majority of those people, on some estimates around eighty five per cent,
receive no treatment at all. Nigeria spends a very small fraction of its
already stretched health budget on mental health, and has fewer than one
psychiatrist for every hundred thousand people. Now, hold those numbers
against the definition we just read. If your professional identity is built
only around one-to-one psychotherapy in a consulting room, then in this
country you can reach a tiny sliver of the people who need help, and you
will spend your career with a waiting list you cannot shorten. That is
precisely why counseling psychology has always insisted on prevention, on
psychoeducation, on group work, on vocational and educational intervention,
and on working with systems rather than only with individuals. Those are not
secondary activities for us. They are how the profession answers the
arithmetic of need. When you write your assignments this semester, I want
you to check the current figures against the WHO Mental Health Atlas,
because these numbers move, and part of being a scientist-practitioner is
refusing to quote a statistic you have not verified.

SLIDE 6 of 22   |   BULLETS   |   00:02   |   00:05 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: Three Professions, Three Trainings
EYEBROW: Key Concept 2
  on screen: Distinction rests on training and legal scope, not on prestige or effectiveness.
    • Psychiatry — a medical specialty
      – Trained first as a physician, then as a psychiatric specialist. May diagnose, prescribe and admit. Usually sees the highest-acuity presentations.
    • Clinical psychology — a psychological specialty
      – Trained in psychological science and in psychopathology. Specialises in assessment and treatment of significant mental disorder.
    • Counseling psychology — a psychological specialty
      – Trained in psychological science, with emphasis on lifespan development, strengths, prevention and vocational or educational functioning.
    • Counselling — a related but distinct profession
      – Training, licensure and scope vary widely by country; not always a psychology qualification.

NARRATION
Let us now do the disambiguation properly. There are three professions that
the public routinely confuses, and a fourth that is related but genuinely
distinct. Psychiatry is a medical specialty. A psychiatrist is a physician
first, holding a medical degree, and then completing specialist training in
psychiatry. Because of that medical training, the psychiatrist is the one
member of this group who may prescribe medication and admit a patient to
hospital. Psychiatry tends, though not exclusively, to see the highest
acuity presentations, and its lens is substantially biological and
pharmacological, though modern psychiatric practice is far more integrated
than the stereotype suggests. Clinical psychology is a psychological
specialty. A clinical psychologist holds a psychology degree and completes
doctoral training in psychological science, psychopathology and its
treatment. Clinical psychology historically oriented itself towards
significant mental disorder, psychological assessment, and the treatment of
severe psychopathology, and it is trained to work in health care settings
with people who are unwell. Counseling psychology is also a psychological
specialty. Like the clinical psychologist, the counseling psychologist is
trained in psychological science. But the historical emphasis falls
differently. Counseling psychology emphasises lifespan development, the
strengths and resources of the person, prevention, and vocational and
educational functioning. In practice, the two overlap enormously, and in
most settings you will find counseling psychologists treating depression,
anxiety and trauma, and clinical psychologists doing career and adjustment
work. Fourth, counselling. Here I need you to be careful. Counselling is a
related profession with its own traditions, and in many countries it has its
own training and licensure pathway that is not a psychology qualification at
all. In Nigeria, and across much of Africa, the picture is further
complicated by lay counsellors, religious counsellors, and paraprofessional
workers who deliver a very large share of actual frontline support. They are
often highly effective and deeply trusted. Understanding that ecology,
rather than dismissing it, is part of our job. So, the headline: these
professions are distinguished by training and by legal scope of practice,
not by prestige, and not by which one is more helpful. Anyone who tells you
one profession is inherently more effective is not giving you a scientific
answer.

This table consolidates what we have just discussed, and I want you to use
it as a reference sheet for the rest of the semester. Read down the columns.
The first column names the profession. The second states the core training.
Notice that two of these are psychology qualifications and two are not, and
that only one is a medical qualification. The third column asks about
prescribing, and the answer is a single yes. This is the cleanest, least
ambiguous boundary in the whole picture, and you should hold onto it firmly,
because it is also the boundary most often blurred by clients and,
occasionally, by practitioners. Only a physician may prescribe. A counseling
psychologist does not prescribe, and a counselling psychologist who implies
otherwise is acting outside their competence and, in most jurisdictions,
outside the law. The fourth column describes typical focus. Read these as
tendencies, not as fences. In contemporary practice the boundary between
clinical and counseling psychology is porous by design, and many services
deliberately hire both because the training produces complementary
strengths. I want to add a caution about this table. It describes the
situation in jurisdictions with well developed regulatory systems. In
Nigeria, and in many African countries, the legal framework for
psychological practice has been developing rapidly, and the scope question
is partly a matter of national law and partly a matter of professional
ethics. Before you take a placement or a job, find out what your national
regulator or professional association actually permits you to do. That is
not a bureaucratic formality. Practising outside your scope is the fastest
way to harm a client, and it is one of the most common grounds for
disciplinary action in our profession.

SLIDE 8 of 22   |   TWOCOL   |   00:03   |   00:09 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: Common Misconceptions
EYEBROW: Key Concept 5
  on screen: Each of these beliefs is widespread, and each of them damages either access to care or the quality of it.
  What people often believe:
    • Counseling is only for people with serious mental illness.
    • A counselor mainly listens and then gives advice.
    • Counseling is essentially the same as talking to a friend or a religious leader.
    • It only works if you already believe in it.
  What is actually the case:
    • It addresses the whole range of functioning, from a career decision to severe distress.
    • Listening is a disciplined, trained skill, and advice-giving is generally avoided on purpose.
    • Natural support is genuinely valuable, but it carries no confidentiality duty, no ethical code and no outcome measurement.
    • Outcomes are measurable, and they are comparable across treatments.

NARRATION
Before we leave definition behind, let me address five beliefs that you will
meet constantly, in your family, in your church or mosque, in your
workplace, and, I am sorry to say, sometimes in your own head. Each of them
is widespread, and each of them does real damage. The first misconception is
that counseling is only for people with serious mental illness. This belief
keeps millions of people away from help, because nobody wants to be told
they are mad, and in many communities the stigma attached to that label is
severe. The truth is that this profession addresses the full range of human
functioning, from a twenty-two year old choosing a career, to a couple
navigating a difficult transition, to a person in acute crisis. Coming to
see a counseling psychologist is not a declaration that something is deeply
wrong with you. It is a decision to use a professional resource for a
problem that professional training happens to address. The second
misconception is that a counselor mainly listens and then gives advice.
Listening is indeed central, but it is a disciplined, trained skill, not a
passive one. And advice-giving is generally avoided on purpose, which
surprises many students. Rogers' argument, which we have already met, is
that the client's own agency is the mechanism of change. If I tell you what
to do, I have borrowed your problem for an hour and handed it straight back.
If instead I help you clarify what you want and what stands in the way, the
change belongs to you and it outlives the session. The third misconception
is that counseling is essentially the same as talking to a friend or a
religious leader. Let me be careful here, because this is not a claim that
professional help is superior to the support of people who love you. It is a
claim about what is different. Your friend is not bound by confidentiality,
is not obliged by an ethical code, is not trained to assess risk, is not
required to keep records, and is not measuring whether what they are doing
is working. Those five differences are the profession. The fourth
misconception is that it only works if you already believe in it. This is
partly true of every treatment, medicine included, and it is called the
placebo effect. But it is not the whole story. Psychotherapy produces
measurable change that persists, and the effect is larger than placebo
alone. The fifth misconception, which I meet in students as often as in the
public, is that all psychological treatments are equally effective and
therefore it does not matter what you learn. It matters a great deal. We
will come to the evidence in a few minutes.

TITLE: Four Moments That Made the Profession
EYEBROW: Historical Development
  on screen: Each moment added a layer that the profession still carries.
    1. 1908: Frank Parsons opens the Vocation Bureau in Boston; systematic vocational guidance begins.
    2. 1942: Carl Rogers publishes Counseling and Psychotherapy; the counselling relationship becomes a subject of science.
    3. 1951: Division 17 of the APA takes the name Counseling Psychology; the specialty is formally named.
    4. 1990s: Multicultural counselling competencies and social justice enter the professional mainstream.

NARRATION
The profession has four founding moments, and I want to take them in turn
because each one left a permanent deposit in our identity. The first moment
is nineteen oh eight. Frank Parsons, an engineer and social reformer in
Boston, opened the Vocation Bureau, and began offering young people a
systematic process of matching their aptitudes and interests to the demands
of available occupations. Parsons' book, Choosing a Vocation, published the
following year, is often treated as the founding text of vocational
guidance. Notice the character of that founding act. It was not clinical. It
was educational and social reformist. It began in the settlement house
movement, in the attempt to help immigrants and working class youth find
decent work. That origin is why vocational and educational concerns remain
central to this profession, and why we are comfortable in schools and
universities, in careers services, and in community settings, in a way that
some other specialties are not. The second moment is nineteen forty two,
when Carl Rogers published Counseling and Psychotherapy. Rogers'
contribution was, in a sense, an inversion. Rather than treating the
counsellor's technique as the active ingredient, he made the quality of the
relationship the subject of serious study, and he insisted that the client
holds the capacity for growth. That optimism, that faith in the person's own
resources, is still one of our defining commitments, and it is why strengths
based language appears in every definition of this field. The third moment
is nineteen fifty one, when Division Seventeen of the American Psychological
Association renamed itself Counseling Psychology. That is when the specialty
got its name and its formal organisational identity, and much of the
internal debate about training standards, accreditation and scope dates from
that period. The fourth moment belongs to the nineteen nineties, when a
sustained critique from within the profession, led by scholars such as
Derald Wing Sue, forced a reckoning with culture, race, gender and power.
The multicultural counselling competencies, and later the social justice
turn, reshaped what competence is understood to mean. I want to flag
honestly that this fourth moment is unfinished. We will return to it in Week
Twelve, in the Multicultural Counseling course, and again in Week Thirteen.

SLIDE 11 of 22   |   QUOTE   |   00:01   |   00:16 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EYEBROW: Carl Rogers, 1942
    It is the client who knows what hurts, what directions to go, what problems are crucial, what experiences have been deep...

NARRATION
I want to read you Rogers in his own words, because paraphrase softens the
argument. He wrote: It is the client who knows what hurts, what directions
to go, what problems are crucial, what experiences have been deeply buried.
It began to occur to me that unless I had a need to demonstrate my own
cleverness and learning, I would do better to rely upon the client for the
direction of movement in the process. There are two claims in that passage,
and they are worth separating. The first is a claim about knowledge. Rogers
is saying that the client's experiential knowledge of their own life is real
knowledge, and that it is not automatically inferior to the clinician's
theoretical knowledge. The second is a claim about the counsellor's motives.
He names, with unusual candour, the temptation to perform expertise, the
need to demonstrate cleverness. Every one of you will feel that temptation.
It arrives the first time a client looks at you expectantly and you have no
idea what to say. The discipline Rogers is recommending is not passivity. It
is the discipline of staying close to the person's actual account rather
than reaching for the impressive formulation. Hold that thought, because in
Week Three, when we study technique, you will meet this tension again in a
practical form.

TITLE: The Scientist-Practitioner
EYEBROW: Part Three
  on screen: How research and practice are supposed to inform one another.

NARRATION
Part Three turns to the model of the professional that this programme is
training you to become. It is called the scientist-practitioner model, and
it was formalised at a conference in Boulder, Colorado, in nineteen forty
nine. The idea is simple to state and demanding to live. A psychologist is
not a technician who applies a fixed procedure, and not a detached
researcher who studies other people's work. A psychologist is a professional
who brings scientific habits of mind to every act of practice, and who
brings the questions raised by practice back to the evidence.

TITLE: The Reflective Practice Cycle
EYEBROW: Key Concept 3
  on screen: Six steps you will be expected to run, explicitly, for every client you see this year.
    1. Observe: Client work raises a question worth answering
    2. Read: Locate and appraise the best available evidence
    3. Formulate: Integrate evidence with the client's values and context
    4. Act: Deliver the intervention with fidelity
    5. Measure: Monitor outcome session by session
    6. Reflect: Revise, or share what was learned

NARRATION
Here is the cycle, expressed as six concrete steps that I expect you to be
able to run and, in your later placements, to document. Step one, observe.
Your client work raises a question. Suppose a client you are treating for
anxiety is not improving after four sessions of the protocol you chose. That
is not a failure. That is a question. Step two, read. You go to the
evidence. What does the literature say about non-response to that protocol
at four sessions? Are there moderators, things about this client's
presentation that predict a different response? Step three, formulate. You
take what you learned and integrate it with what you know about this
particular client, their values, their resources, their culture, and their
circumstances. The evidence never tells you what to do for a named person.
It constrains your options. Step four, act. You deliver the intervention,
and you deliver it with fidelity, meaning you actually do what the protocol
says rather than an approximation of it. Step five, measure. You monitor
outcome session by session rather than waiting until the end. This is the
step most often skipped in busy services, and it is the one that makes the
whole cycle scientific. Without measurement you are not running a cycle at
all, you are merely accumulating impressions, and clinical impressions are
demonstrably unreliable. Step six, reflect, revise, or share what you
learned. If the change worked, you have something worth telling a colleague.
If it did not, you have something equally worth telling. The cycle is not a
staircase. You will loop through it many times with the same client. And
notice that it is also a description of your thesis, which begins in Week
Nine of this semester. Milestone one, topic selection, is nothing more than
step one, observe, applied systematically to a question you care about.

TITLE: What the Model Demands of You
EYEBROW: Key Concept 4
  As a consumer of research:
    • Read primary sources, not only textbooks and summaries
    • Appraise study quality rather than accepting conclusions
    • Recognise the difference between evidence and enthusiasm
    • Notice whose populations are missing from the evidence base
  As a reflective practitioner:
    • Monitor your own outcomes rather than trusting memory
    • Seek supervision and use it honestly
    • Record your reasoning so it can be examined
    • Treat your own errors as data

NARRATION
Let me make the demands concrete, because the phrase scientist-practitioner
can float about as a slogan if we are not careful. On the left, what the
model demands of you as a consumer of research. You must read primary
sources, not only textbooks and lecture summaries, including, I should say,
not only these lectures. You must learn to appraise the quality of a study
rather than simply accepting its conclusion, which means understanding
sampling, measurement, comparison conditions and the difference between
statistical and practical significance. You must learn to recognise the
difference between evidence and enthusiasm, because those two things wear
similar clothes. And you must notice whose populations are missing from the
evidence base. This last point is not a political footnote. If the
randomised controlled trials that inform a protocol were conducted almost
entirely on European and North American samples, then applying that protocol
in Ibadan or Kano is an extrapolation, and you should know that it is an
extrapolation and be honest about the uncertainty. On the right, what the
model demands of you as a reflective practitioner. You must monitor your own
outcomes rather than trusting your memory of how clients are doing, because
memory is systematically flattering. You must seek supervision and use it
honestly, including the parts of your work you are least proud of. You must
record your reasoning so that it can be examined by another person. And you
must treat your own errors as data rather than as shameful secrets. I will
tell you plainly that the practitioners who harm clients are rarely the ones
who make mistakes. Everyone makes mistakes. The ones who harm clients are
the ones who cannot tolerate being wrong, and who therefore never inspect
their own work.

SLIDE 15 of 22   |   BULLETS   |   00:02   |   00:22 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: What the Evidence Says
EYEBROW: Research Spotlight
  on screen: Five findings every trainee in this programme should be able to state from memory.
    • Psychotherapy works, and the effect is substantial
      – Meta-analytic estimates place the average effect at roughly 0.7 to 0.8 standard deviations — comparable to, or larger than, many medical interventions in routine use.
    • The alliance predicts outcome across every orientation
      – Agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and the bond between client and clinician predict outcome regardless of the model being used.
    • Common factors and specific techniques both matter
      – The long-running debate between them is a false dichotomy in practice. Effective treatment deliberately uses both.
    • Transplanting an evidence base without cultural adaptation costs you engagement
      – Adapting interventions to the client's cultural context reliably improves retention and outcome for minoritised populations.

NARRATION
Now the evidence, because everything I have said so far could be dismissed
as professional self-description. Five findings, and I want you to be able
to state each of them from memory, with the reasoning behind it. First.
Psychotherapy works, and the effect is substantial. When you pool hundreds
of trials, the average effect of psychological treatment is somewhere around
zero point seven to zero point eight standard deviations. That number means
very little until you compare it, so let me compare it. It is comparable to,
and in many cases larger than, the effect sizes reported for widely used
medical interventions, including several that are routinely prescribed in
this country. If you would not describe a drug with that effect size as
merely talking, you should not describe psychotherapy that way either.
Second. The therapeutic alliance predicts outcome across every theoretical
orientation. This is one of the most replicated findings in the whole field.
Whatever model you practise, the agreement you reach with your client about
what the goals are, the agreement you reach about what tasks you will both
do to pursue those goals, and the quality of the bond between you, all
predict how well the client does. Notice what this means for your training.
It means that your relational skill is not a soft extra sitting on top of
your technique. It is an evidence-based component of treatment in its own
right. Third, common factors and specific techniques both matter, and the
long argument about which is doing the work is a false dichotomy in
practice. The alliance may be a common factor, but I cannot build an
alliance in the abstract. I build it while doing something, and what I do is
the specific technique. Effective clinicians deliberately use both. Fourth.
Transplanting an evidence base without cultural adaptation costs you
engagement. The trials that established the protocols were largely conducted
on European and North American samples. When interventions are sensitively
adapted to the client's cultural context, retention and outcome reliably
improve for minoritised groups. That finding is the empirical foundation for
the multicultural commitment I described earlier. Fifth, and most
uncomfortably for you as a trainee, the therapist is a measurable source of
variance. Some clinicians consistently achieve better outcomes than others
with comparable caseloads, and, crucially, most clinicians believe they are
already above average, which cannot be true of all of them. The gap can be
narrowed, but only by measuring your own outcomes and using that feedback
deliberately. That is the scientist-practitioner cycle again, and it is why
I keep returning to it.

TITLE: Counseling Psychology in the Nigerian Context
EYEBROW: Application
    • Cultural concepts of distress shape presentation
      – Clients may express psychological distress somatically, spiritually, or through family and community language rather than through the vocabulary of DSM categories.
    • Care is plural
      – Families, religious communities and traditional healers are often the first and most trusted points of contact. Effective practice works with them, not against them.
    • Support is socially structured
      – Emotional and financial support is frequently organised through extended family networks, mutual aid and religious associations as much as through state services.
    • Legal and regulatory frameworks have changed recently
      – Nigeria's mental health legislation was modernised by the National Mental Health Act signed in 2023, with further professional regulation under continuous development.

NARRATION
Four features of the Nigerian context that should shape your practice, and
that I want you to carry into every course this year. The first is that
cultural concepts of distress shape presentation. A client may describe
their suffering through bodily symptoms, through spiritual affliction,
through family conflict, or through a sense of social and moral failure,
rather than through the vocabulary of mood, anxiety and trauma that our
diagnostic systems use. This is not evasion or ignorance on the client's
part. It is a different, and internally coherent, explanatory model. If you
listen only for DSM categories, you will frequently miss what you are being
told. The second feature is that care is plural. Families, religious
communities, traditional healers, and lay counsellors are often the first,
and frequently the most trusted, points of contact for people in distress.
The practical question for you is not whether this is scientifically ideal.
It is how to work alongside these resources with respect and without
abandoning your own professional judgement. A counselor who tells a client
that their pastor, their mother, and their imam are all obstacles to proper
treatment will lose the client. The third feature is that support is
socially structured. Where the state provides little, people provide for one
another through extended family, savings associations, religious
congregations, and community networks. Those structures are a genuine
resource, and part of your assessment should be understanding which of them
a given client can access, and which of them are themselves a source of
strain. The fourth feature is legal and regulatory change. Nigeria's mental
health legislation was modernised by the National Mental Health Act, signed
into law in January 2023, replacing a colonial era statute. This is a
significant development, and the professional and regulatory landscape
around it continues to evolve. You should read the Act itself at some point
this semester. It is not long, and it tells you what the law expects of you.

SLIDE 18 of 22   |   CASESTUDY   |   00:03   |   00:27 in
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE: Vignette: Tolu
EYEBROW: Case Vignette
    2 paragraphs, 4 discussion questions

NARRATION
Let us apply all of this to a case. Tolu is twenty nine years old, works in
a bank, and comes to a general hospital complaining of persistent headache,
poor sleep, and a heaviness in the chest that has lasted about four months.
Physical investigations have found no explanation for her symptoms. On
further discussion, and note that it took further discussion, she describes
growing worry about job security, a recent restructuring at work, and a
sense that she is falling behind her peers. She has not told her family,
because, she says, they are already carrying enough. She asks whether she
needs a doctor or a counsellor, and she reports that she has been told to
stop thinking too much. I want to work through the questions on this slide
with you, and I want you to notice how each one draws on a different part of
the lecture. First, which profession is the appropriate point of entry, and
what would change your answer? On what we have seen, this presentation is
consistent with an anxiety or adjustment difficulty, and there is no
indication of psychosis, of risk to life, or of a condition requiring
medication as a first step. So the appropriate entry point is a
psychological professional. But what would change the answer? If she
disclosed active suicidal intent with a plan, the urgency and the
composition of the team change immediately. If the sleep disturbance were
severe and persistent with early morning waking and marked weight loss, you
would want a psychiatric opinion. So, the answer is provisional, and it is
revised as information arrives. That is what competent formulation looks
like. Second, what is she communicating through her physical symptoms that
she has not said in words? In many cultural contexts, and this is very much
the case in Nigeria, bodily idioms are a legitimate and often more
acceptable way of expressing psychological distress. Her headache and chest
heaviness are not a distraction from the real problem. They are the form in
which she can bring the problem to a hospital at all. Third, what strengths
and social resources would you want to assess? She is employed, in a
demanding job, and she is seeking help, which took considerable courage. She
has a family she is protecting, which suggests both connection and a burden
of obligation. What else does she have? Does she have savings, colleagues, a
religious community, a friend in whom she has confided? Those resources are
the basis of any plan you build with her. Fourth, what would a strengths
based formulation add to a purely symptomatic one? It would add the question
of what she is trying to protect, what she is good at, and what she wants
her life to look like. A purely symptomatic formulation asks how we remove
the headache. A strengths based formulation also asks who she is when she is
well, and what she needs in order to get back there. We will return to Tolu
at several points this semester.

TITLE: Key Takeaways
EYEBROW: Summary
    1. Counseling psychology is a psychological specialty defined by lifespan functioning, strengths, prevention and context, not by the removal of symptoms alone.
    2. Its boundaries with neighbouring professions rest on training and legal scope of practice, not on prestige. Only physicians prescribe.
    3. The profession carries four historical deposits: vocational guidance, the Rogerian relationship, the formal naming of the specialty, and the multicultural and social justice turn.
    4. The scientist-practitioner model is a working cycle, not a slogan: observe, read, formulate, act, measure, reflect.
    5. In Nigeria the arithmetic of need makes prevention, group work, task-sharing and community-level practice core professional duties.

NARRATION
Let me consolidate. Five takeaways. One. Counseling psychology is a
psychological specialty defined by functioning across the lifespan, by
strengths, by prevention, and by context. It is not defined by the removal
of symptoms alone. Two. Its boundaries with neighbouring professions rest on
training and on legal scope of practice, not on prestige and not on claims
of superiority. Only physicians prescribe. Three. The profession carries
four historical deposits. Vocational guidance, from Parsons. The primacy of
the therapeutic relationship, from Rogers. The formal naming and standard
setting of the specialty in the nineteen fifties. And the multicultural and
social justice turn of the nineteen nineties, which is still unfinished.
Four. The scientist-practitioner model is a working cycle, not a slogan.
Observe, read, formulate, act, measure, reflect, and loop. Five. In Nigeria,
the arithmetic of need makes prevention, group work, task-shifting and
community level practice core professional duties rather than optional
extras. If you remember only one thing from this lecture, remember the
fourth takeaway, because it is the one you will be assessed on continuously
and it is the one that will keep your clients safer.
