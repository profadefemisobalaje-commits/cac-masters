# Energy Project Finance and Fiscal Evaluation Templates {.appendix letter="K"}

This appendix provides templates and formulas for the financial and fiscal evaluation of energy and resource projects, to support the legal analysis in Chapter 16. Lawyers need not be financial modellers, but they must understand the metrics used by lenders, sponsors, and governments, because these metrics drive negotiation, are embedded in contractual covenants, and are central to quantum in disputes. **All numerical examples are hypothetical.**

## Core Investment Metrics

**Net present value (NPV).** The sum of a project's discounted net cash flows:

> NPV = Σ (CF~t~ ÷ (1 + r)^t^), for t = 0 to n

where CF~t~ is the net cash flow in year *t* and *r* is the discount rate. A positive NPV at the investor's cost of capital indicates value creation.

**Internal rate of return (IRR).** The discount rate at which NPV equals zero. Sponsors compare project or equity IRR against hurdle rates reflecting their cost of capital and country risk.

**Payback period.** The time required for cumulative cash flows to become positive.

**Levelised cost of electricity (LCOE).** The constant real price per unit of electricity at which the present value of revenues equals the present value of costs over the project's life:

> LCOE = Σ ((I~t~ + O&M~t~ + F~t~) ÷ (1 + r)^t^) ÷ Σ (E~t~ ÷ (1 + r)^t^)

where I is investment, O&M is operating and maintenance cost, F is fuel cost, and E is electricity generated.

## Debt Metrics Used in Loan Covenants

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR).** Cash flow available for debt service in a period divided by debt service (principal and interest) due in that period. Lenders typically require minimum DSCRs (for example, 1.20–1.40× for contracted power projects, higher for merchant or resource-exposed projects); a DSCR below a "lock-up" level may block distributions to equity, and below a lower level may constitute an event of default.

**Loan life coverage ratio (LLCR).** The NPV of cash flow available for debt service over the remaining loan term divided by outstanding debt.

**Reserve tail.** In upstream reserve-based lending, the proportion of reserves remaining after the scheduled repayment of debt.

## Worked Example: Solar IPP (HYPOTHETICAL figures)

A 50 MW solar plant costs USD 40 million, financed 70 per cent debt and 30 per cent equity. Annual output is 90 GWh, sold at USD 0.07/kWh (revenue USD 6.3 million). Operating costs are USD 0.8 million per year. Annual debt service on an 18-year loan is USD 2.9 million. Ignoring tax for simplicity, CFADS is USD 5.5 million and DSCR is 5.5 ÷ 2.9 ≈ 1.90×. If curtailment reduced output by 15 per cent without deemed energy compensation, revenue would fall to USD 5.355 million, CFADS to USD 4.555 million, and DSCR to about 1.57×. The example illustrates why lenders insist on deemed energy and curtailment protections (Appendix E, Clause 6).

## Fiscal Evaluation: Government Take

**Government take** is the share of a project's pre-tax net cash flow (revenue less exploration, development, and operating costs) received by the government through all fiscal instruments — royalties, taxes, production shares, bonuses, state participation (net of the state's contributions), and other levies:

> Government take (%) = Government's undiscounted (or discounted) net cash flow ÷ Project's pre-tax net cash flow × 100

**Effective royalty rate.** The minimum share of gross revenue the government receives in any period — revealing the degree to which the regime front-loads government revenue.

**Progressivity.** Whether government take rises with project profitability (for instance, as prices rise or costs fall). Instruments such as resource rent taxes, R-factor PSC splits, and windfall taxes increase progressivity; royalties and bonuses are regressive with respect to profitability.

## Nigeria: Key Fiscal Features under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021

The PIA introduced a fiscal framework comprising royalties (with rates varying by terrain and production levels, and price-based royalties), hydrocarbon tax (applicable to crude oil from onshore and shallow water areas), and companies income tax, together with cost price ratio limits on deductible costs, gas-related incentives, and contributions to the Host Communities Development Trusts (3 per cent of the previous year's actual annual operating expenditure) and the Frontier Exploration Fund (30 per cent of NNPC Ltd's profit oil and profit gas). Readers should consult the Act, subsequent fiscal and tax legislation (including the tax reform statutes of 2025), and NUPRC regulations for current rates and conditions.

## Quantum in Disputes: Valuation Methods

| Method | Description | Typical use |
|:----------------------------|:-----------------------------|:--------------------------------|
| Discounted cash flow (income approach) | Present value of projected future cash flows | Going concerns with record of profitability or reliable contracted revenues |
| Market approach (comparable transactions or trading multiples) | Value by reference to prices paid for similar assets | Where reliable comparables exist |
| Asset-based / cost approach (sunk costs) | Amounts invested, sometimes with interest | Early-stage projects with speculative future profits |
| Liquidation value | Value on sale of assets | Non-going concerns |

Table: Valuation methods in energy disputes.

Tribunals have often been reluctant to award DCF valuations for projects without a track record of profitability, preferring sunk costs, as in several ICSID cases involving early-stage projects. Interest, the valuation date (date of the measure versus date of the award, particularly in unlawful expropriation following the principle of full reparation in the *Factory at Chorzów* case), and the treatment of country risk in discount rates are frequently decisive issues.

# Corporate Human Rights Due Diligence Checklist {.appendix letter="L"}

This checklist supports the design and review of human rights due diligence (HRDD) by companies in the energy and extractive sectors. It is structured around the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011), the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct (2023 update) and the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct (2018), and the mandatory due diligence regimes examined in Chapters 21 and 22, including the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (2024/1760) — whose scope and timetable were subsequently revised through the EU's 2025 "Omnibus" simplification process, so that readers should verify the current requirements — the French Duty of Vigilance Law (2017), and the German Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (2021).

## Step 1 — Policy Commitment and Governance (UNGP 16)

- [ ] A human rights policy approved at the most senior level, publicly available, and communicated internally and to business partners.
- [ ] Commitment references the International Bill of Human Rights, the ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work, and, where relevant, UNDRIP and the Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights.
- [ ] Board oversight and senior management responsibility assigned; human rights integrated into risk management, incentives, and procurement.
- [ ] Resources and expertise allocated.

## Step 2 — Identify and Assess Actual and Potential Adverse Impacts (UNGP 18)

- [ ] Mapping of operations, joint ventures, and value chain (suppliers, contractors, security providers, customers).
- [ ] Assessment of salient human rights risks in each context, prioritised by severity (scale, scope, irremediability) and likelihood.
- [ ] Meaningful consultation with potentially affected groups and their representatives, including women, indigenous peoples, workers, and human rights defenders.
- [ ] Specific risks in energy and extractives assessed: land acquisition and resettlement; FPIC; security forces and use of force; water and pollution; health and safety; labour rights (including in contractors and the supply chain, and forced and child labour risks in mineral supply chains); gender-based violence; conflict and fragility; corruption; climate impacts; and impacts on human rights defenders.
- [ ] Heightened due diligence in conflict-affected and high-risk areas.

## Step 3 — Integrate and Act to Cease, Prevent, and Mitigate (UNGP 19)

- [ ] Findings integrated into decision-making (investment, project design, procurement, contracting).
- [ ] Actions to cease impacts the company causes, prevent and mitigate those to which it contributes, and use leverage regarding those directly linked to it through business relationships.
- [ ] Contractual clauses with suppliers and partners, with verification and support (not merely transfer of responsibility).
- [ ] Responsible disengagement considered where leverage fails, taking account of the impacts of withdrawal.

## Step 4 — Track Effectiveness (UNGP 20)

- [ ] Qualitative and quantitative indicators.
- [ ] Feedback from affected stakeholders.
- [ ] Internal and independent audits; review of grievance data.

## Step 5 — Communicate (UNGP 21)

- [ ] Public reporting on how impacts are addressed, sufficient to allow evaluation (for example, under CSRD/ESRS, where applicable, or the UNGP Reporting Framework).
- [ ] Communication with affected stakeholders in accessible forms.
- [ ] Protection of confidentiality and safety of informants.

## Step 6 — Remediation and Grievance Mechanisms (UNGPs 22, 29, 31)

- [ ] Process to provide for or cooperate in remediation of impacts caused or contributed to.
- [ ] Operational-level grievance mechanism meeting the effectiveness criteria of UNGP 31: legitimate, accessible, predictable, equitable, transparent, rights-compatible, a source of continuous learning, and based on engagement and dialogue.
- [ ] Non-retaliation and protection of complainants.
- [ ] Mechanism does not preclude access to judicial or other non-judicial remedies (including OECD National Contact Points).

## Step 7 — Climate and Environment

- [ ] Assessment of environmental impacts that affect human rights (water, health, livelihoods).
- [ ] Climate transition plan, where required (for example, under the CSDDD as adopted, or applicable disclosure rules), consistent with the 1.5°C goal.

## Step 8 — Legal Risk Review

- [ ] Exposure to parent company liability in home-state courts (see *Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe* [2019] UKSC 20; *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* [2021] UKSC 3; *Four Nigerian Farmers and Milieudefensie v Shell* (Court of Appeal of The Hague, January 2021)).
- [ ] Compliance with mandatory due diligence and reporting laws applicable to the company.
- [ ] Import restrictions on goods made with forced labour (for example, the EU Forced Labour Regulation (2024/3015) and United States customs measures).
- [ ] Anti-corruption and sanctions compliance in connection with security and community payments.

## Red Flags Requiring Escalation

1. Allegations of killings, torture, or arbitrary detention linked to security forces protecting operations.
2. Forced eviction without due process or adequate compensation.
3. Absence of consent from indigenous peoples where required.
4. Child or forced labour in operations or the supply chain.
5. Serious pollution affecting drinking water or health.
6. Threats or attacks against human rights defenders who oppose the project.
7. Operations in areas of armed conflict without heightened due diligence.
