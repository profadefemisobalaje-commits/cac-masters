# Energy Law Terminology and Technical Glossary {.appendix letter="S"}

This glossary defines legal, commercial, and technical terms frequently used in energy and natural resources law. Definitions are general; the meaning of a term in a particular statute or contract depends on its definition there.

**Abandonment.** The plugging of wells and removal or making safe of facilities at the end of their life; often used interchangeably with decommissioning.

**Acreage.** An area offered for or covered by a petroleum licence or contract.

**Ancillary services.** Services supporting the stable operation of an electricity system, such as frequency response, reserve, voltage control, and black start.

**Annual Contract Quantity (ACQ).** The quantity of gas a buyer is contracted to take in a year under a gas sales agreement.

**Appraisal.** Drilling and studies following a discovery to determine its size and commerciality.

**Associated gas.** Natural gas found in association with crude oil, either dissolved in the oil or as a gas cap.

**Barrel (bbl).** Standard unit of crude oil volume, equal to 42 US gallons (about 159 litres).

**Benefit-sharing.** Arrangements by which communities or states share in the benefits of resource development, through revenues, employment, services, or equity.

**Bonus.** A lump-sum payment to the state on signature of a contract, discovery, or attainment of production levels.

**Capacity factor.** The ratio of actual electricity output over a period to the maximum possible output at full nameplate capacity.

**Carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS).** Technologies that capture carbon dioxide from emission sources or the atmosphere and either use it or store it permanently in geological formations.

**Carried interest.** An interest whose share of costs is paid by other parties (typically until a commercial discovery), to be recovered later from production.

**Commercial discovery.** A discovery that the contractor (and, where required, the state) considers economically viable to develop.

**Concession.** A grant by the state of the right to explore for and exploit resources, under which the concessionaire typically acquires title to the resources produced, subject to royalty and tax.

**Contract for difference (CfD).** An arrangement under which a generator receives (or pays) the difference between a strike price and a market reference price.

**Cost oil / cost petroleum.** Under a PSC, the share of production allocated to the contractor to recover its costs.

**Critical minerals.** Minerals essential to economic or national security and subject to supply risk, as defined by the relevant jurisdiction's lists.

**Curtailment.** Instruction to a generator to reduce output, typically because of network constraints or oversupply.

**Decommissioning.** The process of ending operations at a facility and removing, making safe, or re-using its infrastructure and restoring the site.

**Deemed energy.** Energy a generator is deemed to have delivered (and is paid for) when prevented from delivering by curtailment or grid unavailability for which it is not responsible.

**Delivery point.** The point at which product is delivered and title and risk pass.

**Distribution.** The transport of electricity or gas over lower-voltage or lower-pressure networks to end users.

**Domestic market obligation.** A requirement to supply a portion of production to the domestic market, sometimes at regulated prices.

**Economic equilibrium clause.** A contractual provision requiring adjustment of terms to restore the parties' economic balance after specified changes, such as changes in law.

**Energy community.** A legal entity, often citizen-controlled, engaged in energy generation, supply, aggregation, storage, or related services for members or the local community.

**Energy justice.** A framework concerned with fair distribution of the benefits and burdens of energy systems, fair procedures, and recognition of affected groups.

**EPC contract.** Engineering, procurement and construction contract, typically lump-sum and turnkey.

**Expropriation.** The taking of property by the state, either directly (by transfer of title) or indirectly (by measures having equivalent effect).

**Fair and equitable treatment (FET).** An investment treaty standard protecting investors against arbitrary, discriminatory, grossly unfair, or non-transparent treatment, and, in some formulations, frustration of legitimate expectations.

**Farm-in / farm-out.** A transaction in which a party acquires an interest in a licence (farm-in) from the holder (farm-out), usually in return for paying a share of costs.

**Feed-in tariff.** A guaranteed price for renewable electricity fed into the grid, typically for a fixed period.

**Final investment decision (FID).** The decision by project sponsors to proceed with construction.

**Flaring.** The controlled burning of natural gas, often associated gas, at production sites.

**Force majeure.** An event beyond a party's reasonable control that prevents performance, excusing the affected party from liability to the extent provided in the contract.

**Free, prior and informed consent (FPIC).** The principle that indigenous peoples should be able to give or withhold consent to projects affecting their lands, territories, and resources, through processes free from coercion, before authorisation, and with full information.

**Gas-to-power.** The use of natural gas to generate electricity.

**Government take.** The share of a project's net cash flow received by government through all fiscal instruments.

**Green hydrogen.** Hydrogen produced by electrolysis of water using renewable electricity.

**Grid code.** Technical rules governing connection to and operation of the electricity network.

**Guarantee of origin / renewable energy certificate.** An instrument certifying that a unit of electricity was produced from a particular source.

**Host government agreement (HGA).** An agreement between a host state and investors governing a major project, often a cross-border pipeline.

**Independent power producer (IPP).** A privately owned generator selling electricity to utilities or customers.

**Interconnector.** A transmission line or pipeline connecting the networks of different jurisdictions or zones.

**Joint operating agreement (JOA).** An agreement among co-venturers governing the conduct of operations under a licence, including the role of the operator, voting, and default.

**Levelised cost of electricity (LCOE).** The constant price per unit of electricity at which a project's revenues would equal its costs over its life in present value terms.

**Liquefied natural gas (LNG).** Natural gas cooled to about −162°C to become liquid for transport by ship.

**Local content.** Requirements or policies promoting the use of domestic labour, goods, services, and companies in resource projects.

**Mini-grid.** An isolated or grid-connected small-scale electricity network serving a community.

**Nationally determined contribution (NDC).** A state's climate action plan under the Paris Agreement.

**Net metering / net billing.** Arrangements under which consumers who generate electricity are credited for power exported to the grid.

**Non-associated gas.** Natural gas found in reservoirs without significant crude oil.

**Off-taker.** The purchaser of a project's output under a long-term contract.

**Operator.** The party responsible for conducting day-to-day operations on behalf of co-venturers.

**Permanent sovereignty over natural resources (PSNR).** The principle that peoples and states have sovereignty over the natural resources within their territory.

**Power purchase agreement (PPA).** A long-term contract for the sale of electricity.

**Price review.** A contractual mechanism allowing periodic adjustment of the price in a long-term contract.

**Production sharing contract (PSC).** A contract under which the state retains title to resources and the contractor recovers costs and receives a share of profit from production.

**Profit oil / profit petroleum.** Production remaining after royalty and cost oil, shared between the state and contractor under a PSC.

**Prosumer.** A consumer who also produces energy, typically through rooftop solar.

**Regulatory sandbox.** A framework allowing innovators to test products or business models under relaxed or tailored regulatory requirements, subject to safeguards.

**Relinquishment.** The surrender of parts of a licence area at specified times.

**Reserves.** Quantities of petroleum or minerals that are anticipated to be commercially recoverable, classified by certainty (for example, proved, probable, possible) under systems such as the Petroleum Resources Management System.

**Ring-fencing.** Limitation of the deduction of costs to the project or licence area that generated them.

**Royalty.** A payment to the resource owner calculated as a share of production or its value.

**Service contract.** A contract under which a contractor performs exploration or production services for a fee, without acquiring title to production.

**Ship-or-pay.** A transport contract obligation to pay for reserved capacity whether or not used.

**Stabilisation clause.** A contractual provision protecting an investor against adverse changes in law, through freezing, economic equilibrium, or allocation of burden.

**Stranded asset.** An asset that suffers premature write-down or conversion to a liability, for example because of climate policy or market changes.

**Take-or-pay.** An obligation to pay for a minimum quantity whether or not taken.

**Third-party access.** The right of third parties to use network infrastructure (pipelines, transmission) owned by another, on regulated or negotiated terms.

**Transit.** The carriage of energy across the territory of a state that is neither the origin nor the destination.

**Transmission.** The transport of electricity at high voltage, or gas at high pressure, over long distances.

**Unbundling.** Separation of network activities (transmission, distribution) from competitive activities (generation, supply), legally, functionally, or by ownership.

**Unitisation.** Joint development of a reservoir straddling licence or international boundaries as a single unit.

**Windfall tax.** A tax on unexpectedly high profits, often in response to price spikes.

**Work programme.** The exploration or development activities a licensee commits to carry out.

# Further Research Topics and Emerging Scholarly Debates {.appendix letter="T"}

This appendix supplements the doctoral research agenda in Chapter 30, section 30.8, and the doctoral research questions at the end of each chapter, by setting out a wider range of research topics and the scholarly debates to which they relate. Topics are intended as starting points; any doctoral project must be refined through a literature review, methodological design, and discussion with supervisors (Chapter 28; Appendix N).

## Debate 1: Sovereignty, Development, and Climate Obligations

*The debate.* Do producer states, particularly developing states, retain a right to develop their fossil fuel resources, or do climate obligations require them to leave resources in the ground? How should burdens be shared?

*Research topics.* (1) The implications of the ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion for petroleum licensing in Africa. (2) The legal status of the "phase-out" and "transition away" language in COP decisions. (3) Compensation for forgone extraction: legal bases and precedents. (4) The proposed Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty: legal design and prospects. (5) The right to development and the energy transition.

## Debate 2: Investment Law after the Energy Charter Treaty

*The debate.* Is investment treaty protection an obstacle to climate action, a necessary safeguard for investment in the transition, or both? Can it be reformed?

*Research topics.* (1) The effect of the sunset clause on withdrawals from the ECT. (2) Climate carve-outs in new-generation investment treaties. (3) Counterclaims by states for environmental harm. (4) The future of ISDS in Africa under the AfCFTA Investment Protocol. (5) Compensation standards for climate-motivated measures.

## Debate 3: Critical Minerals and Value Capture

*The debate.* Can resource-rich developing states capture greater value from transition minerals through export restrictions, local processing, and state participation, consistently with trade and investment law? Will the critical minerals boom repeat the "resource curse"?

*Research topics.* (1) Export bans and WTO law: lessons from *China — Raw Materials* and *Indonesia — Raw Materials*. (2) Critical minerals agreements and partnerships: legal nature and equity. (3) Mineral producer cooperation (cartels or coordination) and international law. (4) Due diligence regulation in consumer markets and its effects on producer states. (5) Artisanal cobalt mining and formalisation.

## Debate 4: Deep Seabed Mining and the Common Heritage

*The debate.* Should deep seabed mining proceed, and if so under what rules? What does the common heritage of mankind require?

*Research topics.* (1) The legality of unilateral authorisation of seabed mining outside the ISA framework. (2) The "two-year rule" and the ISA Mining Code negotiations. (3) Benefit-sharing under article 140 UNCLOS. (4) The relationship between the BBNJ Agreement and the ISA. (5) Precaution and moratoria.

## Debate 5: Justice and Energy Access

*The debate.* How should the law address the needs of those without modern energy? Is there a right to energy? How should justice be integrated into transition law?

*Research topics.* (1) A right to electricity under African human rights law. (2) Legal frameworks for mini-grids and distributed energy in Nigeria's state electricity markets. (3) Clean cooking regulation. (4) Just transition law for coal and oil-dependent regions. (5) Legal design of Just Energy Transition Partnerships.

## Debate 6: Corporate Accountability for Transnational Harm

*The debate.* How far should parent companies and value chain actors be liable for harm caused abroad? What role for mandatory due diligence and home-state litigation?

*Research topics.* (1) The impact of the EU's Omnibus revisions on the CSDDD. (2) Parent company liability after *Okpabi* and the Dutch Shell cases. (3) Remediation of Niger Delta pollution: legal frameworks and implementation. (4) A binding treaty on business and human rights: progress and prospects. (5) Corporate climate liability after *Lliuya v RWE*.

## Debate 7: Nigeria's Energy Reforms

*The debate.* Will the PIA and the Electricity Act 2023 achieve their objectives of investment, efficiency, accountability, and access? What are the constitutional and federal dimensions?

*Research topics.* (1) The commercialisation of NNPC Ltd and corporate governance. (2) Implementation of the host community provisions of the PIA. (3) The Frontier Exploration Fund and the future of frontier exploration. (4) State electricity markets: constitutional allocation, regulatory capacity, and investment. (5) Gas commercialisation and the "decade of gas". (6) Refining policy and the domestic crude supply obligation after the commissioning of the Dangote refinery.

## Debate 8: Digital, AI, and Energy

*The debate.* How should law govern the digitalisation of energy systems and the energy demands of digital technologies?

*Research topics.* (1) Liability for AI in grid operations. (2) Data centres and grid connection queues: legal responses. (3) Energy data governance and consumer rights. (4) Cyber security regulation of energy infrastructure in Africa. (5) Smart contracts and peer-to-peer energy trading.

## Debate 9: Climate Finance and Financial Regulation

*The debate.* How should financial regulation address climate risk, and how can climate finance flows to developing countries be scaled and governed?

*Research topics.* (1) The legal architecture of the New Collective Quantified Goal. (2) Climate risk in African financial regulation. (3) Sovereign debt and climate: debt-for-nature swaps and climate-resilient debt clauses. (4) Green taxonomies in emerging markets. (5) Article 6 carbon markets: legal integrity and host country frameworks.

## Debate 10: Theory and Method

*The debate.* What is energy and natural resources law as a field, and how should it be studied?

*Research topics.* (1) Energy law as a discipline: coherence and boundaries. (2) TWAIL perspectives on resource law. (3) Empirical legal studies of energy regulation in Africa. (4) Legal histories of the oil concession. (5) Interdisciplinary methods combining law with energy systems modelling.

## Identifying a Research Gap: Practical Steps

1. Read the most recent review articles and leading monographs on the topic.
2. Search databases (Appendix P) for recent articles, theses, and reports.
3. Identify questions that existing works pose but do not answer, contradictions between authorities, jurisdictions or contexts not studied, and new developments not yet analysed.
4. Discuss emerging ideas with supervisors, practitioners, and peers.
5. Test feasibility: access to sources, methods, time, and resources.
6. Formulate a precise question and articulate the expected contribution (Appendix N, sections 4 and 9).
