# Annotated Production Sharing Contract {.appendix letter="C"}

This appendix presents the core economic and governance clauses of a production sharing contract (PSC), with annotations explaining their purpose, the main negotiating issues, and the points at which disputes most commonly arise. The clauses are an **illustrative teaching text** prepared for this book; they do not reproduce any particular state's model contract. The PSC form originated in Indonesia in the 1960s and has since been adopted, in many variants, across Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America. Nigeria has used PSCs extensively, particularly for deep offshore acreage under the Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contracts Act (as amended in 2019), and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 provides for production sharing contracts among the forms of petroleum mining lease arrangements available for new acreage.

## Clause 1 — Nature of the Contract

> 1.1 The Contractor is appointed as the exclusive contractor of the State [acting through the National Oil Company] to conduct Petroleum Operations in the Contract Area. Title to Petroleum shall remain with the State until the Delivery Point, at which point title to the Contractor's share of Cost Petroleum and Profit Petroleum shall pass to the Contractor.

*Annotation.* The defining feature of the PSC is that the state retains ownership of the resource and the contractor is remunerated in kind with a share of production. This distinguishes it from a concession (in which the licensee owns the petroleum produced, subject to royalty and tax) and from a service contract (in which the contractor is paid a fee in cash or kind without acquiring title). The legal distinction may matter for accounting (booking of reserves), for constitutional ownership provisions, and for the characterisation of the contractor's investment under investment treaties. In economic substance, however, a well-designed PSC and a concession with royalty and tax can produce similar outcomes.

## Clause 2 — Risk and Financing

> 2.1 The Contractor shall provide all funds and bear all risks of Petroleum Operations. The Contractor shall recover its costs only from Cost Petroleum in accordance with Clause 6. If no Commercial Discovery is made, the Contractor shall bear all costs incurred without right of recovery.

*Annotation.* Exploration risk sits wholly with the contractor. The state (or NOC) may be "carried" through exploration — that is, its share of costs is paid by the contractor and recovered later from production — so that the state participates only once a commercial discovery has been made.

## Clause 3 — Royalty

> 3.1 The Contractor shall pay royalty on the gross production of Crude Oil and Natural Gas at the rates specified in [Annex / the Petroleum Law], before the allocation of Cost Petroleum and Profit Petroleum.

*Annotation.* Not all PSCs impose a royalty; some rely wholly on the profit split. Royalty assures the state an early revenue stream regardless of profitability ("front-loading"), but, being insensitive to costs, can deter development of marginal fields. Sliding-scale royalties by water depth, production rate, or price (as in Nigeria's PIA regime) seek to mitigate this.

## Clause 4 — Measurement and Valuation

> 4.1 Petroleum shall be measured at the Delivery Point in accordance with GIPIP using metering systems approved by the Regulator. 4.2 Crude Oil shall be valued at the arm's length market price realised in sales to third parties or, where sales are not at arm's length, by reference to [a basket of comparable crudes / a formula published by the Regulator].

*Annotation.* Valuation provisions are vital to prevent transfer mispricing, for instance through sales to affiliates at below-market prices. Regulators increasingly publish official selling prices or reference prices.

## Clause 5 — Cost Recovery Limit

> 5.1 In each calendar quarter, after deduction of royalty, up to [60] per cent of the remaining Crude Oil produced ("**Cost Oil**") shall be allocated to the Contractor for the recovery of Recoverable Costs. Any Recoverable Costs not recovered in a quarter shall be carried forward to subsequent quarters [without uplift / with an uplift of x per cent].

*Annotation.* The cost recovery limit ("cost stop") guarantees the state a share of production (profit oil) from first oil, even if the contractor's costs are high. A low limit delays cost recovery and reduces the contractor's internal rate of return; a high limit (or none) accelerates recovery but defers state revenue. Disputes frequently concern whether particular costs are recoverable.

## Clause 6 — Recoverable Costs and the Accounting Procedure

> 6.1 Recoverable Costs shall comprise exploration, appraisal, development, and operating costs incurred in Petroleum Operations, determined in accordance with the Accounting Procedure in Annex [x]. 6.2 The following shall not be Recoverable Costs: signature and production bonuses; costs of arbitration between the parties; fines and penalties; financing costs [unless approved]; costs of marketing beyond the Delivery Point; donations [other than approved community projects]; costs incurred as a result of the Contractor's gross negligence or wilful misconduct; and costs not incurred in accordance with an approved Work Programme and Budget.

*Annotation.* The Accounting Procedure is often the most contested part of the PSC in practice. Key issues include allocation of overheads from the parent company (frequently capped as a percentage of expenditure), the treatment of related-party services, depreciation schedules for capital costs, the ring-fencing of costs by contract area, and the state's audit rights (Clause 13). Weak audit capacity can lead to significant revenue loss through cost inflation ("gold-plating").

## Clause 7 — Profit Petroleum Split

> 7.1 Crude Oil remaining after royalty and Cost Oil ("**Profit Oil**") shall be shared between the State and the Contractor according to the [R-factor / daily production / cumulative production] tranches in the following table:

| R-factor (cumulative revenue ÷ cumulative costs) | State share | Contractor share |
|:----------------------------------------------------------|:-------------|:-------------------|
| Less than 1.0 | 40% | 60% |
| 1.0 to less than 1.5 | 50% | 50% |
| 1.5 to less than 2.0 | 60% | 40% |
| 2.0 and above | 70% | 30% |

Table: Illustrative R-factor profit oil split (hypothetical figures).

*Annotation.* Progressive splits allow the state's share to rise with profitability, providing some protection against the need to renegotiate when prices rise sharply. The R-factor links the split to the contractor's recovery of costs; production-based tranches are simpler to administer but less responsive to costs and prices. Some PSCs use an internal rate of return trigger.

## Clause 8 — Income Tax

> 8.1 The Contractor shall be liable to income tax on its income from Petroleum Operations [at the rate of x per cent] / [The State shall pay the Contractor's income tax on its behalf out of the State's share of Profit Oil ("tax paid" PSC)].

*Annotation.* Under the "tax paid" variant, the contractor's tax is discharged by the state from its share of profit oil, and the contractor receives a tax receipt — relevant to whether it can claim foreign tax credits in its home jurisdiction.

## Clause 9 — Natural Gas

> 9.1 Associated Natural Gas shall be used, in priority, for Petroleum Operations, reinjection, or domestic supply. Flaring shall not be permitted except as authorised. 9.2 Where a Non-Associated Gas Discovery is made, the parties shall negotiate in good faith specific terms for gas development, including cost recovery, profit gas split, and marketing arrangements.

*Annotation.* Many older PSCs gave little attention to gas, contributing to flaring. Modern PSCs and legislation (including Nigeria's PIA, with its emphasis on gas utilisation and flare penalties) address gas explicitly.

## Clause 10 — State Participation

> 10.1 The NOC shall have the option to participate in any Development Area with an interest of up to [x] per cent, its share of past costs being recovered by the Contractor from the NOC's share of Cost Oil.

## Clause 11 — Ownership of Assets

> 11.1 Ownership of all fixed and movable assets acquired for Petroleum Operations shall pass to the State [upon acquisition / upon full cost recovery / upon termination], the Contractor having the exclusive right to use such assets for Petroleum Operations.

*Annotation.* Transfer of asset ownership to the state has implications for decommissioning liability. Contracts should clarify who bears decommissioning obligations for assets owned by the state but used by the contractor.

## Clause 12 — Decommissioning Fund

> 12.1 From the date on which [50] per cent of the reserves under an approved Development Plan have been produced, the Contractor shall make contributions to an escrow account calculated on a unit-of-production basis to fund the estimated cost of Decommissioning, such contributions being Recoverable Costs.

## Clause 13 — Accounts and Audit

> 13.1 The Contractor shall maintain accounts in accordance with the Accounting Procedure. 13.2 The State may audit the Contractor's accounts within [x] years after the end of each Calendar Year, with access to all relevant records, including those of Affiliates providing services. Exceptions not contested within [x] days shall be deemed accepted.

## Clause 14 — Lifting and Marketing

> 14.1 The parties shall agree a Lifting Procedure governing nominations, scheduling, and the correction of overlifts and underlifts. Each party shall be entitled to lift and market separately its share of Petroleum.

## Worked Example (HYPOTHETICAL figures)

**This example uses invented figures for teaching purposes only.** In a quarter, a field produces 1,000,000 barrels, sold at USD 70 per barrel (gross revenue USD 70 million). Royalty is 10 per cent; the cost recovery limit is 60 per cent of post-royalty production; recoverable costs carried into the quarter total USD 50 million; the R-factor is 1.2; income tax is 30 per cent on the contractor's profit oil; and the state does not participate.

| Step | Calculation | Barrels | USD million |
|:--------------------------------------|:--------------------|-------------:|--------------------:|
| Gross production | — | 1,000,000 | 70.0 |
| Royalty (10%) | 1,000,000 × 10% | 100,000 | 7.0 |
| Net production | — | 900,000 | 63.0 |
| Cost oil available (60%) | 900,000 × 60% | 540,000 | 37.8 |
| Cost oil taken (costs USD 50m exceed limit) | — | 540,000 | 37.8 |
| Costs carried forward | 50.0 − 37.8 | — | 12.2 |
| Profit oil | 900,000 − 540,000 | 360,000 | 25.2 |
| State profit oil (50% at R 1.2) | — | 180,000 | 12.6 |
| Contractor profit oil (50%) | — | 180,000 | 12.6 |
| Income tax (30% of contractor profit oil) | — | — | 3.78 |

*Questions.* (1) How would the state's take change if the cost recovery limit were 50 per cent? (2) What incentives does the cost recovery mechanism create for the contractor in relation to cost control? (3) How would you design audit provisions to protect against inflated costs?

# Model International Gas Sales Agreement {.appendix letter="D"}

This appendix outlines the principal provisions of a long-term international gas sales agreement (GSA) for pipeline gas or LNG, with drafting notes. It is an **illustrative teaching model** and should be read with Chapter 6 (gas law and markets) and Chapter 15 (contracts and risk allocation). Published models, such as those of the AIPN, are widely used as starting points in practice.

## Part 1 — Parties, Definitions, and Conditions Precedent

The GSA is concluded between a **Seller** (often a project company or upstream consortium) and a **Buyer** (often a utility, aggregator, or trading company). Definitions of key quantities and units are critical, including: Contract Year; Day (often defined as a gas day beginning at a specified hour); Annual Contract Quantity ("**ACQ**"); Daily Contract Quantity ("**DCQ**"); Maximum Daily Quantity; Delivery Point; British thermal unit or gigajoule; and Specifications.

Conditions precedent typically include: final investment decision on the supply project; regulatory approvals and export or import licences; completion of transportation or regasification arrangements; and financing. A long-stop date allows termination if they are not satisfied.

## Part 2 — Term and Build-Up

The term may run for fifteen to twenty-five years from the start of deliveries, with a build-up period during which quantities ramp up, and provisions for the start date ("window" narrowing). Delays in start-up must be allocated: the Seller may pay liquidated damages for late delivery and the Buyer for late readiness to receive.

## Part 3 — Quantities and Nominations

The Buyer commits to take, and the Seller to deliver, the ACQ, with flexibility through: (a) downward quantity tolerance (for example, up to 10 per cent of ACQ); (b) daily swing above DCQ up to the Maximum Daily Quantity; and (c) nomination procedures (annual programmes, monthly and daily nominations). For LNG, an annual delivery programme schedules cargoes, and provisions address cargo size, vessel compatibility, and laytime.

## Part 4 — Take-or-Pay and Make-Up

> If, in any Contract Year, the quantity taken by the Buyer is less than the Minimum Annual Quantity (ACQ less permitted reductions, Seller shortfalls, and force majeure quantities), the Buyer shall pay for the shortfall at [the Contract Price / a percentage of the Contract Price] ("**Take-or-Pay Quantity**"). The Buyer may take the Take-or-Pay Quantity in subsequent Contract Years ("**Make-Up Gas**") after taking the Minimum Annual Quantity for that year, paying only the difference (if any) between the then-current price and the price paid.

*Drafting note.* Take-or-pay underpins project finance by assuring revenue. Its enforceability as a primary obligation rather than a penalty has generally been upheld in English law (see *M & J Polymers Ltd v Imerys Minerals Ltd* [2008] EWHC 344 (Comm)), and the reformulated penalty doctrine in *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi* [2015] UKSC 67 reinforces the position for commercially justified primary obligations. Carry-forward provisions may allow the Buyer to credit quantities taken above the ACQ against future obligations.

## Part 5 — Delivery, Title, and Risk

The Delivery Point determines where title and risk pass. For pipeline gas, this is often the outlet flange of a processing plant or a border interconnection point. For LNG, sales may be FOB (title and risk pass at the loading port, Buyer arranges shipping), DES/DAP (passing at the discharge port, Seller arranges shipping), or ex-ship variants. Destination restrictions in LNG contracts have been challenged on competition grounds, notably in the European Union and Japan, and newer contracts tend to allow greater destination flexibility.

## Part 6 — Quality and Measurement

Gas must meet specifications (gross calorific value, Wobbe index, hydrogen sulphide, water dew point, hydrocarbon dew point, carbon dioxide, oxygen, and, increasingly, hydrogen blending limits). Off-specification gas may be rejected or accepted at a discount. Measurement provisions specify metering, calibration, testing, and resolution of disputes by independent expert.

## Part 7 — Price

> The Contract Price for each Month shall be calculated as: P = P₀ + a × (Brent₃ − B₀) [oil indexation], or P = HH × [1.15] + [constant] [hub indexation], or a hybrid formula, as specified in Annex [x].

*Drafting note.* Long-term gas prices have traditionally been indexed to oil products (common in Asia and historically in Europe), increasingly replaced in Europe by hub prices (such as the Dutch TTF) and in US LNG exports by Henry Hub. Floors, ceilings, and S-curves dampen volatility.

## Part 8 — Price Review

> Either party may, [once every three years] and additionally [once] at any time during the term, request a review of the Contract Price if: (a) there has been a significant change in economic circumstances in the Buyer's market, beyond the control of the parties, compared with those prevailing at the time of the last price determination; and (b) as a result, the Contract Price no longer reflects the value of gas in the Buyer's market [for a buyer acting prudently and efficiently]. Failing agreement within [x] months, either party may refer the matter to arbitration, and the tribunal shall determine the revised Contract Price in accordance with this Clause.

*Drafting note.* Price review clauses have generated many high-value arbitrations. Key issues are the threshold for review, the definition of the relevant market and comparators, whether the tribunal may revise the structure of the price formula or only its parameters, and the date from which a revised price applies. See Chapter 6, section 6.6, and Chapter 26.

## Part 9 — Seller Shortfall and Remedies

If the Seller fails to deliver nominated quantities (other than for force majeure or Buyer's fault), the Buyer may receive a discount on shortfall quantities or recover cover costs, subject to caps. The Seller's liability is often limited to these remedies.

## Part 10 — Force Majeure and Hardship

Force majeure provisions excuse performance of non-payment obligations for events beyond reasonable control, with specific lists (including, for example, pipeline failure, sanctions, and governmental action) and exclusions (such as lack of funds and changes in market conditions). Hardship provisions may require renegotiation. The treatment of sanctions has become a central issue since 2022.

## Part 11 — Invoicing, Payment, and Security

Monthly invoicing, payment within a specified period, interest on late payment, rights to suspend delivery for non-payment, and credit support (parent guarantees or letters of credit) are standard.

## Part 12 — Liability, Assignment, Confidentiality

Mutual exclusion of consequential losses; caps on liability; restrictions on assignment; confidentiality subject to legal and regulatory disclosure.

## Part 13 — Governing Law and Dispute Resolution

English or New York law is frequently chosen for international GSAs. Disputes are referred to arbitration (commonly ICC, LCIA, or SIAC rules, or UNCITRAL Rules), with expert determination for technical matters such as measurement and quality. See Appendix I.

## Part 14 — Decarbonisation Provisions (Emerging Practice)

Newer GSAs may include provisions on methane emissions reporting and intensity, verification under recognised frameworks (such as the Oil and Gas Methane Partnership 2.0), the treatment of carbon costs, the sharing of costs of compliance with the EU Methane Regulation, and the delivery of "carbon-neutral" or "certified" cargoes, together with rights to adjust or terminate if regulatory requirements cannot be met.
