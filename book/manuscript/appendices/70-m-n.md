# Climate and Energy Transition Legal Compliance Framework {.appendix letter="M"}

This framework assists organisations — energy companies, financial institutions, public authorities, and their advisers — to map and manage legal obligations and risks arising from climate change and the energy transition. It draws on Chapters 18, 27, and 30. It is general in nature; applicable obligations depend on jurisdiction, sector, size, and listing status, and many regimes have been revised recently, so that current texts must always be checked.

## Module 2 — Governance

- Board-level responsibility for climate strategy and risk; competence and training of directors.
- Integration of climate considerations into directors' duties, having regard to legal opinions and guidance in relevant jurisdictions and to litigation such as *ClientEarth v Shell plc* [2023] EWHC 1897 (Ch), in which permission to bring a derivative claim against directors was refused.
- Executive remuneration linked to climate targets, where appropriate.
- Clear accountability for compliance with each obligation identified in Module 1.

## Module 3 — Risk Assessment

- Physical risk assessment (acute and chronic) for assets and supply chains.
- Transition risk assessment: policy, legal, technology, market, and reputational.
- Scenario analysis, including scenarios consistent with 1.5°C, using recognised scenarios (such as the IEA's and the NGFS's).
- Assessment of stranded asset risk and impairment, and of decommissioning and closure liabilities.
- Litigation risk assessment: exposure to claims for misleading disclosure, greenwashing, failure to assess or disclose, breach of duties, and contribution to climate harm.

## Module 4 — Targets and Transition Plan

- Emissions inventory (scopes 1, 2, and, where material, 3) using the GHG Protocol.
- Science-based targets (interim and long-term), with clarity on reliance on offsets and carbon removals.
- A credible transition plan setting out actions, capital expenditure, milestones, and governance, consistent with applicable frameworks (such as the ESRS E1 or national guidance).
- Just transition considerations for workers and communities.

## Module 5 — Disclosure and Claims

- Disclosures consistent with the applicable standard and with financial statements.
- Substantiation of all environmental claims (for example, "net zero", "carbon neutral", "green") with robust, verifiable evidence; avoidance of misleading omissions.
- Assurance of sustainability information where required.
- Review by legal counsel of all public climate statements.

## Module 6 — Contracts and Transactions

- Climate and transition clauses in supply, offtake, and financing agreements (emissions data, carbon cost allocation, change in law, methane verification, termination rights).
- Due diligence in mergers and acquisitions on climate liabilities and transition risk.
- Treatment of carbon credits (quality, integrity, corresponding adjustments).

## Module 7 — Monitoring, Review, and Reporting

- Annual review of the obligations map and risk assessment.
- Internal audit of climate data and controls.
- Reporting to the board and to regulators.

# Doctoral Research Proposal Template {.appendix letter="N"}

This template supports the preparation of a doctoral research proposal in energy and natural resources law, complementing Chapter 28. Requirements vary between institutions — in length (often 3,000 to 8,000 words), structure, and emphasis — and candidates must follow the rules of their own institution and the advice of their prospective supervisors.

## 1. Title

A concise, informative working title that identifies the subject, scope, and (where appropriate) the approach — for example, "Managing Decline: A Comparative Legal Analysis of Petroleum Licensing Restrictions in Producer States, with Particular Reference to Nigeria". Avoid titles that are too broad ("Energy Law in Africa") or rhetorical without content.

## 2. Abstract (200–300 words)

State the problem, the research question(s), the method, the expected contribution, and the significance of the research.

## 3. Introduction and Background

Introduce the subject, explaining the legal, policy, and practical context and why the topic matters now. Identify the "puzzle" or "problem" that the research addresses — a gap, a contradiction, an unresolved question, or a practical difficulty that existing law or scholarship does not adequately address.

## 4. Research Questions

State one main research question and two to five subsidiary questions that, together, will answer it. Questions should be clear, focused, answerable within the time and resources available, and open (not answerable simply "yes" or "no"). For example:

- *Main question:* To what extent do the legal frameworks of selected producer states enable the managed decline of petroleum production consistently with their international climate obligations and the right to development?
- *Subsidiary questions:* (1) What do international climate law and the ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion require of producer states with respect to new licensing? (2) What legal instruments have producer states used to restrict or phase out licensing? (3) How do investment protections constrain such measures? (4) What lessons arise for Nigeria?

## 5. Literature Review

Critically review the principal scholarship, demonstrating command of the field and identifying the gap the research will fill. Organise thematically, not as a list of summaries. Show how the proposed research engages with and advances existing debates.

## 6. Theoretical or Conceptual Framework

Identify the theoretical perspective(s) that will inform the research — for example, legal positivism and doctrinal analysis, theories of resource sovereignty, law and development, energy justice, transnational legal ordering, Third World Approaches to International Law, or regulatory theory — and explain why they are appropriate.

## 7. Methodology

Explain and justify the methods: doctrinal analysis of primary sources; comparative method (with criteria for selection of jurisdictions and attention to functional equivalence); empirical methods (interviews, surveys, case file analysis), with consideration of access, sampling, and analysis; historical method; law and economics; or mixed methods. Identify the sources to be used and how they will be accessed. Address limitations.

## 8. Ethical Considerations

Where research involves human participants (interviews, surveys, fieldwork in affected communities), explain how informed consent, confidentiality, data protection (for example, under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 or the GDPR), risk to participants and researcher, and institutional ethics approval will be addressed.

## 9. Originality and Contribution

State clearly the original contribution to knowledge — new knowledge, new interpretation, new synthesis, new method, or application to a new context — and its significance for scholarship, policy, and practice.

## 10. Structure of the Thesis (Provisional)

Provide a provisional chapter outline showing how the research questions will be addressed.

## 12. Resources and Feasibility

Identify resources needed (library and database access, travel, fieldwork costs, language skills) and how they will be secured.

## 13. Preliminary Bibliography

List key primary and secondary sources in a consistent citation style (for example, OSCOLA).

## Self-Check Before Submission

1. Is the research question clear, focused, and significant?
2. Is the gap in existing knowledge convincingly identified?
3. Are the methods appropriate and justified?
4. Is the project feasible in the time available?
5. Is the expected original contribution clearly stated?
6. Are ethical issues identified and addressed?
7. Is the proposal well written, accurately referenced, and within the word limit?
8. Have all sources been verified, and has any use of generative AI tools complied with institutional policy (see Chapter 28)?
