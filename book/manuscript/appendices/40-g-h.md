# Environmental and Social Impact Assessment Framework {.appendix letter="G"}

This appendix provides a framework for the conduct and review of an environmental and social impact assessment (ESIA) for an energy or natural resources project. It draws on the general principles of international environmental law examined in Chapter 17 — including the customary obligation to conduct an environmental impact assessment where there is a risk of significant transboundary harm, recognised by the International Court of Justice in *Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay* (*Argentina v Uruguay*) (2010) and *Certain Activities Carried Out by Nicaragua in the Border Area* / *Construction of a Road in Costa Rica along the San Juan River* (2015) — on national EIA legislation (in Nigeria, the Environmental Impact Assessment Act, Cap E12 Laws of the Federation of Nigeria 2004), and on international lender standards, in particular the IFC Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability (2012).

## Stage 1 — Screening

Determine whether an ESIA is required and at what level, by reference to: (a) statutory lists of mandatory projects (for example, oil and gas fields, pipelines, refineries, power stations, large dams, and mines above specified thresholds); (b) the sensitivity of the location (protected areas, wetlands, indigenous territories, cultural heritage, densely populated areas); (c) the potential for transboundary impacts, triggering obligations under customary law and, where applicable, the Espoo Convention; and (d) lender categorisation (for example, Category A for projects with potential significant adverse impacts that are diverse, irreversible, or unprecedented).

## Stage 2 — Scoping

Identify the key issues, alternatives, study area, baseline data needs, methods, and stakeholders. Scoping should be consultative and documented in terms of reference approved by the competent authority. Consider: project alternatives (including location, technology, and the "no project" option); associated facilities; cumulative impacts with other existing and planned projects; and climate change (both the project's emissions and its vulnerability to climate impacts). The UK Supreme Court's decision in *R (Finch) v Surrey County Council* [2024] UKSC 20 — holding that the downstream (scope 3) combustion emissions of extracted oil were effects of the project that had to be assessed — illustrates the expanding scope of climate assessment.

## Stage 3 — Baseline Studies

Collect data on the existing physical, biological, and social environment, including: geology, soils, hydrology, groundwater, air quality, noise, and climate; flora, fauna, habitats, and ecosystem services; land use and tenure; demography, livelihoods, health, education, and gender; vulnerable groups; indigenous peoples; and cultural heritage. Baseline data must be adequate in duration and coverage (for example, seasonal variation) to allow meaningful prediction.

## Stage 4 — Impact Identification, Prediction, and Evaluation

For each project phase (construction, operation, decommissioning), identify impacts, predict their magnitude, extent, duration, reversibility, and likelihood, and evaluate their significance against legal standards and the sensitivity of receptors. Social impacts must be assessed with attention to differential effects on women, children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, and other vulnerable groups, and to human rights (see Appendix L).

## Stage 5 — Mitigation and the Mitigation Hierarchy

Apply the mitigation hierarchy: avoid, minimise, restore or rehabilitate, and, as a last resort, offset or compensate. For biodiversity, aim for no net loss (or net gain) of natural habitat and net gains for critical habitat, consistent with IFC Performance Standard 6.

## Stage 6 — Public Participation and Disclosure

Ensure meaningful consultation throughout, with: disclosure of information in accessible languages and formats; adequate time for comment; public hearings; specific engagement with affected communities, women, and vulnerable groups; free, prior and informed consent where indigenous peoples are affected in circumstances identified in IFC Performance Standard 7 and international human rights law; and responses to comments. The Aarhus and Escazú Conventions set out regional standards of access to information, participation, and justice.

## Stage 7 — The ESIA Report and Environmental and Social Management Plan

The ESIA report should contain: a non-technical summary; project description; legal and institutional framework; baseline; alternatives; impacts; mitigation; residual impacts; and an Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP) setting out mitigation measures, monitoring, responsibilities, timelines, budgets, and reporting.

## Stage 8 — Review and Decision

The competent authority reviews the ESIA, often with an independent panel and public hearing, and decides whether to approve the project, with or without conditions, or to refuse it. The decision should be reasoned and published, and subject to administrative appeal and judicial review.

## Stage 9 — Implementation, Monitoring, and Compliance

Implement the ESMP; monitor environmental and social performance; report to the authority and the public; conduct audits; and take corrective action. Monitoring obligations continue through the life of the project, as the ICJ emphasised in *Pulp Mills*.

## Stage 10 — Decommissioning and Closure

Plan and assess decommissioning or closure impacts and rehabilitation, with financial assurance.

## Review Checklist for an ESIA Report

1. Was the ESIA prepared by competent, independent experts?
2. Were alternatives genuinely considered, including the "no project" option?
3. Are baseline data adequate in scope and duration?
4. Are direct, indirect, cumulative, and transboundary impacts assessed, including greenhouse gas emissions and climate vulnerability?
5. Are social and human rights impacts, including on vulnerable groups, assessed?
6. Is the significance evaluation transparent and justified?
7. Is the mitigation hierarchy properly applied?
8. Was public participation meaningful, and were comments addressed?
9. Is the ESMP specific, budgeted, and enforceable?
10. Are monitoring, reporting, grievance, and closure arrangements adequate?

# Model Energy Project Risk Allocation Matrix {.appendix letter="H"}

A risk allocation matrix identifies the risks of a project, allocates each to the party best able to manage it (or to bear it at least cost), and records the contractual and other instruments through which the allocation is effected. The following matrix is a **model for a hypothetical independent power project or upstream development** financed on a limited-recourse basis. Allocations vary by project, market, and bargaining power; this matrix illustrates a typical starting point and should be read with Chapter 15 and Chapter 16.

| Risk | Description | Primary bearer | Mitigation / instrument |
|:-------------|:-----------------------|:-----------------|:--------------------------------------|
| Resource / reserves | Insufficient resource (reserves, wind, solar irradiance, hydrology) | Sponsor / project company | Independent resource studies; P90 financing case; reserve tail |
| Site and land | Title defects, access, resettlement, archaeological finds | Project company (with host state support) | Due diligence; land acquisition by state; ESIA; insurance |
| Permitting | Delay or refusal of consents | Project company; state for changes after award | Conditions precedent; change in law clause; government support agreement |
| Construction — cost overrun | Costs exceed budget | EPC contractor (fixed price) / sponsors | Lump-sum turnkey EPC; contingency; sponsor completion support |
| Construction — delay | Late completion | EPC contractor | Delay liquidated damages; performance bonds; insurance |
| Technology / performance | Plant underperforms | EPC contractor; equipment suppliers | Performance tests; performance liquidated damages; warranties |
| Operation and maintenance | Poor operation, availability | O&M contractor | O&M agreement with KPIs, bonuses, and penalties |
| Fuel / input supply | Unavailability or price of fuel | Off-taker (pass-through) or fuel supplier | Fuel supply agreement; tariff pass-through |
| Market / demand | Insufficient demand or price | Off-taker under PPA or GSA | Take-or-pay; capacity payments; long-term contract |
| Off-taker credit | Non-payment by off-taker | Lenders/sponsors, mitigated by state | Letters of credit; escrow; sovereign guarantee; partial risk guarantee |
| Grid / transport | Grid unavailability, curtailment, pipeline outages | Off-taker / grid operator | Deemed energy; ship-or-pay transport agreements |
| Currency convertibility and transfer | Inability to convert or transfer funds | State / political risk insurers | Government support; offshore accounts; MIGA or private political risk insurance |
| Exchange rate | Devaluation of local currency | Off-taker (indexation) / project | Foreign currency tariff or indexation; hedging |
| Interest rate | Rising rates | Project company | Swaps and hedging |
| Inflation | Rising costs | Shared | Tariff indexation |
| Change in law | New taxes or regulations | State / off-taker | Change in law clause; stabilisation; economic equilibrium |
| Expropriation | Taking or creeping expropriation | State | Termination compensation; investment treaty; political risk insurance |
| Political violence | War, terrorism, civil disturbance | State (political force majeure) / insurers | Political force majeure clauses; insurance |
| Natural force majeure | Floods, earthquakes, storms | Shared | Force majeure relief; property insurance; business interruption insurance |
| Environmental and social | Pollution, community opposition, human rights impacts | Project company | ESIA; ESMP; community agreements; grievance mechanism; lender standards |
| Climate — physical | Increased extreme weather, water scarcity | Project company / shared | Climate risk assessment; design standards; insurance |
| Climate — transition | Carbon pricing, phase-out, demand decline | Project company / sponsors | Scenario analysis; contractual protections; diversification |
| Corruption and integrity | Bribery, fraud | Project company / all parties | Anti-corruption clauses; due diligence; compliance programmes |
| Sanctions | Imposition of sanctions affecting parties or supply chain | Shared | Sanctions clauses; diversification; termination rights |
| Cyber security | Attack on control systems | Project company | Cyber security standards; insurance |
| Decommissioning | Cost of decommissioning exceeds provision | Project company / owners | Decommissioning fund; security; regulator approval |

Table: Model risk allocation matrix for a limited-recourse energy project (illustrative).

## Using the Matrix

1. **Identify** risks comprehensively, by project phase.
2. **Assess** each risk's likelihood and impact, ideally quantitatively.
3. **Allocate** each risk to the party best able to control it or to bear it at the lowest cost — a principle that lenders and development institutions often state as the "golden rule" of project finance risk allocation.
4. **Document** the allocation in the relevant contracts and ensure consistency across the contract suite (for example, that force majeure definitions in the EPC contract, PPA, and loan agreement align, avoiding "gaps" in which the project company bears risk it cannot pass on).
5. **Monitor** risks through the life of the project and adjust mitigation as necessary.
