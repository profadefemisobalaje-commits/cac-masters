# Doctoral Dissertation Structure and Evaluation Criteria {.appendix letter="O"}

This appendix sets out a typical structure for a doctoral dissertation (thesis) in energy and natural resources law and the criteria commonly applied by examiners. It complements Chapter 28 and Appendix N. Institutional regulations — on length (commonly 80,000 to 100,000 words in law, inclusive or exclusive of footnotes and bibliography), format, and examination procedure — prevail and must be consulted.

## Typical Structure

**Preliminary pages.** Title page; declaration of originality; abstract (300–500 words); acknowledgements; table of contents; tables of cases, legislation, and treaties; list of abbreviations; and, where applicable, lists of figures and tables.

**Chapter 1 — Introduction.** The problem and its significance; the research questions; the argument (thesis) in summary; the scope and limitations; the methodology and theoretical framework (or these may form a separate chapter); the original contribution; and a roadmap of the chapters.

**Chapter 2 — Literature, theory, and method (optional separate chapter).** A critical review of the scholarship, the conceptual framework, and a fuller account of methods, including any empirical work and ethics.

**Substantive chapters (typically three to five).** Each addresses one or more subsidiary research questions, builds the argument, and ends with a conclusion that links back to the main question. Comparative theses may devote chapters to individual jurisdictions or, preferably, to themes analysed comparatively.

**Concluding chapter.** Answers to the research questions; synthesis of the argument; contribution to knowledge; implications for law, policy, and practice (including recommendations for reform, where appropriate); limitations; and avenues for further research.

**Bibliography and appendices.** Full bibliography of primary and secondary sources; appendices with interview schedules, consent forms, datasets, or other supporting material.

## Writing the Argument

A doctoral thesis is an extended argument, not a report. Each chapter should advance the thesis, and the reader should be able to trace the argument from introduction to conclusion. Signposting at the start and end of chapters and sections, consistent terminology, and careful structuring are essential. The writer should engage critically with opposing views, acknowledge limitations, and avoid both overclaiming and excessive description.

## Common Evaluation Criteria

| Criterion | What examiners look for |
|:----------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Originality and contribution | A significant, original contribution to knowledge, worthy of publication in whole or in part |
| Research question and argument | A clear, significant question and a coherent, sustained, persuasive argument |
| Knowledge of the field | Comprehensive, critical command of the relevant law and literature |
| Methodology | Appropriate, justified, rigorously applied methods; awareness of limitations |
| Use of sources | Accurate, authoritative, and current primary and secondary sources; correct interpretation |
| Critical analysis | Depth of analysis; evaluation, not mere description; engagement with counter-arguments |
| Structure and presentation | Logical organisation; clear, precise writing; accurate citation and referencing; compliance with regulations |
| Integrity | Independence of the work; absence of plagiarism; transparent and ethical research practice; compliance with institutional policies on generative AI |
| Defence (viva voce) | Ability to explain, justify, and defend the thesis and to situate it in the field |

Table: Common criteria for the evaluation of a doctoral thesis.

## Typical Examination Outcomes

Outcomes vary by institution but commonly include: (a) award of the degree without amendments; (b) award subject to minor corrections (often within three to six months); (c) award subject to major corrections or revisions (often within twelve months), sometimes with a further oral examination; (d) resubmission for re-examination; (e) award of a lower degree (such as an MPhil); and (f) failure.

## Preparing for the Viva Voce

1. Re-read the thesis carefully, noting errors and points of weakness.
2. Prepare a short (five-minute) summary of the thesis, its contribution, and its significance.
3. Anticipate questions: Why this topic? What is original? Why these methods and jurisdictions? What are the limitations? How does the work relate to [leading scholar]'s argument? What would you do differently? What are the implications for policy? What next?
4. Check developments since submission (new cases, legislation, or scholarship) and consider their implications.
5. Hold a mock viva with supervisors or colleagues.

# Legal Research, Citation, and Case Analysis Guide {.appendix letter="P"}

This guide summarises techniques for legal research, verification, citation, and case analysis in energy and natural resources law. It complements Chapter 28.

## Research Strategy

1. **Define the question** precisely, identifying the jurisdiction(s), the time period, and the legal issues.
2. **Begin with secondary sources** — textbooks, treatises, encyclopaedias, and review articles — to understand the framework and identify key authorities.
3. **Locate primary sources**: constitutions, treaties, legislation, regulations, cases, and awards.
4. **Update and verify** every authority: check amendments, repeals, appeals, annulment and set-aside proceedings, and subsequent treatment.
5. **Record** sources and search methods systematically, using reference management software.

## Verification Protocol

Before relying on any authority:

1. Obtain the authoritative text from an official or reputable source — never rely solely on a secondary description, a summary, or an AI-generated output.
2. Confirm the citation details (name, year, court, report, paragraph).
3. Confirm the proposition attributed to the authority by reading the relevant passage in context.
4. Check subsequent history (appeal, annulment, set aside, reversal, overruling, distinguishing) and amendments to legislation.
5. Record the date of verification.

The judgments in *Mata v Avianca, Inc* (SDNY 2023) and *R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey* [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin) illustrate the serious professional consequences of citing non-existent authorities generated by AI tools.

## Citation: OSCOLA Examples

The Oxford University Standard for the Citation of Legal Authorities (OSCOLA) is widely used in English-language legal scholarship. Illustrative formats:

- **UK case:** *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* [2021] UKSC 3.
- **ICJ judgment:** *Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (Argentina v Uruguay)* (Judgment) [2010] ICJ Rep 14.
- **ICJ advisory opinion:** *Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change* (Advisory Opinion) ICJ, 23 July 2025.
- **Investment award:** *Yukos Universal Ltd (Isle of Man) v Russian Federation*, PCA Case No AA 227, Final Award (18 July 2014).
- **Treaty:** Paris Agreement (adopted 12 December 2015, entered into force 4 November 2016) (with the United Nations Treaty Series reference, verified from the UN Treaty Collection).
- **UK statute:** Climate Change Act 2008, s 1.
- **Nigerian statute:** Petroleum Industry Act 2021, s [x].
- **EU regulation:** Regulation (EU) 2023/956 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 10 May 2023 establishing a carbon border adjustment mechanism [2023] OJ L130/52.
- **Book:** Author, *Title* (edn, Publisher Year) page.
- **Journal article:** Author, 'Title' (Year) Volume Journal Abbreviation First page, pinpoint.

Footnotes should be used for citations; a bibliography lists secondary sources (and, in a thesis, may separately list primary sources). Use pinpoint references wherever possible. Institutions may prescribe other styles (such as the Bluebook or Chicago); consistency is essential.

## Case Analysis Template

1. **Citation and court/tribunal.** Full citation; level of court; composition.
2. **Facts.** Material facts only.
3. **Procedural history.** How the case reached this forum.
4. **Issues.** The legal questions to be decided.
5. **Arguments.** The principal arguments of each party.
6. **Holding.** The decision on each issue.
7. **Reasoning.** The court's reasoning, including the authorities relied on and the interpretive approach.
8. **Separate and dissenting opinions.** Their reasoning and significance.
9. **Ratio and obiter.** Distinguish binding reasoning from observations (noting that arbitral awards and international decisions do not create binding precedent in the common law sense, although they may be highly persuasive).
10. **Subsequent history and treatment.** Appeals, annulment, set aside, citation in later cases.
11. **Evaluation.** Is the reasoning sound? Is the decision consistent with other authorities? What are its implications for law, policy, and practice — particularly in energy and resources?

## Treaty Interpretation Template (VCLT arts 31–33)

1. Ordinary meaning of the terms, in their context, and in the light of the object and purpose of the treaty.
2. Context: the text, preamble, annexes, and related agreements and instruments.
3. Subsequent agreements and practice of the parties.
4. Relevant rules of international law applicable between the parties (systemic integration, art 31(3)(c)).
5. Special meaning, if established.
6. Supplementary means (art 32): *travaux préparatoires* and circumstances of conclusion — to confirm the meaning or where the meaning is ambiguous, obscure, or leads to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result.
7. Treaties authenticated in more than one language (art 33).
