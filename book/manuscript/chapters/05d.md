## 5.8 Decommissioning and Abandonment Liabilities

### The Decommissioning Challenge

At the end of their productive lives, petroleum installations must be decommissioned: wells must be plugged and abandoned, platforms and pipelines removed or made safe, and sites remediated. Decommissioning is technically complex and extremely expensive. Estimates of the total cost of decommissioning offshore infrastructure on the United Kingdom Continental Shelf alone run to tens of billions of pounds over the coming decades, and globally the liability runs to hundreds of billions of dollars. The energy transition is likely to accelerate decommissioning as fields become uneconomic earlier than anticipated.

Decommissioning raises four principal legal questions: what standard of decommissioning is required; who is liable to carry it out and pay for it; how the state can ensure that funds will be available when decommissioning becomes due; and how liability is affected by transfers of interests during the life of a field.

### International Standards

UNCLOS Article 60(3) provides that "any installations or structures which are abandoned or disused shall be removed to ensure safety of navigation, taking into account any generally accepted international standards established in this regard by the competent international organization", and that "such removal shall also have due regard to fishing, the protection of the marine environment and the rights and duties of other States". The competent organisation is the International Maritime Organization, whose *Guidelines and Standards for the Removal of Offshore Installations and Structures on the Continental Shelf and in the Exclusive Economic Zone* (1989) require the removal of installations standing in less than 75 metres of water and weighing less than 4,000 tonnes in air (excluding the deck and superstructure), and of all installations placed after 1 January 1998 in waters of less than 100 metres depth, while permitting partial removal of larger installations subject to specified conditions.

In the North-East Atlantic, OSPAR Decision 98/3 on the Disposal of Disused Offshore Installations, adopted in 1998 following the controversy over Shell's proposal to dispose of the Brent Spar storage buoy in deep water, prohibits the dumping and leaving wholly or partly in place of disused offshore installations, subject to derogations for the footings of large steel jackets weighing more than 10,000 tonnes and for concrete gravity-based installations, where the competent authority is satisfied that there are significant reasons why an alternative disposal option is preferable to reuse, recycling, or final disposal on land. The Brent Spar episode illustrates the role of public opinion and civil society in shaping decommissioning law.

The London Convention 1972 and its 1996 Protocol on the prevention of marine pollution by dumping also regulate the disposal of platforms at sea.

### Domestic Regimes

In the United Kingdom, Part IV of the Petroleum Act 1998 establishes a detailed regime under which the Secretary of State may require persons responsible for offshore installations and pipelines to submit decommissioning programmes for approval. Section 29 notices may be served on a wide class of persons, including current and former licensees, owners of installations, and companies associated with them. The Energy Act 2008 extended the power to require security for decommissioning liabilities. Crucially, liability under Part IV can extend to former licensees: a company that has sold its interest may be called upon to carry out decommissioning if the purchaser defaults. This "residual liability" regime gives the state a powerful protection against insolvency of current operators but complicates transactions, since sellers must manage the risk of being called back.

In Nigeria, the PIA requires the holder of a petroleum mining lease to submit a decommissioning and abandonment plan and to establish a decommissioning and abandonment fund, to be maintained with a financial institution and managed in accordance with regulations issued by the NUPRC. Contributions to the fund are to be made over the life of the field, ensuring that adequate resources will be available. The PIA also provides that the obligations relating to decommissioning and abandonment are not extinguished by the transfer of an interest without the express agreement of the regulator, and the NUPRC's regulations on decommissioning and abandonment set out detailed requirements. The adequacy of decommissioning provisioning has been a central issue in the review of onshore divestments by international oil companies, given the age of much of the onshore infrastructure in the Niger Delta and the legacy of environmental contamination.

### Liability Allocation in Contracts

Contracts allocate decommissioning liabilities among co-venturers and between buyers and sellers. JOAs typically provide that decommissioning costs are shared in proportion to participating interests at the time decommissioning is carried out, but require parties to provide security in advance — often through decommissioning security agreements (DSAs) — to protect against the default of any party. In asset sales, the seller typically seeks an indemnity from the buyer for decommissioning liabilities, supported by security such as letters of credit or parent company guarantees, because the seller may remain exposed to residual statutory liability. Disputes over the adequacy of security and the calculation of decommissioning cost estimates are frequent.

### Decommissioning and the Energy Transition

The energy transition creates new opportunities and challenges for decommissioning. Infrastructure that would otherwise be removed may be repurposed for carbon dioxide transport and storage, hydrogen production or transport, or offshore wind support. Such repurposing requires legal frameworks that address the transfer of liability for legacy infrastructure, the long-term liability for stored carbon dioxide, and the interaction between petroleum, carbon storage, and renewable energy licensing regimes. The United Kingdom's Energy Act 2023 and the European Union's CCS Directive 2009/31/EC address some of these questions, examined in Chapter 12. At the same time, the risk that fields will be abandoned prematurely, by operators lacking the resources to decommission them properly, raises concerns about stranded liabilities that may ultimately fall on the state and taxpayers.

### Onshore Abandonment and Legacy Pollution

Onshore, decommissioning intersects with the remediation of legacy pollution. In the Niger Delta, decades of oil spills, gas flaring, and inadequate decommissioning of wells and pipelines have left extensive environmental damage. The United Nations Environment Programme's *Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland* (2011) found widespread contamination of land, groundwater, and surface water and recommended a clean-up programme that it estimated would take up to thirty years. The Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project (HYPREP) was established by the Federal Government in 2016 to implement the recommendations. The PIA's environmental remediation fund provisions, discussed in Chapter 17, are designed to ensure that operators provide for the remediation of environmental damage arising from their operations. The legal liability of parent companies for pollution caused by their subsidiaries in Nigeria has been addressed by English and Dutch courts, notably in *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* (2021) and *Four Nigerian Farmers v Shell* (2021), considered in Chapter 21.

::: casebox
**Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire Maritime Delimitation (ITLOS Special Chamber, 2017) — petroleum activities in disputed areas**

*Facts.* Ghana had for decades licensed petroleum activities up to a line it regarded as the customary equidistance boundary with Côte d'Ivoire, and significant discoveries had been made, including the TEN fields. Côte d'Ivoire contested the boundary and claimed that Ghana's unilateral petroleum activities in the disputed area violated Côte d'Ivoire's sovereign rights and Article 83(3) of UNCLOS, which requires states, pending agreement on delimitation, to "make every effort to enter into provisional arrangements of a practical nature" and "not to jeopardize or hamper the reaching of the final agreement". In 2015 the Special Chamber had prescribed provisional measures requiring Ghana not to undertake new drilling in the disputed area.

*Holding.* The Special Chamber delimited the boundary using the equidistance/relevant circumstances methodology, producing a line close to that claimed by Ghana. It rejected Ghana's argument that a tacit agreement on the boundary had arisen from the parties' oil practice, but also held that Ghana had not violated Côte d'Ivoire's sovereign rights, because the activities had been carried out in an area that was ultimately attributed to Ghana, and that Ghana had not violated Article 83(3).

*Significance.* The decision is important for upstream petroleum practice in disputed maritime areas. It confirms that oil practice alone rarely establishes a tacit maritime boundary, and it illustrates the risks — including provisional measures halting new drilling — that investors face when operating in contested waters.

::: summary
- Petroleum is defined by statute and contract; the distinctions between crude oil and gas, associated and non-associated gas, and conventional and unconventional resources have significant legal and fiscal consequences, as illustrated by Nigeria's gas flaring provisions.
- Petroleum titles may be characterised as administrative licences, contracts, property rights, or hybrids; the characterisation affects the state's power to vary terms and the investor's protection.
- Competitive and transparent allocation of rights is increasingly required by law, as under the PIA, in reaction to the abuses associated with discretionary award.
- Petroleum arrangements fall into concessionary/licensing systems, production sharing contracts, and service contracts, distinguished by ownership of production and the form of the state's return; the economic substance matters more than the label.
- Joint operating agreements govern relations among co-venturers, including the operator's role and liability, operating committee decisions, sole risk, default, and transfers; NNPC cash call problems illustrate the difficulties of state participation.
- National oil companies' dual commercial and regulatory roles create conflicts of interest that reforms such as the PIA seek to resolve.
- Shared reservoirs require unitisation; transboundary fields are governed by mineral deposits clauses, framework agreements, and joint development zones.
- Decommissioning is governed by UNCLOS, IMO guidelines, regional instruments such as OSPAR Decision 98/3, and domestic regimes that increasingly require advance funding and impose residual liability.
:::
