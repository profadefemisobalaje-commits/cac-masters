## 24.3 United States Energy Regulation

### Federalism and the Division of Authority

United States energy law is shaped by the **federal structure** of the Constitution, which divides authority between the federal government and the states. The federal government's authority over energy derives principally from the **Commerce Clause** (Article I, section 8), which empowers Congress to regulate commerce among the states, and from the Property Clause, which grants Congress power over federal lands. States retain authority over matters within their borders, including the siting of power plants and transmission lines, retail electricity sales, the regulation of local distribution utilities, and the regulation of oil and gas production on state and private lands. The boundary between federal and state jurisdiction has been the subject of extensive litigation, and the resulting division is complex.

### Ownership and the Oil and Gas Industry

As noted in section 24.1, the United States follows the **accession** principle: the owner of the surface owns the minerals beneath, unless they have been severed. Most onshore oil and gas production on private land is governed by private **oil and gas leases** between mineral owners and producers, providing for bonuses, royalties, and the obligations of the lessee, and by state law, including the **rule of capture**, under which a landowner owns the oil and gas produced from wells on its land even if drained from beneath neighbouring land, tempered by state **conservation laws** regulating well spacing, preventing waste, and providing for pooling and unitisation. This framework, combined with private ownership, well-developed capital markets, a large independent sector, and technological innovation in horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, enabled the **shale revolution** from the late 2000s, which made the United States the world's largest producer of oil and gas and, from 2023, the largest exporter of LNG. Hydraulic fracturing is regulated principally by states, as the Energy Policy Act of 2005 exempted it from the underground injection control provisions of the Safe Drinking Water Act (except where diesel fuels are used), as noted in Chapter 19.

Oil and gas on **federal lands** and the **outer continental shelf** are owned by the federal government and leased under the **Mineral Leasing Act** of 1920 and the **Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act** of 1953, respectively, administered by the Bureau of Land Management and the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management. Federal leasing policy has varied with administrations: a pause on new leasing was announced in 2021, constrained by litigation and by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which conditioned renewable energy leasing on continued oil and gas lease sales, and in 2025 the federal government adopted an "energy dominance" policy expanding leasing and reducing royalty rates under legislation enacted in July 2025.

### The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission

The **Federal Energy Regulatory Commission** (FERC), an independent agency established in 1977 as the successor to the Federal Power Commission, regulates the interstate transmission and wholesale sale of electricity under the **Federal Power Act** of 1935, the interstate transportation and sale for resale of natural gas under the **Natural Gas Act** of 1938, the siting of interstate natural gas pipelines and LNG terminals, the licensing of non-federal hydropower projects, and the interstate transportation of oil by pipeline. Under both Acts, FERC must ensure that rates are **"just and reasonable"** and not unduly discriminatory, a standard rooted in the public utility tradition of cost-of-service regulation.

FERC has been the principal architect of the restructuring of the US gas and electricity industries. In the gas sector, **Order No. 636** (1992) required interstate pipelines to unbundle their transportation services from gas sales, enabling a competitive market in gas supply. In the electricity sector, building on the **Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act** of 1978 (PURPA), which required utilities to purchase power from qualifying cogeneration and small renewable facilities, and the **Energy Policy Act** of 1992, which expanded FERC's authority to order transmission access, **Order No. 888** (1996) required transmission-owning utilities to provide **open access** transmission service on non-discriminatory terms under a pro forma tariff, and **Order No. 2000** (1999) encouraged the formation of **Regional Transmission Organisations** (RTOs). RTOs and Independent System Operators, such as PJM, MISO, CAISO, NYISO, ISO New England, and SPP, operate transmission systems and wholesale markets covering much of the country, while the Southeast and much of the West retain vertically integrated utilities. Texas's grid, operated by **ERCOT**, is largely isolated from interstate commerce and therefore outside FERC's jurisdiction — a factor in the regulatory response to **Winter Storm Uri** in February 2021, in which prolonged outages led to hundreds of deaths and billions of dollars in losses, prompting reforms of Texas law on weatherisation and market design.

More recent FERC orders address the integration of new resources: **Order No. 1000** (2011) on transmission planning and cost allocation; **Order No. 841** (2018) on the participation of electric storage in wholesale markets; **Order No. 2222** (2020) on the participation of aggregations of distributed energy resources; **Order No. 2023** (2023) on the reform of generator interconnection procedures, to address queues of thousands of projects awaiting connection; and **Order No. 1920** (2024) on long-term regional transmission planning. The Supreme Court has addressed the boundary between federal and state jurisdiction in ***FERC v Electric Power Supply Association*** (2016), upholding FERC's authority to regulate demand response in wholesale markets, and ***Hughes v Talen Energy Marketing*** (2016), striking down a Maryland programme that set the rate received by a new generator in the federal wholesale market, while leaving room for state measures not tethered to wholesale market participation.

### State Regulation and Policy

States regulate retail electricity rates and the planning of vertically integrated utilities through **public utility commissions**, and many have adopted their own energy and climate policies. **Renewable portfolio standards**, requiring utilities to obtain a specified share of electricity from renewable sources, have been adopted by around thirty states, and many states have adopted targets for 100 per cent clean electricity. **California** has been a leader in climate policy, with a cap-and-trade programme linked to Québec's and, as examined in Chapter 22, climate disclosure laws. The **Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative** is a cap-and-trade programme for the power sector among north-eastern states. Around half of the states have restructured their electricity markets to allow retail competition. State and local regulation of siting is a significant constraint on the deployment of renewable energy and transmission.

### Environmental Regulation and Administrative Law

Energy projects in the United States are subject to environmental laws including the **Clean Air Act**, the **Clean Water Act**, and the **National Environmental Policy Act** of 1969 (NEPA), which requires federal agencies to prepare environmental impact statements for major federal actions significantly affecting the environment. NEPA reviews have been a source of delay for both fossil fuel and clean energy projects, and Congress amended NEPA in the **Fiscal Responsibility Act** of 2023 to impose page and time limits. In ***Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v Eagle County*** (2025), concerning a railway to transport crude oil from Utah, the Supreme Court held that courts must afford substantial deference to agencies' determinations of the scope of NEPA review, and that agencies need not consider the environmental effects of separate upstream and downstream projects — a decision that significantly narrowed NEPA's reach.

The Supreme Court has reshaped the administrative law governing energy regulation. In ***West Virginia v Environmental Protection Agency*** (2022), the Court applied the **major questions doctrine** to hold that the Clean Air Act did not authorise the EPA to adopt emission caps based on "generation shifting" from coal to gas and renewables, as contemplated by the Clean Power Plan of 2015, stating that agencies must point to clear congressional authorisation for regulations of vast economic and political significance. In ***Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo*** (2024), the Court overruled the *Chevron* doctrine, under which courts deferred to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment in determining the meaning of statutory provisions. These decisions constrain the ability of agencies such as the EPA and FERC to adopt ambitious climate and energy regulations without express statutory authority, increasing the importance of legislation and the scope for litigation.

### Federal Energy Legislation and the Energy Transition

The **Inflation Reduction Act** of 2022 (IRA) was the most significant climate legislation in US history, providing tax credits for clean electricity, clean hydrogen, carbon capture, electric vehicles, and domestic manufacturing of clean energy components, many with domestic content and labour requirements, as examined in Chapters 10, 12, and 13. In July 2025, Congress enacted legislation that substantially curtailed the IRA's tax credits, phasing out credits for wind and solar projects placed in service after specified dates, ending consumer credits for electric vehicles, and imposing restrictions related to foreign entities of concern, while largely preserving credits for nuclear, geothermal, storage, and carbon capture. The executive branch also withdrew from the Paris Agreement, as examined in Chapter 18, lifted a pause on approvals of LNG exports to non-free trade agreement countries that had been imposed in January 2024, and sought to reverse regulations limiting emissions from power plants and vehicles. The volatility of US federal energy policy across administrations illustrates the importance of legislative entrenchment and the limits of executive action in securing long-term investment.

## 24.4 United Kingdom and European Union Energy Law

### The United Kingdom: From Nationalisation to Liberalisation

The United Kingdom's energy sector was largely nationalised after the Second World War, with state-owned enterprises in coal (the National Coal Board), electricity (the Central Electricity Generating Board and area boards), and gas (British Gas). From the 1980s, the Conservative government pursued **privatisation and liberalisation**, which became a model for reforms worldwide: British Gas was privatised in 1986 under the Gas Act 1986; the electricity industry was restructured and privatised under the **Electricity Act 1989**, which separated generation, transmission, distribution, and supply and established an independent regulator; and British Coal was privatised in 1994. The regulators for gas and electricity were merged in 2000 to form the **Office of Gas and Electricity Markets** (Ofgem), governed by the Gas and Electricity Markets Authority, whose principal objective is to protect the interests of existing and future consumers, including their interests in the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions. Network companies are regulated under the **RIIO** (Revenue = Incentives + Innovation + Outputs) framework of performance-based regulation, a successor to the RPI-X price cap model pioneered in the United Kingdom.

The **Energy Act 2013** introduced **Electricity Market Reform**, including **contracts for difference** (CfDs) for low-carbon generation, under which generators receive the difference between a strike price and a market reference price, and a capacity market, examined in Chapter 10. The CfD model has supported the expansion of offshore wind, making the United Kingdom a world leader in the technology, and has been widely emulated. The **Energy Act 2023** provided for an independent **National Energy System Operator**, established in 2024, licensing of carbon dioxide transport and storage and hydrogen transport, and the regulation of heat networks. The **Great British Energy Act 2025** established a publicly owned company, Great British Energy, to invest in clean energy projects.

### UK Petroleum Law

The United Kingdom vests petroleum in the Crown under the **Petroleum Act 1998** and licenses exploration and production on the UK continental shelf through licensing rounds. The **North Sea Transition Authority** (formerly the Oil and Gas Authority), established as an independent regulator under the Energy Act 2016 following the Wood Review of 2014, regulates licensing and the stewardship of resources. The principal objective of **maximising economic recovery** (MER UK), introduced in 2015, was revised in 2021 to require the industry to support the achievement of net zero, including by reducing emissions from production. The fiscal regime comprises ring-fence corporation tax, a supplementary charge, and, from 2022, the **Energy Profits Levy**, a windfall tax introduced in response to high prices following Russia's invasion of Ukraine and extended to 2030. The government elected in 2024 committed not to issue new licences to explore new fields and to end the Energy Profits Levy's investment allowance, while maintaining production from existing fields, and following the Supreme Court's decision in *Finch* (2024), examined in Chapter 17, issued guidance requiring assessment of the scope 3 emissions of new field developments. Decommissioning on the UK continental shelf is governed by the Petroleum Act 1998, OSPAR Decision 98/3, and tax relief arrangements, as examined in Chapters 5 and 17.

### The European Union: Legal Basis and Liberalisation

The European Union's energy competence was historically founded on the internal market and environmental provisions of the treaties, together with the Euratom and the (expired) European Coal and Steel Community Treaties. The **Treaty of Lisbon** (2009) introduced an express energy competence in **Article 194 TFEU**, under which EU energy policy aims, in a spirit of solidarity, to ensure the functioning of the energy market, security of supply, energy efficiency and the development of new and renewable forms of energy, and the interconnection of energy networks — without affecting a member state's right to determine the conditions for exploiting its energy resources, its choice between different energy sources, and the general structure of its energy supply. The tension between EU-level integration and national sovereignty over the energy mix is a recurring theme of EU energy law.

The **liberalisation** of EU gas and electricity markets proceeded through successive legislative packages: the First Package (1996 for electricity and 1998 for gas), which introduced gradual market opening; the Second Package (2003), which required legal unbundling of network operators and full market opening by 2007; the **Third Package** (2009), which introduced **ownership unbundling** of transmission system operators (with alternatives of an independent system operator or independent transmission operator), strengthened national regulators, and established the **Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators** (ACER) and the European Networks of Transmission System Operators (ENTSO-E and ENTSOG); and the **Clean Energy for All Europeans Package** (2018–2019), which revised the electricity market design to integrate renewables, empower consumers, and establish citizen energy communities, and adopted the **Governance Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2018/1999), requiring member states to adopt integrated national energy and climate plans. The Court of Justice has enforced the internal market and competition rules in the energy sector, including in cases on the "third country clause" and Nord Stream 2, examined in Chapter 20, and on the principle of energy solidarity in ***Germany v Poland*** (Case C-848/19 P, 2021), in which the Court held that energy solidarity under Article 194 is a legally binding principle requiring the EU institutions and member states to take into account the interests of other member states in energy security.

### The European Green Deal and the Energy Crisis

The **European Green Deal** (2019) and the **European Climate Law** (Regulation (EU) 2021/1119), which made climate neutrality by 2050 and a fifty-five per cent net emissions reduction by 2030 legally binding, led to the **"Fit for 55"** package, which revised the EU Emissions Trading System, established a second emissions trading system for buildings and road transport, adopted the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, raised the binding renewable energy target to at least 42.5 per cent by 2030 under the revised Renewable Energy Directive (RED III), and raised energy efficiency targets, as examined in Chapters 10, 13, and 18. The Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022 triggered an energy crisis in Europe, leading to **REPowerEU** (2022), which aimed to end dependence on Russian fossil fuels through diversification, efficiency, and accelerated renewables deployment, and a series of emergency regulations on gas storage, demand reduction, joint purchasing of gas, and a market correction mechanism capping gas prices. The **electricity market design reform** of 2024 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1747 and Directive (EU) 2024/1711) responded to the price crisis by requiring that direct price support for new low-carbon generation take the form of **two-sided contracts for difference**, promoting power purchase agreements, and strengthening consumer protection. The **Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package** (2024) established a framework for hydrogen networks. In 2025, the EU proposed a target of a ninety per cent reduction in net emissions by 2040, with flexibilities including the limited use of international carbon credits, which was the subject of agreement among member states later that year.

The EU's withdrawal from the **Energy Charter Treaty**, approved in 2024 and effective in 2025, and the modernisation of the Treaty, examined in Chapter 14, illustrate the EU's reassessment of investment protection in the energy sector in light of its climate objectives.

### The UK after Brexit

Following its withdrawal from the European Union, the United Kingdom left the EU internal energy market, and electricity and gas trading across interconnectors is governed by the energy provisions of the **EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement** (2020), which provide for cooperation on interconnector trading arrangements, security of supply, and offshore renewable energy in the North Seas. The UK established its own emissions trading scheme in 2021 and agreed with the EU in 2025 to work towards linking the UK and EU emissions trading systems, with a view to mutual exemption from carbon border adjustment mechanisms. The UK's experience after Brexit illustrates the costs of disintegration from a regional energy market and the incentives for continued cooperation, relevant to regional integration efforts in Africa examined in Chapter 9.
