::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 20: Negotiating a Joint Development Agreement in a Disputed Maritime Area (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The states, companies, geological features, and events described are fictitious and do not refer to any real countries, companies, or disputes.**

The Republic of Azuria and the Republic of Corvenne are adjacent coastal states on a gulf. Both are parties to UNCLOS; Corvenne has made a declaration under Article 298 excluding maritime boundary disputes from compulsory procedures entailing binding decisions. Their maritime boundary has never been delimited. Azuria claims a boundary based on an equidistance line; Corvenne claims a boundary based on a perpendicular to the general direction of the coast, which would give it a substantially larger area. The overlapping claims define a triangular disputed area of around 9,000 square kilometres.

Five years ago, Azuria granted an exploration licence covering part of the disputed area to Meridian Offshore Ltd, a company incorporated in a third state. Corvenne protested diplomatically and has itself offered blocks overlapping the same area in a licensing round. Meridian acquired seismic data across the area and recently drilled an exploration well, the Halcyon-1 well, which discovered a significant gas field that appears, on the basis of the seismic data, to extend into areas claimed by both states and possibly beyond the equidistance line. Corvenne's navy has since shadowed Meridian's support vessels and warned that further drilling will be prevented. Both governments, under pressure from their populations, which suffer from frequent power shortages, wish to develop the gas for domestic power generation and export. A regional economic community has offered to facilitate negotiations.

**Tasks.**

(a) As legal adviser to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Corvenne, prepare a memorandum assessing whether Azuria and Meridian have breached international law by acquiring seismic data and drilling Halcyon-1, with reference to Articles 74(3) and 83(3) of UNCLOS and the relevant jurisprudence, and advising on the options available to Corvenne, including the possibility of compulsory conciliation, and the legal constraints on the use of naval force.

(b) As counsel to Meridian, prepare an advice on the company's legal and commercial risks, including the validity of its licence, the risk of a future boundary placing the field in Corvenne's area, investment treaty protection, and the implications for its financing.

(c) In teams representing Azuria and Corvenne, negotiate the heads of terms of a **joint development agreement**, addressing: the definition of the joint development zone; the institutional structure (a joint authority, joint ministerial council, or other arrangement); the revenue-sharing ratio and its justification; the treatment of Meridian's existing licence and Corvenne's offered blocks; the applicable law and fiscal regime; environmental protection and decommissioning; a without-prejudice clause preserving boundary claims; dispute settlement; and the relationship between the agreement and any future boundary.

(d) As an independent expert, write a short reflection comparing the joint development model with the alternatives of adjudication and the delimitation of a boundary followed by a unitisation agreement, with reference to the experience of the Nigeria–São Tomé and Príncipe Joint Development Zone and the Timor Sea.
:::

::: summary
- UNCLOS, adopted in 1982 and in force since 1994, is the comprehensive framework for the law of the sea, supplemented by the 1994 Part XI Agreement, the Fish Stocks Agreement, and the BBNJ Agreement; it established the ISA, the CLCS, and ITLOS and a system of compulsory dispute settlement.
- The coastal state has sovereignty over its territorial sea of up to twelve nautical miles and sovereign rights in its EEZ of up to two hundred nautical miles over natural resources and the production of energy from water, currents, and winds, together with jurisdiction over installations, subject to the freedoms of other states and the obligation of due regard.
- The continental shelf extends to at least two hundred nautical miles and, where the margin extends further, to its outer edge within maximum limits; coastal state rights exist *ipso facto* and *ab initio*; outer limits established on the basis of CLCS recommendations are final and binding; and Article 82 requires payments for exploitation beyond two hundred nautical miles.
- Offshore petroleum is governed by national licensing and safety regimes within the UNCLOS framework of coastal state jurisdiction over installations, safety zones, and decommissioning; maritime security, especially in the Gulf of Guinea, and new uses such as offshore wind, carbon storage, and hydrogen raise further legal issues.
- All states may lay submarine cables and pipelines on the continental shelf, subject to coastal state consent to the course of pipelines and reasonable measures; recent incidents of damage in the Baltic Sea have exposed gaps in the protection of submarine infrastructure.
- The Area and its resources are the common heritage of mankind administered by the ISA; exploitation regulations have not yet been adopted, a growing number of states support a moratorium, and unilateral licensing by the United States has challenged the regime.
- Part XII of UNCLOS imposes a general obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment; the ITLOS advisory opinion of 2024 held that greenhouse gas emissions constitute marine pollution subject to stringent due diligence obligations; and the BBNJ Agreement extends governance to areas beyond national jurisdiction.
- Maritime delimitation follows a three-stage methodology to achieve an equitable solution; resources and oil practice rarely affect the boundary; states must make every effort to agree provisional arrangements and must not jeopardise or hamper agreement; and joint development and unitisation provide cooperative solutions for disputed and straddling resources.
:::

::: research
1. Has the three-stage methodology of maritime delimitation achieved predictability at the cost of equity? Consider the treatment of natural resources and oil practice as relevant circumstances.
2. Evaluate the adequacy of the international legal framework for the protection of submarine pipelines, power interconnectors, and cables against deliberate damage, and propose reforms.
3. Does the common heritage of mankind principle, as implemented by the ISA, provide an adequate basis for the governance of deep-seabed mining in the context of demand for transition minerals? Assess the legality of unilateral licensing by non-parties to UNCLOS.
4. What are the implications of the ITLOS advisory opinion of 2024 for the licensing and environmental impact assessment of new offshore oil and gas projects by coastal states, including Nigeria?
5. Evaluate the institutional design and performance of the Nigeria–São Tomé and Príncipe Joint Development Zone, and identify lessons for future joint development arrangements in the Gulf of Guinea.
6. How should the law of the sea accommodate the large-scale development of offshore wind and hybrid interconnector projects in the EEZ, and is the "due regard" standard sufficient to manage conflicts among marine uses?
:::
