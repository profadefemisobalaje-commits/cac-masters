## Introduction

Water and energy are inseparable. Water is used to generate electricity directly through hydropower, which supplies around one-sixth of global electricity and is the largest source of renewable electricity, and indirectly as a coolant in thermal and nuclear power stations, which are among the largest users of freshwater in many countries. Water is essential to the extraction and processing of petroleum and minerals — in drilling, hydraulic fracturing, enhanced oil recovery, ore processing, and tailings management — and to the production of biofuels and green hydrogen. Conversely, energy is required to pump, treat, desalinate, and distribute water. This "water-energy nexus" means that decisions about energy are frequently decisions about water, and that the law governing water resources is an essential part of the legal framework of energy and natural resources.

Freshwater is also a quintessentially shared resource. There are more than three hundred international river basins and a comparable number of transboundary aquifers, shared by two or more states, which together account for a large share of the world's freshwater flows and are home to a large proportion of the world's population. In Africa, nearly every major river — the Nile, the Niger, the Congo, the Zambezi, the Senegal, the Orange-Senqu, the Volta, and the Lake Chad basin — is shared by several states. The development of hydropower and other water uses in one state can therefore profoundly affect the interests of others, and disputes over shared waters, including those concerning large dams, are among the most persistent sources of tension in international relations. Climate change, which is altering rainfall patterns, increasing the frequency of droughts and floods, and reducing glacier-fed flows, is intensifying these pressures.

This chapter examines the legal status of freshwater resources (section 19.1), the law of international watercourses (section 19.2), the principle of equitable and reasonable utilisation (section 19.3), transboundary water cooperation (section 19.4), hydropower development and river basin management (section 19.5), groundwater and aquifer governance (section 19.6), water rights, human rights, and public access (section 19.7), and the resolution of international water disputes (section 19.8). Chapter 10 has examined hydropower as a renewable energy technology, including its licensing and social impacts at the project level; the present chapter focuses on the transboundary and basin-level dimensions of hydropower and on the law of water resources more generally.

## 19.1 Legal Status of Freshwater Resources

### Water as a Resource and as a Right

Freshwater occupies a distinctive position among natural resources. Like petroleum and minerals, it is a resource that can be allocated, used, and, to some extent, traded, and its use for hydropower, irrigation, industry, and mining generates substantial economic value. Unlike most other resources, however, it is essential to life, to human health and dignity, and to ecosystems; it is renewable but finite, and its availability varies greatly across space and time; and it flows across boundaries, linking upstream and downstream users in relationships of interdependence. These characteristics have shaped the legal regimes that govern it, which must reconcile the treatment of water as an economic good with its recognition as a human right and an essential component of the environment.

### Sovereignty over Shared Waters

In international law, the question of the rights of states over shared waters has been answered by several competing theories.

**Absolute territorial sovereignty**, associated with the "Harmon Doctrine" — named after an opinion of the United States Attorney General in 1895 concerning the diversion of the Rio Grande, which asserted that the United States had the right to use the waters within its territory without regard to the effects on Mexico — holds that a state may do as it wishes with waters within its territory, regardless of the effects on other states. This position, which favours upstream states, has been rejected in state practice and judicial decisions, and the United States itself did not adhere to it in its subsequent treaty practice with Mexico and Canada.

**Absolute territorial integrity** holds that a downstream state is entitled to the continued natural flow of a river, undiminished in quantity and quality, by upstream states. This position, which favours downstream states and would effectively prohibit upstream development, has likewise been rejected, although elements of it are reflected in the claims of downstream states to "historic" or "acquired" rights.

**Limited territorial sovereignty** — the position that has prevailed in modern international law — holds that each state has the right to use the waters within its territory, but must exercise that right in a manner that respects the corresponding rights of other riparian states. This position is expressed in the principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation and the obligation not to cause significant harm, examined in section 19.3.

A further approach, the **community of interest** in shared waters, was articulated by the Permanent Court of International Justice in the *Territorial Jurisdiction of the International Commission of the River Oder* case (1929), in which the Court stated that the community of interest in a navigable river becomes the basis of a common legal right, the essential features of which are the perfect equality of all riparian states in the use of the whole course of the river and the exclusion of any preferential privilege of any one riparian state in relation to the others. The International Court of Justice endorsed this statement in the *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* case and extended it to non-navigational uses. The community of interest approach underpins the integrated management of river basins through joint institutions, examined in section 19.4.

### National Ownership and Control of Water

Within states, legal systems have adopted different approaches to the ownership and control of water. In many civil law and post-colonial systems, water resources are declared to be public property or vested in the state. In Nigeria, the **Water Resources Act** of 1993 vests the right to the use and control of all surface and groundwater, and of any watercourse affecting more than one state, in the Federal Government, and provides for the licensing of water uses by the federal authorities, subject to exceptions for domestic and certain other uses. The constitutional distribution of competences over water between the federal government and the states, and the role of river basin development authorities established under federal legislation, have complicated water governance, and successive attempts since 2017 to enact a comprehensive National Water Resources Bill, intended to establish a regulatory commission and a framework for integrated water resources management, have been controversial, owing in part to concerns among some states about federal control of water resources.

South Africa's **National Water Act** of 1998, adopted after the end of apartheid, abolished the system of private water rights linked to riparian land ownership and declared the national government to be the public trustee of the nation's water resources. It established a **Reserve** — comprising a basic human needs reserve and an ecological reserve — which has priority over all other uses, and requires all other significant uses to be authorised by licence. The Act is frequently cited as a model of water law reform based on principles of equity, sustainability, and public trusteeship.

### Doctrines of Water Rights

In common law jurisdictions, rights to use surface water have traditionally been based on two principal doctrines. Under the **riparian doctrine**, derived from English common law and applied in the eastern United States and other common law jurisdictions, owners of land adjoining a watercourse have the right to make reasonable use of the water, subject to the equal rights of other riparian owners. Under the **prior appropriation doctrine**, developed in the arid western United States during the nineteenth century to meet the needs of miners and farmers, water rights are acquired by diverting water and putting it to beneficial use, and rights are ranked by priority in time — "first in time, first in right" — so that in times of shortage, senior rights holders are entitled to their full allocation before junior rights holders receive any water. The prior appropriation doctrine was originally developed in mining camps in California, and illustrates the historical connection between water law and the extractive industries.

Many jurisdictions have replaced or supplemented these doctrines with **administrative permit systems**, under which the use of water above specified thresholds requires a permit issued by a public authority, which may specify the quantity, purpose, location, and duration of the use, and may be revised in response to changing conditions. Permit systems allow governments to allocate water in accordance with policy priorities, to protect environmental flows, and to respond to scarcity, but raise questions about the security of rights required for investment, including in hydropower and mining.

### The Public Trust Doctrine

The **public trust doctrine** holds that certain natural resources, including navigable waters, are held by the state in trust for the public and cannot be alienated in a manner that would defeat the public interest. Originating in Roman law and English common law, the doctrine was applied by the United States Supreme Court in *Illinois Central Railroad Co v Illinois* (1892) and extended by the Supreme Court of California in *National Audubon Society v Superior Court* (1983), the Mono Lake case, to require the state to reconsider water diversions by the City of Los Angeles that were damaging a lake ecosystem. The Supreme Court of India adopted the doctrine in *MC Mehta v Kamal Nath* (1997), holding that the state, as trustee of natural resources, could not grant a lease to a private company that interfered with the natural flow of a river. The doctrine has been incorporated into the constitutions and water legislation of several states, including South Africa and Kenya, and provides a basis for judicial review of decisions allocating water to energy and extractive projects.

## 19.2 International Watercourse Law

### Historical Development

International law relating to rivers developed first in relation to **navigation**. The Final Act of the Congress of Vienna of 1815 proclaimed the principle of freedom of navigation on international rivers for riparian states, and international commissions were established for the Rhine (1815, reorganised by the Mannheim Convention of 1868) and the Danube (1856), which are among the oldest international organisations. The Barcelona Convention and Statute on the Regime of Navigable Waterways of International Concern of 1921 sought to generalise these principles. During the twentieth century, as the use of rivers for hydropower, irrigation, and industry grew, attention shifted to **non-navigational uses**, which are the principal subject of modern watercourse law.

The **Helsinki Rules on the Uses of the Waters of International Rivers**, adopted by the International Law Association in 1966, articulated the principle of equitable utilisation of the waters of an international drainage basin and identified factors relevant to its application. The ILA's **Berlin Rules on Water Resources** (2004) sought to update the Helsinki Rules in the light of developments in international environmental law and human rights, giving greater emphasis to the obligation not to cause significant harm, ecological integrity, and the human right to water, although they were controversial among some members of the Association.

### The UN Watercourses Convention

In 1970, the UN General Assembly requested the International Law Commission to study the law of the non-navigational uses of international watercourses. After more than two decades of work, the Commission adopted draft articles in 1994, on the basis of which the General Assembly adopted the **Convention on the Law of the Non-Navigational Uses of International Watercourses** in 1997, by a vote of 103 to 3 with 27 abstentions. The states voting against were Burundi, China, and Turkey — all significant upstream states — and the abstentions included Egypt, Ethiopia, France, India, and Pakistan. The Convention entered into force in 2014, after the deposit of the thirty-fifth instrument of ratification, and has some forty parties, including Nigeria, which acceded in 2010, and several other West African states, but not most of the major upstream states.

The Convention applies to **international watercourses** — systems of surface waters and groundwaters constituting by virtue of their physical relationship a unitary whole and normally flowing into a common terminus, parts of which are situated in different states. It is a framework convention, which does not displace existing watercourse agreements and encourages states to conclude agreements applying and adjusting its provisions to the characteristics of particular watercourses (Article 3). Its principal provisions are:

- the principle of **equitable and reasonable utilisation and participation** (Article 5), with the factors relevant to its application (Article 6), examined in section 19.3;
- the **obligation not to cause significant harm** (Article 7), requiring watercourse states to take all appropriate measures to prevent the causing of significant harm to other watercourse states, and, where harm is caused, to take measures to eliminate or mitigate it and, where appropriate, to discuss compensation;
- the **general obligation to cooperate** (Article 8) and the regular exchange of data and information (Article 9);
- the principle that no use enjoys inherent **priority** over other uses, subject to special regard to the requirements of vital human needs (Article 10);
- **procedural obligations** relating to **planned measures** that may have a significant adverse effect on other watercourse states (Articles 11–19), including prior notification, a six-month period for reply during which the notifying state may not implement the measures without the consent of the notified states, consultation and negotiation, and procedures in the absence of notification;
- obligations to **protect and preserve ecosystems** (Article 20), to prevent, reduce, and control pollution (Article 21), and to take measures concerning harmful conditions and emergencies (Articles 27–28);
- provisions on the protection of watercourses and installations in time of armed conflict (Article 29); and
- **dispute settlement** (Article 33), including a compulsory fact-finding procedure at the request of any party, and optional submission to the International Court of Justice or arbitration.

Although the Convention has attracted relatively limited participation, many of its core provisions are regarded as reflecting customary international law, as confirmed by the International Court of Justice in *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* (which referred to the draft articles before the Convention was adopted) and in the *Silala* case (*Chile v Bolivia*, 2022), in which the parties agreed, and the Court accepted, that the customary principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation, prevention of significant harm, and notification and consultation applied to the Silala, a watercourse that rises in Bolivia and flows into Chile.

### The UNECE Water Convention

The **Convention on the Protection and Use of Transboundary Watercourses and International Lakes**, adopted under the auspices of the UN Economic Commission for Europe at Helsinki in 1992 (the "Water Convention"), emphasises the prevention, control, and reduction of transboundary impacts, and requires riparian parties to conclude bilateral or multilateral agreements and to establish joint bodies for the management of shared waters. Its **Protocol on Water and Health** (1999) addresses access to safe drinking water and sanitation. Amendments that entered into force in 2013 opened the Convention to accession by all UN member states, and since 2016 a number of states outside the UNECE region, including Chad, Senegal, Ghana, Togo, Guinea-Bissau, Cameroon, and others in Africa, have acceded, making it, alongside the UN Watercourses Convention, a global framework for transboundary water cooperation. The two conventions are regarded as compatible and complementary, and the secretariats cooperate in promoting both.

### Regional and Basin Agreements

The most detailed rules governing shared waters are found in regional and basin agreements. In southern Africa, the **SADC Revised Protocol on Shared Watercourses** (2000) closely follows the UN Watercourses Convention and provides the framework for basin agreements such as the agreement establishing the Zambezi Watercourse Commission (ZAMCOM, 2004). In West Africa, the **Water Charter of the Niger Basin** (2008) and the conventions establishing the Senegal River Basin Development Organisation (OMVS) and the Volta Basin Authority provide detailed frameworks for cooperation, examined in section 19.4. In Europe, the **Water Framework Directive** (Directive 2000/60/EC) requires the management of water at the level of river basin districts, including international districts, with the objective of achieving good status for all waters. Hundreds of bilateral treaties govern specific rivers, lakes, and projects.
