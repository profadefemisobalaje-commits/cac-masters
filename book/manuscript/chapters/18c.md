## 18.4 Climate Mitigation and Adaptation Obligations

### The Sources of States' Climate Obligations

The obligations of states in relation to climate change arise from several sources: the climate treaties — the UNFCCC, the Kyoto Protocol, and the Paris Agreement; other treaties, including UNCLOS and human rights treaties; and customary international law, including the obligation to prevent significant transboundary harm and the duty to cooperate. For many years, the relationship between these sources was uncertain. Some states argued that the climate treaties constitute a *lex specialis* that exclusively governs states' obligations in relation to climate change, displacing general international law; others argued that general international law, including the prevention principle and human rights law, applies concurrently. Between 2024 and 2025, three international courts and tribunals delivered advisory opinions that substantially clarified these questions.

### The ITLOS Advisory Opinion (2024)

In response to a request from the Commission of Small Island States on Climate Change and International Law, the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea delivered a unanimous advisory opinion on 21 May 2024. The Tribunal held that anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions into the atmosphere constitute "pollution of the marine environment" within the meaning of Article 1(1)(4) of UNCLOS, because they introduce substances or energy into the marine environment — through ocean warming, sea level rise, and ocean acidification — resulting in deleterious effects. Consequently, states parties to UNCLOS have specific obligations under Article 194 to take all necessary measures to prevent, reduce, and control marine pollution from anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, and to endeavour to harmonise their policies. The Tribunal held that this is an obligation of due diligence, the standard of which is "stringent" given the high risks of serious and irreversible harm, and that the obligations under UNCLOS are not satisfied simply by complying with the Paris Agreement, since UNCLOS and the Paris Agreement are separate agreements with separate sets of obligations, although the Paris Agreement's temperature goal and the best available science are relevant to determining the necessary measures. The opinion is examined further in Chapter 20.

### The Advisory Opinion of the International Court of Justice (2025)

The most authoritative statement of states' climate obligations is the advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice requested by the General Assembly.

::: casebox {title="Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change, Advisory Opinion (ICJ, 23 July 2025)"}
**The request.** In March 2023, following a campaign initiated by students in the Pacific and led diplomatically by Vanuatu, the United Nations General Assembly adopted by consensus Resolution 77/276, requesting an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice on two questions: what are the obligations of states under international law to ensure the protection of the climate system and other parts of the environment from anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions for states and for present and future generations; and what are the legal consequences under these obligations for states where they, by their acts and omissions, have caused significant harm to the climate system and other parts of the environment. The proceedings attracted the largest number of written and oral submissions in the Court's history.

**The applicable law.** The Court unanimously held that the obligations of states are found in the climate treaties, in UNCLOS and other environmental treaties, in customary international law, and in international human rights law. It rejected the argument that the climate treaties constitute a *lex specialis* excluding the application of other rules, holding that the various sources apply concurrently and inform each other.

**The climate treaties.** The Court held that the 1.5°C goal has become the primary temperature goal agreed by the parties to the Paris Agreement. While the content of NDCs is nationally determined, the discretion of parties is limited: their NDCs must reflect their highest possible ambition and must, taken together, be capable of achieving the temperature goal; and parties have an obligation of conduct, assessed by a stringent standard of due diligence, to pursue measures capable of achieving their NDCs.

**Customary law.** The Court held that states have customary obligations to prevent significant harm to the climate system and other parts of the environment, by acting with due diligence, and to cooperate in good faith. The standard of due diligence is stringent, and requires states to use all means at their disposal, including the adoption of regulatory and legislative measures and their enforcement, in the light of the best available science and taking into account the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.

**Fossil fuels.** In a passage of particular significance for energy law, the Court stated that a state's failure to take appropriate action to protect the climate system from greenhouse gas emissions — including through fossil fuel production, fossil fuel consumption, the granting of fossil fuel exploration licences, or the provision of fossil fuel subsidies — may constitute an internationally wrongful act attributable to that state.

**Human rights.** The Court held that the protection of the environment is a precondition for the enjoyment of human rights, that the human right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment is essential for the enjoyment of other human rights, and that states must take measures to protect human rights against the adverse effects of climate change.

**Legal consequences.** The Court held that the breach of these obligations entails the full range of legal consequences under the law of state responsibility: cessation of the wrongful conduct, assurances and guarantees of non-repetition, and full reparation, in the form of restitution, compensation, and satisfaction, subject to the establishment of a sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus between the wrongful act and the injury, which may be established in the climate context even though climate change results from the cumulative emissions of many states. The obligations concerned are owed *erga omnes*, so that all states have a legal interest in their performance.

**Significance.** Although advisory opinions are not binding, the opinion represents the most authoritative statement of the law by the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, and will be relied upon in domestic and international litigation, in negotiations, and in the assessment of national energy policies, including decisions to license new fossil fuel production. Its implications for producer states, and for the relationship between climate obligations and investment protection, will be debated for years to come.

### The Inter-American Court and the European Court of Human Rights

The **Inter-American Court of Human Rights**, in its Advisory Opinion OC-32/25 on the climate emergency and human rights, delivered in 2025 at the request of Chile and Colombia, held that states have obligations under the American Convention on Human Rights to mitigate climate change and to adapt to its effects, applying a standard of enhanced due diligence, and recognised the right to a healthy climate as part of the right to a healthy environment. In *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz v Switzerland*, examined in section 18.6, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held in April 2024 that Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights encompasses a right to effective protection by the state authorities from the serious adverse effects of climate change on life, health, well-being, and quality of life.

### Mitigation Obligations in Practice

Taken together, these developments indicate that states' mitigation obligations involve: an obligation to prepare and implement NDCs reflecting their highest possible ambition, consistent with the 1.5°C goal and their fair share of the global effort; an obligation to adopt and enforce effective domestic regulatory frameworks; an obligation to regulate private actors, including energy companies, within their jurisdiction; and an obligation to assess the climate effects of projects and policies. The extent to which these obligations constrain particular energy decisions — such as the licensing of new oil and gas fields, the construction of coal plants, or the provision of fossil fuel subsidies — remains contested, and will depend on the application of the due diligence standard to particular facts. Producer states and developing countries have emphasised the principle of CBDR-RC, their right to development, and the need for international finance as conditions for ambitious action.

### Adaptation

**Adaptation** — adjustment in natural or human systems in response to actual or expected climatic effects — has assumed growing importance as the effects of climate change have intensified. Article 7 of the Paris Agreement establishes the global goal on adaptation and requires parties to engage in adaptation planning, including through national adaptation plans, vulnerability assessments, and monitoring. The **UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience**, adopted in 2023, set targets for adaptation in areas including water, food, health, ecosystems, infrastructure, poverty, and cultural heritage, and work continues on indicators to measure progress.

The energy sector is both a driver of adaptation needs and vulnerable to climate impacts. Hydropower is affected by droughts and changes in river flows, as illustrated by power shortages in Zambia and Zimbabwe resulting from low water levels at the Kariba dam in 2015–2016 and 2024; thermal power plants depend on cooling water; coastal energy infrastructure, including refineries, LNG terminals, and power stations, is exposed to sea level rise and storms; and transmission networks are vulnerable to heat, wildfires, and extreme weather. Legal frameworks increasingly require climate risk assessment in the planning, permitting, and financing of energy infrastructure, and the EIA Directive, as amended, requires assessment of a project's vulnerability to climate change.

### Loss and Damage

**Loss and damage** refers to the adverse effects of climate change that cannot be avoided through mitigation or adaptation, including both extreme weather events and slow-onset events such as sea level rise and desertification. The Warsaw International Mechanism for Loss and Damage was established in 2013, and the Santiago Network, established in 2019, provides technical assistance. At COP27 in 2022, after decades of advocacy by vulnerable developing countries, parties agreed to establish new funding arrangements for loss and damage, including a dedicated fund, which was operationalised at COP28 in 2023, hosted initially by the World Bank as a financial intermediary fund, with initial pledges of several hundred million dollars — a small fraction of the estimated needs. The relationship between loss and damage and legal liability remains contested; the decision adopting the Paris Agreement excludes Article 8 as a basis for liability or compensation, but the ICJ's advisory opinion confirms that the general law of state responsibility, including the obligation to make reparation, applies to breaches of climate obligations.

### Climate Finance

Climate finance — the flow of financial resources for mitigation and adaptation, particularly from developed to developing countries — is central to the bargain of the climate regime. The commitment of developed countries to mobilise USD 100 billion a year by 2020 was, according to the OECD, met only in 2022, two years late, and its accounting — including the counting of loans at face value and the reclassification of existing development aid — has been criticised. The New Collective Quantified Goal agreed in 2024, examined in section 18.2, sets a higher target for 2035. The adequacy, predictability, and accessibility of climate finance are critical to the ability of developing countries to implement the conditional elements of their NDCs, and to the transformation of their energy systems, as examined in Chapter 16 in the context of blended finance and JETPs.

## 18.5 Fossil Fuel Regulation and Transition Policies

### The Policy Toolkit

States use a wide range of legal and policy instruments to reduce emissions from the energy sector: carbon pricing through taxes and emissions trading; regulatory standards for power plants, vehicles, buildings, and appliances; support for renewable energy and low-carbon technologies, examined in Chapters 10 and 12; the phase-out of coal and other high-emitting technologies; restrictions on the supply of fossil fuels; the reform of fossil fuel subsidies; and the regulation of methane, examined in Chapter 17. The choice and design of instruments raises legal questions under constitutional law, administrative law, trade law, and investment law.

### Carbon Pricing

**Carbon pricing** places a price on greenhouse gas emissions, internalising their external costs in accordance with the polluter-pays principle. According to the World Bank, carbon taxes and emissions trading systems now cover roughly a quarter of global emissions, although prices in most systems remain below the levels considered necessary to achieve the Paris goals.

**Emissions trading systems** (ETS) set a cap on total emissions from covered sectors and issue allowances, which may be traded, so that emissions reductions occur where they are cheapest. The **European Union Emissions Trading System**, established by Directive 2003/87/EC and operating since 2005, is the largest and longest-established system, covering power generation, energy-intensive industry, aviation within Europe, and, since 2024, maritime transport. After a period of low prices caused by an oversupply of allowances, the introduction of the **Market Stability Reserve** in 2019 and the tightening of the cap under the Fit for 55 package raised prices substantially. A separate system for emissions from buildings and road transport fuels ("ETS2") is due to begin operation later in the decade, and the **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** (Regulation (EU) 2023/956), examined in Chapter 13, applies a carbon price to imports of specified goods to prevent carbon leakage. **China's national ETS**, which began trading in 2021 covering the power sector, is the largest by covered emissions, and is being extended to other sectors. Other systems operate in the United Kingdom, Korea, New Zealand, California, the north-eastern United States, and elsewhere.

**Carbon taxes** impose a price per tonne of emissions. Sweden introduced a carbon tax in 1991, which now has one of the highest rates in the world. In Africa, **South Africa's Carbon Tax Act 2019** imposes a tax on fossil fuel combustion and industrial process emissions, with substantial allowances in its initial phases, and Nigeria's Climate Change Act provides for the development of carbon tax mechanisms.

### Carbon Markets under Article 6

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement provides for voluntary cooperation among parties in implementing their NDCs. Under **Article 6.2**, parties may engage in cooperative approaches involving the use of **internationally transferred mitigation outcomes** (ITMOs) towards their NDCs, subject to guidance on robust accounting, including **corresponding adjustments** to avoid the double counting of emission reductions by both the transferring and acquiring parties. Bilateral agreements under Article 6.2 have been concluded by states such as Switzerland, Japan, Singapore, and Sweden with host countries including Ghana, Senegal, Kenya, and others. **Article 6.4** establishes a centralised mechanism — the **Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism** — supervised by a body under the CMA, as a successor to the CDM, whose methodological standards and procedures were substantially agreed at Baku in 2024. The **voluntary carbon market**, in which companies purchase credits to offset their emissions, has been subject to criticism about the quality of credits, particularly those from forest conservation projects, leading to initiatives such as the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market's Core Carbon Principles (2023). African governments have sought to expand their participation in carbon markets through the **Africa Carbon Markets Initiative**, launched in 2022, while also asserting control over the benefits through legislation, such as Kenya's Climate Change (Amendment) Act 2023 and regulations in Zimbabwe, Nigeria, and other states, which require benefit-sharing with communities and government.

### Coal Phase-Out

Coal is the most carbon-intensive fossil fuel, and its phase-out from electricity generation is a central element of climate policy. The **Powering Past Coal Alliance**, launched by the United Kingdom and Canada in 2017, brings together governments, businesses, and organisations committed to phasing out unabated coal power. The United Kingdom closed its last coal-fired power station, at Ratcliffe-on-Soar, in September 2024, completing a transition from coal providing the majority of electricity within living memory. Germany's **Coal Phase-Out Act** of 2020 provides for the closure of all coal-fired power plants by 2038 at the latest, with compensation for operators of lignite plants and support for affected regions, the compensation for which required approval by the European Commission under the state aid rules. The legal design of coal phase-out — whether through regulation, auctions for compensated closure, carbon pricing, or negotiated agreements — has implications under constitutional property protections and investment treaties, as illustrated by the claims of RWE and Uniper against the Netherlands under the Energy Charter Treaty, examined in Chapter 14.

### Supply-Side Measures

Traditionally, climate policy has focused on reducing the demand for fossil fuels. Increasingly, attention has turned to **supply-side** measures that restrict the exploration and production of fossil fuels. France enacted a law in 2017 ending the grant of new exploration permits and providing for the end of hydrocarbon production in its territory by 2040. Denmark decided in 2020 to cancel future licensing rounds and to end oil and gas extraction in the North Sea by 2050, and with Costa Rica launched the **Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance** at COP26 in 2021. New Zealand banned new offshore oil and gas exploration permits in 2018, but legislation enacted in 2025 reversed the ban. A group of states, led by Pacific island nations, has called for the negotiation of a **Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty**, which would end new fossil fuel exploration and production and manage a global transition. The International Energy Agency's Net Zero by 2050 roadmap, published in 2021, concluded that no new oil and gas fields would need to be approved for development in its net zero pathway — a finding frequently cited in litigation and policy debates, and contested by producer states and companies.

Supply-side measures raise the problem of "leakage" — production restricted in one country may be replaced by production elsewhere — and concerns about equity, since developing producer states argue that they should not forgo the development of their resources when developed countries built their prosperity on fossil fuels and continue to produce them. The 2025 advisory opinion of the ICJ, in recognising that the granting of exploration licences may in principle constitute an internationally wrongful act, has given new legal significance to supply-side policy.

### Fossil Fuel Subsidies

Fossil fuel subsidies — whether consumer subsidies that reduce the price of fuels below market levels, or producer subsidies such as tax incentives for exploration — encourage consumption and production of fossil fuels, burden public finances, and frequently benefit wealthier households disproportionately. The G20 committed in 2009 to phase out inefficient fossil fuel subsidies over the medium term, and the commitment was repeated in the Glasgow Climate Pact. The IMF has estimated that explicit fossil fuel subsidies — undercharging for supply costs — amounted to more than USD 1 trillion in 2022, and that total subsidies, including the failure to price environmental costs, were several times higher. Reform is politically difficult, as illustrated by protests following attempts to remove fuel subsidies in Nigeria in 2012, Ecuador in 2019, and Kazakhstan in 2022. Nigeria removed its petrol subsidy in May 2023, with significant effects on prices, inflation, and public finances, examined in Chapter 24. Within the World Trade Organization, a group of members has sought to advance disciplines on fossil fuel subsidies, and the WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies illustrates the possibility of trade disciplines on environmentally harmful subsidies.

### Industrial Policy and the Energy Transition

The energy transition has been accompanied by a resurgence of industrial policy, in which governments provide large subsidies and incentives for clean energy manufacturing and deployment. The United States **Inflation Reduction Act** of 2022 provided tax credits for renewable energy, electric vehicles, hydrogen, carbon capture, and clean manufacturing, many of which were curtailed or accelerated to expiry by legislation enacted in July 2025. The European Union responded with the Green Deal Industrial Plan and the **Net-Zero Industry Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1735), examined in Chapter 12, and China's dominance of clean energy supply chains, examined in Chapter 8, reflects decades of state support. These policies raise questions under the WTO rules on subsidies and local content, examined in Chapter 13, and reflect the intersection of climate policy with geopolitics and energy security, examined in Chapter 25.
