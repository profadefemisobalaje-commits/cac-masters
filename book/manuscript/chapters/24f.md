::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 24: A Comparative Law Reform Brief for a New Producer (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The state, discovery, institutions, and political circumstances described are fictitious and do not refer to any real country or event.**

The Republic of Lundara, a coastal West African state with a population of fifteen million, a civil law tradition inherited from its colonial period, and a unitary constitution that vests all natural resources in "the people, represented by the State", has announced a major offshore oil and gas discovery. The discovery lies in a block granted in 2014 under a production sharing contract governed by Lundara's Hydrocarbons Code of 1998, which provides for a ten per cent royalty, a profit oil split that rises from forty to sixty per cent for the state according to cumulative production, a ten per cent carried interest for the state oil company, a twenty-year stability clause, and ICC arbitration. Lundara's state oil company, Petrolund, both regulates the sector and holds the state's participating interests. Lundara has no sovereign wealth fund, no local content law, and no legal framework for community benefits. Forty per cent of its population lacks access to electricity, and the government has pledged in its NDC to reach net zero by 2060. A newly elected government, which campaigned on "sovereignty over our resources", has established a Law Reform Commission to recommend a new legal framework before the first production, expected in five years.

**Tasks.** You are appointed as comparative law adviser to the Law Reform Commission.

(a) Prepare a methodological note (of no more than 1,000 words) identifying the jurisdictions you propose to examine and justifying your selection, explaining how you will address the risks of inappropriate legal transplantation.

(b) Prepare a comparative brief (of no more than 4,000 words) addressing the following questions, drawing on the jurisdictions examined in this chapter: (i) whether, and how, regulatory and commercial functions should be separated, with reference to Nigeria's PIA and Norway; (ii) the design of a petroleum revenue management law and sovereign wealth fund, with reference to Ghana, Norway, Nigeria, and Chad; (iii) the design of a host community benefit-sharing regime, with reference to Nigeria's host communities development trusts and other models; (iv) whether the existing PSC should be renegotiated, and the legal constraints on doing so, with reference to the experience of Tanzania, Senegal, and Guyana and the stability clause; (v) how the new framework should address domestic gas supply and electricity access; and (vi) how the framework should reconcile petroleum development with the government's climate commitments.

(c) Draft the headings and principal provisions of a proposed Petroleum Governance Bill, indicating for each major provision its comparative source of inspiration.

(d) In a group, debate the motion: "Lundara should adopt the Norwegian model of petroleum governance."
:::

::: summary
- Comparative law serves knowledge, law reform, practice, harmonisation, and the identification of general principles; functional, contextual, and socio-legal methods each have strengths and limitations, and the transplantation of energy law models requires attention to local institutions and context.
- Nigeria's constitution vests petroleum in the federation; the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 separated policy, regulation (NUPRC and NMDPRA), and commercial functions (NNPC Limited), introduced a new licensing and fiscal regime, host communities development trusts, and environmental funds; the Electricity Act 2023 decentralised electricity regulation to the states; and the removal of the petrol subsidy in 2023 and the Dangote refinery are transforming the downstream sector.
- United States energy law is shaped by federalism, private mineral ownership, FERC's regulation of interstate gas and electricity, state regulation, and environmental review; recent Supreme Court decisions (*West Virginia v EPA*, *Loper Bright*, *Seven County*) have constrained agency power, and federal energy policy has been volatile.
- The United Kingdom pioneered privatisation, independent regulation, and contracts for difference; the European Union has built an internal energy market through successive liberalisation packages and is implementing the Green Deal, REPowerEU, and electricity market reform.
- Middle Eastern petroleum governance ranges from exclusive NOC control (Saudi Arabia) through service contracts (Iran, federal Iraq) to concession and joint venture models (UAE, Qatar), with Iraq illustrating federal disputes over resources.
- Asian frameworks include China's state-led system and new Energy Law, India's federal competences and auction-based allocation, Indonesia's constitutional resource control and nickel export ban, Japan's post-Fukushima reforms, and Australia's federal resource ownership and market institutions.
- Latin America has oscillated between liberalisation and resource nationalism, from Mexico's 1938 expropriation and 2013 reform and counter-reform to Venezuela's nationalisations, Brazil's mixed regime, Argentina's YPF expropriation and Vaca Muerta, divergent lithium models, and environmental constitutionalism.
- African experience — Botswana's institutions, Ghana's revenue management, Tanzania's and the DRC's resource nationalism, new producers such as Senegal, and South Africa's reforms — shows that institutions, transparency, balanced stability, revenue management rules, community benefits, and attention to the energy transition are critical.
:::

::: research
1. To what extent has the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 addressed the governance failures of Nigeria's previous petroleum legal framework? Evaluate its implementation in comparative perspective.
2. Assess the legal and institutional challenges of the decentralisation of electricity regulation under Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023, drawing on comparative experience of multilevel electricity governance in the United States, India, and Australia.
3. Is resource nationalism a rational strategy for African resource-rich states? Evaluate with reference to Tanzania, the DRC, and Latin American experience.
4. Can the Norwegian model of petroleum governance be successfully transplanted to African producers? Assess using comparative legal methodology.
5. Compare the legal approaches of Chile, Bolivia, and Argentina to lithium and evaluate their lessons for African producers of transition minerals, including Nigeria.
6. How have recent changes in United States administrative law affected the capacity of federal agencies to regulate the energy transition, and what lessons do they hold for the design of regulatory mandates elsewhere?
:::
