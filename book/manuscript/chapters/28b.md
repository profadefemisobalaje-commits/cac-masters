## 28.2 Doctrinal Legal Research

### The Nature of Doctrinal Research

**Doctrinal** or **"black-letter"** legal research is the traditional and still predominant method of legal scholarship. It involves the identification, analysis, and synthesis of the **authoritative sources** of law — constitutions, treaties, legislation, subsidiary instruments, judicial decisions, and, in international law, custom and general principles — in order to state the law on a particular question, explain its structure and rationale, identify inconsistencies and gaps, and propose solutions. In a widely cited formulation derived from the Pearce Committee's review of Australian law schools (1987), and popularised by Terry Hutchinson and Nigel Duncan, doctrinal research provides a systematic exposition of the rules governing a particular legal category, analyses the relationship between rules, explains areas of difficulty and, perhaps, predicts future development. It is the method of most of the analysis in this book.

Doctrinal research is sometimes dismissed as merely descriptive, but at its best it is a sophisticated intellectual activity involving **interpretation** (determining the meaning of texts in context), **analysis** (breaking down complex legal materials into their components), **synthesis** (constructing coherent principles from diverse sources), **criticism** (identifying inconsistencies, errors, and injustices), and **prescription** (proposing reforms or preferred interpretations). It is particularly well suited to questions about the content and coherence of the law and is indispensable as a foundation for other methods: an empirical study of the implementation of a law, or a comparative study of several legal systems, presupposes an accurate understanding of the law itself.

### The Process of Doctrinal Research

The doctrinal research process typically involves the following steps:

1. **Identifying the legal issue** precisely, including its jurisdictional context (international, regional, national, or subnational) and the relevant area of law.
2. **Locating the primary sources**: the relevant treaties and their *travaux préparatoires*, constitutional provisions, statutes and subsidiary legislation, judicial decisions and arbitral awards, and, in international law, evidence of state practice and *opinio juris*.
3. **Locating the secondary sources**: textbooks, treatises, commentaries, journal articles, and reports that analyse the primary sources, which assist in understanding the law and identifying debates, but are not themselves authoritative (except, in international law, the "teachings of the most highly qualified publicists" as a subsidiary means for the determination of rules under Article 38(1)(d) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice).
4. **Reading and analysing the sources**, applying the canons of interpretation appropriate to each type of source.
5. **Synthesising** the sources into a coherent statement of the law, identifying principles, exceptions, and areas of uncertainty.
6. **Evaluating** the law against criteria — internal coherence, consistency with higher norms, fidelity to purpose, justice, efficiency, or international standards — which must be made explicit.
7. **Drawing conclusions** and, where appropriate, proposing reforms.

### Interpretation of Legal Sources

Each type of source has its own interpretive conventions. **Treaties** are interpreted in accordance with Articles 31 to 33 of the **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties** (1969), which require interpretation in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning of the terms in their context and in the light of the treaty's object and purpose, taking into account subsequent agreements and practice and relevant rules of international law applicable between the parties (Article 31(3)(c)) — a provision of growing importance for the interpretation of investment treaties in the light of environmental and human rights obligations — with recourse to supplementary means, including the preparatory work, to confirm the meaning or resolve ambiguity. **Statutes** in common law systems are interpreted according to principles that have evolved from the literal, golden, and mischief rules towards a **purposive** approach that reads the words in context in the light of the statute's purpose, as the UK Supreme Court has emphasised, and Nigerian courts apply similar principles, together with the rules of interpretation of the Constitution, which favour a broad and liberal construction. **Judicial decisions** are analysed to identify the *ratio decidendi* — the principle necessary for the decision — as distinct from *obiter dicta*, and their authority depends on the hierarchy of courts and the doctrine of precedent. **Arbitral awards** have no formal precedential value, but investment tribunals frequently refer to earlier awards, giving rise to a *jurisprudence constante* in some areas and to persistent divergence in others; the researcher must evaluate awards critically, considering the composition of the tribunal, the quality of reasoning, and subsequent reception. **Custom** is identified by evidence of general state practice accepted as law, in accordance with the International Law Commission's **Conclusions on Identification of Customary International Law** (2018).

### Strengths and Limitations

Doctrinal research has significant strengths: it engages directly with the materials that legal actors use; it produces knowledge directly useful to courts, practitioners, and legislators; and its methods are well understood and transparent. Its limitations are equally significant. It tends to focus on **law in the books** rather than **law in action**, and may therefore miss the gap between formal rules and their implementation — a gap that is often wide in the energy and resource sector, where, as observed throughout this book, impressive legislation frequently coexists with weak enforcement. It may be **parochial**, confined to a single legal system. Its evaluative criteria may be implicit and unexamined. And it may struggle with questions that require empirical evidence, such as the effects of a law on investment, revenues, or environmental outcomes. The doctoral researcher using doctrinal methods should therefore articulate the evaluative framework explicitly, acknowledge the limits of doctrinal knowledge, and consider whether the research question requires supplementation by other methods.

### Doctrinal Research in Energy Law: Distinctive Challenges

Doctrinal research in energy law faces specific challenges. The **multi-level** structure of energy law requires the researcher to trace the interaction of international, regional, national, and contractual norms — for example, how a host state's obligations under an investment treaty interact with a stabilisation clause in a petroleum agreement and with domestic environmental legislation. The **confidentiality** of key sources — petroleum agreements, commercial arbitral awards, and regulatory decisions — limits the materials available, although contract disclosure initiatives such as ResourceContracts.org, the EITI's contract transparency requirements, and the publication of investment awards by ICSID and the PCA have improved access. The **technicality** of energy law requires an understanding of the industry's operations, economics, and terminology. And the **rapidity of change** requires constant updating; a thesis submitted several years after its research began may describe law that has since changed, and the researcher should state the date at which the law is stated.

## 28.3 Comparative and Transnational Legal Research

### The Uses of Comparative Research

Chapter 24 introduced comparative law as a method and applied it to the energy and resource laws of major jurisdictions. This section considers comparative research from the perspective of research design. Comparative research may serve several purposes in a doctoral thesis: to **understand** one's own legal system better by seeing it in the light of others; to **identify solutions** to legal problems by examining how other systems have addressed them; to **explain** differences and similarities among legal systems by reference to historical, economic, political, and cultural factors; to **test hypotheses** about the relationship between legal rules and outcomes; and to contribute to the **harmonisation** or **transnationalisation** of law.

### Designing a Comparative Study

The design of a comparative study involves several key decisions:

1. **The comparative question.** The study must ask a question that can be answered by comparison, such as how different systems address a common problem, and the problem must be defined in functional terms that do not presuppose the concepts of any one system — for example, "how do legal systems allocate the risk of regulatory change in long-term petroleum investments?" rather than "how do legal systems treat stabilisation clauses?", since some systems address the risk through other mechanisms.
2. **The selection of jurisdictions.** Jurisdictions must be selected on a principled basis related to the research question. The **"most similar systems"** design compares jurisdictions that are similar in most respects but differ in the variable of interest, allowing differences in outcomes to be attributed to that variable; the **"most different systems"** design compares jurisdictions that differ in most respects but share an outcome, allowing the common factor to be identified. Researchers may also select jurisdictions as **exemplars** of particular models (for example, Norway as an exemplar of the separation of commercial and regulatory functions), as **sources of influence** on the home jurisdiction, or as **leading or innovative** systems. The number of jurisdictions involves a trade-off between depth and breadth: a study of two or three jurisdictions permits contextual depth; a study of many permits broader generalisation but risks superficiality.
3. **The *tertium comparationis*.** The common point of reference — the problem, function, or standard against which the jurisdictions are compared — must be clearly defined.
4. **The method of analysis.** The **functional method**, associated with Zweigert and Kötz, compares the solutions that different systems provide to the same social problem, on the assumption that legal systems face similar problems even if they solve them differently. **Contextual** or **cultural** approaches, associated with scholars such as Pierre Legrand, emphasise that legal rules are embedded in cultures and cannot be understood or transplanted in isolation. **Legal origins** research, associated with economists such as La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, and Shleifer, uses quantitative methods to test the relationship between legal traditions (common law or civil law) and economic outcomes, though its methods and conclusions have been heavily criticised.
5. **Access to sources.** The researcher must have access to the primary sources of each jurisdiction, in the original language or reliable translation, and an understanding of their context; reliance on secondary sources in English may lead to errors.

### Transnational Legal Research

Energy law is increasingly **transnational**, involving norms that cross the boundaries between national and international law and between public and private ordering: model contracts such as the AIPN model joint operating agreement; industry standards developed by bodies such as the American Petroleum Institute and the International Organization for Standardization; soft law instruments such as the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights and the OECD Guidelines; the standards of international financial institutions, such as the IFC Performance Standards, examined in Chapter 21; certification schemes such as the Kimberley Process and responsible mining standards; and arbitral practice, sometimes described as a *lex petrolea*, examined in Chapter 1. **Transnational legal research** studies how these norms are created, diffused, and enforced, and how they interact with state law. Concepts such as **transnational legal orders** (Halliday and Shaffer), **global administrative law** (Kingsbury, Krisch, and Stewart), and **legal pluralism** provide frameworks for such research. Methods include the tracing of norm diffusion across jurisdictions, the analysis of the "life cycle" of transnational norms, and the study of the interaction between transnational standards and national law in specific contexts, such as the incorporation of the IFC Performance Standards into project finance agreements and their effect on community outcomes.

### Pitfalls of Comparative and Transnational Research

The doctoral researcher should be alert to the pitfalls identified in Chapter 24: superficial comparison that juxtaposes descriptions without analysis; the assumption that a foreign model will work in the home jurisdiction regardless of context ("legal transplant" fallacies); ethnocentric bias that evaluates other systems by the standards of one's own; the selection of jurisdictions to confirm a preconceived conclusion; and neglect of the gap between formal law and practice in the jurisdictions compared. A comparative thesis should explicitly address the transferability of lessons, considering the institutional, economic, and political conditions on which the success of a foreign model depends.
