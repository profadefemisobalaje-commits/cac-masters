## Introduction

Energy is capital. A large hydroelectric dam, an LNG export terminal, a deepwater oil development, a transmission interconnector, or a utility-scale solar programme requires capital investment measured in hundreds of millions or billions of dollars, committed years before the first revenue is earned and recovered over decades. The International Energy Agency has estimated that global energy investment now exceeds USD 3 trillion a year, with clean energy accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total, and that the investment required to meet climate and access goals in emerging and developing economies — outside China — must increase several-fold. How that capital is raised, secured, priced, and protected is therefore a central question of energy law, and one in which legal design directly affects the cost of capital and hence the price that consumers pay for energy.

At the same time, energy and natural resources are a principal source of public revenue for many states. Petroleum and mineral revenues finance a large share of government budgets in Nigeria, Angola, Algeria, Saudi Arabia, Norway, Chile, Zambia, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and many other states. The design of fiscal regimes — the balance between royalties, taxes, production sharing, and state participation — determines how the economic rent generated by natural resources is divided between the state and investors, and the effectiveness of tax administration, transparency, and anti-corruption controls determines whether that rent reaches the public treasury and is used for the benefit of the population. The phenomenon frequently described as the "resource curse" — the tendency of resource-rich states to experience slower growth, weaker institutions, greater inequality, and more conflict than resource-poor states — has been attributed in significant part to failures of fiscal governance.

This chapter brings together these two dimensions — finance and fiscal governance — because they are inseparable in practice. Lenders assess the fiscal regime and its stability when deciding whether to finance a project; host governments assess the financing structure when negotiating fiscal terms; and sovereign guarantees, tax holidays, and contingent liabilities link the private balance sheet of the project to the public balance sheet of the state. The chapter examines project finance structures (section 16.1), debt, equity, and risk allocation (section 16.2), sovereign guarantees and government support (section 16.3), petroleum and mining fiscal regimes (section 16.4), royalties, resource rent taxes, and production levies (section 16.5), transfer pricing and international taxation (section 16.6), revenue transparency and anti-corruption controls (section 16.7), and debt restructuring and financial distress (section 16.8). It builds on the analysis of host government agreements and contractual risk allocation in Chapter 15 and of investment protection in Chapter 14, and anticipates the treatment of Nigeria's petroleum fiscal regime in Chapter 24 and of damages and enforcement in Chapter 26.

## 16.1 Project Finance Structures

### The Concept of Project Finance

**Project finance** is a method of financing a discrete, capital-intensive asset in which lenders look primarily to the cash flows generated by the project, and to the project's assets and contracts, for repayment, rather than to the general balance sheet of the project's sponsors. It is commonly described as **non-recourse** or, more accurately, **limited-recourse** finance, because lenders usually have some recourse to the sponsors during the construction phase or in specified circumstances, but not general recourse for the repayment of the debt. Project finance contrasts with **corporate finance**, in which a company borrows on the strength of its overall balance sheet and credit rating, and the lenders have recourse to all its assets.

The essential features of a project financing are:

- a **special purpose vehicle** (SPV), or project company, established solely to develop, own, and operate the project, with no other business, so that the project's assets, liabilities, and cash flows are ring-fenced from those of the sponsors;
- **high leverage**, with debt typically representing seventy to eighty per cent or more of project costs, and equity the balance;
- a **contractual structure** that allocates the project's risks to the parties best able to bear them, through the web of contracts examined in Chapter 15 — construction risk to the EPC contractor, operating risk to the operator, fuel supply risk to the supplier, market risk to the offtaker, and political risk to the host government or insurers;
- a **security package** giving lenders security over all the project company's assets, contracts, accounts, and shares, and rights to step in and cure defaults under key project contracts;
- **cash flow controls**, under which project revenues are paid into accounts controlled by the lenders and applied in a strict order of priority — the **cash-flow waterfall** illustrated in Figure 16.1 — with distributions to equity permitted only if financial ratios are satisfied.

### The Rationale for Project Finance

Sponsors choose project finance, despite its higher transaction costs and complexity, for several reasons. It allows sponsors to undertake projects that are large in relation to their balance sheets, without exposing their other assets to the risks of the project. It permits the sharing of risk with lenders and other participants. It may allow the debt to be kept off the sponsors' balance sheets, subject to accounting rules. It enables participation by parties, such as national oil companies, local partners, and infrastructure funds, which could not otherwise finance their share of a large project. And it may reduce political risk, because the involvement of multilateral lenders, export credit agencies, and international banks increases the reputational and financial cost to a host government of interfering with the project — the so-called "halo effect" or "umbrella" of multilateral participation.

For host governments and the public, project finance has advantages and disadvantages. It mobilises private capital for infrastructure that the state could not otherwise finance, and the discipline imposed by lenders' due diligence may improve project selection and design. But the cost of capital is typically higher than for public borrowing, the complexity of the contractual structure creates information asymmetries, and the risk allocation often leaves the state bearing significant contingent liabilities through offtake commitments, guarantees, and termination payments, as examined in section 16.3.

### Historical Development

Modern project finance has its roots in the energy sector. Oil production payments in the United States in the early twentieth century, under which lenders were repaid from the proceeds of oil produced from specific properties, were an early form of limited-recourse lending. The financing of the development of the North Sea in the 1970s, including BP's financing of the Forties field, established the modern model of limited-recourse lending against petroleum reserves. In the 1980s and 1990s, the liberalisation of electricity markets and the emergence of independent power producers (IPPs) in the United States, the United Kingdom, and developing countries — including Pakistan, India, Indonesia, the Philippines, and China — led to the widespread use of project finance for power generation, and the financing of LNG projects, such as those in Qatar, demonstrated its use for the largest energy developments. Since the 2000s, project finance has become the principal method of financing utility-scale wind and solar projects worldwide.

The experience of the 1990s also revealed the vulnerabilities of project finance in emerging markets. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–1998 left several Indonesian power projects with dollar-denominated tariffs that the state utility could no longer afford, leading to the suspension and renegotiation of numerous PPAs and to arbitration, as illustrated by the *Karaha Bodas* dispute examined in section 16.3. The Dabhol power project in India, sponsored by Enron and supplying the Maharashtra State Electricity Board, collapsed in 2001 after the board refused to pay tariffs it considered excessive, generating extensive litigation and arbitration. These experiences shaped the subsequent design of risk allocation and credit enhancement in emerging market energy projects.

### Project Finance in Africa and Nigeria

Project finance has been used for a growing number of energy projects in Africa, including the Lake Turkana wind farm in Kenya, South Africa's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP), and gas-fired IPPs in Ghana, Senegal, and Côte d'Ivoire. In Nigeria, the **Azura-Edo** independent power project, an open-cycle gas turbine plant of approximately 460 MW near Benin City, reached financial close in 2015 and commenced commercial operation in 2018. It was one of the first privately financed IPPs following the privatisation of the Nigerian power sector, and was supported by a PPA with the Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Plc (NBET), a put and call option agreement with the Federal Government, a World Bank partial risk guarantee, and political risk insurance from the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA). The project illustrates both the possibilities and the difficulties of project finance in a market with severe liquidity problems, as payment shortfalls in the Nigerian electricity market subsequently placed pressure on the credit support arrangements. The reform of the Nigerian electricity market under the Electricity Act 2023, examined in Chapters 9 and 24, has significant implications for the creditworthiness of offtakers and the bankability of future IPPs.

### Variants and Emerging Structures

Several variants of the basic project finance model are used in the energy sector.

**Reserve-based lending** (RBL) is a form of limited-recourse finance used in the upstream petroleum sector, under which a revolving credit facility is secured on a portfolio of producing or near-producing fields, and the amount available — the "borrowing base" — is periodically redetermined by reference to the estimated value of the reserves, using agreed price assumptions. RBL facilities have been widely used in the North Sea and West Africa, including by Nigerian independents acquiring onshore assets divested by international oil companies.

**Portfolio financing** and **holding company financing** are increasingly used for renewable energy, where individual projects are too small to justify separate financing, and developers aggregate projects into portfolios financed on a combined basis.

**Green, social, and sustainability bonds** allow project companies and sponsors to raise debt in capital markets for eligible environmental projects. The International Capital Market Association's Green Bond Principles, first published in 2014, provide voluntary guidance on the use of proceeds, project evaluation, management of proceeds, and reporting, and the European Union's **European Green Bond Standard**, established by Regulation (EU) 2023/2631, provides a voluntary standard aligned with the EU taxonomy. **Sustainability-linked loans and bonds**, whose pricing varies according to the borrower's performance against sustainability targets, are also widely used.

**Blended finance** combines concessional finance from public or philanthropic sources with commercial capital to improve the risk-return profile of projects in challenging markets. The **Just Energy Transition Partnerships** (JETPs) announced from 2021 — with South Africa (initially USD 8.5 billion), Indonesia (USD 20 billion), Viet Nam (USD 15.5 billion), and Senegal (EUR 2.5 billion) — combine grants, concessional loans, guarantees, and commercial finance to support the transition from coal, although their implementation has been slower than anticipated, and the United States withdrew from the partnerships in 2025. Just transition is examined further in Chapter 18.

### Environmental and Social Standards in Project Finance

The financing of energy projects is subject to environmental and social standards imposed by lenders. The **IFC Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability** (2012) cover assessment and management of environmental and social risks, labour, resource efficiency and pollution prevention, community health and safety, land acquisition and involuntary resettlement, biodiversity, indigenous peoples, and cultural heritage. The **Equator Principles**, a voluntary framework adopted by more than one hundred financial institutions, the fourth version of which (EP4) took effect in 2020, require participating banks to apply the IFC Performance Standards to project finance and related transactions in non-designated countries, and require climate change risk assessment for projects with significant emissions. The World Bank's Environmental and Social Framework (2018) applies to World Bank-financed projects. These standards function as a form of private or transnational regulation, often more demanding than host state law, and are enforced through loan covenants, independent monitoring, and, in the case of the multilateral institutions, independent accountability mechanisms such as the IFC's Compliance Advisor Ombudsman and the World Bank Inspection Panel. Their legal significance, and the immunity of international financial institutions from suit — addressed by the United States Supreme Court in *Jam v International Finance Corp* (2019), examined in Chapter 7 — are considered further in Chapters 21 and 22.

## 16.2 Debt, Equity, and Risk Allocation

### The Capital Structure

The capital structure of a project-financed energy project comprises equity contributed by the sponsors and debt provided by lenders. The ratio of debt to equity — the **gearing** or **leverage** — is determined by the project's risk profile and the predictability of its cash flows. Projects with long-term contracted revenues from creditworthy offtakers, such as renewable energy projects with government-backed PPAs, may achieve gearing of eighty per cent or more, while projects exposed to market or commodity price risk, such as merchant power plants or upstream petroleum developments, typically have lower gearing. Lenders size the debt by reference to the projected cash flows and the required **debt service coverage ratio** (DSCR) — the ratio of cash flow available for debt service to scheduled debt service in a given period — and other ratios such as the **loan life coverage ratio** (LLCR).

**Equity** is the first-loss capital, bearing the residual risk of the project and receiving distributions only after operating costs, taxes, debt service, and reserve accounts have been funded. Sponsors may contribute equity in the form of share capital or subordinated shareholder loans, which provide tax and repatriation advantages and rank behind senior debt. Lenders typically require that equity be contributed before or pro rata with debt drawdowns, and may require sponsors to provide **equity contribution guarantees** or letters of credit to secure their commitments.

**Senior debt** is provided by commercial banks, export credit agencies, development finance institutions, and capital markets investors, and ranks ahead of all other claims on the project's cash flows and assets. **Mezzanine** or **subordinated debt** ranks between senior debt and equity, and carries higher returns to compensate for its greater risk.

### Sources of Debt

**Commercial banks** have historically been the principal providers of project finance debt, through syndicated loans arranged by lead banks and distributed among participating banks. Regulatory capital requirements under the Basel framework have constrained the willingness of banks to provide long-tenor loans, increasing the role of other sources.

**Export credit agencies** (ECAs), such as the United States Export-Import Bank, the Export-Import Bank of China, China Export and Credit Insurance Corporation (Sinosure), UK Export Finance, Germany's Euler Hermes, and Japan's JBIC and NEXI, provide loans, guarantees, and insurance to support exports of goods and services from their countries, and play a major role in financing energy projects that use imported equipment. The terms of officially supported export credits from OECD members are governed by the **OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits**, a "gentlemen's agreement" that sets minimum interest rates, maximum repayment terms, and minimum premium rates, with sector understandings for specific industries. In 2021, participants agreed to end officially supported export credits for unabated coal-fired power plants, reflecting the influence of climate policy on export finance. Chinese state banks and ECAs, which are not bound by the Arrangement, became major financiers of energy infrastructure in Africa, Asia, and Latin America during the 2000s and 2010s, although China announced in 2021 that it would not build new coal-fired power projects abroad.

**Development finance institutions** (DFIs) — multilateral institutions such as the IFC, the African Development Bank, the Asian Development Bank, and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, and bilateral institutions such as British International Investment, the Netherlands' FMO, Germany's DEG, France's Proparco, and the United States International Development Finance Corporation — provide long-term loans, equity, and guarantees for projects in developing countries, often on terms unavailable from commercial lenders. Their participation provides the political risk mitigation and environmental and social standards described in section 16.1. Under the **A/B loan** structure, a multilateral institution acts as lender of record for a loan whose "B" portion is funded by commercial banks, extending to the commercial participants the multilateral institution's preferred creditor status in practice.

**Capital markets** provide finance through project bonds, which may be issued under United States Rule 144A to institutional investors or listed on international exchanges, and which offer longer tenors and fixed rates but less flexibility than bank loans in the event of restructuring. Institutional investors, such as pension funds and insurance companies, are increasingly significant investors in operational energy infrastructure.

**Islamic finance** is used for energy projects in the Gulf states, Malaysia, and elsewhere, through structures compliant with Shariah principles prohibiting interest (*riba*) and excessive uncertainty (*gharar*), such as *istisna'a* (construction financing), *ijara* (leasing), and *sukuk* (asset-based certificates). Islamic tranches are often combined with conventional tranches in multi-source financings, requiring intercreditor arrangements to coordinate the rights of different classes of lenders.

### The Finance Documents

The principal finance documents in a project financing include:

- the **facility agreement** or common terms agreement, setting out the terms of the loans, the conditions precedent to drawdown, representations and warranties, covenants, events of default, and remedies, often based on the Loan Market Association (LMA) recommended forms for loans governed by English law;
- the **intercreditor agreement**, governing the relationship among different classes of lenders, including voting, ranking, enforcement, and the sharing of proceeds;
- the **security documents**, creating security over the project company's assets, contracts, accounts, and shares;
- the **direct agreements** (also called consent agreements or tripartite agreements) between the lenders, the project company, and the counterparties to key project contracts, under which the counterparties agree to give the lenders notice of defaults and an opportunity to cure them, and permit the lenders to step in and assume the project company's rights and obligations, or to transfer the contract to a substitute entity;
- the **accounts agreement**, establishing the project accounts and the cash-flow waterfall;
- **hedging agreements**, protecting against interest rate, currency, and commodity price risks.

The transition from the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR) to risk-free reference rates, such as the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR) for US dollars and SONIA for sterling, completed for most US dollar settings in June 2023, required the amendment of thousands of project finance documents and hedging agreements.

### Conditions Precedent, Covenants, and Events of Default

Before the lenders advance funds, the project company must satisfy **conditions precedent**, including the execution of all project contracts and finance documents, the grant of all necessary permits and licences, the delivery of legal opinions, the satisfaction of environmental and social requirements, the injection of equity, and confirmation by independent engineers, insurance advisers, and model auditors of the technical, insurance, and financial assumptions. Lenders' due diligence, conducted by legal, technical, environmental, insurance, and market advisers, is extensive and costly.

Throughout the life of the loan, the project company is bound by **covenants**: positive covenants, such as obligations to maintain permits, insurance, and books, and to comply with law and environmental and social action plans; negative covenants, restricting the project company from incurring additional debt, granting security, disposing of assets, amending project contracts, or changing its business; and financial covenants, requiring the maintenance of specified DSCRs and other ratios. **Distribution lock-up** tests prevent distributions to equity unless historical and projected DSCRs exceed specified levels and no default is continuing.

**Events of default** include non-payment, breach of covenants, misrepresentation, insolvency, loss of key permits, termination of key project contracts, expropriation, and failure to achieve completion by a long-stop date. On an event of default, the lenders may accelerate the loan, enforce their security, and exercise step-in rights, although in practice lenders generally prefer to negotiate waivers, amendments, or restructurings rather than enforce, as examined in section 16.8.

### Risk Allocation from the Lenders' Perspective

Lenders evaluate each category of risk and require that it be mitigated through contractual, financial, or insurance arrangements. **Completion risk** — the risk that the project will not be completed on time, on budget, and to specification — is mitigated by the fixed-price, date-certain EPC contract examined in Chapter 15, supported by liquidated damages and performance security, and frequently by **completion support** from the sponsors, such as completion guarantees, cost overrun facilities, or contingent equity, which falls away once the project passes completion tests. **Operating risk** is mitigated by experienced operators, O&M contracts with performance incentives, and reserve accounts. **Supply risk** is mitigated by long-term fuel supply agreements, and for upstream projects by independent reserves reports. **Market risk** is mitigated by long-term offtake agreements, such as take-or-pay PPAs and gas sales agreements, or, in merchant projects, by hedging and conservative debt sizing. **Currency risk** arises where project revenues are denominated in local currency while debt is denominated in foreign currency, and is mitigated by indexing tariffs to foreign currency, hedging, or local currency financing; currency convertibility and transfer risk is mitigated by offshore accounts and political risk insurance. **Political and regulatory risk** is mitigated by government support agreements, stabilisation and change-in-law provisions, political risk insurance, and the participation of multilateral institutions. **Environmental and social risk** is mitigated by compliance with the standards described in section 16.1.

The allocation of these risks is not merely a technical exercise but a negotiation in which the bargaining power of the parties, the state of the market, and the availability of credit enhancement determine the outcome. Where the host government or state-owned offtaker assumes risks that private parties will not bear, those risks become contingent liabilities of the state, to which we now turn.
