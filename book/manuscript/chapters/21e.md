::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 21: A Lithium Mine on Customary Lands (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The state, communities, companies, and events described are fictitious and do not refer to any real country, people, company, or dispute.**

The Federal Republic of Mbandara, a party to the ICCPR, the ICESCR, and the African Charter, but not to ILO Convention No. 169, has discovered substantial lithium deposits on the Kalunde plateau. The plateau is the customary land of the Wasila, a semi-nomadic pastoralist people of around 30,000 who identify as indigenous, whose identity has been recognised in a report of the African Commission's Working Group, and who use the plateau for seasonal grazing, water sources, and sacred sites. Mbandara's Constitution vests all minerals in the state, and its Land Act vests all land in the President in trust for the people, providing for compensation only for "unexhausted improvements" upon revocation of rights of occupancy. Mbandara's government has declared that it does not recognise any group as "indigenous" in the international sense.

The Ministry of Mines granted an exploration licence, and subsequently a mining lease, to Kalunde Lithium Ltd, a subsidiary of Veridian Metals plc, a company listed in London and headquartered in a European Union member state, which has a supply agreement with a European electric vehicle manufacturer. Before the grant, the Ministry held two public meetings in the district headquarters, conducted in the national language, which few Wasila elders speak, and obtained the signature of a "community representative" appointed by the district commissioner. The project will require the relocation of four Wasila settlements, the fencing of 8,000 hectares of grazing land, and the abstraction of significant volumes of groundwater. The company has offered cash compensation for houses and crops and a community development fund equal to 0.5 per cent of revenues. Wasila youth have blockaded the access road; the police have arrested twenty protesters, and two have been injured. The project is seeking financing from a development finance institution that applies the IFC Performance Standards.

**Tasks.**

(a) As counsel to the Wasila Council of Elders, prepare an opinion identifying the rights of the Wasila under international and regional human rights law, the breaches committed by Mbandara, and the forums available, including the domestic courts, the African Commission and Court, and the ECOWAS-type regional court of which Mbandara is a member (assume it has jurisdiction over human rights claims by individuals). Advise on the prospects of a claim against Veridian Metals plc in its home jurisdiction.

(b) As head of human rights at Veridian Metals plc, prepare a memorandum to the board assessing the company's responsibilities under the UNGPs, the OECD Guidelines, the applicable EU and member state due diligence legislation, and the IFC Performance Standards, and recommending a course of action, including whether the project should be paused, how a genuine FPIC process could be conducted, how resettlement and compensation should be redesigned, and how the company should respond to the arrests of protesters.

(c) As adviser to the development finance institution, identify the conditions that should be satisfied before financing is approved.

(d) In groups, design an operational-level grievance mechanism for the project that satisfies the effectiveness criteria of Principle 31 of the UNGPs, specifying its governance, accessibility for a semi-nomadic population, procedures, remedies, and relationship with judicial remedies.
:::

::: summary
- Resource development engages a wide range of state human rights obligations under universal and regional instruments, including the rights to life, health, food, water, housing, culture, and property, and the peoples' rights to dispose of natural resources and to a satisfactory environment under Articles 21 and 24 of the African Charter; the right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment was recognised by the UN General Assembly in 2022.
- *SERAC v Nigeria* established the state's duty to protect against harm by oil companies and gave content to the right to environment; the African system has developed the rights of indigenous peoples in *Endorois* and *Ogiek*.
- Indigenous peoples' rights are protected by ILO Convention No. 169, UNDRIP, and regional jurisprudence, notably of the Inter-American Court; national systems in Canada, Australia, Latin America, and Norway have developed duties to consult, and the concept of indigenous peoples in Africa emphasises marginalisation and attachment to land rather than first occupancy.
- Free, prior, and informed consent requires consultation free of coercion, before decisions are taken, with full information, and through the people's own institutions; its status as a right of veto is contested, but *Saramaka* requires consent for large-scale projects with major impacts, and FPIC is required in specified circumstances by IFC PS7 and the Equator Principles.
- International standards prohibit forced evictions and require resettlement and compensation at full replacement cost and livelihood restoration; Nigeria's Land Use Act provides compensation only for unexhausted improvements, falling short of these standards.
- ILO fundamental principles, now including a safe and healthy working environment, and sectoral conventions govern labour in the energy and extractive industries; forced and child labour in transition mineral and solar supply chains have prompted import bans and due diligence obligations.
- Grievance mechanisms should satisfy the effectiveness criteria of the UNGPs; accountability mechanisms of international financial institutions and OECD National Contact Points provide non-judicial avenues for redress.
- The UNGPs established the corporate responsibility to respect human rights through human rights due diligence, now increasingly mandatory under national and EU legislation; negotiations on a binding treaty continue.
- Victims face significant barriers to remedy; after the narrowing of the US Alien Tort Statute, litigation against parent companies in the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Canada has opened new avenues, alongside regional human rights mechanisms and emerging criminal accountability.
:::

::: research
1. Does the principle of free, prior, and informed consent entail a right of veto? Evaluate the competing positions with reference to UNDRIP, the jurisprudence of the Inter-American Court and the African Commission, and the standards of financial institutions.
2. Assess the application of the concept of "indigenous peoples" in Africa, and its implications for the protection of communities affected by resource development in Nigeria.
3. Evaluate the compatibility of Nigeria's Land Use Act 1978 and the compensation provisions of its petroleum and mining legislation with international standards on resettlement and compensation, and propose reforms.
4. To what extent has mandatory human rights due diligence legislation in Europe improved the human rights performance of extractive companies operating in Africa? Consider the French Duty of Vigilance Law and the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive.
5. Evaluate the effectiveness of transnational litigation against parent companies as a means of securing remedies for communities affected by oil pollution in the Niger Delta.
6. How can the human rights risks associated with transition minerals and renewable energy projects be addressed without unduly delaying the energy transition?
:::
