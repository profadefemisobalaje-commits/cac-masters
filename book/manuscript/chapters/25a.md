## Introduction

Energy has always been entangled with power. The control of coal shaped the industrial and military strength of nations in the nineteenth century; the conversion of the Royal Navy from coal to oil before the First World War, and the struggle for oil in the Second World War, made petroleum a strategic commodity; the oil embargo of 1973–1974 demonstrated the vulnerability of importing states and the power of producers; and the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022 precipitated the most severe energy crisis in Europe in half a century and a fundamental reorientation of global energy flows. The energy transition is reshaping, rather than ending, energy geopolitics: as dependence on oil and gas declines, the control of critical minerals, clean energy manufacturing, technology, and electricity networks is becoming a new focus of strategic competition.

International law operates within, and seeks to structure, this geopolitical context. It establishes the sovereignty of states over their resources and their freedom to determine their energy policies; it constrains the use of force and economic coercion; it regulates trade and transit; it establishes cooperative mechanisms for managing supply emergencies; it protects energy infrastructure in armed conflict; and it provides frameworks for maritime security and cyber security. Yet energy security has also been invoked to justify measures that test the limits of international law — unilateral sanctions with extraterritorial effects, export restrictions, the weaponisation of supply, and attacks on infrastructure. This chapter examines energy security and state sovereignty (section 25.1), the international legal framework of sanctions affecting energy trade (section 25.2), strategic petroleum reserves (section 25.3), pipeline politics and cross-border dependencies (section 25.4), resource conflict and international humanitarian law (section 25.5), maritime security and energy infrastructure (section 25.6), energy supply disruptions and emergency powers (section 25.7), and geopolitical risk and international legal responses (section 25.8). Sanctions compliance by companies has been examined in Chapter 22, and the law of the sea governing submarine pipelines and cables in Chapter 20; this chapter focuses on the inter-state and systemic dimensions.

## 25.1 Energy Security and State Sovereignty

### The Concept of Energy Security

**Energy security** is a contested and multidimensional concept. The International Energy Agency defines it as the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an affordable price, distinguishing **long-term energy security**, concerned with timely investments to supply energy in line with economic developments and environmental needs, and **short-term energy security**, concerned with the ability of the energy system to react promptly to sudden changes in the supply-demand balance. The Asia Pacific Energy Research Centre proposed in 2007 an influential framework of **"four As"**: **availability** (the geological existence of resources), **accessibility** (the ability to obtain them, given geopolitical and infrastructural constraints), **affordability** (prices), and **acceptability** (environmental and social sustainability). Later scholarship has added **resilience** — the capacity of systems to withstand and recover from shocks — and dimensions such as governance and technology. Figure 25.1 relates these dimensions to illustrative legal instruments.

Energy security means different things to different states. For **importing states**, it is principally security of supply — the reliable availability of energy at reasonable prices — pursued through diversification of sources and routes, strategic stocks, domestic production, efficiency, and international cooperation. For **exporting states**, it is principally **security of demand** — stable markets and revenues — pursued through long-term contracts, market management (as through OPEC), and investment in downstream markets. For **transit states**, it involves the revenues and leverage derived from transit and the risks of disruption. For **energy-poor states**, energy security is inseparable from energy access, as examined in Chapter 23. The energy transition is adding new dimensions: the security of supply of critical minerals and clean energy technologies, the stability of electricity systems with high shares of variable renewables, and climate security, including the vulnerability of energy infrastructure to extreme weather.

### Energy Security and Sovereignty

International law affirms the **sovereignty of states over their natural resources** and their freedom to determine their energy policies, as examined in Chapter 1. The principle of **permanent sovereignty over natural resources**, articulated in General Assembly Resolution 1803 (XVII) of 1962 and reflected in common Article 1 of the human rights Covenants, entitles states to control the exploration, development, and disposition of their resources. The Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States (1974) proclaimed that every state has and shall freely exercise full permanent sovereignty, including possession, use, and disposal, over all its wealth, natural resources, and economic activities. At the regional level, Article 194(2) of the TFEU preserves a member state's right to determine the conditions for exploiting its energy resources, its choice between energy sources, and the general structure of its energy supply, while Article 18 of the **Energy Charter Treaty** recognises state sovereignty over energy resources, examined in Chapter 14.

Sovereignty over resources implies the freedom of producing states to decide whether, when, and how to develop and export their resources — including the freedom to restrict exports, subject to their international obligations, notably under the WTO. It also implies the freedom of importing states to choose their suppliers and to pursue energy independence. These freedoms are constrained by international law in several respects: by the prohibition of the use of force and of intervention; by treaty obligations on trade, transit, and investment; by obligations of cooperation, for example under the IEA's International Energy Program and the EU's principle of energy solidarity; and by environmental and human rights obligations. The tension between sovereignty and interdependence is the central theme of the international law of energy security.

### Energy as a Weapon

The use of energy supply as an instrument of coercion — the "energy weapon" — has a long history. The **Arab oil embargo** of October 1973, imposed by Arab members of OPEC against the United States, the Netherlands, and other states supporting Israel in the Yom Kippur War, together with production cuts, quadrupled oil prices and led to the creation of the International Energy Agency. Russia's interruptions of gas supplies to Ukraine in 2006 and 2009, which affected supplies to Europe, and its reduction and eventual cessation of most pipeline gas supplies to Europe in 2022 are more recent examples. The use of energy as a weapon raises questions about the legality of economic coercion under international law. The principle of non-intervention, as articulated in the **Friendly Relations Declaration** (General Assembly Resolution 2625 (XXV), 1970), provides that no state may use or encourage the use of economic, political, or any other type of measures to coerce another state in order to obtain from it the subordination of the exercise of its sovereign rights and to secure from it advantages of any kind. In ***Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua*** (1986), the International Court of Justice held that intervention is wrongful when it uses methods of coercion in regard to matters in which each state is permitted to decide freely, but it found that the economic measures taken by the United States against Nicaragua, including the cessation of economic aid and a trade embargo, did not constitute a breach of the customary principle of non-intervention. The threshold at which economic pressure becomes unlawful coercion remains contested, particularly where supply is cut off in breach of contract or as part of a broader campaign of aggression.

### Energy Security in Africa and Nigeria

For Africa, energy security has distinctive features. Many African states are energy exporters, yet face domestic energy insecurity in the form of low access rates, unreliable electricity, and dependence on imported refined products — the paradox of exporting crude oil while importing petrol, which characterised Nigeria for decades until the start-up of the Dangote refinery, examined in Chapter 24. Energy insecurity in Africa is also linked to physical insecurity: crude oil theft and pipeline vandalism in the Niger Delta, which at times reduced Nigeria's production substantially below its OPEC quota; attacks on energy infrastructure by insurgents in the Sahel and Mozambique; and the vulnerability of electricity grids to vandalism and sabotage. Regional integration through the **West African Power Pool**, examined in Chapter 9, and regional gas infrastructure, such as the West African Gas Pipeline, can enhance energy security by diversifying supply and sharing reserves, while creating new interdependencies. The **ECOWAS Energy Protocol** (2003), modelled on the Energy Charter Treaty, provides a legal framework for investment and transit in the region, though its implementation has been limited.

## 25.2 International Sanctions and Energy Trade

### Scope of this Section

Chapter 22 examined sanctions from the perspective of corporate compliance and contract law. This section examines the international legal framework governing the imposition of sanctions affecting energy trade: their legality under the UN Charter and general international law, their consistency with WTO law and investment treaties, and the controversy over unilateral and extraterritorial sanctions.

### UN Sanctions

Sanctions adopted by the **UN Security Council** under Chapter VII of the Charter are binding on all member states, and under Article 103 of the Charter, obligations under the Charter prevail over conflicting obligations under other international agreements. Energy-related UN sanctions have included the oil embargo on Southern Rhodesia (1966), the comprehensive sanctions on Iraq (1990–2003), the measures against illicit petroleum exports from Libya under Resolution 2146 (2014), the prohibition of trade in oil with the Islamic State and other terrorist groups under Resolution 2199 (2015), the sanctions on Iran's nuclear programme, and the restrictions on North Korea's imports of refined petroleum products and exports of coal. The Security Council has also addressed the role of natural resources in financing conflict, through measures against "conflict diamonds" from Angola and Sierra Leone and natural resources in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, examined in section 25.5. The UN sanctions regime is subject to concerns about its humanitarian consequences, which led to the shift from comprehensive to "targeted" or "smart" sanctions after the experience of Iraq, and to due process concerns about the listing of individuals, addressed in part by the Office of the Ombudsperson for the ISIL and Al-Qaida sanctions regime and, in the European Union, by the *Kadi* judgments of the Court of Justice.

### Unilateral Sanctions and International Law

**Unilateral** or **autonomous sanctions**, imposed by states or regional organisations without Security Council authorisation, are more controversial. Their legality under general international law depends on their character. Measures that are within a state's sovereign discretion — such as the refusal to trade, the suspension of aid, or the freezing of assets of individuals within its jurisdiction — are generally regarded as **retorsion**, lawful acts that are unfriendly but do not breach any international obligation. Measures that would otherwise breach the imposing state's international obligations — such as obligations under trade agreements, investment treaties, or rules on state immunity — may be justified as **countermeasures** under the law of state responsibility, if they respond to a prior internationally wrongful act of the target state and comply with the conditions in Articles 49 to 54 of the ILC Articles on State Responsibility (2001), including proportionality and the requirement that they be directed at inducing compliance. Whether states not directly injured by a breach of an obligation owed to the international community as a whole, such as the prohibition of aggression, may take countermeasures ("third-party" or "collective" countermeasures) is unsettled, although Article 54 of the ILC Articles reserves the possibility of lawful measures and state practice — including the sanctions against Russia since 2022 — has been invoked as supporting such a right.

Many states of the Global South, together with Russia and China, regard **unilateral coercive measures** as unlawful. The UN General Assembly has adopted resolutions condemning such measures, and the Human Rights Council established in 2014 the mandate of a **Special Rapporteur on the negative impact of unilateral coercive measures on the enjoyment of human rights**, whose reports have criticised the humanitarian effects of sanctions, including on access to energy, medicines, and food, and the "over-compliance" by banks and companies that extends their impact beyond their formal scope. The General Assembly has also, since 1992, annually called for an end to the US embargo against Cuba, by overwhelming majorities.

### Extraterritorial and Secondary Sanctions

The **extraterritorial** application of sanctions — particularly US **secondary sanctions**, which threaten non-US persons with penalties for dealing with targeted states even in the absence of any US connection — raises questions of jurisdiction under international law. Jurisdiction to prescribe is generally based on territoriality, nationality, the protective principle, or universality, and the application of sanctions to foreign persons for conduct abroad with no connection to the regulating state has been widely criticised as exceeding these bases. The **European Union** responded to the extraterritorial application of US sanctions against Cuba, Iran, and Libya in the 1990s by adopting the **Blocking Statute** (Council Regulation (EC) No 2271/96), updated in 2018 following the US withdrawal from the Iran nuclear agreement, and the US–EU dispute over the extraterritorial reach of the *Helms-Burton Act* and the *Iran and Libya Sanctions Act* was referred to the WTO before a political understanding was reached in 1998. The extraterritorial sanctions imposed by the United States on companies involved in the construction of the **Nord Stream 2** pipeline, under the Protecting Europe's Energy Security Act of 2019, prompted objections from Germany and the EU. In 2023, the European Union adopted the **Anti-Coercion Instrument** (Regulation (EU) 2023/2675), which empowers the EU to respond to economic coercion by third countries — defined as measures affecting trade or investment to prevent or obtain the cessation, modification, or adoption of a particular act by the EU or a member state — through countermeasures, including restrictions on trade, investment, and access to public procurement.

### Sanctions and WTO Law

Sanctions restricting trade in energy goods may be inconsistent with obligations under the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade**, including the most-favoured-nation obligation (Article I), the prohibition of quantitative restrictions (Article XI), and the freedom of transit (Article V), as examined in Chapter 13. States imposing sanctions typically invoke the **security exceptions** of **Article XXI**, which provide that nothing in the GATT shall be construed to prevent any contracting party from taking any action which it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests, relating to fissionable materials, traffic in arms, or "taken in time of war or other emergency in international relations", or to prevent any contracting party from taking action in pursuance of its obligations under the UN Charter for the maintenance of international peace and security.

In ***Russia — Measures concerning Traffic in Transit*** (2019), the first panel decision interpreting Article XXI, a WTO panel rejected Russia's argument that Article XXI is entirely self-judging. It held that the existence of an "emergency in international relations" is an objective fact subject to review, while the determination of the essential security interests and the necessity of the measures is for the invoking state, subject to the obligation of good faith, which requires that the measures meet a minimum requirement of plausibility in relation to the proffered security interests. The panel found that the situation between Russia and Ukraine since 2014 constituted an emergency in international relations and that Russia's transit restrictions satisfied the requirements of Article XXI. Subsequent panels, including in *United States — Steel and Aluminium Products* (2022), followed this approach, finding that US tariffs justified by national security did not fall within the exception, a conclusion the United States rejected, maintaining that Article XXI is self-judging. The trade measures adopted against Russia since 2022, including the suspension of most-favoured-nation treatment by many WTO members, have been justified by reference to Article XXI. The paralysis of the WTO Appellate Body, examined in Chapter 13, limits the possibility of authoritative resolution of these questions.

### Sanctions and Investment Law

Sanctions may also interfere with foreign investments, potentially breaching obligations under bilateral investment treaties and the Energy Charter Treaty, including obligations of fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation, and free transfer of funds. Many investment treaties contain **security exceptions** or "non-precluded measures" clauses, whose interpretation — including whether they are self-judging — has been addressed in arbitral awards arising from the Argentine financial crisis, such as *CMS v Argentina* (2005) and *LG&E v Argentina* (2006), examined in Chapter 14. The Energy Charter Treaty contains, in Article 24, exceptions for measures necessary for the protection of essential security interests, including those relating to the supply of energy materials and products to military establishments and those taken in time of war, armed conflict, or other emergency in international relations. The wave of claims arising from sanctions against Russia and Russian countermeasures since 2022, including claims by Western investors against Russia for the seizure of their assets and claims by Russian investors against Western states, will test the application of these exceptions.

### Sanctions and Inter-State Litigation

Inter-state litigation has addressed sanctions in several cases. In ***Alleged Violations of the 1955 Treaty of Amity, Economic Relations, and Consular Rights*** (*Iran v United States*), the International Court of Justice indicated provisional measures in October 2018 requiring the United States to remove impediments arising from the reimposed sanctions to the free exportation to Iran of medicines, foodstuffs, agricultural commodities, and goods and services necessary for the safety of civil aviation, and in 2021 rejected the US preliminary objections. In ***Certain Iranian Assets*** (*Iran v United States*, 2023), the Court found that the United States had violated the Treaty of Amity by measures allowing the attachment of the assets of Iranian state-owned companies to satisfy judgments in terrorism-related cases, but held that it lacked jurisdiction over the claims concerning the assets of the Central Bank of Iran. These cases illustrate the potential, and the limits, of treaty-based adjudication of sanctions disputes.
