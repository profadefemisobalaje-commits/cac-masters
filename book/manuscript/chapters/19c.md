## 19.5 Hydropower Development and River Basin Management

### Hydropower in Shared Basins

Hydropower is the form of energy most directly governed by the law of shared waters. Large hydropower projects on international rivers alter the timing and volume of flows downstream, trap sediment, block fish migration, affect water quality, and change the risk of floods and droughts, and their reservoirs may inundate land in neighbouring states. They may also provide significant benefits to downstream states, including flood control, regulated dry-season flows, and electricity for export. The law governing hydropower in shared basins therefore draws on all the principles examined in this chapter: equitable and reasonable utilisation, the obligation not to cause significant harm, prior notification and consultation, environmental impact assessment, and cooperation through joint institutions. Chapter 10 has examined the project-level regulation of hydropower, including licensing, dam safety, resettlement, and the recommendations of the World Commission on Dams; this section examines hydropower at the level of the river basin, through four major basins that illustrate the legal issues.

### The Nile

The Nile basin, shared by eleven states, is the setting of the most prominent contemporary dispute over hydropower on a shared river. The legal regime has its origins in the colonial period. Under the **1929 Exchange of Notes** between the United Kingdom (on behalf of Sudan and its East African territories) and Egypt, Egypt's "natural and historic rights" to the waters of the Nile were recognised, and no irrigation or power works were to be constructed on the Nile or its tributaries in territories under British administration that would reduce the volume of water reaching Egypt, without Egypt's agreement. Under the **1959 Agreement for the Full Utilization of the Nile Waters** between Egypt and Sudan, concluded in connection with the construction of the Aswan High Dam, the two states allocated the entire average annual flow of the Nile, as measured at Aswan, between themselves — 55.5 billion cubic metres to Egypt and 18.5 billion cubic metres to Sudan — with no allocation to the upstream states, which were not parties. Egypt has relied on these agreements and on its historic uses, while upstream states, including Ethiopia — the source of the Blue Nile, which contributes the majority of the Nile's flow at Aswan — reject them as colonial instruments to which they are not bound, invoking the "clean slate" principle of state succession and the principle of equitable utilisation.

The **Nile Basin Initiative**, launched in 1999, sought to negotiate a permanent framework. The resulting **Cooperative Framework Agreement** (CFA), opened for signature at Entebbe in 2010, establishes principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation and the obligation not to cause significant harm, and would establish a Nile River Basin Commission. Egypt and Sudan refused to sign, objecting to the omission of a provision protecting their existing uses and rights, and to the replacement of the obligation of prior notification by a weaker formulation. The CFA entered into force in October 2024 following its ratification by a sixth state, South Sudan, among upstream riparians, but without the participation of the downstream states, so that the basin now has a framework agreement that binds only part of the basin.

Ethiopia began construction of the **Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam** (GERD) on the Blue Nile near the border with Sudan in 2011. With an installed capacity of more than five thousand megawatts, it is the largest hydropower plant in Africa, intended to supply electricity to Ethiopia, where a large proportion of the population lacked access, and for export to neighbouring states. Egypt regards the dam as an existential threat to its water security, particularly during the filling of the reservoir and in periods of drought, while Sudan has expressed concern about dam safety and the coordination of operations with its own dams, while acknowledging potential benefits from regulated flows and reduced sedimentation. In 2015, the three states signed a **Declaration of Principles** at Khartoum, which affirmed principles of cooperation, equitable and reasonable utilisation, and the obligation not to cause significant harm, and committed the parties to agree guidelines and rules on the first filling and annual operation of the dam. Negotiations, facilitated at various times by the United States, the World Bank, and the African Union, failed to produce a binding agreement, and Ethiopia proceeded to fill the reservoir in stages from 2020. The United Nations Security Council considered the matter in 2020 and 2021, encouraging the parties to resume negotiations under the auspices of the African Union. Ethiopia formally inaugurated the dam in 2025. The dispute illustrates the difficulty of reconciling historic uses with the development needs of upstream states, the importance of procedural obligations and agreed operating rules, and the limits of the existing legal framework in the absence of a basin-wide agreement and a mechanism for binding dispute settlement.

### The Indus

The Indus basin, shared principally by India and Pakistan, is governed by the **Indus Waters Treaty** of 1960, concluded with the assistance of the World Bank, which is also a signatory for certain purposes. The treaty allocates the waters of the three "eastern rivers" (the Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej) to India for unrestricted use, and the waters of the three "western rivers" (the Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab) to Pakistan, subject to India's right to make limited uses of the western rivers, including the generation of hydroelectric power through run-of-river plants meeting detailed design criteria set out in an annex. The treaty establishes a Permanent Indus Commission, with a commissioner from each state, and a graduated dispute settlement mechanism: "questions" are addressed by the Commission, "differences" of a technical nature may be referred to a **neutral expert**, and "disputes" may be referred to a **court of arbitration**. The treaty survived several wars between the two states and was long regarded as one of the most successful water treaties.

Several disputes over Indian hydropower projects on the western rivers have been resolved under the treaty's mechanisms. In 2007, a neutral expert largely upheld the design of India's Baglihar project on the Chenab, subject to modifications. The *Kishenganga* arbitration addressed a project involving the diversion of water between tributaries of the Jhelum.

::: casebox {title="Indus Waters Kishenganga Arbitration (Pakistan v India), Partial Award (2013) and Final Award (2013)"}
**Background.** India planned the Kishenganga Hydro-Electric Project, which would divert water from the Kishenganga (known in Pakistan as the Neelum) river, a tributary of the Jhelum, through a tunnel to a power station on another tributary, before returning it to the Jhelum. Pakistan objected that the diversion would reduce flows in the Neelum, interfering with its own planned Neelum-Jhelum hydroelectric project downstream and causing environmental harm, and that India's proposed technique of drawing down the reservoir below dead storage level for sediment flushing was prohibited by the treaty. Pakistan initiated arbitration in 2010 before a Court of Arbitration constituted under the treaty, administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration.

**Partial Award.** In its Partial Award of February 2013, the Court held that the project was a run-of-river plant permitted by the treaty, and that India was entitled to divert water for power generation, but that India was under an obligation to construct and operate the project in such a way as to maintain a minimum flow in the Kishenganga/Neelum downstream of the diversion, in accordance with the treaty and with customary international environmental law. Referring to *Pulp Mills* and *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros*, the Court held that states are required under contemporary customary international law to take environmental protection into consideration when planning and developing projects that may cause injury to a bordering state, and that the treaty should be interpreted in the light of such principles. The Court also held that India could not use drawdown flushing for sediment control at the project, and that the prohibition applied to future run-of-river plants on the western rivers.

**Final Award.** In its Final Award of December 2013, the Court determined that India must release a minimum flow of nine cubic metres per second into the Kishenganga/Neelum downstream of the diversion at all times, balancing India's right to generate hydropower against the need to mitigate adverse effects on Pakistan's hydroelectric use and on the environment, and providing for reconsideration of the minimum flow after seven years.

**Significance.** The awards demonstrate the integration of customary environmental law into the interpretation of a water treaty concluded before the emergence of modern environmental norms, the use of environmental flows as a legal tool, and the capacity of treaty-based arbitration to resolve technical disputes over hydropower on shared rivers.
:::

Subsequent disputes over the Kishenganga and Ratle projects led, from 2016, to parallel proceedings, with India requesting the appointment of a neutral expert and Pakistan the constitution of a court of arbitration, and the World Bank eventually appointing both. India declined to participate in the court of arbitration. In April 2025, following a terrorist attack in Indian-administered Kashmir, India announced that it would hold the treaty "in abeyance", a step without basis in the treaty's provisions, which do not provide for unilateral suspension; the court of arbitration subsequently issued an award holding that the treaty could not be suspended unilaterally and that its own competence was unaffected. The developments placed in jeopardy one of the most durable water treaties in the world and illustrate the vulnerability of water cooperation to wider political conflict.

### The Mekong

The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau in China, where it is known as the Lancang, and flows through Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and Viet Nam. China has constructed a cascade of large dams on the upper Mekong, and Laos — which aspires to become the "battery of South-East Asia" by exporting hydropower to Thailand and other neighbours — has developed numerous dams on the lower mainstream and tributaries, including the Xayaburi and Don Sahong dams. The 1995 Mekong Agreement requires prior consultation for mainstream projects during the dry season, through the Procedures for Notification, Prior Consultation and Agreement, but does not give other members a veto, and the consultations on the Xayaburi and Don Sahong projects were widely regarded as having failed to address the concerns of downstream states and communities about effects on fisheries — which provide the principal source of protein for millions of people — and on sediment flows to the Mekong Delta in Viet Nam. China, which is not a member of the Mekong River Commission, established the Lancang-Mekong Cooperation framework in 2016, and has agreed to share year-round hydrological data. The Mekong illustrates the challenges of governing cumulative effects from multiple dams in a basin where the most powerful state is upstream and outside the principal basin institution.

### The Niger, the Congo, and the Zambezi

In Nigeria, the **Kainji**, **Jebba**, and **Shiroro** hydropower stations on the Niger and Kaduna rivers, constructed between the 1960s and 1990s, were privatised under concession arrangements in 2013. The **Zungeru** hydropower project on the Kaduna river, with an installed capacity of 700 MW, built with Chinese financing, began generating in 2023 and has been placed under a private concession. The proposed **Mambilla** hydropower project on the Donga river in Taraba State, with a planned capacity of over 3,000 MW, has been delayed for decades and became the subject of an ICC arbitration brought by a project developer against the Federal Government. The performance of Nigeria's hydropower stations depends on flows from upstream states in the Niger basin, and the planned dams in Guinea, Mali, and Niger, examined in section 19.4, will affect them; the coordination of reservoir operations through the Niger Basin Authority is therefore of direct importance to Nigeria's electricity supply.

The **Congo** River, with the second largest discharge of any river in the world, has enormous hydropower potential, concentrated at the Inga Falls in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where the proposed **Grand Inga** scheme could provide tens of thousands of megawatts. The DRC and South Africa concluded a treaty in 2013 on the development of the Grand Inga project, under which South Africa would purchase a significant share of the power from the first phase, Inga 3, but the project has faced repeated delays owing to its cost, governance concerns, and the withdrawal of the World Bank in 2016. On the **Zambezi**, the Kariba dam, operated by the Zambezi River Authority, has required extensive rehabilitation of its plunge pool to ensure its structural safety, and severe droughts in 2015–2016, 2019, and 2024 reduced generation at Kariba and Cahora Bassa, causing prolonged power cuts in Zambia and Zimbabwe — illustrating the vulnerability of hydropower-dependent electricity systems to climate variability.

### Basin-Level Planning and Environmental Flows

The experience of these basins suggests several principles for the governance of hydropower at the basin level. First, **cumulative impact assessment** and strategic planning at the basin level are necessary to identify the combination of projects that will maximise benefits and minimise harm, since project-by-project assessment cannot capture cumulative effects. Second, **environmental flows** — the quantity, timing, and quality of water flows required to sustain freshwater ecosystems and the livelihoods that depend on them — should be defined and protected, as recognised in the *Kishenganga* awards and in the Brisbane Declaration on environmental flows (2007, revised 2018). Third, agreed **operating rules** for dams, particularly in periods of drought and flood and during reservoir filling, are essential to avoid disputes. Fourth, **dam safety** requires robust regulation and transboundary cooperation, as tragically illustrated by the collapse of two dams above the city of Derna in Libya during a storm in September 2023, which killed thousands of people. Fifth, financing institutions play a significant role: the World Bank's operational policy on Projects on International Waterways requires that, before financing a project on an international waterway, the Bank must ensure that the riparian states have been notified and have had an opportunity to raise objections, and similar requirements are applied by other development banks, giving financiers influence over the observance of procedural obligations. Finally, the **Hydropower Sustainability Standard**, launched in 2021 by the International Hydropower Association with the participation of industry and civil society, provides a certification scheme for the environmental, social, and governance performance of hydropower projects.

## 19.6 Groundwater Regulation and Aquifer Governance

### The Importance of Groundwater

Groundwater constitutes the vast majority of the world's liquid freshwater, and provides drinking water for around half of the world's population and a substantial share of water for irrigation. In many arid and semi-arid regions, including large parts of Africa and the Middle East, groundwater is the principal or only reliable source of water. Groundwater is used extensively in the energy and extractive sectors: for cooling, for hydraulic fracturing and enhanced oil recovery, for mineral processing, and, increasingly, for the production of green hydrogen in water-scarce regions. Mining and petroleum operations can also contaminate aquifers and lower water tables through dewatering, and the injection of fluids and the storage of carbon dioxide in geological formations, examined in Chapter 12, require the protection of overlying aquifers.

Groundwater presents particular governance challenges. Aquifers are invisible and poorly understood; their recharge may be very slow, so that many aquifers in arid regions contain "fossil" water that is effectively non-renewable; over-abstraction can cause irreversible depletion, land subsidence, and saline intrusion; and pollution can persist for decades. The **International Groundwater Resources Assessment Centre** has identified several hundred transboundary aquifers.

### International Law of Transboundary Aquifers

The UN Watercourses Convention applies to groundwater only where it forms part of a system of surface waters and groundwaters constituting a unitary whole and flowing into a common terminus; it does not apply to "confined" aquifers unrelated to surface waters. To fill this gap, the International Law Commission adopted **Draft Articles on the Law of Transboundary Aquifers** in 2008, which the General Assembly took note of in Resolution 63/124, commending them to states for guidance in bilateral and regional arrangements, while deferring the question of their final form. The Draft Articles affirm the sovereignty of each aquifer state over the portion of a transboundary aquifer located within its territory, to be exercised in accordance with international law; establish the principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation — with specific attention to the recharge of the aquifer and, for non-recharging aquifers, to maximising long-term benefits — and the obligation not to cause significant harm; and set out obligations of cooperation, the exchange of data, the protection of recharge and discharge zones, the prevention of pollution, monitoring, and prior notification of planned activities. The emphasis on sovereignty reflected the concerns of states and was controversial among some commentators, who favoured a stronger emphasis on the shared nature of the resource. The UNECE **Model Provisions on Transboundary Groundwaters** (2012) provide additional guidance.

### Aquifer Agreements and Cooperation

Formal agreements on transboundary aquifers remain few. The **Guarani Aquifer Agreement**, concluded in 2010 by Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay, which entered into force in 2020, applies principles derived from the ILC Draft Articles to one of the world's largest aquifers. In North Africa, Chad, Egypt, Libya, and Sudan established a Joint Authority for the Study and Development of the **Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System** in the early 1990s, and later adopted a regional strategy for its management; the system, a largely non-renewable fossil aquifer, supplies Libya's "Great Man-Made River" project, one of the largest water transfer projects in the world. Algeria, Libya, and Tunisia established a consultation mechanism for the **North Western Sahara Aquifer System**. In West Africa, the states sharing the **Iullemeden** and associated aquifer systems, including Mali, Niger, and Nigeria, have cooperated in assessing the aquifers through projects coordinated by the Sahara and Sahel Observatory. In the *Silala* case, the International Court of Justice addressed a watercourse with a significant groundwater component, confirming the application of customary principles to such systems.

### National Groundwater Regulation

At the national level, groundwater was historically treated as an incident of land ownership in many legal systems, with landowners entitled to abstract water beneath their land with few restrictions — a regime that encouraged over-abstraction. Most modern legal systems now subject significant groundwater abstraction to permits, require the registration of boreholes, and provide for the designation of protected zones. In Nigeria, the Water Resources Act vests control of groundwater in the Federal Government, and the **Nigeria Hydrological Services Agency**, established in 2010, is responsible for the assessment of surface and groundwater resources, but the regulation of borehole drilling, which is widespread owing to the inadequacy of public water supply, remains weak.

In the energy and extractive sectors, specific regulations address the protection of groundwater. In the United Kingdom, the Infrastructure Act 2015 prohibited hydraulic fracturing in protected groundwater source areas; in the United States, the exemption of hydraulic fracturing from the Safe Drinking Water Act's underground injection control program, enacted in 2005, has been controversial, and states regulate well construction, fluid disclosure, and wastewater disposal. Mining legislation and the EIA process typically require the assessment of effects on groundwater, the management of dewatering and acid mine drainage, and long-term monitoring after closure. The protection of groundwater in carbon storage regulation, including under the EU CCS Directive, is examined in Chapter 12.
