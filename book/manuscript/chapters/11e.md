::: comparative {title="Comparative Perspective: Regulatory Models for Nuclear Newcomers"}
Newcomer states have adopted different institutional models for nuclear regulation, each with distinctive strengths and risks.

- **The United Arab Emirates** enacted a comprehensive Federal Law by Decree No 6 of 2009 on the Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy, establishing the Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation as an independent regulator before contracting for its first plant. It renounced domestic enrichment and reprocessing in a cooperation agreement with the United States, brought an additional protocol into force, and acceded to the CSC and the Vienna Convention. The Barakah plant, built by a Korean consortium, entered commercial operation between 2021 and 2024. The UAE is frequently cited as a model of sequencing, establishing law and institutions before construction.
- **Turkey**, under the Akkuyu intergovernmental agreement with Russia (2010), adopted a build-own-operate model in which a Russian-owned project company owns and operates the plant. This model reduces the host state's financial burden but places heavy demands on the regulator's capacity to oversee a powerful foreign operator.
- **Bangladesh**, constructing the Rooppur plant with Russian financing and technology, enacted the Bangladesh Atomic Energy Regulatory Act 2012 to separate regulation from promotion. It has relied heavily on IAEA support and the vendor's regulatory documentation.
- **Ghana** created an independent Nuclear Regulatory Authority by the Nuclear Regulatory Authority Act 2015 (Act 895), separating regulation from the Ghana Atomic Energy Commission. It has established Nuclear Power Ghana as an owner-operator entity and has considered both large reactors and SMRs.
- **Nigeria** separated promotion (NAEC) from regulation (the NNRA) by statute in 1995, well before any decision to construct a power reactor. Commentators have identified the modernisation of the 1995 Act, the strengthening of the NNRA's financial independence, and adherence to a liability convention as priorities before any power reactor project proceeds.

The common lesson is that the legal framework must precede, rather than follow, the commitment to construct. The regulator's independence and competence also depend as much on funding, staffing, and culture as on statutory form.
:::

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise: The Republic of Kesena SMR Programme (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
*This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The Republic of Kesena, the Kesena Atomic Energy Agency, NordReactor Corporation, and all other entities named are fictional.*

The Republic of Kesena is a West African state with an electricity grid of 4,500 MW peak demand. It is a party to the NPT and the Treaty of Pelindaba, and has a comprehensive safeguards agreement with a small quantities protocol but no additional protocol. It is not a party to any nuclear liability convention. Its Atomic Energy Act 1988 establishes the Kesena Atomic Energy Agency (KAEA). The KAEA is responsible for both "the promotion of nuclear energy" and "the licensing and inspection of nuclear facilities", and its Director General reports to the Minister of Energy, who chairs the national nuclear power programme.

The government has signed a memorandum of understanding with NordReactor Corporation (HYPOTHETICAL), a state-owned vendor from a non-African state. Under the memorandum, NordReactor will build, own, and operate two 150 MW(e) SMR units at Port Abeni, 40 km from the capital, and will sell electricity to the state utility under a twenty-five-year PPA. The draft intergovernmental agreement includes the following provisions:

- (a) "The Republic shall recognise the design certification issued by the vendor state's regulator as satisfying all Kesenan licensing requirements relating to reactor design."
- (b) "The Republic shall indemnify NordReactor and its suppliers against all claims for nuclear damage in excess of USD 50 million."
- (c) "Spent fuel shall be stored at the site for the life of the plant. Responsibility for its final disposal shall be agreed by the Parties in due course."
- (d) "NordReactor may, with the consent of the Minister, relocate a third floating SMR unit to Kesenan territorial waters to supply the Port Abeni industrial zone."

Answer the following:

1. Conduct a gap analysis of Kesena's legal framework against the Convention on Nuclear Safety, the IAEA Safety Fundamentals, and the IAEA Milestones Approach. What legislative and institutional reforms should be made before a construction licence is issued?
2. Is clause (a) compatible with the principle that the host state retains responsibility for safety and must maintain an independent and competent regulator? Redraft it to permit reliance on the vendor state's assessment without abdicating Kesena's regulatory responsibility.
3. Advise Kesena on whether to accede to the revised Vienna Convention, the CSC, or both. Evaluate clause (b) in the light of the principles of channelling and the position of potential victims in neighbouring Pelindaba states.
4. Evaluate clause (c) against the Joint Convention, the Treaty of Pelindaba, and the Bamako Convention. Draft an alternative clause addressing spent fuel management, decommissioning, and their financing.
5. What safeguards, security, and law of the sea issues are raised by clause (d)? Should Kesena bring an additional protocol into force and rescind its small quantities protocol before proceeding?

::: summary
- The international nuclear order rests on the bargain of the NPT. Non-nuclear-weapon states renounce nuclear weapons and accept safeguards, in exchange for the inalienable right to the peaceful uses of nuclear energy under Article IV, which must be exercised in conformity with non-proliferation obligations.
- International nuclear law is organised around safety, security, safeguards, and liability, together with waste management and decommissioning. Each pillar has its own conventions, and the IAEA, with its dual mandate of promotion and control, is the central institution.
- The detailed rules of nuclear safety are found in the IAEA Safety Standards. These are not binding as such but acquire legal effect through incorporation into national law, treaty review processes, and Euratom directives.
- The prime responsibility for safety rests with the operator. The Convention on Nuclear Safety requires an effective separation between the regulator and bodies promoting nuclear energy. Fukushima demonstrated that regulatory independence and the continuous reassessment of external hazards are substantive safety requirements.
- Safeguards agreements, strengthened by the Additional Protocol, verify the non-diversion of nuclear material. The cases of Iraq, the DPRK, and Iran show both the importance and the political limits of verification.
- The amended CPPNM, ICSANT, and Security Council Resolution 1540 form the core of nuclear security law. The security of radioactive sources is a significant practical concern, including in the oil and gas industry.
- The nuclear liability conventions channel strict, limited, and insured liability to the operator in a single forum. Their limits, fragmented participation, and the Indian departure from channelling are central issues, and newcomers need liability treaty relations to attract suppliers.
- Deep geological disposal is the accepted solution for high level waste, and Finland leads its implementation. Consent-based siting and secure financing of waste management and decommissioning are critical, as the United States and German experiences demonstrate.
- SMRs and advanced reactors raise issues of licensing modernisation, international harmonisation, emergency planning, transportable reactors, and safeguards by design. They do not relieve newcomer states of the obligation to establish a comprehensive legal and institutional infrastructure.
:::
