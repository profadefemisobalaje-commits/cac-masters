## 12.8 Regulatory Sandboxes and Technological Innovation

### The Regulatory Challenge of Innovation

Energy regulation faces a recurrent dilemma in responding to technological innovation. Rules designed for existing technologies and business models may inadvertently prohibit, disadvantage, or create uncertainty for innovations — such as peer-to-peer trading, community energy, vehicle-to-grid services, local flexibility markets, and novel tariff structures — that do not fit established categories. Yet regulators cannot simply suspend the rules that protect consumers, ensure system security, and prevent the abuse of market power, and they often lack the information needed to design new rules for technologies whose characteristics and risks are not yet understood. This is a variant of the "Collingridge dilemma": at an early stage, when a technology can still be shaped, its impacts are difficult to predict; once its impacts are known, it may be entrenched and difficult to regulate.

Several instruments have been developed to address this dilemma, including **experimentation clauses**, which authorise regulators or ministers to permit deviations from statutory requirements for pilot projects; **innovation funding** schemes, such as the network innovation allowances and competitions administered by Ofgem under the RIIO price control framework; **principles-based regulation**, which sets outcomes rather than prescriptive rules; and **regulatory sandboxes**.

### The Concept of a Regulatory Sandbox

A regulatory sandbox is a framework established by a regulator that allows innovators to test new products, services, or business models in a real-world environment, for a limited period and subject to safeguards, with temporary relief from specified regulatory requirements or with regulatory guidance on how existing rules apply. The concept originated in financial regulation, with the United Kingdom Financial Conduct Authority's sandbox launched in 2016, and was rapidly adopted in the energy sector and elsewhere. The OECD's **Recommendation of the Council for Agile Regulatory Governance to Harness Innovation** (2021) encourages the use of experimental approaches, including sandboxes, as part of agile regulatory governance.

Energy regulatory sandboxes have been established in many jurisdictions. **Ofgem's Energy Regulation Sandbox**, launched in 2017, offers innovators bespoke guidance, comfort, and, where Ofgem has the power to do so, derogations from licence conditions and industry codes for trials, and has supported trials of peer-to-peer trading, local energy markets, and innovative tariffs. **Singapore's** Energy Market Authority launched a regulatory sandbox in 2017. **Germany** adopted the SINTEG Regulation (2017), which permitted participants in the "Smart Energy Showcases" programme to be compensated for certain economic disadvantages arising from trials, and subsequently developed the concept of "real-world laboratories" (*Reallabore*) for the energy transition. In the European Union, the **Net-Zero Industry Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1735) requires member states to establish regulatory sandboxes for net-zero technologies, and the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) requires the establishment of AI regulatory sandboxes, which may encompass energy applications.

::: casebox {title="Regulatory Sandboxing in the Australian National Electricity Market (2022)"}
**Background.** In 2019, the Australian Energy Market Commission (AEMC) recommended the establishment of a regulatory sandboxing framework for the National Electricity Market. The Commission concluded that the existing regulatory framework, which is set out in detailed national legislation and rules, contained barriers to the trialling of innovative technologies and business models. It also found that the regulators lacked the powers needed to grant relief for trials.

**The framework.** Legislation amending the National Energy Laws, enacted in 2022, together with the National Electricity Amendment (Regulatory sandboxing) Rule 2022, established a framework with three components:

- an **innovation enquiry service**, provided by the Australian Energy Regulator (AER), which offers guidance to innovators on how the existing framework applies to their proposals;
- **trial waivers**, which the AER may grant to exempt a trial project from specified provisions of the National Electricity Rules for a limited period, subject to conditions;
- **trial rules**, which the AEMC may make to enable trials that require exemptions beyond the AER's waiver powers, such as exemptions from requirements of the national legislation.

Trial projects must be genuinely innovative and must have the potential to contribute to the achievement of the national energy objective. They must be subject to appropriate consumer protections and must be of limited duration and scale.

**Significance.** The Australian framework is notable for giving sandboxing an express statutory basis, for distinguishing guidance, waivers, and rule-making, and for linking trials to the possibility of permanent rule changes where they succeed. It addresses the principle of legality by ensuring that regulators have clear authority to grant relief from binding legal requirements. That authority was absent from some earlier sandboxes, which could offer only guidance or comfort.
:::

### Legal Issues Raised by Sandboxes

Regulatory sandboxes raise several legal questions.

**Legal basis.** Under the principle of legality, a regulator may waive or modify binding legal requirements only if it has statutory authority to do so. Where a sandbox involves relief from requirements contained in primary legislation, express legislative authorisation is required, as in Australia. Where the regulator lacks such authority, it may be able to offer only guidance, informal comfort, or the non-enforcement of rules — which may provide limited legal certainty for participants and may raise questions about the legitimacy of selective non-enforcement.

**Equal treatment and competition.** The selection of participants for a sandbox, and the grant of exemptions to some market participants but not others, may confer competitive advantages and raise questions of equal treatment, non-discrimination, and, in the European Union, State aid. Sandboxes therefore require transparent and objective eligibility criteria, open application processes, and publication of decisions.

**Consumer protection.** Consumers participating in trials may be exposed to risks — of higher prices, interruption of supply, or loss of the protections they would otherwise enjoy. Sandbox frameworks typically require informed consent, maintenance of core protections (such as protections for vulnerable customers and access to dispute resolution), exit arrangements returning consumers to standard arrangements at the end of a trial, and limits on the number of participating consumers.

**From experiment to rule.** The value of sandboxes lies not only in enabling individual trials but also in informing permanent regulatory change. Frameworks that provide for the systematic evaluation and publication of trial results, and for the consideration of rule changes where trials succeed, are more likely to achieve systemic reform. Conversely, sandboxes that merely grant temporary exemptions without a pathway to rule change may create a cycle of trials without lasting effect.

**Regulatory capacity and capture.** Sandboxes demand significant regulatory resources and close engagement between regulators and innovators, which raises concerns about regulatory capture and the regulator's ability to maintain its independence and impartiality.

### Innovation and Regulation in Developing Countries

Regulatory sandboxes and innovation-friendly frameworks are of particular relevance to developing countries, where new technologies and business models — such as pay-as-you-go solar, mobile money payment for electricity, mini-grids, productive use appliances, and digital metering — have expanded energy access faster than traditional utility models, often operating in gaps or grey areas of the regulatory framework. In Nigeria, the **Nigeria Startup Act 2022** provides a framework for supporting technology start-ups, including through regulatory sandboxes, and financial regulators have adopted sandbox frameworks for financial technology, relevant to the payment systems used in energy access business models. The NERC Mini-Grid Regulations, examined in Chapter 10, are an example of a regulatory framework designed to accommodate a new delivery model with tailored, lighter-touch requirements. Regulators in developing countries face the challenge of encouraging innovation while protecting consumers, ensuring quality, and integrating new models with the expansion of the main grid, and comparative study of the design of energy sandboxes is a promising area for doctoral research.

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise: The Daruna Green Ammonia Export Project (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
*This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The Republic of Daruna, the Port Asaba Special Economic Zone, Solveig Hydrogen AS, and all other entities named are fictional.*

The Republic of Daruna is a coastal West African state with excellent solar and wind resources, limited freshwater, and a national grid in which 70 per cent of electricity is generated from natural gas. Solveig Hydrogen AS (HYPOTHETICAL), a European developer, proposes a USD 8 billion project in the Port Asaba Special Economic Zone. The project comprises:

- 3 GW of solar and wind generation on 40,000 hectares of land, part of which is held under customary tenure by the Ilaro and Kemi communities;
- a 1.5 GW electrolyser;
- a desalination plant;
- an ammonia synthesis plant and export terminal.

The ammonia will be sold to European buyers seeking to qualify it as an RFNBO, including under an H2Global-style tender. Daruna has no hydrogen legislation. Its Electricity Act requires all generators above 1 MW to hold a licence and to sell to the national grid company, and its Water Act vests all water in the state.

Solveig proposes the following arrangements:

- (a) the renewable plants will be connected both directly to the electrolyser and to the national grid, so that surplus electricity can be sold to the grid and grid electricity can be used when renewable output is low;
- (b) a twenty-five-year host government agreement will grant Solveig exemption from the Electricity Act, a ten-year tax holiday, stabilisation of the legal framework, and the right to export all of the ammonia;
- (c) the government will acquire a 10 per cent carried interest in the project company.

Community groups and civil society organisations have argued that the project should supply electricity and water to local communities, which have low rates of access, and that the government should obtain a larger share of the benefits.

1. Identify the legislative reforms required to enable the project, including the classification of hydrogen and ammonia, licensing of production, electricity sector rules, water rights, and land acquisition.
2. Advise Solveig on whether the ammonia can qualify as an RFNBO under Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/1184, given arrangement (a), and on the certification arrangements required. How do the additionality and temporal correlation requirements affect the project's design?
3. Evaluate arrangement (b) from the perspective of Daruna's long-term interests, with reference to the material on stabilisation clauses in Chapter 14 and on resource governance in Chapter 7. Draft alternative provisions that would better balance investor protection and the state's regulatory autonomy.
4. Propose legal mechanisms by which the project could contribute to domestic energy access and water supply and share benefits with the Ilaro and Kemi communities. Consider the risk of "green extractivism" and the requirements of free, prior, and informed consent discussed in Chapter 21.
5. Would a regulatory sandbox be an appropriate instrument for enabling a pilot phase of the project? Explain your answer.
:::

::: summary
- Hydrogen, storage, CCUS, offshore renewables, and smart grids are essential to decarbonisation. Each strains legal categories designed for an energy system with different technologies and boundaries.
- The classification of hydrogen determines the applicable regimes of gas, chemical, and safety law. The EU's 2024 Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package creates a dedicated framework for hydrogen networks, with unbundling and regulated third-party access from 2033.
- The colour terminology for hydrogen is legally imprecise, and law has moved towards carbon intensity criteria. The EU's RFNBO rules require additionality, temporal correlation, and geographical correlation. The US 45V credit adopted analogous requirements before it was curtailed in 2025.
- Certification, through book-and-claim or mass balance systems, underpins regulatory and commercial claims about hydrogen. International harmonisation through ISO 19870 and mutual recognition is incomplete. International trade in hydrogen raises questions under the GATT, the TBT Agreement, the SCM Agreement, and CBAM.
- EU law defines electricity storage as a distinct activity, protects storage owners against double charging, and restricts network operator ownership of storage. FERC Order No 841, upheld in *NARUC v FERC*, opened wholesale markets to storage.
- CCUS is governed by the London Protocol, whose 2006 and 2009 amendments permit sub-seabed storage and its export, and by domestic regimes such as the EU CCS Directive and the US Class VI programme. Pore space ownership and long-term liability are distinctive issues.
- UNCLOS grants coastal states sovereign rights over energy production from winds and currents in the EEZ, subject to obligations regarding installations, safety zones, navigation, and removal. Offshore grids and hybrid projects raise complex cross-border questions.
- Smart grids raise issues of smart metering, data protection, data access, and cybersecurity. Mandatory cybersecurity regulation of energy infrastructure has expanded, as the NERC CIP standards, NIS2, and the response to Colonial Pipeline show.
- Regulatory sandboxes enable controlled experimentation but require a clear legal basis, equal treatment, consumer protection, and a pathway from successful trials to permanent rule change.
:::
