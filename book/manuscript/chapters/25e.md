::: casebox {title="Case Study: Russia — Measures concerning Traffic in Transit (WTO Panel, 2019)"}
**Background.** From 2014, following the events in Crimea and eastern Ukraine, Russia imposed restrictions on the transit through its territory of goods, including road and rail traffic, from Ukraine destined for Kazakhstan and the Kyrgyz Republic, and bans on the transit of certain goods subject to Russian import bans. Ukraine challenged the measures as inconsistent with the freedom of transit under Article V of the GATT and Russia's accession commitments.

**Russia's defence.** Russia invoked Article XXI(b)(iii) of the GATT, arguing that the measures were taken in time of an emergency in international relations and were necessary for the protection of its essential security interests, and that Article XXI is entirely self-judging, so that the panel lacked jurisdiction to review its invocation.

**Decision.** The panel rejected the argument that Article XXI is entirely self-judging. It held that whether a measure is "taken in time of war or other emergency in international relations" is a matter for objective determination by the panel. An emergency in international relations refers generally to a situation of armed conflict, latent armed conflict, heightened tension or crisis, or general instability engulfing or surrounding a state; political or economic differences between members are not sufficient. The determination of what constitutes the invoking member's "essential security interests" is left to that member, but the obligation of **good faith** requires it to articulate those interests sufficiently to demonstrate their veracity, and requires that the measures meet a **minimum requirement of plausibility** in relation to those interests. Applying these standards, the panel found that the situation between Ukraine and Russia since 2014 constituted an emergency in international relations, and that the transit restrictions were not implausible as measures protective of Russia's essential security interests. Russia's measures were therefore justified.

**Significance.** The decision established that security exceptions in trade law are subject to a limited but real degree of international review — a principle with significant implications for energy trade restrictions justified on security grounds, including sanctions, export controls, and transit restrictions. Subsequent panels have applied the same approach, but its authority has been contested by the United States. The case illustrates the tension between the sovereignty of states over security matters and the integrity of rules-based trade, which lies at the heart of energy geopolitics.
:::

## Conclusion

Energy geopolitics is the terrain on which the sovereignty of states over their resources and energy policies meets the realities of interdependence, rivalry, and conflict. International law structures this terrain through the principles of sovereignty and non-intervention, the law of sanctions and countermeasures, the trade and investment regimes and their security exceptions, cooperative mechanisms for emergency stocks and solidarity, the protection of energy infrastructure in armed conflict, the law of the sea, and the emerging law of cyber security. The crises of the 2020s — the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the sabotage of Nord Stream, the attacks on Ukraine's grid and the occupation of Zaporizhzhia, the Red Sea attacks, and the weaponisation of critical mineral supply chains — have tested this framework and exposed its gaps: the contested legality of unilateral sanctions, the uncertain limits of security exceptions, the weakness of enforcement against aggressors, the inadequate protection of undersea infrastructure, and the absence of binding rules on cyber attacks against critical infrastructure. At the same time, they have prompted legal innovation, from the EU's emergency measures and Anti-Coercion Instrument to the ICC's warrants for attacks on energy infrastructure and the IAEA's principles for nuclear safety in armed conflict. For Africa and Nigeria, energy security remains inseparable from domestic energy access, physical security, and regional integration, and the fragmentation of the international order calls for strategic use of the legal tools available. The resolution of the disputes generated by energy geopolitics is the subject of Chapter 26.

::: summary
- Energy security is multidimensional — availability, accessibility, affordability, acceptability, and resilience — and its meaning differs for importing, exporting, transit, and energy-poor states; international law affirms state sovereignty over resources, constrained by obligations of non-intervention, trade, investment, and cooperation.
- UN Security Council sanctions are binding and prevail under Article 103 of the Charter; unilateral sanctions may be lawful as retorsion or countermeasures, but their extraterritorial application and humanitarian effects are contested; WTO panels have held that the security exception in Article XXI of the GATT is subject to limited review for good faith and plausibility.
- The IEP Agreement requires IEA members to hold ninety days of net oil imports and has supported five collective actions; EU law requires oil stocks and, since 2022, minimum gas storage levels; Nigeria and most African states lack adequate strategic stocks; stockpiling is being extended to critical minerals.
- Pipelines create mutual dependence; the history of Russian gas supplies to Europe, ending with the cessation of transit through Ukraine in 2025, illustrates the risks; legal responses include diversification standards, solidarity, regulation of third-country pipelines, investment screening, and transit dispute resolution.
- International humanitarian law prohibits pillage, limits the exploitation of resources in occupied territory to usufruct, protects civilian objects and works containing dangerous forces, and requires proportionality in attacks on dual-use energy infrastructure; the ICJ's DRC v Uganda judgments, the ICC's warrants concerning Ukraine's grid, and the IAEA's principles for Zaporizhzhia illustrate the application of these rules.
- Maritime chokepoints, the Tanker War, the Red Sea attacks, and piracy in the Gulf of Guinea demonstrate the vulnerability of seaborne energy trade; UNCLOS transit passage, regional codes, and national anti-piracy laws provide the legal framework.
- Emergency powers enable governments to respond to supply disruptions, but raise questions of legality and accountability, as in the EU's use of Article 122 TFEU in 2022; force majeure, anti-suit injunctions, and arbitration have followed Russian supply cuts.
- The energy transition creates new geopolitical risks in critical minerals, clean technology supply chains, and cyber security, addressed by industrial policies, mineral partnerships, cyber regulation, and investment screening, in an increasingly fragmented international order.
:::
