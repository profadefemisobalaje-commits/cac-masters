## 9.2 Generation, Transmission, and Distribution Regulation

### Licensing

Most electricity regimes require persons carrying out generation, transmission, distribution, supply, or trading activities to hold a licence (or, in some jurisdictions, an authorisation, permit, or concession) issued by the regulator or the ministry. Licensing serves several functions: it enables the state to verify that licensees have the technical, financial, and managerial capacity to perform their activities safely and reliably; it provides the legal instrument through which conditions — technical standards, tariff rules, consumer protection obligations, reporting requirements, and compliance with codes — are imposed; and it gives the regulator enforcement powers, including the ultimate sanction of revocation.

Licence conditions are typically of two kinds: standard conditions applicable to all licensees of a given class, and special conditions applicable to particular licensees. In Great Britain, for example, the Electricity Act 1989 provides for licences for generation, transmission, distribution, supply, interconnection, and the operation of the electricity system, each subject to standard conditions modifiable by the Gas and Electricity Markets Authority (acting through Ofgem) under statutory procedures. Licence conditions require licensees to comply with industry codes, such as the Grid Code, the Distribution Code, the Balancing and Settlement Code, and the Connection and Use of System Code, which set out detailed technical and commercial rules and are themselves subject to regulated modification procedures.

Under Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023, no person may construct, own, or operate an undertaking for generation (above a specified threshold capacity), transmission, system operation, distribution, supply, or trading of electricity without a licence issued by NERC or, within a state that has established its own market, by the state regulator, subject to the Act's provisions on interstate and national functions. Small generation facilities below the threshold capacity are exempt from licensing but may be required to obtain a permit. The Act empowers the regulator to impose licence conditions, to amend licences, and to suspend or cancel licences for breach.

### Generation Regulation

In liberalised systems, generation is in principle a competitive activity, and regulation focuses on safety, environmental performance, and compliance with technical standards and market rules rather than on prices. Entry into generation is subject to licensing, planning, and environmental permitting, and may be subject to resource adequacy policies (examined in section 9.3). The environmental regulation of generation — emissions limits under instruments such as the EU Industrial Emissions Directive (Directive 2010/75/EU), carbon pricing under emissions trading systems, and restrictions on coal-fired generation — has become a principal driver of the generation mix.

In single-buyer systems, generation investment is determined by the procurement of PPAs, often through competitive tenders conducted by the single buyer or a procurement agency, and the regulation of generation is largely contractual. In vertically integrated systems, generation investment is determined by the utility's integrated resource planning, subject to regulatory approval.

### Transmission Regulation

Transmission is a natural monopoly and is regulated in all systems. Transmission regulation addresses five principal matters.

**Ownership and unbundling.** As in the gas sector, liberalised electricity regimes require the separation of transmission from generation and supply, to prevent discrimination against competing generators. EU law, under Directive (EU) 2019/944, requires member states to apply ownership unbundling, the independent system operator (ISO) model, or the independent transmission operator (ITO) model to transmission system operators (TSOs), and the third country certification provisions apply to electricity as to gas.

**Tariffs.** Transmission tariffs must allow the transmission owner to recover efficient costs and earn a reasonable return, while sending efficient signals for the location of generation and load. Tariff methodologies are subject to regulatory approval, and the allocation of costs between generators and consumers and between locations is often contentious.

**Investment and planning.** The expansion of transmission is essential for the integration of renewable generation, which is often located far from load centres, and for the electrification of the economy. Transmission planning involves the identification of needs, the selection of projects, the allocation of costs, and the obtaining of permits. In the United States, FERC Order No 1000 (2011) required transmission providers to participate in regional transmission planning processes and to adopt cost allocation methods for regional projects, and Order No 1920 (2024) required long-term, forward-looking regional transmission planning over a twenty-year horizon that takes account of factors including changes in the resource mix and policy requirements. In the European Union, the Ten-Year Network Development Plan prepared by the European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) and the Trans-European Networks for Energy (TEN-E) Regulation (EU) 2022/869 identify projects of common interest that benefit from accelerated permitting and eligibility for EU financing.

**System operation.** The system operator is responsible for real-time balancing, dispatch, procurement of ancillary services (such as frequency response and reserves), management of congestion, and emergency actions. System operation may be performed by the TSO, by an ISO, or, in the United States, by regional transmission organisations (RTOs) and independent system operators that operate across the networks of multiple transmission owners. The independence of the system operator from market participants is essential to non-discriminatory dispatch. The establishment of the Nigerian Independent System Operator under the Electricity Act 2023 reflects this principle.

**Reliability standards.** System operators and transmission owners must comply with reliability standards governing the security of the system. In North America, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC), certified by FERC as the Electric Reliability Organization under the Energy Policy Act of 2005, develops and enforces mandatory reliability standards, which became legally binding following the Northeast blackout of August 2003. In the European Union, network codes adopted under Regulation (EU) 2019/943, such as the System Operation Guideline (Regulation (EU) 2017/1485), establish harmonised operational security requirements.

### Distribution Regulation

Distribution networks are natural monopolies regulated by tariff controls, quality of service standards, and connection obligations. The role of distribution networks is changing fundamentally with the growth of distributed energy resources — rooftop solar, small-scale storage, electric vehicles, heat pumps, and demand response — which transform distribution networks from passive conduits delivering electricity in one direction into active systems managing bidirectional flows. Distribution system operators (DSOs) are increasingly required to procure flexibility services, to facilitate the connection of distributed resources, and to coordinate with TSOs. EU law, under Directive (EU) 2019/944, requires DSOs above a specified size to be legally and functionally unbundled from supply activities and empowers them to procure flexibility services through market-based procedures.

### Tariff Regulation

Tariff regulation is the core of economic regulation of electricity networks and, in non-competitive systems, of end-user prices. The two principal approaches, introduced in Chapter 6 in the context of gas, are cost-of-service (rate-of-return) regulation and incentive regulation. In Great Britain, Ofgem's RIIO framework ("Revenue = Incentives + Innovation + Outputs") sets revenue allowances for network companies over five-year price control periods, with incentives linked to outputs such as reliability, connections, customer satisfaction, and environmental performance. In the United States, state public utility commissions regulate retail rates and distribution, while FERC regulates wholesale rates and interstate transmission under the Federal Power Act of 1935, which requires that rates be "just and reasonable" and not unduly discriminatory.

In Nigeria, NERC has regulated end-user tariffs through the Multi-Year Tariff Order (MYTO) methodology, which sets tariffs for each DisCo on the basis of projected costs, including generation, transmission, and distribution costs, losses, and a return on investment. Because full cost-reflective tariffs would have been politically unacceptable, the federal government historically subsidised the gap between cost-reflective and allowed tariffs, accumulating large subsidy obligations. In April 2024, NERC introduced a significant increase in tariffs for customers in "Band A" — those receiving a minimum of twenty hours of supply per day — while maintaining lower tariffs for customers receiving less reliable supply. The service-based tariff structure links tariffs to the quality of supply, and DisCos that fail to deliver the specified hours of supply to Band A feeders must reclassify them, reducing the applicable tariff. The tariff reform illustrates the use of regulatory design to link price increases to service improvements and to address the political economy of tariff reform.

### Federal and State Regulation

In federal systems, the allocation of regulatory competence between federal and state authorities is a fundamental feature of electricity law. In the United States, the Federal Power Act divides jurisdiction: FERC regulates interstate transmission and wholesale sales of electricity in interstate commerce, while states regulate retail sales, distribution, and generation facilities. The boundary has been the subject of frequent litigation. In *FERC v Electric Power Supply Association* (2016), the Supreme Court upheld FERC's jurisdiction to regulate compensation for demand response in wholesale markets, holding that demand response directly affects wholesale rates.[^epsa] In *Hughes v Talen Energy Marketing LLC* (2016), decided the same year, the Court held that a Maryland programme guaranteeing a new generator a rate different from the wholesale market clearing price was pre-empted, because it set an interstate wholesale rate in contravention of FERC's exclusive authority.[^hughes]

Nigeria's constitutional and statutory reforms of 2023 introduced a comparable division. Under the Electricity Act 2023, NERC retains regulatory oversight of interstate and international electricity matters, including the transmission network operated by TCN, system operation by NISO, and trading across state boundaries, while state regulators oversee markets within their states, including distribution and retail supply. The transition raises questions about the treatment of existing DisCo licences, many of which cover multiple states; the determination of tariffs where wholesale costs are regulated federally and retail tariffs by states; and the coherence of technical codes and standards. Several DisCos have established subsidiaries to hold licences in individual states, and the relationship between federal and state regulators will be shaped by the courts and by intergovernmental cooperation.

## 9.3 Electricity Market Liberalization

### The Rationale for Liberalisation

Electricity market liberalisation — the introduction of competition in generation and supply, combined with the regulation of networks and the unbundling of competitive and monopoly activities — was driven by a combination of economic theory, technological change, and political ideology. Economists argued that the natural monopoly characteristics of electricity were confined to networks, and that competition in generation and supply would promote efficiency, innovation, and lower prices. The development of combined-cycle gas turbines, which could be built at smaller scale and lower cost than traditional coal and nuclear plants, reduced economies of scale in generation and lowered barriers to entry. The broader privatisation and deregulation movement of the 1980s and 1990s, and the influence of international financial institutions, spread the model across the world.

### Pioneers: Chile, England and Wales, and Norway

Chile was the first country to liberalise its electricity industry, under the General Electricity Services Law of 1982, which introduced a wholesale market based on marginal cost dispatch and the separation of generation, transmission, and distribution. England and Wales followed with the Electricity Act 1989, which privatised the Central Electricity Generating Board and the area boards and established a mandatory wholesale pool, later replaced in 2001 by the New Electricity Trading Arrangements (NETA), based on bilateral contracts and a balancing mechanism, and extended to Great Britain as a whole in 2005 as the British Electricity Trading and Transmission Arrangements (BETTA). Norway's Energy Act of 1990 introduced competition while retaining substantial public ownership, and the Norwegian market became the nucleus of the Nordic power exchange, Nord Pool, which has expanded to cover the Nordic and Baltic countries.

### The European Union Internal Electricity Market

The European Union has built a supranational internal electricity market through four successive legislative packages: the First Electricity Directive (96/92/EC), the Second Directive (2003/54/EC), the Third Energy Package (2009), and the Clean Energy for All Europeans package (2019), comprising Directive (EU) 2019/944 on common rules for the internal market for electricity and Regulation (EU) 2019/943 on the internal market for electricity, together with Regulation (EU) 2019/942 establishing the European Union Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER). The legislation requires retail competition for all consumers, unbundling of networks, regulated third-party access, independent national regulatory authorities, and the integration of national markets through common rules for cross-border trading, examined in section 9.6.

In response to the energy price crisis of 2021–2023, the European Union adopted a reform of electricity market design in 2024, through Regulation (EU) 2024/1747 and Directive (EU) 2024/1711. The reform retained the marginal pricing model of the wholesale market but sought to shield consumers from short-term price volatility by promoting long-term contracts: it requires that direct price support schemes for new investments in renewable and nuclear generation take the form of two-way contracts for difference (under which the generator receives a top-up payment when the market price falls below a strike price and repays the difference when it rises above it), facilitates power purchase agreements, strengthens consumer rights to fixed-price contracts, and empowers the Council to declare a regional or Union-wide electricity price crisis permitting member states to set regulated prices below cost for households and small businesses.

### The United States

The United States has a patchwork of market structures. FERC Order No 888 (1996) required public utilities owning interstate transmission to provide open-access transmission service on a non-discriminatory basis under an open access transmission tariff, and encouraged the formation of independent system operators. Order No 2000 (1999) encouraged the formation of regional transmission organisations. Today, RTOs and ISOs — including PJM Interconnection, the Midcontinent ISO, the California ISO, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), the Southwest Power Pool, ISO New England, and the New York ISO — operate organised wholesale markets covering around two-thirds of United States electricity demand, while the South-east and much of the West retain vertically integrated utilities. Retail competition was introduced in some states, including Texas, Pennsylvania, Illinois, and much of the North-east, but abandoned or never adopted in others, particularly following the California electricity crisis of 2000–2001, in which flawed market design, market manipulation by traders including Enron, and supply shortages caused soaring prices, rolling blackouts, and the bankruptcy of Pacific Gas and Electric.

### Wholesale Market Design

Wholesale electricity markets may be organised in various ways. In a **mandatory pool**, all generation is sold into a central pool, which dispatches generation in order of bid price and pays all dispatched generators the marginal (clearing) price. In **bilateral markets**, generators and buyers contract directly, with a balancing mechanism operated by the system operator to resolve imbalances. In **power exchanges**, standardised products are traded anonymously on an exchange. Most markets combine these elements, with forward bilateral contracting, day-ahead and intraday auctions on power exchanges, and real-time balancing by the system operator.

A central feature of most wholesale markets is **marginal pricing**, under which all generators dispatched in a given period receive the price bid by the most expensive generator needed to meet demand. Marginal pricing provides efficient short-term dispatch signals, but it means that when gas-fired generators set the marginal price, all generators, including those with low costs such as renewable and nuclear plants, receive the high gas-based price. During the 2021–2023 crisis, this produced very high electricity prices and windfall profits for inframarginal generators, prompting the EU to adopt Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854, which imposed a temporary cap on market revenues of inframarginal generators and a temporary solidarity contribution on fossil fuel companies, and prompting the electricity market design reform described above.

Markets also differ in their treatment of location. **Nodal pricing** (locational marginal pricing), used in most United States RTOs, sets different prices at each node of the network, reflecting transmission constraints and losses. **Zonal pricing**, used in Europe, sets a single price across large bidding zones, often corresponding to national borders, with congestion between zones managed through cross-border capacity allocation. The configuration of bidding zones is politically sensitive; Great Britain considered and, in 2025, rejected the introduction of zonal or nodal pricing in its Review of Electricity Market Arrangements.

### Resource Adequacy and Capacity Mechanisms

A persistent concern in liberalised markets is whether energy-only markets — in which generators are paid only for the electricity they produce — provide sufficient incentives for investment in capacity needed to ensure security of supply at times of peak demand. Many jurisdictions have introduced **capacity mechanisms** that pay generators (and, increasingly, storage and demand response providers) for being available. Capacity mechanisms include capacity markets, strategic reserves, and capacity payments. In the European Union, capacity mechanisms are subject to the State aid rules and to conditions in Regulation (EU) 2019/943, including emission limits that exclude new coal-fired plants. In *Tempus Energy Ltd v European Commission* (2018), the General Court annulled the Commission's decision approving the British capacity market under the State aid rules without opening a formal investigation, on the ground that the Commission should have had doubts about the treatment of demand-side response; the British capacity market was suspended for around a year until the Commission re-approved it following a formal investigation, and in 2021 the Court of Justice set aside the General Court's judgment on appeal.

### Liberalisation in Developing Countries

Electricity sector reform in developing countries, promoted from the 1990s by the World Bank and other development institutions, typically followed a standard sequence: corporatisation of the state utility, establishment of an independent regulator, introduction of IPPs, unbundling, privatisation of distribution and generation, and the eventual creation of a competitive wholesale market. Experience has shown that the standard model often fails to deliver its expected benefits in contexts characterised by tariffs below cost-recovery levels, weak payment discipline, high losses, limited regulatory capacity, and political interference. Scholars and practitioners have increasingly argued for "hybrid" models adapted to local conditions, which may retain significant state roles while introducing private participation and competition selectively. The Nigerian experience since privatisation in 2013 illustrates both the difficulty of implementing a liberalised model in the absence of financial viability and the potential of decentralisation to create new dynamics of reform.
