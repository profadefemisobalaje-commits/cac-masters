## Introduction

The development of energy and natural resources has profound consequences for the people who live where resources are found. Oil fields, mines, pipelines, dams, wind farms, and solar parks require land, water, and the cooperation of local populations; they bring employment, revenues, infrastructure, and access to energy, but they may also displace communities, pollute their environment, disrupt their livelihoods and cultures, and expose workers to danger. Where governance is weak, resource development has been associated with repression, violence, and corruption. The history of oil production in the Niger Delta, examined in Chapters 17 and 24, of mining in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, of hydropower in the Amazon and the Mekong, and of resource extraction on indigenous lands throughout the world illustrates that the benefits and burdens of resource development are often distributed unequally, and that the people who bear the burdens are frequently those with the least voice in decisions.

International human rights law provides a framework for addressing these consequences. It imposes obligations on states to respect, protect, and fulfil the human rights of persons within their jurisdiction, including in the context of resource development undertaken by state enterprises or private companies. Over the past three decades, it has been supplemented by a body of law and practice addressing the specific rights of **indigenous peoples**, including the principle of **free, prior, and informed consent**, and by the framework of **business and human rights**, under which business enterprises have a responsibility to respect human rights and to conduct human rights due diligence — a responsibility increasingly given legal force by national and regional legislation. Victims of abuses have sought remedies in national courts, regional human rights systems, and non-judicial mechanisms, including through transnational litigation against parent companies in their home states.

This chapter examines the human rights obligations engaged by resource development (section 21.1), indigenous peoples' rights and the duty to consult (section 21.2), free, prior, and informed consent (section 21.3), land acquisition, resettlement, and compensation (section 21.4), labour rights and occupational safety (section 21.5), community grievance mechanisms (section 21.6), business and human rights due diligence (section 21.7), and judicial and non-judicial remedies (section 21.8). Chapter 22 examines corporate governance, compliance, and anti-corruption, including the governance dimensions of due diligence, and Chapter 23 examines energy justice and the equitable distribution of resource benefits.

## 21.1 Human Rights Obligations in Resource Development

### The Sources of Obligation

The human rights obligations of states engaged by resource development derive from universal treaties, regional treaties, and customary international law. The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights** (ICCPR) protects, among other rights, the right to life (Article 6), freedom from torture and inhuman treatment (Article 7), the right to liberty and security of the person (Article 9), the right to privacy and home (Article 17), freedoms of expression, assembly, and association (Articles 19, 21, and 22), the right to participate in public affairs (Article 25), and the rights of members of ethnic, religious, and linguistic minorities to enjoy their own culture (Article 27). The **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights** (ICESCR) protects the rights to work and to just and favourable conditions of work (Articles 6 and 7), to an adequate standard of living, including adequate food, clothing, and housing (Article 11), to the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health, including through the improvement of environmental and industrial hygiene (Article 12), and to take part in cultural life (Article 15). Common Article 1 of both Covenants recognises the right of all peoples to self-determination, including the right freely to dispose of their natural wealth and resources, and provides that in no case may a people be deprived of its own means of subsistence — a provision linked to the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources examined in Chapter 1.

Other universal instruments relevant to resource development include the **International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination** (ICERD), whose monitoring committee has addressed the impact of extractive projects on indigenous peoples; the **Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women**; the **Convention on the Rights of the Child**; and the conventions of the International Labour Organization examined in section 21.5.

### The African Charter

The **African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights** (1981), to which all members of the African Union except Morocco are parties and which Nigeria has incorporated into domestic law by the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (Ratification and Enforcement) Act, is distinctive in protecting collective "peoples' rights" alongside individual rights. **Article 21** provides that all peoples shall freely dispose of their wealth and natural resources, that this right shall be exercised in the exclusive interest of the people, and that in no case shall a people be deprived of it; that in case of spoliation, the dispossessed people shall have the right to lawful recovery of its property and adequate compensation; and that states undertake to eliminate all forms of foreign economic exploitation, particularly that practised by international monopolies, so as to enable their peoples to benefit fully from the advantages derived from their national resources. **Article 24** provides that all peoples shall have the right to a general satisfactory environment favourable to their development — the first binding recognition of a right to environment in an international human rights treaty. Article 22 recognises the right of all peoples to their economic, social, and cultural development.

::: casebox {title="SERAC v Nigeria: Oil, Rights, and the Ogoni People"}
**Social and Economic Rights Action Center and Center for Economic and Social Rights v Nigeria (African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, Communication 155/96, decided October 2001).**

**Facts.** The communication alleged that the military government of Nigeria had been directly involved in oil production through the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation, the majority shareholder in a consortium with Shell Petroleum Development Company, and that their operations had caused environmental degradation and health problems among the Ogoni people through the contamination of soil, water, and air. It further alleged that the government had failed to monitor operations, to require environmental and social impact assessments, and to provide information to communities, and that its security forces had attacked, burned, and destroyed Ogoni villages and homes in response to non-violent protests led by the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People.

**Decision.** The Commission found violations of Articles 2, 4, 14, 16, 18(1), 21, and 24 of the African Charter. It held that states have duties to **respect, protect, promote, and fulfil** all rights, and that Nigeria had violated its duty to protect by failing to protect the Ogoni from harmful acts of private parties, in particular the oil companies. It held that Article 24 imposes obligations on states to take reasonable measures to prevent pollution and ecological degradation, to promote conservation, and to secure ecologically sustainable development and use of natural resources, including by ordering independent scientific monitoring, requiring environmental and social impact studies before industrial development, providing information to affected communities, and giving them meaningful opportunities to be heard. It found a violation of Article 21 because the government had given the green light to private actors, and the oil companies in particular, to devastatingly affect the wellbeing of the Ogonis. Reading the rights to health, property, and family protection together, it found implied rights to **food** and **housing**. It appealed to the government to stop all attacks on Ogoni communities, conduct an investigation, compensate victims, ensure environmental and social impact assessments for future operations, and undertake a comprehensive clean-up.

**Significance.** *SERAC* is a landmark in the protection of economic, social, and cultural rights and in the application of human rights law to extractive industries. It established the state's duty to protect against harm by companies, gave content to the right to a satisfactory environment, and has been widely cited by courts and commentators. Its recommendations on clean-up resonate with the UNEP Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland (2011) and the subsequent Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project, examined in Chapter 17.
:::

The African Commission and the **African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights** have developed this jurisprudence in subsequent cases. In ***Centre for Minority Rights Development and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of the Endorois Welfare Council v Kenya*** (2010), the Commission found that the eviction of the Endorois from their ancestral lands around Lake Bogoria for the creation of a game reserve, without adequate consultation or compensation, violated their rights to property, religion, culture, natural resources, and development, and held that the right to development requires that development be both equitable and participatory and that, for development projects with a major impact, the state must obtain the free, prior, and informed consent of the community. In ***African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights v Kenya*** (the *Ogiek* case, 2017), the African Court held that the eviction of the Ogiek from the Mau Forest violated their rights, including under Articles 14, 21, and 22, and in its reparations judgment of 2022 ordered Kenya to take measures to delimit, demarcate, and title Ogiek ancestral land and to consult the Ogiek on development projects in that land. The **ECOWAS Community Court of Justice**, which has jurisdiction over human rights cases brought by individuals against ECOWAS member states without a requirement to exhaust domestic remedies, held in ***SERAP v Nigeria*** (2012) that Nigeria had violated Articles 1 and 24 of the African Charter by failing to prevent and remedy oil pollution in the Niger Delta and to hold perpetrators accountable.

### The Right to a Clean, Healthy, and Sustainable Environment

The recognition of a human right to a healthy environment has advanced rapidly. Beyond Article 24 of the African Charter, the right is recognised in the **San Salvador Protocol** to the American Convention on Human Rights, the **Arab Charter on Human Rights**, the **Aarhus Convention** (in its procedural dimensions), and the **Escazú Agreement** (2018), the first regional environmental treaty in Latin America and the Caribbean, which also contains specific provisions on the protection of environmental human rights defenders. More than a hundred and fifty states recognise the right in their constitutions, legislation, or regional treaties. In October 2021, the UN Human Rights Council adopted **Resolution 48/13**, recognising the right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment, and in July 2022, the UN General Assembly adopted **Resolution 76/300** to the same effect by 161 votes to none, with eight abstentions. Although these resolutions are not binding, they reflect a broad consensus and have been relied upon by courts and in the advisory opinions on climate change examined in Chapter 18. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights, in its **Advisory Opinion OC-23/17** (2017), recognised the right to a healthy environment as an autonomous right under the American Convention, and in ***Lhaka Honhat v Argentina*** (2020), it found for the first time a violation of the right to a healthy environment in a contentious case, together with the rights to food, water, and cultural identity of indigenous communities.

The **Constitution of Nigeria** includes environmental protection among the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy in section 20, which are not directly justiciable under section 6(6)(c), but Nigerian courts have interpreted the rights to life and dignity in Chapter IV of the Constitution and the African Charter to protect against environmental harm. In ***Gbemre v Shell Petroleum Development Company of Nigeria Ltd*** (Federal High Court, Benin, 2005), the court held that gas flaring in the applicant's community violated the rights to life and dignity of the human person under sections 33 and 34 of the Constitution and Articles 4, 16, and 24 of the African Charter, and ordered that flaring be stopped — an order that was not complied with. The decision, although of limited precedential weight, is often cited as an early example of rights-based environmental litigation.

### The Tripartite Typology of Obligations

Human rights treaty bodies and regional mechanisms have articulated a tripartite typology of state obligations: to **respect** rights, by refraining from interfering with their enjoyment; to **protect** rights, by preventing, investigating, punishing, and redressing their violation by third parties, including business enterprises; and to **fulfil** rights, by taking positive measures to facilitate and provide for their enjoyment. In resource development, the obligation to respect is engaged when the state, or a state-owned enterprise, directly causes harm — for example, through forced evictions or violent responses to protests. The obligation to protect is engaged when private companies cause harm, requiring the state to regulate, monitor, and enforce, as in *SERAC*. The obligation to fulfil may require the state to ensure that resource revenues are used for the realisation of rights, as examined in Chapter 23.

### Extraterritorial Obligations

Whether states have human rights obligations with respect to the conduct of companies domiciled in their territory that cause harm abroad is a contested question of importance for the regulation of multinational extractive enterprises. The **Maastricht Principles on Extraterritorial Obligations of States in the area of Economic, Social and Cultural Rights** (2011), adopted by experts, articulate obligations to regulate companies over which states are in a position to exercise influence. The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, in **General Comment No. 24** (2017) on state obligations in the context of business activities, stated that states parties are required to take the necessary steps to prevent human rights violations abroad by corporations domiciled in their territory or jurisdiction, without infringing the sovereignty of host states. The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, examined in section 21.7, take a more cautious position, stating that states are not generally required under international human rights law to regulate the extraterritorial activities of businesses domiciled in their territory, but are not generally prohibited from doing so, and that they should set out clearly the expectation that such businesses respect human rights throughout their operations. The mandatory due diligence legislation and transnational litigation examined in sections 21.7 and 21.8 give practical effect to home state regulation.

### Human Rights Defenders and Civic Space

Resource development is associated with serious risks to **environmental and land defenders**. Global Witness has recorded the killing of more than two thousand land and environmental defenders worldwide between 2012 and 2023, many in connection with mining, agribusiness, hydropower, and logging, with the largest numbers in Latin America and the Philippines. The execution of Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight other Ogoni leaders by the Nigerian military government in November 1995, following a trial widely condemned as unfair, remains one of the most prominent cases connecting resource development with the suppression of dissent; it led to Nigeria's suspension from the Commonwealth and, later, to litigation in the United States examined in section 21.8. The **UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders** (1998), the Escazú Agreement, and the guidance of the UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights defenders require states to protect defenders, and companies are expected under the UNGPs to avoid contributing to attacks on them. The **Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights** (2000), a multi-stakeholder initiative of governments, extractive companies, and non-governmental organisations, provide guidance on risk assessment and on the relationship between companies and public and private security forces, an issue of particular importance in conflict-affected areas such as the Niger Delta and the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo.

## 21.2 Indigenous Peoples' Rights and Consultation

### Who Are Indigenous Peoples?

International law contains no formal definition of indigenous peoples. The working definition proposed by José Martínez Cobo in his study for the UN Sub-Commission in the 1980s emphasised historical continuity with pre-invasion and pre-colonial societies, distinctiveness from other sectors of society, non-dominance, and a determination to preserve, develop, and transmit ancestral territories and ethnic identity. ILO Convention No. 169 applies to tribal peoples whose social, cultural, and economic conditions distinguish them from other sections of the national community, and to peoples regarded as indigenous on account of their descent from the populations which inhabited the country at the time of conquest or colonisation, and treats **self-identification** as a fundamental criterion. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples deliberately does not define indigenous peoples.

In Africa, where most of the population can claim to be "indigenous" in the sense of pre-colonial descent, the concept has been contested. The African Commission's **Working Group on Indigenous Populations/Communities**, in its report adopted in 2005, emphasised that the concept in Africa is not about first occupancy but about groups whose cultures and ways of life differ considerably from the dominant society, which are often marginalised and discriminated against, and which have a special attachment to and use of their traditional land and territories — such as hunter-gatherers and pastoralists. The Commission's **Advisory Opinion on the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples** (2007) sought to address the concerns of African states that recognising indigenous peoples would threaten national unity. The *Endorois* and *Ogiek* decisions applied the concept to particular communities in Kenya. In Nigeria, the federal government has generally taken the position that all Nigerians are indigenous, and the claims of groups such as the Ogoni and the Ijaw have been framed principally in terms of minority rights, peoples' rights under the African Charter, and resource control, rather than indigenous rights in the international sense — although the Ogoni Bill of Rights of 1990 invoked self-determination and the right to control a fair share of the resources of their land.

### ILO Convention No. 169 and UNDRIP

The **ILO Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989 (No. 169)** is the only binding international treaty devoted specifically to indigenous peoples' rights. It has been ratified by around twenty-four states, most of them in Latin America, together with Denmark, Norway, Spain, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Germany, Fiji, Nepal, and the Central African Republic, the only African state party. It requires governments to **consult** the peoples concerned, through appropriate procedures and in particular through their representative institutions, whenever consideration is being given to legislative or administrative measures which may affect them directly, in good faith and with the objective of achieving agreement or consent (Article 6). It recognises rights of ownership and possession over the lands which they traditionally occupy (Article 14), and provides that where the state retains ownership of mineral or subsurface resources, governments must establish procedures to consult the peoples before undertaking or permitting any programmes for the exploration or exploitation of such resources, and that the peoples shall wherever possible participate in the benefits and receive fair compensation for any damages (Article 15). Relocation may take place only with their free and informed consent or, where consent cannot be obtained, following appropriate procedures (Article 16).

The **UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples** (UNDRIP), adopted by the General Assembly in September 2007 by 144 votes to four (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States), with eleven abstentions, sets out a comprehensive catalogue of rights, including the rights to self-determination (Article 3), to autonomy or self-government in internal and local affairs (Article 4), to lands, territories, and resources which they have traditionally owned, occupied, or otherwise used or acquired (Article 26), to redress for lands taken without their free, prior, and informed consent (Article 28), and to determine and develop priorities and strategies for the development or use of their lands or territories and other resources (Article 32). The four states that voted against later endorsed the Declaration. Although UNDRIP is not a treaty, some of its provisions are regarded as reflecting customary international law or general principles, and it has been relied upon by courts and treaty bodies. Several states have given it domestic legal effect — notably **Canada**, through the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act 2021 at the federal level and the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act 2019 in British Columbia, and **Bolivia**, which incorporated the Declaration into law in 2007.

### The Inter-American System

The Inter-American Court of Human Rights has developed the most extensive jurisprudence on indigenous land and resource rights, interpreting the right to property in Article 21 of the American Convention to protect the communal property of indigenous and tribal peoples. In ***Mayagna (Sumo) Awas Tingni Community v Nicaragua*** (2001), the Court held that the grant of a logging concession on indigenous land without demarcation or consultation violated the community's right to property. In ***Saramaka People v Suriname*** (2007), examined in section 21.3, it held that the state must consult and, for large-scale projects, obtain the consent of tribal peoples before granting concessions affecting their territories. In ***Kichwa Indigenous People of Sarayaku v Ecuador*** (2012), it held that Ecuador had violated the rights to consultation, property, and cultural identity by permitting an oil company to undertake seismic exploration, including the placement of explosives, on Sarayaku territory without prior consultation, and described the obligation to consult as a general principle of international law. In *Kaliña and Lokono Peoples v Suriname* (2015), it addressed the impact of bauxite mining and protected areas on indigenous lands.

### The Duty to Consult in National Law: Comparative Perspectives

Several national legal systems have developed a duty to consult indigenous peoples on resource development.

**Canada.** Section 35 of the Constitution Act 1982 recognises and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. In ***Haida Nation v British Columbia (Minister of Forests)*** (2004), the Supreme Court of Canada held that the Crown has a duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Aboriginal peoples when it has knowledge, real or constructive, of the potential existence of an Aboriginal right or title and contemplates conduct that might adversely affect it. The duty, grounded in the honour of the Crown, varies with the strength of the claim and the seriousness of the potential impact, along a spectrum from notice to deep consultation. In ***Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia*** (2014), the Court for the first time recognised Aboriginal title to a specific area and held that, once title is established, the Crown must obtain the consent of the titleholders or justify an infringement under a stringent test. In ***Clyde River (Hamlet) v Petroleum Geo-Services Inc*** (2017), the Court quashed an authorisation for offshore seismic testing in Nunavut because the regulator's consultation had been inadequate, while in the companion case of *Chippewas of the Thames First Nation v Enbridge Pipelines Inc* (2017), it upheld the approval of a pipeline modification, finding consultation adequate. The Federal Court of Appeal quashed the approval of the **Trans Mountain Expansion** pipeline in *Tsleil-Waututh Nation v Canada* (2018) for inadequate consultation; after renewed consultation, the project was re-approved, the renewed approval was upheld in 2020, and the pipeline entered service in 2024.

**Australia.** In ***Mabo v Queensland (No 2)*** (1992), the High Court rejected the doctrine of *terra nullius* and recognised native title. The **Native Title Act 1993** provides for the recognition of native title and gives registered native title claimants and holders a **right to negotiate** over the grant of mining tenements, with recourse to the National Native Title Tribunal in the absence of agreement, and provides for **Indigenous Land Use Agreements**. In *Northern Territory v Griffiths* (the *Timber Creek* case, 2019), the High Court addressed the assessment of compensation for the extinguishment of native title, including for cultural loss.

**Latin America.** Many Latin American states have enacted prior consultation laws or developed constitutional jurisprudence. **Peru**'s Law on the Right to Prior Consultation of Indigenous Peoples (2011) requires consultation on measures affecting indigenous peoples, although it does not require consent. The **Constitutional Court of Colombia** has developed extensive jurisprudence, holding in Judgment T-129 of 2011 that consent is required for projects that would involve displacement, the storage of toxic waste, or high social, cultural, and environmental impacts threatening the community's survival.

**Norway.** In the ***Fosen*** case, decided in October 2021 and examined in Chapter 10, the Supreme Court of Norway held that the licences for two wind farms on the Fosen peninsula violated the rights of Sámi reindeer herders under Article 27 of the ICCPR, rendering the licences invalid; agreements between the herders and the developers on continued operation, with compensation and mitigation, were concluded in 2023 and 2024 after prolonged protest. The case demonstrates that renewable energy projects, no less than fossil fuel projects, must respect indigenous rights.
