::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 22: A Compliance Crisis in an Offshore Joint Venture (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The companies, persons, states, and events described are fictitious and do not refer to any real company, individual, country, or matter.**

Harrowgate Energy plc is an oil and gas company incorporated in England, with shares listed in London and American depositary receipts listed in New York. It holds a forty per cent non-operated interest in an offshore production sharing contract in the Republic of Tessaly, alongside Tessaly's national oil company (thirty per cent) and Bluewater Petroleum Inc, a privately owned company incorporated in a third state, which is the operator (thirty per cent). Tessaly is a party to UNCAC and has a statutory host community trust regime and local content legislation requiring that a specified proportion of contracts be awarded to Tessalian-owned companies.

Harrowgate's regional finance manager has reported anonymously through the company's speak-up hotline that: (i) Bluewater has awarded a USD 90 million logistics contract to Coastline Services Ltd, a recently incorporated Tessalian company with no track record, whose shareholders, according to the corporate registry, are two nominee companies in a secrecy jurisdiction; (ii) a local newspaper has reported that the brother-in-law of Tessaly's deputy minister of petroleum is a director of Coastline; (iii) Harrowgate's share of the contract costs has been charged to the joint account and paid; (iv) the joint venture has made a "community development contribution" of USD 4 million to a foundation chaired by the governor of the coastal province, outside the statutory host community trust; and (v) one of the joint venture's support vessels, chartered from a third party, appears from vessel tracking data to have conducted a ship-to-ship transfer of fuel with a tanker designated by OFAC as part of a sanctions evasion network.

Harrowgate's board has also received a letter from an investor coalition demanding that it explain how its board oversees climate risk, after Harrowgate announced a new offshore exploration programme, and asking it to submit its transition plan to an advisory shareholder vote.

**Tasks.**

(a) As general counsel of Harrowgate, prepare a memorandum to the board identifying the potential legal exposures of the company and its directors under the UK Bribery Act 2010, the FCPA (including its accounting provisions), the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023, UK and US sanctions law, and Tessalian law, and the contractual rights of Harrowgate under the joint operating agreement (assume a standard agreement containing audit rights and anti-corruption covenants).

(b) Design an internal investigation plan, addressing who should oversee and conduct the investigation, its scope, the preservation of documents, interviews (including of employees seconded to the operator), legal privilege in the relevant jurisdictions, data protection, and the handling of the whistleblower.

(c) Advise the board on whether, when, and to whom Harrowgate should self-report, weighing the incentives under the policies of the relevant authorities against the risks.

(d) Prepare a short paper for the board on the remediation of Harrowgate's compliance programme, with particular reference to non-operated joint ventures, local content partners, community investments, and maritime sanctions risks.

(e) Draft a response to the investor coalition explaining how the board oversees climate risk and setting out the considerations relevant to the decision whether to hold a "say on climate" vote, with reference to the directors' duties under sections 172 and 174 of the Companies Act 2006.
:::

::: summary
- Corporate governance addresses the direction and control of companies; energy enterprises face distinctive challenges arising from state ownership, joint ventures, and high-risk operating environments. The OECD Guidelines on SOEs and the Santiago Principles provide benchmarks for state enterprises, and the PIA transformed NNPC into a limited liability company.
- Directors owe duties to promote the success of the company and to exercise care, skill, and diligence, including the oversight of compliance and mission-critical risks; climate risk must be considered, but courts defer to directors' business judgment, as *ClientEarth v Shell* shows.
- The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, UNCAC, the AU Convention, and national laws, including the FCPA, the UK Bribery Act's failure to prevent offence, France's Sapin II, and Nigeria's ICPC and EFCC legislation, prohibit corruption; the extractive sector accounts for the largest share of foreign bribery cases, illustrated by the Bonny Island LNG and commodity trading cases.
- Beneficial ownership transparency, required by FATF standards, the EITI Standard, and national registers such as Nigeria's under CAMA 2020, and payments-to-governments reporting address the secrecy that facilitates corruption.
- UN and autonomous sanctions, including the unprecedented sanctions on Russian energy since 2022, and export controls, including on critical minerals, require rigorous compliance and raise difficult contractual issues.
- ESG disclosure has moved from voluntary frameworks to mandatory regimes, including the ISSB standards, adopted by Nigeria, and the EU CSRD; greenwashing is a growing source of legal risk.
- Whistleblower protection, internal reporting channels, and properly conducted investigations, with attention to legal privilege, are central to detecting misconduct; self-reporting and cooperation may lead to negotiated resolutions such as DPAs.
- Effective compliance programmes, assessed by authorities on whether they are well designed, resourced, and effective in practice, comprise governance, risk assessment, policies, third-party due diligence, training, speak-up channels, investigation and discipline, and monitoring and review.
:::

::: research
1. Has the transformation of NNPC into a limited liability company under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 improved its governance and accountability? Evaluate against the OECD Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises.
2. To what extent do directors' duties under English and Nigerian law require directors of energy companies to align corporate strategy with the goals of the Paris Agreement?
3. Evaluate the effectiveness of extraterritorial anti-bribery enforcement by home states in addressing corruption in the Nigerian petroleum sector, and consider whether penalties should be shared with or returned to the states whose officials were bribed.
4. Assess the contribution of NEITI and beneficial ownership disclosure to the reduction of corruption in Nigeria's extractive sector.
5. How should energy companies and their lawyers manage conflicts between sanctions regimes, blocking statutes, and contractual obligations? Evaluate with reference to recent English case law.
6. Does the global baseline of the ISSB standards adequately serve the needs of developing countries such as Nigeria, or is a double materiality approach preferable for extractive industries?
:::
