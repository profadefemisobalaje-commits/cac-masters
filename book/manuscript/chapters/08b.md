## 8.2 Lithium, Cobalt, Nickel, Copper, and Rare Earth Elements

Each of the principal energy transition minerals has a distinctive geology, market structure, geography of production, and set of legal issues. This section examines five of the most significant.

### Lithium

**Geology and production.** Lithium is produced from two principal types of deposit: hard-rock deposits, principally spodumene-bearing pegmatites mined in Australia (the world's largest producer), Zimbabwe, Nigeria, Brazil, and China; and brine deposits in the salt flats (*salares*) of the "lithium triangle" of Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile, from which lithium-rich brine is pumped into evaporation ponds and concentrated over many months. Direct lithium extraction technologies, which extract lithium from brine without evaporation ponds, are being developed and may reduce water consumption and environmental impacts. Lithium is processed into lithium carbonate or lithium hydroxide for use in battery cathodes, and the majority of the world's lithium chemical processing capacity is located in China.

**Legal regimes.** Lithium has been subject to distinctive legal regimes in several Latin American states, reflecting its classification as a strategic resource. In Chile, lithium was declared a strategic mineral and "non-concessible" (not subject to ordinary mining concessions) by Decree-Law No 2.886 of 1979, on account of its potential use in nuclear fusion; lithium can be exploited only by the state, by state enterprises, or under special administrative contracts (*contratos especiales de operación de litio*, CEOLs) or leases granted by the state. The leases held by the private companies SQM and Albemarle over the Salar de Atacama, granted through the state development agency CORFO, have been the subject of repeated renegotiation and litigation. In April 2023, the Chilean government announced a National Lithium Strategy providing for state control of strategic salt flats through public-private partnerships in which the state holds a majority, and in 2024 the state copper company Codelco concluded an agreement with SQM to form a joint venture, majority-owned by Codelco from 2031, for the exploitation of the Salar de Atacama.

In Bolivia, which holds some of the world's largest lithium resources in the Salar de Uyuni, the Constitution of 2009 and the state's model of resource sovereignty have reserved lithium exploitation to the state company Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos (YLB), which has sought partnerships with foreign companies for direct lithium extraction technologies. In Mexico, a 2022 reform of the Mining Law declared lithium to be of public utility, reserved its exploration, exploitation, and use to the state, and created a state lithium company (LitioMx). In Argentina, by contrast, a federal country in which mineral resources belong to the provinces under Article 124 of the National Constitution, lithium is subject to ordinary mining concessions, and a combination of provincial ownership, a liberal investment regime, and the incentive regime for large investments (RIGI) introduced in 2024 has attracted substantial foreign investment.

**Africa.** Africa's lithium resources, principally in Zimbabwe, Namibia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mali, Ghana, and Nigeria, have attracted significant investment, much of it from Chinese companies. In December 2022 Zimbabwe banned the export of unprocessed lithium ore, permitting exports only of concentrates, and announced its intention to ban the export of concentrates from 2027 to require domestic refining. Namibia similarly banned the export of unprocessed lithium ore in 2023. Ghana's first lithium mining lease, granted in 2023 to Barari DV Ghana (a subsidiary of the Australian company Atlantic Lithium) for the Ewoyaa project, provided for a royalty of ten per cent, a thirteen per cent free-carried interest for the state, and a further six per cent equity stake for the Minerals Income Investment Fund, and its ratification by Parliament was delayed amid debates over whether the terms secured sufficient value for Ghana. The DRC's Manono deposit, among the largest hard-rock lithium deposits in the world, has been the subject of a dispute between the Australian company AVZ Minerals and the Congolese state and state mining company Cominière, including ICC and ICSID proceedings.

### Cobalt

**Production and market.** Cobalt is produced predominantly as a by-product of copper mining (and, to a lesser extent, nickel mining), which means that its supply responds primarily to copper and nickel prices rather than to cobalt demand. The Democratic Republic of the Congo, principally in the Copperbelt provinces of Lualaba and Haut-Katanga, accounts for around seventy per cent of global production, and a substantial share of that production comes from mines owned wholly or partly by Chinese companies, notably CMOC Group's Tenke Fungurume and Kisanfu mines. A significant share of Congolese cobalt is produced by artisanal miners, often working in hazardous conditions, including in some cases children.

**Legal issues.** The legal issues associated with cobalt include the fiscal terms of the DRC's 2018 Mining Code (including its classification of cobalt as a strategic substance); the renegotiation of the "Sicomines" infrastructure-for-minerals agreement concluded in 2008 between the DRC and a consortium of Chinese companies, under which the Chinese partners were to finance infrastructure in exchange for mining rights, renegotiated in 2024 to increase the DRC's share of benefits; the formalisation of artisanal cobalt mining, including through the creation in 2019 of the state-owned Entreprise Générale du Cobalt (EGC), which holds a monopoly on the purchase, processing, and export of artisanal cobalt; and the due diligence obligations of companies sourcing cobalt, examined in section 8.5. In February 2025, in response to a collapse in cobalt prices caused by oversupply, the DRC government suspended cobalt exports for four months, subsequently extending the suspension and then replacing it with an export quota system — an example of a producing state using export restrictions as an instrument of market management, analogous to production quotas in the oil market.

### Nickel

**Production and market.** Nickel is used in stainless steel and, increasingly, in high-nickel battery cathodes. Indonesia has become the dominant producer, accounting for around half of global mined supply following a dramatic expansion of production since 2020, predominantly from laterite deposits processed through rotary kiln-electric furnace and high-pressure acid leaching (HPAL) technologies, in industrial parks developed largely with Chinese investment. Other significant producers include the Philippines, Russia, New Caledonia, Canada, and Australia. The expansion of Indonesian supply has caused prices to fall sharply, forcing the closure of mines in Australia and New Caledonia.

**Legal issues.** Indonesia's nickel policy is the most prominent example of a resource-rich state using export restrictions to drive downstream industrialisation. Indonesia's Mining Law No 4 of 2009 required domestic processing and refining of minerals; after a partial relaxation, the government banned exports of nickel ore with effect from January 2020. The ban attracted substantial investment in nickel smelting and refining in Indonesia, increasing the value of Indonesian nickel exports dramatically, but it was challenged by the European Union before the WTO in *Indonesia — Measures Relating to Raw Materials* (DS592). In November 2022, the panel found that the export ban was inconsistent with Article XI:1 of the GATT 1994 and could not be justified under Article XI:2(a), as a measure temporarily applied to prevent or relieve a critical shortage, or under Article XX(d), as a measure necessary to secure compliance with laws not inconsistent with the GATT. Indonesia appealed the report to the non-functioning Appellate Body, preventing its adoption — an "appeal into the void" — and has maintained the ban. The dispute is examined further in section 8.3 and in Chapter 13.

Indonesia's nickel boom has also raised serious environmental and social concerns, including deforestation, coastal and marine pollution from mine runoff and tailings, the reliance of smelters on captive coal-fired power plants, labour conditions and fatal accidents at smelters, and impacts on indigenous communities. These concerns have led some purchasers to scrutinise the environmental and social performance of Indonesian nickel suppliers, and the question whether Indonesian nickel processed with Chinese investment qualifies for incentives under the United States Inflation Reduction Act has been politically contested.

### Copper

**Production and market.** Copper is the archetypal transition metal, essential for electricity generation, transmission, and distribution, electric vehicles, and renewable energy installations. Chile and Peru are the largest producers, followed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo, China, the United States, Australia, Russia, Zambia, and Mexico. Copper demand is projected to grow strongly, and many analysts anticipate a structural deficit by the end of the 2020s, as ore grades in existing mines decline and the development of new mines faces permitting delays, community opposition, water constraints, and rising costs.

**Legal issues.** The legal issues associated with copper reflect the challenges of developing large new mines. The closure of the Cobre Panamá mine illustrates the risks. In 2023, the government of Panama and First Quantum Minerals concluded a revised concession contract for the mine, one of the largest new copper mines in the world, following a 2017 Supreme Court ruling that the original concession law was unconstitutional. The new contract, approved by the National Assembly as Law 406, provoked mass protests over environmental concerns and perceived inadequate benefits for Panama. In November 2023, the Supreme Court of Panama unanimously declared Law 406 unconstitutional, and the government ordered the closure of the mine. First Quantum commenced arbitration proceedings under the contract and under the Canada–Panama free trade agreement, although it subsequently suspended the proceedings to facilitate negotiations with the government. The case illustrates the vulnerability of mining projects to constitutional challenges and social opposition, and the interaction between domestic constitutional law and international investment protection.

In Chile, the Mining Royalty Law (Law No 21.591) of 2023 introduced a new royalty on large copper producers, combining an *ad valorem* component with a component based on operating margins, with an effective tax burden capped at a specified percentage of operating margin. In Zambia, frequent changes in mining fiscal terms have been a source of tension with investors, and in 2022 the government introduced changes to make mineral royalties deductible for income tax purposes in order to attract investment.

### Rare Earth Elements

**Production and market.** The rare earth elements comprise the fifteen lanthanides, together with scandium and yttrium. They are not, in fact, geologically rare, but economically viable concentrations are uncommon, and their separation from one another is chemically complex, environmentally hazardous (generating radioactive and acidic waste), and technically demanding. Neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium are used in high-performance permanent magnets for wind turbines, electric vehicle motors, electronics, and defence systems. China dominates every stage of the rare earth supply chain: it accounts for around sixty to seventy per cent of mining, around ninety per cent of separation and refining, and a similar proportion of magnet manufacturing. Other producers include the United States (Mountain Pass, California), Australia (Mount Weld, with processing by Lynas in Malaysia and Australia), Myanmar (where unregulated mining in Kachin State has caused severe environmental damage), and, increasingly, Brazil, Vietnam, and several African countries.

**Legal issues.** Rare earths have been at the centre of trade disputes and export control controversies. In *China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Rare Earths, Tungsten, and Molybdenum* (2014), the WTO Appellate Body upheld findings that China's export duties and export quotas on rare earths were inconsistent with its WTO obligations and not justified on conservation grounds, examined in section 8.3. China's subsequent export controls on rare earth processing technologies (2023) and on rare earth elements and magnets (2025) have prompted urgent efforts by importing states to develop alternative supply chains, including through public financing, offtake agreements, and price floors. In 2025, the United States Department of Defense agreed to acquire an equity stake in MP Materials, the owner of the Mountain Pass mine, and to guarantee a minimum price for its neodymium-praseodymium output — an unprecedented intervention illustrating the shift towards state capitalism in critical mineral supply chains.
