## Introduction

The oceans cover more than seventy per cent of the earth's surface and are among the most important sources and conduits of energy. Offshore fields account for around thirty per cent of global oil production and a similar share of gas production; offshore wind is one of the fastest-growing sources of renewable electricity; and the seabed hosts the pipelines that carry gas between continents, the cables that transmit electricity between national grids and nearly all intercontinental data, and potentially vast deposits of the minerals required for the energy transition. The sea also carries the tankers and LNG carriers through which most internationally traded oil and much internationally traded gas moves, as examined in Chapter 6. The law of the sea, which allocates jurisdiction over marine spaces and their resources among states and the international community, is therefore a foundational element of international energy and natural resources law.

The modern law of the sea is codified principally in the **United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea** (UNCLOS), often described as a "constitution for the oceans". It divides the oceans into maritime zones — internal waters, the territorial sea, the contiguous zone, the exclusive economic zone, the continental shelf, the high seas, and the Area — in each of which coastal states and other states have different rights and obligations, as illustrated in Figure 20.1. The extension of coastal state jurisdiction over offshore resources, which began with the Truman Proclamation on the continental shelf in 1945, was driven to a large extent by the desire of states to control offshore petroleum, and the rules governing the continental shelf and the exclusive economic zone remain central to the development of offshore energy.

This chapter examines UNCLOS (section 20.1), the territorial sea and exclusive economic zone (section 20.2), the continental shelf (section 20.3), offshore petroleum and gas development and other marine energy activities (section 20.4), submarine pipelines and cables (section 20.5), deep-seabed minerals (section 20.6), marine environmental protection (section 20.7), and maritime boundaries and offshore resource disputes (section 20.8). The regulation of pollution from offshore petroleum operations under regional seas agreements and national law has been examined in Chapter 17, the licensing of offshore petroleum in Chapter 5, and decommissioning in Chapters 5 and 17.

## 20.1 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea

### From Freedom of the Seas to UNCLOS

For centuries, the law of the sea was dominated by the doctrine of the **freedom of the seas**, articulated by Hugo Grotius in *Mare Liberum* (1609), under which the oceans beyond a narrow coastal belt were open to all states for navigation and fishing. Coastal state jurisdiction was confined to a territorial sea, whose breadth — often linked to the range of a cannon shot and conventionally fixed at three nautical miles — was contested.

The transformation of this regime began with the **Truman Proclamation** of 28 September 1945, in which the United States declared that it regarded the natural resources of the subsoil and seabed of the continental shelf beneath the high seas but contiguous to its coasts as appertaining to the United States, subject to its jurisdiction and control. The proclamation, motivated principally by the prospect of offshore oil production in the Gulf of Mexico, was rapidly followed by similar claims by other states, some of which — notably Chile, Ecuador, and Peru in the Santiago Declaration of 1952 — claimed sovereignty and jurisdiction over a maritime zone extending two hundred nautical miles from their coasts, primarily to protect fisheries.

The **First United Nations Conference on the Law of the Sea**, held in Geneva in 1958, adopted four conventions: on the Territorial Sea and the Contiguous Zone, the High Seas, Fishing and Conservation of the Living Resources of the High Seas, and the **Continental Shelf**. The Convention on the Continental Shelf defined the shelf by reference to a depth of two hundred metres or, beyond that limit, to where the depth of the superjacent waters admitted of the exploitation of the natural resources — the "exploitability" criterion, which threatened to allow technologically advanced states to extend their jurisdiction indefinitely. The conference failed to agree on the breadth of the territorial sea, as did a second conference in 1960.

In 1967, Ambassador Arvid Pardo of Malta proposed to the UN General Assembly that the seabed beyond national jurisdiction should be declared the **common heritage of mankind**, and in 1970 the General Assembly adopted a Declaration of Principles Governing the Sea-Bed and the Ocean Floor. The **Third United Nations Conference on the Law of the Sea**, which met from 1973 to 1982, negotiated a comprehensive convention by consensus as a "package deal", balancing the interests of coastal states, maritime powers, landlocked and geographically disadvantaged states, and developing countries. UNCLOS was adopted on 10 December 1982 at Montego Bay, Jamaica, and entered into force on 16 November 1994.

### Structure and Content

UNCLOS comprises 320 articles in seventeen parts and nine annexes. Its principal parts address the territorial sea and contiguous zone (Part II); straits used for international navigation (Part III); archipelagic states (Part IV); the exclusive economic zone (Part V); the continental shelf (Part VI); the high seas (Part VII); the regime of islands (Part VIII); enclosed or semi-enclosed seas (Part IX); the rights of landlocked states (Part X); the Area (Part XI); the protection and preservation of the marine environment (Part XII); marine scientific research (Part XIII); the development and transfer of marine technology (Part XIV); and the settlement of disputes (Part XV). Article 309 prohibits reservations, reflecting the package-deal character of the Convention.

### Implementing Agreements

Three implementing agreements supplement UNCLOS. The **Agreement relating to the Implementation of Part XI** (1994) substantially modified the deep-seabed mining regime of Part XI, which had been unacceptable to the United States and other industrialised states, in order to secure their participation, as examined in section 20.6. The **UN Fish Stocks Agreement** (1995) addresses straddling and highly migratory fish stocks. The **Agreement under UNCLOS on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Marine Biological Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction** (BBNJ Agreement), adopted in June 2023, reached the sixty ratifications required for its entry into force in September 2025, and entered into force in January 2026, as examined in section 20.7.

### Participation

UNCLOS has near-universal participation, with around 170 parties, including the European Union. Nigeria ratified the Convention in 1986. The most notable non-party is the **United States**, which signed the 1994 Implementation Agreement but has not ratified either instrument, owing to opposition in the Senate, principally to the deep-seabed mining regime. The United States nevertheless accepts most of the Convention's provisions, other than Part XI, as reflecting customary international law, as declared in President Reagan's Ocean Policy Statement of 1983 and subsequent practice, and has proclaimed a territorial sea, a contiguous zone, and an exclusive economic zone consistent with the Convention. In 2023, the United States published the outer limits of its extended continental shelf on the basis of the criteria of Article 76, without making a submission to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf. In 2025, the United States took steps to authorise deep-seabed mining beyond national jurisdiction under its domestic legislation, outside the framework of Part XI, as examined in section 20.6.

### Institutions

UNCLOS established three institutions. The **International Seabed Authority** (ISA), based in Kingston, Jamaica, is the organisation through which states parties organise and control activities in the Area, in particular with a view to administering its resources. The **Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf** (CLCS), a body of twenty-one experts in geology, geophysics, and hydrography, considers the data submitted by coastal states concerning the outer limits of the continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles and makes recommendations. The **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea** (ITLOS), based in Hamburg, is a standing tribunal of twenty-one judges with jurisdiction over disputes concerning the interpretation or application of the Convention, and its **Seabed Disputes Chamber** has jurisdiction over disputes relating to activities in the Area and may give advisory opinions at the request of the ISA. The **Meeting of States Parties** elects the members of ITLOS and the CLCS and addresses budgetary matters.

### Dispute Settlement

Part XV establishes a system of compulsory dispute settlement, which was an essential element of the package deal. States parties must settle disputes concerning the interpretation or application of the Convention by peaceful means, and if a dispute is not settled by means chosen by the parties, it must, at the request of any party, be submitted to a court or tribunal having jurisdiction under Section 2 of Part XV. Under Article 287, states may choose one or more of four forums: ITLOS, the International Court of Justice, an arbitral tribunal constituted under Annex VII, and a special arbitral tribunal under Annex VIII for certain categories of disputes. If the parties have not accepted the same procedure, the dispute is submitted to Annex VII arbitration, which is therefore the default procedure. The system is subject to limitations and exceptions: Article 297 excludes certain disputes concerning the exercise of coastal state sovereign rights in the EEZ, and Article 298 permits states to declare that they do not accept compulsory procedures for disputes concerning maritime boundary delimitation, historic bays or titles, military activities, and matters before the Security Council. States that make a declaration excluding boundary disputes remain subject to compulsory **conciliation** under Annex V for disputes arising after the Convention's entry into force — a procedure first used in the conciliation between **Timor-Leste and Australia** concerning the Timor Sea, which led to a maritime boundary treaty in 2018, examined in section 20.8. The dispute settlement system has been used extensively, including in cases with significant energy dimensions such as the *South China Sea Arbitration* (2016) and the *Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire* case (2017).

## 20.2 Territorial Seas and Exclusive Economic Zones

### Baselines and Internal Waters

Maritime zones are measured from **baselines**. The normal baseline is the low-water line along the coast as marked on large-scale charts officially recognised by the coastal state (Article 5). Where the coastline is deeply indented or fringed with islands, the coastal state may draw **straight baselines** joining appropriate points (Article 7), a method approved by the International Court of Justice in the *Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries* case (1951), provided the lines do not depart to any appreciable extent from the general direction of the coast. Archipelagic states may draw archipelagic baselines enclosing their islands (Article 47). Waters on the landward side of the baseline are **internal waters**, over which the coastal state has full sovereignty, including in ports and many estuaries where energy terminals, refineries, and LNG facilities are located.

### The Territorial Sea

Every state has the right to establish a **territorial sea** of up to twelve nautical miles from its baselines (Article 3), over which it has sovereignty extending to the airspace, seabed, and subsoil (Article 2), subject to the right of **innocent passage** of foreign ships (Articles 17–19). The coastal state has exclusive authority over the exploration and exploitation of resources in its territorial sea, including oil and gas, minerals, and the generation of renewable energy, and may adopt laws relating to innocent passage concerning, among other matters, the protection of navigational aids and facilities and other facilities or installations, and the protection of cables and pipelines (Article 21(1)). Nigeria's **Territorial Waters Act** establishes a territorial sea of twelve nautical miles. Much offshore wind development in Europe began in the territorial sea, close to shore, before moving further offshore into the EEZ.

### The Contiguous Zone

In a **contiguous zone** extending up to twenty-four nautical miles from the baselines, the coastal state may exercise the control necessary to prevent and punish infringement of its customs, fiscal, immigration, or sanitary laws within its territory or territorial sea (Article 33). The zone has limited direct significance for energy activities, although customs enforcement against smuggled fuel and stolen crude oil — a serious problem in the Gulf of Guinea — may rely on it.

### The Exclusive Economic Zone

The **exclusive economic zone** (EEZ), which may extend up to two hundred nautical miles from the baselines (Article 57), is a *sui generis* zone in which the coastal state has specified sovereign rights and jurisdiction, and other states enjoy specified freedoms. Under Article 56(1), the coastal state has:

- **sovereign rights** for the purpose of exploring and exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, whether living or non-living, of the waters superjacent to the seabed and of the seabed and its subsoil, and with regard to other activities for the economic exploitation and exploration of the zone, **such as the production of energy from the water, currents and winds**; and
- **jurisdiction** with regard to the establishment and use of artificial islands, installations, and structures; marine scientific research; and the protection and preservation of the marine environment.

The express reference to the production of energy from water, currents, and winds — included at a time when offshore renewable energy was largely theoretical — provides the legal basis for coastal state jurisdiction over offshore wind, wave, tidal, and ocean thermal energy conversion projects in the EEZ. Article 56(3) provides that the rights with respect to the seabed and subsoil are to be exercised in accordance with Part VI on the continental shelf, so that offshore petroleum and mineral rights within two hundred nautical miles rest on both regimes.

Under Article 58, all states enjoy in the EEZ the freedoms of navigation, overflight, and the **laying of submarine cables and pipelines**, and other internationally lawful uses of the sea related to these freedoms. Both the coastal state and other states must have **due regard** to each other's rights and duties (Articles 56(2) and 58(3)). Disputes over the attribution of rights not expressly allocated by the Convention are to be resolved on the basis of equity and in the light of all relevant circumstances (Article 59).

Article 60 grants the coastal state the exclusive right to construct and to authorise and regulate the construction, operation, and use of artificial islands, installations, and structures for economic purposes in the EEZ, and exclusive jurisdiction over them, including with regard to customs, fiscal, health, safety, and immigration laws. The coastal state may establish reasonable **safety zones** around installations, not exceeding five hundred metres, unless authorised by generally accepted international standards or recommended by the competent international organisation (Article 60(4)–(5)). Installations may not be established where they may cause interference with the use of recognised sea lanes essential to international navigation (Article 60(7)), and abandoned or disused installations must be removed to ensure safety of navigation, taking into account generally accepted international standards (Article 60(3)). Installations do not possess the status of islands and have no territorial sea of their own (Article 60(8)).

Nigeria established its EEZ by the **Exclusive Economic Zone Act** of 1978, which asserts sovereign rights for the exploration and exploitation of the natural resources of the zone. Nigeria's deepwater oil fields, including Bonga, Agbami, Akpo, Egina, and Erha, lie within its EEZ and on its continental shelf, more than one hundred kilometres offshore.

### Offshore Wind and Marine Spatial Planning

The rapid development of offshore wind in the EEZs of European states, China, the United States, and increasingly other states has raised legal questions about the interaction between wind farms and other uses of the sea, including navigation, fishing, cables and pipelines, defence, and environmental protection. States have developed legal frameworks for designating zones for offshore wind, leasing the seabed, and permitting projects. In the United Kingdom, the Energy Act 2004 established a Renewable Energy Zone corresponding to the EEZ, and seabed leases are granted by the Crown Estate. In the United States, the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act of 1953, as amended in 2005, authorises the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management to lease areas of the outer continental shelf for offshore wind; in 2025, the federal government suspended new offshore wind leasing and issued orders halting work on certain projects under construction, leading to litigation by developers and states. The European Union's **Offshore Renewable Energy Strategy** (2020) set an objective of at least 300 GW of offshore wind by 2050, and in the **Ostend Declaration** of 2023, nine North Seas states committed to developing the North Sea as a "green power plant" with a combined 300 GW by 2050. **Marine spatial planning**, required in the European Union by Directive 2014/89/EU, allocates marine space among competing uses and is increasingly used to designate zones for offshore wind while protecting navigation, fisheries, and ecosystems. The "due regard" obligation and the restrictions on interference with sea lanes in Article 60(7) provide the international framework within which such planning operates.

### Islands and Rocks

Under Article 121, islands generate the same maritime zones as land territory, but rocks which cannot sustain human habitation or economic life of their own have no EEZ or continental shelf (Article 121(3)). The interpretation of this provision has major implications for offshore resources, since a single feature may generate an EEZ of more than 125,000 square nautical miles. In the *South China Sea Arbitration* (*Philippines v China*, 2016), an Annex VII tribunal held that none of the high-tide features in the Spratly Islands was capable of sustaining human habitation or economic life of their own in their natural condition, so that none generated an EEZ or continental shelf, and that China's claims to historic rights within the "nine-dash line" were without legal basis to the extent that they exceeded the limits of its maritime zones under the Convention. The tribunal also found that China had violated the Philippines' sovereign rights in its EEZ, including by interfering with oil and gas exploration at Reed Bank. China rejected the award, which it regards as null and void, and the dispute over resources in the South China Sea, which is believed to contain significant hydrocarbon reserves, continues.
