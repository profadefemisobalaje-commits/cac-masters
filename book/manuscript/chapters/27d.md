## 27.7 Injunctions, Remedies, and Enforcement Orders

### The Range of Remedies

The effectiveness of energy litigation depends on the **remedies** available. Courts may award **damages** to compensate for loss; grant **injunctions** prohibiting or requiring conduct; make **declarations** of rights or of the unlawfulness of conduct; issue **public law orders** quashing decisions or compelling authorities to act; order **restitution** or the **account of profits**; and, in environmental cases, order **remediation** and restoration. Regulatory authorities have an additional range of **enforcement tools**, from warnings and compliance notices to civil penalties, licence suspension and revocation, and criminal prosecution. The choice of remedy reflects the nature of the harm, the objectives of the claimant, and the constitutional limits of judicial power.

### Damages

**Damages** are the principal remedy for breach of contract and tort. In contract, damages aim to put the claimant in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed, subject to rules of remoteness (*Hadley v Baxendale*, 1854), mitigation, and contractual limitations of liability, which are ubiquitous in energy contracts, as examined in Chapter 15. In tort, damages aim to restore the claimant to the position it would have been in had the tort not been committed. In oil pollution cases, damages may cover loss of property, loss of income from fishing and farming, the cost of remediation, personal injury, and, in some jurisdictions, general damages for inconvenience and loss of amenity. Nigerian courts have awarded damages for oil pollution under the common law and statute, including under the **Oil Pipelines Act**, which provides for compensation for damage caused by the bursting or leakage of pipelines, and have occasionally awarded substantial sums, though the enforcement of large judgments has often been delayed by appeals. **Exemplary or punitive damages**, which punish egregious conduct, are available in the United States — as in the *Exxon Valdez* litigation, in which the Supreme Court in ***Exxon Shipping Co v Baker*** (2008) reduced a punitive damages award of USD 2.5 billion to around USD 507 million, holding that under maritime law punitive damages should not exceed compensatory damages — and, more restrictively, in England and Nigeria.

### Injunctions

**Injunctions** are particularly important in energy and environmental litigation, because damages may be an inadequate remedy for irreversible environmental harm, and because claimants often seek to prevent harm rather than to be compensated for it. **Interim injunctions** may be granted to preserve the status quo pending trial. In England and in Nigeria, which follows the English approach, the principles in ***American Cyanamid Co v Ethicon*** (1975) require the claimant to show a serious question to be tried, after which the court considers whether damages would be an adequate remedy and where the **balance of convenience** lies, and claimants are usually required to give an **undertaking in damages** to compensate the defendant if the injunction proves to have been wrongly granted — a requirement that may deter community and NGO claimants, although courts may dispense with it in public interest cases, and the Aarhus Convention requires that injunctive relief in environmental cases be available on terms that are not prohibitively expensive. **Final injunctions** may prohibit continuing nuisances, such as pollution, or require positive action, such as remediation. **Mandatory injunctions**, requiring the defendant to take positive steps, are granted more cautiously than prohibitory ones, because they may require continuing supervision by the court.

Energy-related injunctions illustrate the possibilities and limits of the remedy. In ***Gbemre v Shell***, the Federal High Court ordered the cessation of gas flaring, but the order was not complied with and contempt proceedings stalled. In ***Milieudefensie v Shell***, the Hague District Court's order requiring Shell to reduce its emissions was a novel form of **structural injunction**, setting an outcome to be achieved rather than prescribing specific measures, before it was reversed on appeal. In the United States, injunctions have halted pipeline construction pending the completion of environmental reviews, as in the litigation over the **Dakota Access Pipeline**, in which the District Court for the District of Columbia ordered in 2020 that the pipeline be shut down pending a new environmental impact statement — an order reversed by the D.C. Circuit in 2021, which upheld the requirement of an EIS but held that the district court had not made the findings required for an injunction. The US Supreme Court's decision in ***Winter v Natural Resources Defense Council*** (2008) requires a plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction to establish that it is likely to succeed on the merits, likely to suffer irreparable harm, that the balance of equities tips in its favour, and that an injunction is in the public interest, and *Monsanto v Geertson Seed Farms* (2010) held that an injunction does not follow automatically from a NEPA violation.

Injunctions are also used **against protesters**. Energy companies and governments have obtained injunctions restraining protests at energy facilities, including **"persons unknown" injunctions** in England against climate protesters blocking oil terminals and motorways, the legitimacy of which was addressed by the UK Supreme Court in ***Wolverhampton City Council v London Gypsies and Travellers*** (2023), which held that courts may grant injunctions against "newcomers" — persons unknown who have not yet committed the prohibited acts — subject to safeguards. The balance between the protection of energy infrastructure and the rights of freedom of expression and assembly is a recurring issue, as examined in Chapter 21.

### Declarations and Public Law Remedies

**Declarations** — statements of the legal position — are often the primary remedy sought in public law and climate litigation, because they establish the unlawfulness of governmental conduct while leaving the choice of means to the authorities, respecting the separation of powers. In *Urgenda*, the court ordered the state to achieve a minimum reduction target, leaving the means to the government; in *Neubauer*, the Federal Constitutional Court declared the Climate Change Act partly unconstitutional and required the legislature to amend it, which it did within months, adopting more ambitious targets. In *KlimaSeniorinnen*, the European Court of Human Rights found a violation and left the measures to be taken to Switzerland under the supervision of the Committee of Ministers, which has been criticised by Switzerland's parliament as an overreach. Courts in some jurisdictions have adopted **supervisory** or **structural** remedies, retaining jurisdiction to monitor compliance — as in the Indian Supreme Court's **continuing mandamus** in environmental cases, the Colombian Supreme Court's orders in ***Future Generations v Ministry of Environment*** (2018), which required the government to formulate plans to reduce deforestation in the Amazon, and the orders of the South African courts in the *Deadly Air* case (*Trustees for the time being of groundWork Trust v Minister of Environmental Affairs*, 2022), in which the High Court declared that the poor air quality in the Highveld Priority Area, caused largely by coal-fired power stations and a coal-to-liquids plant, violated the constitutional right to an environment not harmful to health and well-being, and ordered the minister to make regulations to implement the area's air quality management plan.

### Regulatory Enforcement

Regulatory authorities enforce energy law through administrative, civil, and criminal tools, arranged in what Ayres and Braithwaite called an **"enforcement pyramid"**, in which regulators begin with persuasion and escalate to more severe sanctions in response to non-compliance, as examined in Chapter 3. In the **United Kingdom**, **Ofgem** may impose financial penalties of up to ten per cent of a licensee's turnover for breaches of licence conditions and statutory requirements, and has secured substantial penalties and redress payments from energy suppliers for mis-selling, billing failures, and breaches of consumer protection rules, often through negotiated settlements that direct payments to consumer redress funds. The **Health and Safety Executive** and the **North Sea Transition Authority** have enforcement powers in their areas. In the **United States**, the **Environmental Protection Agency** enforces environmental laws through administrative orders, civil actions, and **consent decrees** requiring defendants to install pollution controls, pay penalties, and fund environmental projects, and **FERC** has authority to impose civil penalties of more than USD one million per day for violations of the Federal Power Act and the Natural Gas Act, including market manipulation. Criminal prosecutions followed the *Deepwater Horizon* disaster, with BP pleading guilty to felony charges and paying around USD 4 billion in criminal penalties.

In **Nigeria**, the **NUPRC** and the **NMDPRA** have powers under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 to impose administrative penalties, suspend and revoke licences, and refer matters for prosecution; NUPRC regulations provide for penalties for gas flaring, under which flaring penalties are payable per thousand standard cubic feet flared, and for failure to report spills. The **National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency** (NOSDRA), established in 2006, is responsible for detecting and responding to oil spills and enforcing compliance with environmental legislation in the oil sector, and has imposed fines on companies for failure to report or clean up spills, though its powers to impose penalties have been contested in court. **NERC** enforces the Electricity Act through licence conditions, orders, and penalties, including against distribution companies for failure to meet performance standards and metering obligations. The effectiveness of regulatory enforcement in Nigeria has been limited by inadequate resources, regulatory capture, political interference, and the difficulty of monitoring operations in remote and insecure areas — challenges that the institutional reforms of the PIA seek to address.

### Enforcement of Judgments and Contempt

The enforcement of judgments is the final stage of litigation, and often the most difficult. Judgments for money may be enforced through execution against the debtor's assets, garnishee proceedings, and charging orders; injunctions are enforced through **contempt of court**, which may lead to fines, sequestration of assets, or imprisonment. In **Nigeria**, the enforcement of judgments against government agencies is subject to the requirement of the Attorney-General's consent under section 84 of the Sheriffs and Civil Process Act, noted in Chapter 26, and the enforcement of judgments against companies is often delayed by appeals and applications for stays of execution. The challenges of enforcing environmental judgments are illustrated by the slow progress of the clean-up of Ogoniland under the **Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project** (HYPREP), established in 2016 to implement the UNEP report, which demonstrates that even consensual and well-funded remediation obligations face severe implementation difficulties.

## 27.8 Cross-Border Litigation and Jurisdictional Conflicts

### Transnational Litigation Against Energy Companies

The activities of multinational energy and mining companies in host states have generated **transnational litigation**, in which victims of harm in one state sue the parent company, its subsidiaries, or both, in the courts of the parent company's home state. Claimants pursue such litigation because home state courts may offer greater independence, more effective procedures (including disclosure of documents), access to funding and legal expertise, and the ability to enforce judgments against the parent company's assets. Such litigation raises three principal sets of questions: whether the home state courts have **jurisdiction**, and whether they should decline it in favour of the host state's courts; whether the **parent company** can be liable for the conduct of its subsidiary; and what **law** applies to the claim. The substantive question of parent company liability has been examined in Chapters 17 and 22; this section focuses on jurisdiction and enforcement.

### Jurisdiction in the United Kingdom and the European Union

In the **European Union**, the **Brussels I Regulation (Recast)** (Regulation (EU) No 1215/2012) provides that persons domiciled in a member state shall be sued in the courts of that state (Article 4), and the Court of Justice held in ***Owusu v Jackson*** (2005) that a court of a member state may not decline jurisdiction over a defendant domiciled in that state on the ground of **forum non conveniens**, even if the courts of a non-member state would be more appropriate. This enables claimants to sue EU-domiciled parent companies in their home courts as of right. Claims against foreign subsidiaries may be joined, under national rules, as **necessary or proper parties** to the claim against the parent. Following Brexit, the Brussels I Regulation no longer applies in the **United Kingdom**, and English courts have reverted to common law rules for defendants not domiciled in the EU, under which the courts may decline jurisdiction on the ground of forum non conveniens where another forum is clearly more appropriate (*Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex*, House of Lords 1987), though jurisdiction over UK-domiciled defendants remains established as of right by service within the jurisdiction, subject to the discretion to stay.

In ***Lubbe v Cape plc*** (House of Lords, 2000), the House of Lords refused to stay claims by South African asbestos victims against an English parent company on the ground of forum non conveniens, because the claimants would be denied **substantial justice** in South Africa owing to the lack of legal aid and of procedures for group litigation. In ***Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe*** (UK Supreme Court, 2019), concerning pollution from the Nchanga copper mine in Zambia, operated by Vedanta's Zambian subsidiary Konkola Copper Mines, the Supreme Court held that the claims against the English parent company raised a real issue to be tried, as the parent might have assumed a duty of care through its group-wide environmental policies and their implementation, and that, although Zambia was the proper place for the claims, there was a real risk that the claimants would not obtain **substantial justice** in Zambia, owing to the lack of funding and of suitably experienced legal teams, so that the claims against both companies could proceed in England. The claim was settled in 2021. In ***Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc*** (UK Supreme Court, 2021), the Supreme Court applied *Vedanta* to allow claims by the Ogale and Bille communities against Shell's parent company and its Nigerian subsidiary to proceed, holding that the Court of Appeal had erred by conducting a "mini-trial" at the jurisdictional stage and by failing to recognise that the parent company's control and oversight of its subsidiary, including through group-wide standards, could give rise to a duty of care, and that the claimants should have the opportunity to obtain disclosure of internal documents.

### The Netherlands: Four Nigerian Farmers

In ***Four Nigerian Farmers and Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell and Shell Petroleum Development Company of Nigeria***, Nigerian farmers from the villages of Oruma, Goi, and Ikot Ada Udo, supported by Milieudefensie, sued Shell's parent company and its Nigerian subsidiary in the Netherlands for damage caused by oil spills. In **January 2021**, after thirteen years of litigation, the **Hague Court of Appeal**, applying Nigerian law, held SPDC liable for spills at Oruma and Goi, ordering it to pay compensation, and held that the parent company had breached a duty of care by failing to ensure that a leak detection system was installed on the Oruma pipeline. In 2022, Shell agreed to pay EUR 15 million to the communities in settlement. The case was the first in which a Dutch court held a Dutch parent company to a duty of care in relation to the activities of its foreign subsidiary, and demonstrated the role of the Dutch courts, under the Brussels regime, as a forum for transnational claims.

### The United States: The Alien Tort Statute and Forum Non Conveniens

In the **United States**, the **Alien Tort Statute** (ATS) of 1789, which grants the district courts jurisdiction over civil actions by aliens for torts committed in violation of the law of nations, became a vehicle for human rights claims against corporations in the 1990s and 2000s, including claims arising from energy operations in Nigeria, Myanmar, and elsewhere. In ***Kiobel v Royal Dutch Petroleum Co*** (2013), arising from the alleged complicity of Shell in human rights abuses against the Ogoni people in the 1990s, the Supreme Court held that the **presumption against extraterritoriality** applies to the ATS, so that claims must "touch and concern" the territory of the United States with sufficient force to displace the presumption, and that mere corporate presence is insufficient. In ***Jesner v Arab Bank*** (2018), the Court held that foreign corporations may not be sued under the ATS, and in ***Nestlé USA v Doe*** (2021) it held that general corporate activity in the United States, such as decision-making, is insufficient to establish domestic application. These decisions have largely closed the US federal courts to transnational human rights claims against energy companies, shifting litigation to state courts and to other jurisdictions. The US courts also apply a robust doctrine of **forum non conveniens**, as in ***Aguinda v Texaco*** (Second Circuit, 2002), in which claims by Ecuadorian and Peruvian residents for pollution caused by Texaco's oil operations in the Ecuadorian Amazon were dismissed in favour of the Ecuadorian courts — setting the stage for the Lago Agrio litigation examined in the casebox below.

### The Enforcement of Foreign Judgments

A judgment obtained in one state must often be enforced in another, where the defendant's assets are located. The recognition and enforcement of foreign judgments are governed by national law and by treaties. The **Hague Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Judgments in Civil or Commercial Matters** (2019), which entered into force in September 2023 between the European Union and Ukraine, and to which the United Kingdom acceded with effect from July 2025, provides a framework for the circulation of judgments among its parties, subject to grounds for refusal including fraud, lack of notice, and public policy. In the absence of a treaty, common law courts enforce foreign money judgments that are final and conclusive, given by a court of competent jurisdiction (judged by the enforcing court's rules), and not obtained by fraud or in breach of natural justice or contrary to public policy. In **Nigeria**, the **Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1990** provides for the registration of judgments from countries extended by ministerial order on the basis of reciprocity, and the **Reciprocal Enforcement of Judgments Ordinance 1922** continues to apply to judgments of certain Commonwealth countries; judgments from other countries may be enforced by action at common law. Within ECOWAS, the enforcement of judgments of the Community Court of Justice depends on national implementation, which has been weak.

### Inter-State Litigation in Energy and Resource Matters

Energy and resource disputes between states may be adjudicated by the **International Court of Justice**, the **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea**, and inter-state arbitral tribunals. The procedure of the ICJ — which requires the consent of both states, through special agreement, compromissory clauses in treaties, or declarations under the optional clause in Article 36(2) of the Statute — and its powers to indicate **provisional measures** under Article 41 have been applied in resource disputes including ***Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project*** (*Hungary/Slovakia*, 1997), concerning a treaty for the joint construction of a hydroelectric dam system on the Danube, in which the Court emphasised the need to reconcile economic development with environmental protection and the concept of sustainable development; ***Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay*** (*Argentina v Uruguay*, 2010), in which the Court held that the obligation to conduct an environmental impact assessment for industrial activities that may have a significant adverse impact in a transboundary context is a requirement of general international law; and the maritime boundary cases examined in Chapter 20, including ***Land and Maritime Boundary between Cameroon and Nigeria*** (2002), which determined sovereignty over the oil-rich Bakassi Peninsula and the maritime boundary. Inter-state cases face distinctive procedural questions, including the admissibility of scientific evidence, the use of court-appointed experts (as in the reparations phase of *Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo* (2022), in which the Court appointed experts on, among other things, the exploitation of natural resources, and in the maritime delimitation case between Costa Rica and Nicaragua (2018)), and the compliance of states with judgments, which, in the *Bakassi* case, was facilitated by the **Greentree Agreement** of 2006 under UN auspices.
