## 9.4 Grid Access and Third-Party Rights

### The Importance of Access

Access to the grid is the lifeblood of competition and of the energy transition. A generator that cannot connect to the network, or that cannot obtain the transmission capacity necessary to deliver its output to buyers, cannot compete; a consumer that cannot use the network to buy from a competing supplier cannot exercise choice. The law of grid access determines who may connect to and use the network, on what terms, at what cost, and with what priority when capacity is scarce. It has become one of the most important and contested areas of electricity law, as the queue of renewable and storage projects seeking connection has grown far faster than the capacity of networks to accommodate them.

### Third-Party Access to Networks

Liberalised electricity regimes, like gas regimes, require network owners to grant third-party access to their networks on non-discriminatory terms. EU law, under Article 6 of Directive (EU) 2019/944, requires member states to ensure a system of third-party access to transmission and distribution systems based on published tariffs, applicable to all customers and applied objectively and without discrimination between system users. Access may be refused only where the operator lacks the necessary capacity, and refusals must be duly substantiated. The Court of Justice has emphasised that the right of access is a fundamental element of the internal market. In *VEMW* (Case C-17/03, 2005), the Court held that the grant of priority access to cross-border transmission capacity to a former monopoly under pre-liberalisation contracts was discriminatory and contrary to the Electricity Directive, notwithstanding the age of the contracts.

In the United States, FERC Order No 888 required public utilities to provide open access transmission service under an open access transmission tariff, distinguishing between network integration service and point-to-point service, and requiring utilities to take transmission service for their own wholesale sales under the same tariff ("functional unbundling").

### Connection Rights and Procedures

Connection to the network is distinct from use of the network. Connection procedures determine how a generator, storage facility, or large consumer obtains a physical connection to the grid, including the studies required to assess the impact of the connection on the network, the reinforcements needed, the allocation of connection costs, and the timeline. In many jurisdictions, connection queues have become a major bottleneck for the energy transition. In the United States, the capacity of generation and storage projects seeking interconnection grew to more than two thousand gigawatts by the mid-2020s — far exceeding existing installed capacity — and the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation lengthened to several years. FERC Order No 2023 (2023) reformed interconnection procedures by replacing the "first-come, first-served" serial study process with a "first-ready, first-served" cluster study process, imposing stricter financial commitments and site control requirements on applicants, and imposing penalties on transmission providers that fail to complete studies on time.

Great Britain faced a similar challenge, with a connection queue exceeding seven hundred gigawatts by 2024, many of the projects in it speculative or unlikely to proceed. Ofgem approved reforms in 2025 replacing the first-come, first-served approach with a process that prioritises projects that are ready to proceed and needed to meet the government's Clean Power 2030 objectives. The EU's Action Plan for Grids (2023) and the electricity market design reform of 2024 require transparency of available grid capacity and encourage flexible connection agreements, under which projects may connect more quickly in exchange for accepting curtailment in certain conditions.

### Allocation of Connection Costs

The allocation of the costs of connecting new generation — between the connecting party and network users generally — affects the location and economics of generation investment. Under a **deep connection charging** approach, the connecting party pays the full cost of the connection assets and any wider network reinforcement required; under a **shallow** approach, it pays only for the assets connecting it to the nearest point of the network, with wider reinforcement costs socialised through network tariffs; and under **shallowish** approaches, it pays for some reinforcement. Deep charging sends strong locational signals but may deter investment, particularly by first movers in areas that require reinforcement; shallow charging encourages connection but may lead to inefficient location decisions.

### Priority Dispatch and Curtailment

Renewable energy has historically benefited from **priority access** to the grid and **priority dispatch**, under which system operators must dispatch renewable generation ahead of conventional generation, subject to the security of the system. The EU Renewable Energy Directive 2009/28/EC required member states to provide priority or guaranteed access and priority dispatch for renewable electricity. The 2019 Clean Energy package narrowed priority dispatch to small installations and demonstration projects, reflecting the view that renewable generation had matured and should be integrated into markets, while requiring compensation for curtailment of renewable generation in specified circumstances under Article 13 of Regulation (EU) 2019/943. As the share of variable renewable generation grows, curtailment — the reduction of output from renewable generators because of network constraints or oversupply — has increased, and the allocation of the costs and risks of curtailment has become an important issue in grid access law and in PPAs.

### Grid Access in Nigeria and Developing Countries

In Nigeria, the transmission network's limited capacity — wheeling capacity has frequently been below the available generation capacity — has constrained the evacuation of power from generation plants, resulting in "stranded" generation and payments to GenCos and IPPs for capacity that cannot be used. The Grid Code for the Nigeria Electricity Transmission System, issued by NERC, sets out the connection and operational requirements for users of the transmission system, and NERC's regulations on embedded generation, mini-grids, and eligible customers define access rights for distributed generators and large consumers. The **Eligible Customer Regulations**, first issued by NERC in 2017 and since revised, permit large consumers meeting specified thresholds to purchase electricity directly from generators or traders rather than from their local DisCo, subject to payment of network charges — a limited form of retail competition. The **Mini-Grid Regulations** (2016, revised 2023) establish a framework for the development of isolated and interconnected mini-grids, including provisions for the treatment of mini-grids when the main grid arrives, and have supported significant investment in rural electrification, examined in Chapter 23.

### Access for Distributed Energy Resources

Distributed energy resources (DERs) raise new questions of access. Consumers who install rooftop solar may wish to export surplus electricity to the grid ("prosumers"), and aggregators may wish to combine many small resources to participate in wholesale markets. EU law confers rights on "active customers" to generate, store, sell, and participate in flexibility and energy efficiency schemes, and on "citizen energy communities" and "renewable energy communities" to engage in generation, supply, aggregation, and storage. FERC Order No 2222 (2020) requires RTOs and ISOs to allow aggregations of distributed energy resources to participate in wholesale markets. The compensation of prosumers for exported electricity — through net metering, feed-in tariffs, or market-based arrangements — and the allocation of network costs between prosumers and other consumers have been contentious, as illustrated by debates over the revision of net metering in California in 2022 and 2023, which significantly reduced the compensation paid to new rooftop solar customers for exported electricity.

## 9.5 Power Purchase Agreements

### Function and Types

A power purchase agreement (PPA) is a contract for the sale of electricity (and, often, associated capacity, ancillary services, and environmental attributes) by a generator to a buyer over a defined period. PPAs serve different functions in different market structures.

In **single-buyer systems** and developing countries, the PPA between an independent power producer and a state-owned utility or bulk purchaser is the foundation of private investment in generation. The long-term PPA — typically twenty to twenty-five years — provides the revenue certainty that lenders require to finance the project, and allocates the principal risks between the IPP and the offtaker, often with support from the government (examined in Chapter 16).

In **liberalised markets**, PPAs are used by generators and buyers to hedge price risk, and by corporate buyers to procure renewable electricity. **Corporate PPAs** — contracts between generators, usually of renewable energy, and corporate consumers such as technology companies, manufacturers, and retailers — have grown rapidly, driven by corporate sustainability commitments. Corporate PPAs may be **physical** (involving the delivery of electricity through the grid, often via a licensed supplier in a "sleeved" arrangement) or **virtual** or **financial** (a contract for difference under which the parties settle the difference between an agreed strike price and the market price, without physical delivery, while the generator sells its output into the market and the buyer purchases its electricity from its supplier).

Appendix E contains a sample renewable energy PPA with annotations. The following discussion focuses on the long-term IPP PPA in developing countries, which raises the most significant legal issues.

### Key Provisions of an IPP PPA

**Capacity and energy payments.** The tariff structure of an IPP PPA for a dispatchable plant typically comprises a **capacity payment** (or availability payment), payable for making the plant's capacity available to the offtaker regardless of whether it is dispatched, designed to cover the IPP's fixed costs, debt service, and return on equity; and an **energy payment**, payable for electricity actually delivered, designed to cover variable costs, principally fuel. The capacity payment is the economic equivalent of take-or-pay in gas contracts, allocating demand risk to the offtaker. For variable renewable generation, PPAs typically provide for payment per unit of energy delivered, with "deemed energy" payments where the plant is available but cannot deliver because of grid constraints or curtailment by the offtaker.

**Dispatch.** The PPA provides for the offtaker (or system operator) to dispatch the plant within specified technical parameters, and allocates the risk of non-dispatch.

**Fuel supply.** For thermal plants, the PPA allocates fuel supply risk. The IPP may procure fuel itself under a fuel supply agreement, with fuel costs passed through in the energy payment, or the offtaker may supply fuel under a tolling arrangement. In Nigeria, the unreliability of gas supply to power plants has been a major source of risk, and PPAs have addressed whether the IPP is entitled to capacity payments when it cannot generate because of gas shortfalls.

**Currency and indexation.** Because IPPs in developing countries typically finance projects in foreign currency, PPAs commonly denominate tariffs in, or index them to, the United States dollar, transferring currency risk to the offtaker. Large devaluations, such as those experienced by Nigeria in 2016 and 2023–2024, Egypt in 2016, and Pakistan, can render dollar-indexed tariffs unaffordable for the offtaker and consumers, creating pressure for renegotiation.

**Change in law.** PPAs typically protect the IPP against changes in law that increase its costs or reduce its revenues, through tariff adjustments or compensation.

**Force majeure.** PPAs distinguish between **natural force majeure** (events outside the control of either party, such as natural disasters), and **political force majeure** (events within the control of the government, such as changes in law, expropriation, and political violence), and allocate the consequences differently: political force majeure events commonly entitle the IPP to continued capacity payments and, if prolonged, to terminate and receive a termination payment.

**Termination and termination payments.** The PPA specifies events of default by each party and the termination payments payable on termination. Upon termination for offtaker default or prolonged political force majeure, the termination payment typically covers outstanding debt and a return on equity; upon termination for IPP default, it may be limited to outstanding debt or less. Termination payments are often backed by government guarantees or put-and-call option agreements under which the government is obliged to purchase the plant.

**Credit support.** Because state-owned offtakers in many developing countries are not creditworthy, PPAs are supported by credit enhancement mechanisms: letters of credit for short-term payment obligations, escrow accounts, government guarantees or support agreements, and partial risk guarantees from multilateral development banks such as the World Bank's International Development Association (IDA) and the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA).

**Dispute resolution.** PPAs typically provide for expert determination of technical disputes and international arbitration of other disputes.

### The Azura-Edo IPP

The Azura-Edo IPP, a 461 MW open-cycle gas-fired power plant in Edo State that achieved commercial operation in 2018, was the first project-financed IPP in Nigeria to reach financial close under the post-privatisation framework. Its PPA with NBET was supported by a put-call option agreement with the federal government and by a partial risk guarantee from the World Bank backing NBET's payment obligations under a letter of credit, together with political risk insurance from MIGA. The project's financing structure became a template for subsequent projects, but its experience also illustrated the risks: as the liquidity crisis in the Nigerian electricity market deepened, the World Bank's guarantee was reportedly called upon to cover arrears in NBET's payments. The project illustrates the extent to which long-term PPAs in developing countries depend on sovereign and multilateral credit support to compensate for the weakness of the offtaker.

### PPA Disputes and Renegotiation

Long-term PPAs have generated significant disputes, particularly where economic crises, currency devaluations, or changes in government have made tariffs appear excessive. In the aftermath of the Asian financial crisis of 1997–1998, the Indonesian state electricity company PLN sought to renegotiate or cancel numerous IPP PPAs concluded under the Suharto government. In *Himpurna California Energy Ltd v PT (Persero) Perusahaan Listrik Negara* (1999), an UNCITRAL tribunal awarded the claimant more than USD 390 million for PLN's breach of an energy sales contract for a geothermal project, while reducing the damages claimed to take account of the economic crisis; when the award was not paid, the claimants recovered under political risk insurance from the Overseas Private Investment Corporation. In India, the Dabhol power project, developed by a consortium led by Enron, became the subject of disputes with the Maharashtra State Electricity Board in 2001 after the board refused to pay tariffs it regarded as excessive, leading to arbitrations, litigation in India and elsewhere, and eventually a settlement under which the plant was transferred to Indian state ownership.

More recently, Pakistan's government undertook in 2024 a programme of renegotiating or terminating PPAs with IPPs, citing the burden of capacity payments on consumers and the public finances, and several IPPs agreed to early termination or conversion of their contracts to "take-and-pay" terms under which payment is made only for energy delivered. Ghana renegotiated several IPP PPAs from 2020 onwards to reduce excess capacity charges. These episodes illustrate the tension between the sanctity of contracts necessary to attract investment and the political and economic pressures that arise when long-term PPAs impose burdens perceived as unsustainable. They also raise questions about the role of investment treaty protection and the extent to which renegotiation under pressure is consistent with the fair and equitable treatment standard, examined in Chapter 14.

### Corporate PPAs and the Energy Transition

Corporate PPAs have become a major driver of renewable energy investment in Europe and North America and are growing in emerging markets. Their legal issues include the allocation of "shape" and "volume" risk (the mismatch between the generator's variable output and the buyer's demand profile); the treatment of curtailment and negative prices; the transfer of guarantees of origin or renewable energy certificates (examined in Chapter 10); change in law; credit support; and accounting treatment, including whether a virtual PPA must be accounted for as a derivative. The EU electricity market design reform of 2024 requires member states to remove unjustified barriers to PPAs and to ensure the availability of instruments, such as guarantee schemes, to reduce the financial risks of PPAs for buyers. In Nigeria, the eligible customer framework and the bilateral trading opportunities created by the Electricity Act 2023 have facilitated the emergence of corporate PPAs between generators and industrial consumers, including captive and embedded renewable projects.
