## 14.3 Fair and Equitable Treatment

### The Centrality of the FET Standard

The **fair and equitable treatment** (FET) standard is the most frequently invoked and most frequently successful standard in investment treaty arbitration. Its open-textured language gives tribunals considerable latitude in its interpretation, and its scope has been the subject of extensive debate. In the energy sector, FET claims have been central to disputes arising from changes to regulatory regimes — including renewable energy support schemes, tariff regimes, fiscal terms, and environmental regulation — and from the conduct of host state authorities in granting, administering, and revoking licences and permits.

### The Relationship with the Minimum Standard of Treatment

A preliminary question is whether the FET standard is an autonomous treaty standard or a reference to the **customary international minimum standard of treatment** of aliens. The classic formulation of the minimum standard is found in *Neer v Mexico* (1926), in which the United States–Mexico General Claims Commission held that the treatment of an alien, in order to constitute an international delinquency, should amount to an outrage, to bad faith, to wilful neglect of duty, or to an insufficiency of governmental action so far short of international standards that every reasonable and impartial person would readily recognise its insufficiency.

In the context of NAFTA, the Free Trade Commission issued a binding **Note of Interpretation** in 2001 providing that Article 1105(1) of NAFTA prescribes the customary international law minimum standard of treatment of aliens as the minimum standard to be afforded to investments, and that the concepts of "fair and equitable treatment" and "full protection and security" do not require treatment in addition to or beyond that which is required by the customary minimum standard. NAFTA tribunals have held that the customary standard has evolved since *Neer*, but that it imposes a relatively high threshold. In *Waste Management Inc v Mexico (No 2)* (ICSID, 2004), the tribunal held that the minimum standard is infringed by conduct attributable to the state and harmful to the claimant if the conduct is arbitrary, grossly unfair, unjust, or idiosyncratic, is discriminatory and exposes the claimant to sectional or racial prejudice, or involves a lack of due process leading to an outcome which offends judicial propriety. In *Glamis Gold Ltd v United States* (UNCITRAL, 2009), concerning California measures restricting open-pit mining near Native American sacred sites, the tribunal held that the claimant bore the burden of proving the content of customary international law, which required conduct sufficiently egregious and shocking — a gross denial of justice, manifest arbitrariness, blatant unfairness, a complete lack of due process, evident discrimination, or a manifest lack of reasons — and dismissed the claim.

Most BITs and the ECT, however, contain unqualified FET clauses, which many tribunals have interpreted as autonomous standards, not limited to the customary minimum standard, although the practical difference between the two approaches has narrowed as tribunals interpreting the customary standard have recognised its evolution.

### The Elements of FET

Arbitral practice has identified several elements of the FET standard.

**Legitimate expectations.** The protection of the investor's legitimate expectations is the most important and most controversial element of FET in energy disputes. In *Técnicas Medioambientales Tecmed SA v Mexico* (ICSID, 2003), the tribunal articulated an expansive conception, holding that FET requires the host state to provide treatment that does not affect the basic expectations taken into account by the foreign investor in making the investment, and that the investor expects the host state to act in a consistent manner, free from ambiguity and totally transparently, so that it may know beforehand any and all rules and regulations that will govern its investments. This formulation was criticised as setting an unattainable standard of perfect governance, and subsequent tribunals adopted more balanced approaches. In *Saluka Investments BV v Czech Republic* (UNCITRAL, 2006), the tribunal held that the determination of a breach of FET requires a weighing of the claimant's legitimate and reasonable expectations on the one hand and the respondent's legitimate regulatory interests on the other, and that no investor may reasonably expect that the circumstances prevailing at the time the investment is made remain totally unchanged.

The prevailing approach distinguishes between expectations arising from **specific commitments** or representations made by the state to the investor, which are more likely to be protected, and expectations arising from **general legislation**, which states are generally entitled to change. In *Parkerings-Compagniet AS v Lithuania* (ICSID, 2007), the tribunal held that it is each state's undeniable right and privilege to exercise its sovereign legislative power, and that, in the absence of a stabilisation clause or a specific promise, an investor cannot legitimately expect that the legal framework will not change. As examined in Chapter 10, the renewable energy arbitrations against Spain, Italy, and the Czech Republic have extensively explored the circumstances in which legislation targeted at a specific class of investors, and administrative acts such as registration, can create legitimate expectations. Legitimate expectations must be assessed at the time of the investment, must be reasonable in the light of all the circumstances, including the political, socio-economic, cultural, and historical conditions prevailing in the host state, and must take into account the investor's due diligence.

**Arbitrariness and discrimination.** FET prohibits arbitrary conduct — conduct not based on legal standards but on discretion, prejudice, or personal preference — and unreasonable conduct lacking a rational relationship to a legitimate public policy. In *AES Summit Generation Ltd v Hungary* (ICSID, 2010), concerning the reintroduction of regulated electricity prices that reduced the profits of power generators with long-term PPAs, the tribunal held that a measure is reasonable if there is an appropriate correlation between the state's public policy objective and the measure adopted to achieve it, and found that Hungary's measures, adopted in response to political concern about excessive generator profits, were a reasonable response to a rational policy objective, dismissing the claim.

**Transparency and due process.** FET requires a measure of transparency and procedural fairness in administrative decision-making, including notice and an opportunity to be heard in proceedings affecting the investor's rights. In *Metalclad Corp v Mexico* (ICSID, 2000), the tribunal held that Mexico had failed to ensure a transparent and predictable framework for the investor's business planning and investment, when a municipality denied a construction permit for a hazardous waste facility that had been approved by federal authorities, a finding partially set aside by the Supreme Court of British Columbia on the ground that the tribunal had imported a transparency obligation not found in NAFTA's Chapter 11.

**Consistency and good faith.** FET prohibits conduct that is inconsistent, contradictory, or in bad faith, such as the use of regulatory powers for purposes other than those for which they were conferred. In *Bilcon of Delaware Inc v Canada* (UNCITRAL, 2015), the tribunal held, by majority, that Canada had breached the minimum standard when a joint federal–provincial environmental review panel recommended rejecting a quarry and marine terminal project on the basis of "community core values", a criterion not authorised by the applicable law, after the investor had been encouraged to invest. The dissenting arbitrator warned that the decision would chill environmental regulation, a concern widely echoed by commentators.

**Denial of justice.** FET encompasses the customary prohibition of **denial of justice**, which concerns the conduct of the host state's judicial system: the refusal of access to courts, undue delay, proceedings lacking fundamental fairness, and manifestly unjust judgments. Denial of justice generally requires the exhaustion of available local remedies, because the state is responsible only for the failure of its judicial system as a whole. In *Chevron Corp and Texaco Petroleum Co v Ecuador (II)* (PCA, 2018), the tribunal held that the judgment of the Ecuadorian courts in the Lago Agrio litigation — which had ordered Chevron to pay around USD 9.5 billion for environmental damage in the Ecuadorian Amazon attributed to Texaco's operations — had been procured through fraud, bribery, and corruption, including the ghost-writing of the judgment by the plaintiffs' representatives, and constituted a denial of justice in breach of the FET standard and the umbrella clause of the US–Ecuador BIT. The case, which is also examined in Chapter 27, illustrates the intersection of investment arbitration with transnational environmental litigation.

### Full Protection and Security

The standard of **full protection and security** (FPS) requires the host state to exercise due diligence to protect the investor's investment against physical harm, including harm caused by third parties such as rioters, rebels, or criminals. In *Asian Agricultural Products Ltd v Sri Lanka* (ICSID, 1990), the first BIT arbitration, the tribunal held that FPS does not impose strict liability on the state, but requires the exercise of due diligence, and found Sri Lanka liable for the destruction of the claimant's shrimp farm during a counter-insurgency operation by security forces. In *Wena Hotels Ltd v Egypt* (ICSID, 2000), the tribunal found Egypt liable for failing to prevent the seizure of hotels by a state-owned company. Some tribunals have extended FPS beyond physical security to legal security — the stability of the legal framework — but the prevailing view, and the express text of many recent treaties, limits it to physical protection. FPS is of particular significance for energy investments in regions affected by armed conflict, civil unrest, or criminality, including oil theft, pipeline vandalism, and attacks on installations, such as those that have affected the Niger Delta, examined in Chapter 24.

::: casebox {title="Occidental Petroleum Corporation v Republic of Ecuador (ICSID, 2012; Annulment 2015)"}
**Facts.** Occidental, a United States oil company, held a participation contract with Ecuador's state oil company, Petroecuador, for the exploration and exploitation of Block 15 in the Ecuadorian Amazon. The contract prohibited the transfer of rights under it without ministerial authorisation. In 2000, Occidental "farmed out" a forty per cent economic interest in Block 15 to a Canadian company, AEC (later EnCana), without seeking ministerial authorisation. In 2006, after the relationship between Ecuador and Occidental had deteriorated, Ecuador issued a *caducidad* (termination) decree. The decree terminated the contract, and Block 15 and its associated assets were transferred to the state, on the ground that the unauthorised transfer violated the contract and Ecuadorian law.

**Award.** The tribunal held, by majority, that Occidental had breached the contract by failing to obtain authorisation for the farmout. It held, however, that the *caducidad* decree was a disproportionate response to the breach and that Ecuador had therefore breached the FET standard of the US–Ecuador BIT and customary international law. The decree also constituted an expropriation of Occidental's investment. The tribunal emphasised that Ecuador had alternatives, such as negotiating a transfer fee or imposing a lesser sanction. Applying the principle of proportionality, it found that the termination of a contract of such value was out of proportion to the wrongdoing and to the interest Ecuador sought to protect. The majority awarded around USD 1.77 billion plus interest, reduced by twenty-five per cent to reflect Occidental's contributory fault. One arbitrator dissented on the quantum, arguing that the farmout had transferred forty per cent of the investment to AEC, so that Occidental could not claim compensation for that share.

**Annulment.** In 2015, an ICSID *ad hoc* committee partially annulled the award, holding that the tribunal had manifestly exceeded its powers by awarding Occidental compensation for the forty per cent interest that had been transferred to AEC. It reduced the award by forty per cent, to around USD 1.06 billion plus interest. Ecuador subsequently agreed a settlement with Occidental.

**Significance.** The case illustrates the application of proportionality to the exercise of contractual and regulatory sanctions by host states against energy investors. It also illustrates the interaction between contract breach and treaty breach, the role of contributory fault in reducing compensation, and the significance of annulment as a corrective mechanism.
:::
