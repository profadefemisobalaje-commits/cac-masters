## 16.7 Revenue Transparency and Anti-Corruption Controls

### Corruption and the Resource Sector

The extractive sector is widely regarded as one of the sectors most exposed to corruption. The concentration of large rents in a small number of transactions, the discretionary power of officials who allocate licences and approve transfers, the involvement of state-owned enterprises, the complexity of fiscal arrangements, and the opacity of commodity trading combine to create opportunities for bribery, embezzlement, and the misappropriation of public revenues. The OECD's Foreign Bribery Report of 2014, which analysed concluded foreign bribery enforcement actions, found that the extractive sector accounted for the largest share of cases. Corruption in the sector diverts public resources, distorts investment decisions, undermines the legitimacy of contracts, and contributes to the institutional weaknesses associated with the resource curse. The legal response comprises three overlapping elements: transparency regimes, which require the disclosure of payments, contracts, and beneficial ownership; anti-bribery laws, which criminalise the bribery of foreign and domestic officials; and the treatment of corruption in contract law and arbitration.

### The Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative

The **Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative** (EITI), launched in 2002 and formally established in 2003, is a multi-stakeholder initiative involving governments, companies, and civil society, which promotes the disclosure of information on the governance of the extractive sector. Implementing countries commit to publishing information in accordance with the **EITI Standard**, most recently revised in 2023, which requires disclosure across the value chain: the legal and institutional framework, including the allocation of contracts and licences; exploration and production; revenue collection, including the reconciliation of payments reported by companies with revenues reported by governments; revenue allocation; and social and economic spending. The Standard requires the disclosure of the **beneficial owners** of companies holding extractive rights, the publication of contracts and licences entered into or amended from 2021, and information on the sale of the state's share of production, environmental payments, gender, and energy transition issues. Implementation is overseen by a national multi-stakeholder group in each country and assessed through a process of **validation** by the EITI International Board, which may suspend countries that fail to make progress. More than fifty countries implement the Standard.

**Nigeria** was among the first countries to implement the EITI and was the first to give it a statutory basis, through the **Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (NEITI) Act 2007**, which established NEITI as a statutory body with a mandate to conduct audits of the oil, gas, and mining sectors. NEITI's audit reports have documented discrepancies between company payments and government receipts, unremitted revenues by state entities, crude oil losses through theft, and weaknesses in metering and cost recovery, and have contributed to public debate and reform, although their recommendations have not always been implemented.

### Mandatory Payment Disclosure

In addition to the voluntary EITI, several jurisdictions have adopted mandatory requirements for extractive companies to disclose payments to governments on a country-by-country and project-by-project basis, following campaigns by the Publish What You Pay coalition. Section 1504 of the United States **Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act** of 2010 required the Securities and Exchange Commission to adopt rules requiring resource extraction issuers to disclose payments to governments. The SEC's first rule was vacated by a federal court in 2013, and a second rule was disapproved by Congress in 2017 under the Congressional Review Act; a third rule was adopted in 2020. In the European Union, Chapter 10 of the **Accounting Directive** (Directive 2013/34/EU) and the amended Transparency Directive require large extractive and logging companies and listed issuers to publish annual reports on payments to governments, implemented in the United Kingdom by the Reports on Payments to Governments Regulations 2014. Canada's **Extractive Sector Transparency Measures Act**, enacted in 2014 and in force from 2015, imposes similar requirements. Norway has also adopted country-by-country reporting rules for extractive companies.

### Contract Transparency and Beneficial Ownership

The disclosure of the contracts between governments and extractive companies has become an important norm. Historically, petroleum and mining contracts were treated as confidential, often at the insistence of both parties, but the confidentiality of contracts conceals their terms from citizens and parliaments, impedes accountability, and facilitates corruption. Many states now publish extractive contracts, and the EITI Standard, the IMF's Guide on Resource Revenue Transparency, the Natural Resource Charter — a set of principles for governments and societies on how best to harness the opportunities created by extractive resources, whose second edition was published in 2014 — and the IFC's policy on disclosure of extractive contracts support contract transparency. The online repository ResourceContracts.org, developed by the Natural Resource Governance Institute and partners, makes thousands of contracts publicly available. Chapter 24 examines the extent to which Nigeria's PIA requires the publication of licences and contracts.

**Beneficial ownership transparency** addresses the use of anonymous companies to conceal the identity of those who benefit from extractive rights, including politically exposed persons. The EITI requires implementing countries to maintain public registers of beneficial owners of extractive companies, and many countries, including Nigeria through the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, have established beneficial ownership registers. The European Union's anti-money laundering framework required public access to beneficial ownership registers, although the Court of Justice held in *WM and Sovim SA v Luxembourg Business Registers* (2022) that unrestricted public access was incompatible with the rights to privacy and data protection under the Charter of Fundamental Rights, requiring access to be limited to persons with a legitimate interest.

### Anti-Bribery Law

The bribery of foreign public officials is prohibited by national laws with extraterritorial reach and by international conventions.

The United States **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act** of 1977 (FCPA) prohibits issuers of securities in the United States, domestic concerns, and persons acting in the United States from making corrupt payments to foreign officials to obtain or retain business, and requires issuers to maintain accurate books and records and adequate internal controls. The FCPA has been enforced vigorously by the Department of Justice and the SEC, and a large proportion of its most significant enforcement actions have involved the energy and extractive sector, including actions against oil companies, oilfield service companies, and commodity traders. In February 2025, the United States President issued an executive order pausing new FCPA enforcement pending the review of enforcement guidelines, and revised guidelines were subsequently issued focusing enforcement on specified priorities, creating uncertainty about the future intensity of enforcement.

The United Kingdom's **Bribery Act 2010** criminalises the bribery of foreign public officials and creates a corporate offence of **failure to prevent bribery** by persons associated with a commercial organisation, to which it is a defence that the organisation had adequate procedures in place to prevent bribery. The failure to prevent model has influenced legislation elsewhere and has been extended in the United Kingdom to the facilitation of tax evasion and, by the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023, to fraud.

At the international level, the **OECD Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions** of 1997 requires parties to criminalise foreign bribery and is monitored through peer review by the OECD Working Group on Bribery. The **United Nations Convention against Corruption** (UNCAC) of 2003, which has near-universal participation, addresses prevention, criminalisation, international cooperation, and asset recovery. Regional instruments include the **African Union Convention on Preventing and Combating Corruption** (2003) and the **ECOWAS Protocol on the Fight against Corruption** (2001). Nigeria's domestic framework includes the Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Act 2000, the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (Establishment) Act 2004, and the Money Laundering (Prevention and Prohibition) Act 2022.

**Deferred prosecution agreements** (DPAs), available in the United States and, since 2014, in England and Wales, permit companies to resolve corruption charges through the payment of penalties and the implementation of compliance programmes without conviction. Major resolutions in the energy and commodities sector include the 2022 guilty pleas by the commodity trader Glencore in the United States and the United Kingdom, in respect of bribery in several African and Latin American states, involving combined penalties exceeding USD 1 billion; resolutions involving the Brazilian state oil company Petrobras and the construction company Odebrecht arising from the *Lava Jato* investigation; and actions involving oilfield service and offshore contractors.

The World Bank and other multilateral development banks operate **sanctions systems** under which firms found to have engaged in fraud or corruption in bank-financed projects may be debarred from future contracts, and the banks' agreement on mutual enforcement of debarment decisions, concluded in 2010, extends the effect of debarment across institutions.

### Corruption in Contract and Arbitration

Corruption affects the validity and enforceability of contracts and the admissibility of claims in arbitration. Arbitral tribunals have held that contracts procured by bribery are contrary to international public policy and unenforceable. In *World Duty Free Co Ltd v Republic of Kenya* (2006), an ICSID tribunal held that a claimant which had obtained a contract by paying a bribe to the head of state could not maintain claims based on the contract, because bribery is contrary to international public policy. In *Metal-Tech Ltd v Republic of Uzbekistan* (2013), an ICSID tribunal declined jurisdiction over a claim under a bilateral investment treaty, finding that the investment had been made through payments to officials in breach of Uzbek law, so that it did not qualify for protection under the treaty's legality requirement, although the tribunal noted that the state's participation in the corruption was reflected in the allocation of costs.

Corruption may also be raised at the stage of enforcement or set-aside. In *Federal Republic of Nigeria v Process & Industrial Developments Ltd* [2023] EWHC 2638 (Comm), the English Commercial Court set aside arbitral awards worth approximately USD 11 billion, including interest, on the ground that they had been obtained by fraud, including bribery of a Nigerian official and the improper retention of Nigeria's privileged documents, a case examined in detail in Chapter 26. The decision illustrates the role of national courts in policing the integrity of arbitration involving state energy contracts.

The scope of corruption allegations in the allocation of petroleum rights is illustrated by the controversy surrounding Nigeria's **OPL 245**, an offshore oil block allocated in 1998 to a company associated with a former petroleum minister and later sold to Shell and Eni in 2011 in a transaction in which payments by the companies passed through the Nigerian government to the original holder. Criminal prosecutions of the companies and individuals in Italy ended in acquittal in 2021, a result that became final when the prosecution abandoned its appeal, while proceedings in other jurisdictions and civil claims raised continuing questions about the transaction. The case illustrates the difficulty of proving corruption in complex transactions and the multiplicity of forums in which such allegations may be pursued.

### Towards Integrated Governance

Transparency and anti-corruption measures are necessary but not sufficient conditions for good governance of resource revenues. Disclosure has limited effect unless citizens, parliaments, the media, and oversight institutions have the capacity and freedom to use the information to hold governments and companies accountable, and unless law enforcement institutions are able to investigate and prosecute wrongdoing. Research on the effectiveness of the EITI suggests that its effects on corruption and governance outcomes have been modest, although it has contributed to the production of information and the institutionalisation of public debate in implementing countries. The integration of transparency into national law, as in the NEITI Act, the publication of contracts and beneficial ownership, the strengthening of supreme audit institutions and parliamentary oversight, the reform of state-owned enterprises, and the protection of civil society space are complementary elements of an effective framework. For legal practitioners, anti-corruption compliance — including due diligence on agents, partners, and intermediaries, contractual anti-corruption representations and audit rights, and internal controls — has become a core element of transactional practice in the energy sector, as examined further in Chapter 22.
