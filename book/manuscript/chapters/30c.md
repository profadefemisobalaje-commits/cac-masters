## 30.4 Digitalisation, Data Ownership, and Energy Markets

### The Digital Energy System

Energy systems are being transformed by **digitalisation** — the deployment of smart meters, sensors, communication networks, and data platforms that enable the real-time monitoring and control of energy flows. Digitalisation enables **decentralisation**: households and businesses become **prosumers** who generate electricity from rooftop solar, store it in batteries, and sell it to the grid or to neighbours; electric vehicles become mobile storage devices; and **aggregators** combine the flexibility of many small resources into **virtual power plants** that can participate in electricity markets. It enables new market models, including **peer-to-peer trading**, **local energy markets**, **energy communities**, and dynamic tariffs that reflect real-time prices. In Africa, digital technologies — including mobile money and **pay-as-you-go** (PAYG) business models, in which customers pay for solar home systems in small instalments through mobile phones, with remote disabling of systems for non-payment — have enabled the rapid expansion of off-grid solar access, as examined in Chapter 23.

### Legal Questions of Digital Energy Markets

Digitalisation raises legal questions that existing energy law, designed for centralised systems, does not adequately address. **Market access**: can prosumers, aggregators, and energy communities participate in markets on equal terms with incumbent generators? The European Union's Electricity Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/944) gives final customers the right to act as **active customers**, to participate in **citizen energy communities**, and to contract with independent aggregators, and requires member states to remove barriers to demand response; in the United States, **FERC Order No. 2222** (2020), examined in Chapter 24, requires regional markets to allow aggregations of distributed energy resources to participate. **Network tariffs**: as prosumers generate more of their own electricity, the allocation of fixed network costs becomes contested, since customers who self-generate may pay less towards networks they continue to rely on, shifting costs to others — a debate reflected in the reform of net metering schemes in several US states and elsewhere. **The role of distribution system operators**, which must evolve from passive network operators to active managers of distributed resources, raising questions about their neutrality and unbundling. **Consumer protection**: dynamic tariffs, automated control of appliances, and PAYG remote disabling raise issues of transparency, fairness, and the protection of vulnerable consumers, including in relation to disconnection.

### Data Ownership and Access

Digital energy systems generate vast quantities of **data** — on consumption patterns, generation, network conditions, and market transactions — whose ownership, control, and use raise fundamental legal questions. Smart meter data can reveal intimate details of household life, such as when occupants are at home, asleep, or away, raising **privacy** concerns addressed by data protection law, including the EU's GDPR and Nigeria's **Data Protection Act 2023**. Data are also economically valuable: access to consumption data enables new services, such as energy efficiency advice and flexibility aggregation, and control over data may confer market power. The law generally does not recognise **property** in data as such; instead, data are governed by a combination of data protection law, contract, intellectual property (including database rights), competition law, and sector-specific rules on data access. The EU's Electricity Directive requires member states to ensure that final customers can access their consumption data and authorise third parties to access it, and the **EU Data Act** (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854), applicable from September 2025, gives users of connected products, including smart energy devices, rights to access and share the data they generate. The concept of **data spaces** — governed frameworks for data sharing — is being developed for the energy sector in the European Union's **Common European Energy Data Space**.

In developing countries, questions of data governance intersect with concerns about **data sovereignty** and the control of data by foreign technology companies. Data generated by PAYG solar systems and smart meters in Africa are often held by foreign companies, raising questions about who benefits from their value and how they may be used. The African Union's **Data Policy Framework** (2022) and national data protection laws seek to balance the free flow of data with sovereignty and protection. The governance of **geological and resource data** — the seismic and geological information on which resource exploration depends — is also significant: under most petroleum and mining laws, such data are the property of the state, and their management through national data repositories, such as Nigeria's National Data Repository administered by the NUPRC, is an important function of resource governance.

### Cyber Security

Digitalisation increases the exposure of energy systems to **cyber attacks**, as examined in Chapter 25. The proliferation of connected devices — smart meters, inverters, electric vehicle chargers, and home energy management systems — multiplies the points of vulnerability, and concerns have been raised about the security of devices manufactured in states regarded as strategic competitors, leading some jurisdictions to restrict the use of certain foreign components in critical energy infrastructure. Legal responses include cyber security obligations for operators of essential services (such as under the EU's NIS2 Directive), security standards for connected devices (such as the EU's **Cyber Resilience Act**, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, which imposes cyber security requirements on products with digital elements), network codes on cyber security for cross-border electricity flows, and the screening of foreign suppliers.

### Blockchain and Smart Contracts

**Distributed ledger technologies**, including blockchain, have been proposed for applications in energy markets, including peer-to-peer trading, the tracking of renewable energy certificates and guarantees of origin, the traceability of critical minerals through supply chains, and the settlement of transactions through **smart contracts** — self-executing code that automatically performs contractual obligations when specified conditions are met. Legal questions include the enforceability of smart contracts and their relationship with traditional contract law — the Law Commission of England and Wales concluded in 2021 that the existing law is capable of accommodating smart legal contracts — the allocation of liability for errors in code, the application of financial and energy regulation to tokenised energy assets, and the governance of decentralised platforms. Many early blockchain energy projects have not progressed beyond pilots, but the technology's potential for supply chain **traceability**, for example in battery passports required under the EU Battery Regulation, may prove more durable.

## 30.5 Climate-Related Financial and Legal Risks

### Climate Change as Financial Risk

Climate change is now widely recognised as a source of **financial risk**. Following the influential speech by Mark Carney, then Governor of the Bank of England, on the "tragedy of the horizon" (2015), financial regulators have identified two principal categories of climate-related financial risk: **physical risks**, arising from the physical effects of climate change, such as damage to assets from extreme weather and chronic changes in temperature and sea levels; and **transition risks**, arising from the process of adjustment to a low-carbon economy, including changes in policy, technology, and market preferences that may reduce the value of carbon-intensive assets. A third category, **liability risks**, arises from claims against companies and their directors and insurers for contributing to or failing to manage climate change, examined in Chapters 18, 22, and 27. Energy companies are particularly exposed to transition risks, as the value of fossil fuel reserves and infrastructure depends on assumptions about future demand and prices that decarbonisation may undermine.

### Disclosure

The first legal response to climate-related financial risk has been **disclosure**. The **Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures** (TCFD), established by the Financial Stability Board in 2015, published recommendations in 2017 for disclosure of climate-related risks and opportunities across four pillars: governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets, including scenario analysis. The TCFD recommendations have been incorporated into the **IFRS S1 and S2** sustainability disclosure standards issued by the **International Sustainability Standards Board** (ISSB) in June 2023, which a growing number of jurisdictions are adopting. Nigeria was among the early adopters, with the **Financial Reporting Council of Nigeria** announcing a roadmap for the adoption of the ISSB standards, with voluntary adoption from 2024 and mandatory adoption for public interest entities thereafter. The European Union's **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2464) requires extensive sustainability reporting under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, although its scope and timetable were reduced by the 2025 Omnibus simplification package. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted climate disclosure rules in March 2024, which were stayed pending litigation and which the Commission voted in 2025 to stop defending, while California enacted climate disclosure laws applicable to large companies doing business in the state. Disclosure aims to enable investors to price climate risk and to encourage companies to manage it, but its effectiveness depends on the quality and comparability of disclosures and the response of markets.

### Prudential Regulation

Central banks and financial supervisors have begun to integrate climate risk into **prudential regulation**. The **Network of Central Banks and Supervisors for Greening the Financial System** (NGFS), established in 2017 and comprising well over one hundred members, has developed **climate scenarios** used by supervisors and financial institutions to assess climate risk, and guidance on integrating climate risk into supervision. Supervisors in the United Kingdom, the European Union, and elsewhere have conducted **climate stress tests** of banks and insurers, and the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision** issued principles for the effective management and supervision of climate-related financial risks in 2022. These measures may affect the cost and availability of finance for fossil fuel projects, particularly if supervisors impose capital requirements reflecting climate risk. The **Central Bank of Nigeria** has issued Nigerian Sustainable Banking Principles and has engaged with the NGFS, and African central banks are increasingly attentive to climate risk, though concerns have been raised that climate-related financial regulation in developed countries could raise the cost of capital for energy projects in developing countries.

### Sustainable Finance Taxonomies

**Sustainable finance taxonomies** classify economic activities according to their environmental sustainability, to guide investment and prevent greenwashing. The **EU Taxonomy Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2020/852) establishes criteria for activities that contribute substantially to environmental objectives without doing significant harm to others; its inclusion of certain gas and nuclear activities as transitional activities in 2022 was controversial and was challenged by Austria before the EU General Court, which dismissed the challenge in 2025. Taxonomies have also been adopted or developed by China, South Africa (whose Green Finance Taxonomy was published in 2022), and other jurisdictions, and the interoperability of taxonomies is a concern for cross-border finance. The design of taxonomies has significant implications for the financing of gas, nuclear, and transition activities in developing countries.

### Directors' Duties and Fiduciary Duties

Climate risk also raises questions about the **duties of company directors** and **institutional investors**. Legal opinions in several jurisdictions, including Australia (the Hutley opinions, from 2016) and the United Kingdom, have concluded that directors who fail to consider foreseeable climate-related risks may breach their duties of care and diligence. The *ClientEarth v Shell's Directors* case (2023), examined in Chapter 22, illustrated the difficulty of enforcing such duties through derivative actions. For pension funds and other institutional investors, the question whether **fiduciary duties** permit or require the consideration of climate risk has been contested, particularly in the United States, where some states have enacted laws restricting the consideration of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors in public investment, while others require it. The legal treatment of climate risk in fiduciary duties is likely to remain a significant and politically contested area.
