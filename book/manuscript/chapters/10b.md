## 10.3 Wind Energy Permitting and Environmental Compliance

### Onshore and Offshore Wind

Wind energy is the second largest source of renewable electricity after hydropower and a cornerstone of decarbonisation strategies in Europe, China, the United States, and increasingly elsewhere. Onshore wind is a mature and low-cost technology; offshore wind, which benefits from stronger and more consistent winds and fewer conflicts with land uses, has grown rapidly in northern Europe and China and is expanding to the United States, Asia, and other regions, although rising costs, supply chain constraints, and interest rates led to the cancellation or delay of several projects in 2023 and 2024. Wind energy raises distinctive permitting issues relating to its visual and landscape impacts, noise, impacts on birds and bats, interference with radar and aviation, and, offshore, interactions with fisheries, shipping, marine ecosystems, and military activities.

### Permitting of Onshore Wind

Onshore wind projects typically require planning permission or development consent, environmental assessment, a generation licence, grid connection, and, depending on the jurisdiction, consents relating to aviation, radar, and wildlife. Permitting has been widely identified as the principal bottleneck for onshore wind deployment. In Europe, permitting procedures for onshore wind projects historically took many years, and a substantial pipeline of projects was blocked in permitting or litigation. The EU's Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2577, adopted as an emergency measure in December 2022, laid down temporary rules to accelerate the deployment of renewable energy, including a presumption that renewable energy projects are in the overriding public interest; RED III made many of these measures permanent, requiring maximum permitting periods and the designation of renewables acceleration areas.

Germany's experience illustrates the significance of legal reform. Onshore wind deployment in Germany slowed dramatically after 2017, owing to the introduction of auctions, restrictive distance rules (notably Bavaria's "10H rule", which required a distance of ten times the height of the turbine from residential buildings), and litigation. The 2023 amendments to the Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG 2023) declared that the construction and operation of renewable energy installations are in the overriding public interest and serve public security (§ 2 EEG), and the Wind-on-Land Act (Wind-an-Land-Gesetz) of 2022 required the federal states to designate two per cent of the national land area for onshore wind by 2032, overriding restrictive distance rules where states fail to meet their area targets. Deployment and permitting accelerated substantially thereafter.

### Wildlife Protection

The protection of birds and bats is a recurrent issue in wind energy permitting. In the European Union, the **Birds Directive** (Directive 2009/147/EC) and the **Habitats Directive** (Council Directive 92/43/EEC) require the protection of wild birds and of species and habitats of Community interest. Article 6(3) of the Habitats Directive requires an "appropriate assessment" of any plan or project likely to have a significant effect on a Natura 2000 site, and permits approval only if the project will not adversely affect the integrity of the site, unless, under Article 6(4), there are imperative reasons of overriding public interest, no alternative solutions, and compensatory measures are taken. Article 12 of the Habitats Directive and Article 5 of the Birds Directive prohibit the deliberate killing or disturbance of protected species. The Court of Justice has interpreted these prohibitions strictly; in *Föreningen Skydda Skogen* (Joined Cases C-473/19 and C-474/19, 2021), it held that the prohibitions apply irrespective of the conservation status of the species concerned and are not limited to activities aimed at killing or disturbing. The presumption of overriding public interest introduced by RED III is intended to facilitate the derogations necessary for renewable energy projects.

In the United States, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918, the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act of 1940, and the Endangered Species Act of 1973 protect birds and other wildlife, and wind energy operators may obtain incidental take permits for eagles. In 2022, a wind energy company pleaded guilty to violations of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act in connection with the deaths of eagles at its facilities, illustrating the criminal liability risks of wildlife impacts.

### Wind Energy and Indigenous Rights: The Fosen Case

The most significant judicial decision on the relationship between wind energy and indigenous rights is the judgment of the Supreme Court of Norway in the *Fosen* case (2021). The Norwegian government had granted licences and expropriation permits for the Storheia and Roan wind farms on the Fosen peninsula, among the largest onshore wind developments in Europe, located in an area used by Sámi reindeer herders for winter grazing. The reindeer herders argued that the wind farms would interfere with their ability to practise their culture, in violation of Article 27 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which protects the right of persons belonging to minorities, in community with other members of their group, to enjoy their own culture. In October 2021, the Supreme Court held unanimously that the licences and expropriation decisions were invalid because the wind farms would have a significant negative effect on the herders' ability to practise reindeer husbandry, in violation of Article 27, and that the mitigation measures proposed were insufficient. The Court rejected the argument that the interference could be justified by the public interest in renewable energy, noting that the green transition could be achieved through other means that did not infringe Sámi rights.

The judgment did not specify the consequences for the turbines, which had been constructed and were operating. Following protests by Sámi activists and environmentalists in 2023, which drew attention to the continued operation of the turbines more than five hundred days after the judgment, the Norwegian government apologised and negotiated settlements with the affected reindeer herding groups, concluded in 2023 and 2024, providing for compensation, additional winter grazing land, and other measures allowing the wind farms to continue operating. The *Fosen* case is a landmark in the emerging law of "green" or "just" transition, demonstrating that renewable energy projects are subject to the same human rights constraints as fossil fuel projects, and that courts may invalidate projects that disproportionately burden indigenous peoples. It is examined further in Chapter 21.

Comparable conflicts have arisen elsewhere. In Mexico, wind farm development in the Isthmus of Tehuantepec in Oaxaca, one of the windiest regions in the world, has been contested by Zapotec and Ikoots indigenous communities, and the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation in 2018 addressed the adequacy of the consultation conducted for the Eólica del Sur project, upholding the consultation in that case while affirming the right to prior consultation. In Kenya, the Lake Turkana Wind Power project, the largest wind farm in Africa when commissioned in 2019, faced litigation by local communities challenging the acquisition of the land on which it was built; in 2021, the Environment and Land Court at Meru found that the process by which the land had been set aside was irregular and ordered the regularisation of the land acquisition in accordance with the Community Land Act, without ordering the removal of the project.

### Offshore Wind Leasing and Permitting

Offshore wind development requires a legal framework for the allocation of rights to the seabed and water column, environmental assessment, and coordination with other marine users. Coastal states allocate offshore wind rights through several models. In the **leasing model** used in the United Kingdom, the Crown Estate (for England, Wales, and Northern Ireland) and Crown Estate Scotland grant seabed leases through competitive leasing rounds, and developers must separately obtain development consent and a route to market (typically a contract for difference). In the **centralised tender model** used in Denmark, the Netherlands, and Germany for certain sites, the state pre-investigates and pre-consents sites and tenders them, together with grid connection, to developers, in some cases through "negative bidding" or criteria-based tenders. In the United States, the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) leases areas of the outer continental shelf under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, as amended by the Energy Policy Act of 2005, through competitive lease sales, and developers must obtain approval of construction and operations plans following environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act. In 2025, the federal government suspended new offshore wind leasing and halted work on certain projects under construction, giving rise to litigation and illustrating the regulatory risks of offshore wind in the United States.

Offshore wind permitting addresses impacts on marine mammals (particularly from pile-driving noise), seabirds, fish and benthic habitats, fisheries, navigation, aviation, defence, and cultural heritage, and requires coordination through marine spatial planning, which allocates marine space among competing uses. The EU's Maritime Spatial Planning Directive (Directive 2014/89/EU) requires member states to establish maritime spatial plans. Offshore wind and the law of the sea are examined in Chapters 12 and 20.

### Decommissioning and Repowering

Wind turbines have operating lives of twenty to thirty years, and the first generation of wind farms in Europe and the United States is reaching the end of its life. Decommissioning raises issues of financial security (analogous to those in mining and petroleum, examined in Chapters 5 and 7), the recycling of turbine blades (which are made of composite materials that are difficult to recycle, and which several European countries have banned from landfill), and site restoration. **Repowering** — the replacement of old turbines with fewer, larger, and more efficient turbines on the same site — can substantially increase output, and RED III requires member states to facilitate repowering through simplified permitting.

## 10.4 Hydropower Law and Water Resource Allocation

### The Role and Impacts of Hydropower

Hydropower is the largest source of renewable electricity globally and the backbone of electricity systems in many countries, including Norway, Brazil, Canada, Paraguay, Ethiopia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Zambia. Reservoir hydropower provides dispatchable generation and storage that can balance variable wind and solar output, and pumped storage hydropower is the largest form of grid-scale electricity storage. In Nigeria, the Kainji, Jebba, and Shiroro hydropower plants on the Niger and Kaduna rivers, and the more recent Zungeru plant, contribute a significant share of grid generation.

At the same time, large dams have serious environmental and social impacts: displacement of communities (the World Commission on Dams estimated in 2000 that large dams had displaced between forty and eighty million people worldwide), loss of livelihoods dependent on rivers, disruption of fisheries and sediment flows, inundation of forests and cultural heritage, greenhouse gas emissions from reservoirs in tropical areas, and risks of catastrophic dam failure. The report of the **World Commission on Dams**, *Dams and Development: A New Framework for Decision-Making* (2000), proposed a rights-and-risks approach to dam decision-making, emphasising gaining public acceptance, comprehensive options assessment, addressing existing dams, sustaining rivers and livelihoods, recognising entitlements and sharing benefits, ensuring compliance, and sharing rivers for peace, development, and security. Although its recommendations are not binding, they have influenced the policies of development institutions and the industry's **Hydropower Sustainability Standard**, launched in 2021 by the Hydropower Sustainability Council, which provides for independent certification of hydropower projects against environmental, social, and governance criteria.

This section examines the domestic legal framework for hydropower projects — water rights, licensing, concessions, and dam safety. The international law of transboundary watercourses, and the basin-level management of hydropower on shared rivers, are examined in Chapter 19.

### Water Rights and Allocation for Hydropower

Hydropower depends on the right to use water. Legal systems allocate water rights according to several models: **riparian rights**, under which owners of land bordering a watercourse are entitled to reasonable use of the water (the traditional common law position); **prior appropriation**, under which rights are allocated on a "first in time, first in right" basis to those who put water to beneficial use (the position in the western United States); and **public ownership and administrative allocation**, under which water is owned or controlled by the state and allocated through permits or licences (the position in most modern water codes, including those of most developing countries). In most jurisdictions, hydropower developers require a water use permit or licence specifying the quantity of water that may be abstracted or diverted, the conditions of use (including minimum environmental flows), and the duration of the right.

In Nigeria, the **Water Resources Act 1993** (Cap W2, Laws of the Federation of Nigeria 2004) vests the right to the use and control of all surface water and groundwater, and of all water in any watercourse affecting more than one state, in the Government of the Federation, and provides for the licensing of water use, including for hydropower. The **Minerals and Mining Act 2007** separately provides for water use permits for mining. The River Basin Development Authorities, established by statute, are responsible for the development of water resources within their basins, and the **Nigeria Hydrological Services Agency** monitors water resources. A National Water Resources Bill, intended to modernise the framework, has been the subject of controversy, in part because of concerns from states about the concentration of control over water resources in the federal government.

### Environmental Flows

The concept of **environmental flows** — the quantity, timing, and quality of water flows required to sustain freshwater ecosystems and the livelihoods that depend on them — has become a central feature of modern water and hydropower law. Water licences for hydropower increasingly specify minimum flows to be released downstream, and require operators to manage releases to mimic natural flow patterns. The EU Water Framework Directive (Directive 2000/60/EC) requires member states to achieve "good ecological status" of water bodies, which has required the revision of hydropower licences to provide adequate flows and fish passage. In *Commission v Austria* (Case C-346/14, 2016), the Court of Justice upheld Austria's authorisation of a hydropower plant on the Schwarze Sulm river, notwithstanding the deterioration of the river's status, because the national authorities had properly applied the derogation for projects of overriding public interest under Article 4(7) of the Directive. In *Bund für Umwelt und Naturschutz Deutschland* (Case C-461/13, 2015, the "Weser" case), the Court held that member states must refuse authorisation for projects that may cause deterioration of the status of a body of surface water, unless a derogation applies, interpreting "deterioration" to include deterioration of a single quality element.

### Hydropower Concessions and Licences

Hydropower plants are typically developed under concessions or licences granted by the state for long periods — thirty to seventy-five years or more — reflecting the long life of dams. Concessions specify the rights and obligations of the concessionaire, including water use, operation, maintenance, dam safety, environmental obligations, payment of royalties or fees, and the reversion of assets to the state at the end of the concession. In Norway, the Industrial Licensing Act of 1917 introduced the principle of "reversion" (*hjemfall*), under which hydropower plants owned by private companies revert to the state without compensation at the end of the licence period; the principle was upheld by the EFTA Court in *EFTA Surveillance Authority v Norway* (Case E-2/06, 2007) in so far as it applied to public as well as private owners, after Norway amended its legislation to remove discrimination against private and foreign owners.

In France, the renewal of expiring hydropower concessions, many of them held by EDF, has been the subject of a long-running dispute with the European Commission, which considers that EU public procurement and concession law requires competitive tendering, while successive French governments have resisted opening the concessions to competition. In the United States, non-federal hydropower plants are licensed by FERC under the Federal Power Act for terms of thirty to fifty years, and relicensing proceedings involve consideration of environmental conditions imposed by federal and state agencies.

In Nigeria, the Kainji and Jebba hydropower plants were concessioned in 2013 to Mainstream Energy Solutions Ltd, and the Shiroro plant to North South Power Company Ltd, under thirty-year concession agreements as part of the privatisation of the power sector. The 700 MW Zungeru hydropower plant, constructed by a Chinese consortium with Chinese financing, was concessioned in 2023 to Mainstream Energy Solutions for a period of thirty years. The proposed **Mambilla hydropower project**, a planned 3,050 MW project in Taraba State, has been delayed for decades and became the subject of an ICC arbitration brought by Sunrise Power and Transmission Company Ltd against the Federal Government of Nigeria, alleging breach of a contract to develop the project — an illustration of the legal risks associated with large hydropower projects.

### Dam Safety

The failure of dams can cause catastrophic loss of life and property, as the failures of the Derna dams in Libya in September 2023, which killed thousands of people, demonstrated. Dam safety legislation requires owners and operators of dams to design, construct, operate, maintain, and inspect dams according to specified standards, to prepare emergency action plans, and to coordinate releases with downstream authorities. In Nigeria, the release of water from the Lagdo Dam in neighbouring Cameroon has been associated with severe flooding in Nigeria, notably in 2012 and 2022, raising questions about transboundary coordination examined in Chapter 19. The International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD) publishes technical bulletins on dam safety that are widely used as standards.
