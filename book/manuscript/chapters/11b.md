## 11.3 Nuclear Safety and Regulatory Independence

### The Responsibility for Safety

The first principle of nuclear safety law is that the **prime responsibility for safety rests with the person or organisation responsible for facilities and activities that give rise to radiation risks** — that is, the licensee or operator. This principle, set out in Principle 1 of the IAEA Safety Fundamentals and in Article 9 of the Convention on Nuclear Safety, means that the regulator does not assume responsibility for safety by licensing and inspecting a facility; the operator remains responsible at all times, and must establish a management system that gives safety overriding priority. The principle is reinforced by the concept of **safety culture**, defined by the International Nuclear Safety Advisory Group after Chernobyl as the assembly of characteristics and attitudes in organisations and individuals which establishes that, as an overriding priority, nuclear plant safety issues receive the attention warranted by their significance.

### The Requirements of the Convention on Nuclear Safety

The CNS imposes obligations on contracting parties concerning the legislative and regulatory framework (Article 7), the regulatory body (Article 8), responsibility of the licence holder (Article 9), priority to safety (Article 10), financial and human resources (Article 11), human factors (Article 12), quality assurance (Article 13), assessment and verification of safety (Article 14), radiation protection (Article 15), emergency preparedness (Article 16), siting (Article 17), design and construction (Article 18), and operation (Article 19). The legislative and regulatory framework must provide for national safety requirements, a system of licensing, a system of regulatory inspection and assessment, and the enforcement of applicable regulations and licence terms, including suspension, modification, or revocation.

Article 18 requires that the design and construction of a nuclear installation provide for several reliable levels and methods of protection — the concept of **defence in depth** — against the release of radioactive materials, with a view to preventing the occurrence of accidents and mitigating their radiological consequences. Defence in depth is the foundational concept of nuclear safety engineering: it requires multiple independent and redundant layers of protection, so that the failure of one layer is compensated by another, ranging from conservative design and high-quality operation, through control of abnormal operation and engineered safety features, to the control of severe accidents and off-site emergency response.

The CNS is implemented through a peer review process. Each contracting party submits a national report on the measures taken to implement its obligations, which is reviewed by the other parties at review meetings held every three years. Following the Fukushima accident, the parties adopted the **Vienna Declaration on Nuclear Safety** (2015), which set out principles for the implementation of the CNS's objective of preventing accidents and mitigating radiological consequences: new nuclear power plants are to be designed, sited, and constructed with the objective of preventing accidents and, should an accident occur, mitigating possible releases of radionuclides causing long-term off-site contamination and avoiding early radioactive releases or releases large enough to require long-term protective measures and actions; comprehensive and systematic safety assessments are to be carried out periodically for existing installations, and reasonably practicable safety improvements implemented in a timely manner; and national requirements are to take into account the relevant IAEA safety standards. The Declaration was the compromise outcome of a Swiss proposal to amend the CNS, which did not attract sufficient support.

### Regulatory Independence

The independence of the regulatory body is the cornerstone of nuclear safety governance. Article 8(2) of the CNS requires each contracting party to take the appropriate steps to ensure "an effective separation between the functions of the regulatory body and those of any other body or organization concerned with the promotion or utilization of nuclear energy". Principle 2 of the IAEA Safety Fundamentals requires an "effectively independent" regulatory body, and the IAEA's General Safety Requirements Part 1, *Governmental, Legal and Regulatory Framework for Safety*, elaborates the elements of independence: the regulatory body must be functionally separate from entities having responsibilities or interests that could unduly influence its decision-making; it must have adequate legal authority, competence, and financial and human resources; and it must be able to make regulatory judgments and decisions free from undue influence, including political influence and pressure from licensees.

In the European Union, Article 5 of the Euratom Nuclear Safety Directive, as amended in 2014, requires member states to ensure that the competent regulatory authority is functionally separate from any other body or organisation concerned with the promotion or utilisation of nuclear energy, and that it does not seek or take instructions from any such body or organisation when carrying out its regulatory tasks; that it makes regulatory decisions founded on robust and transparent safety-related requirements; that it is given dedicated and appropriate budget allocations; and that it employs an appropriate number of staff with the necessary qualifications.

Independence is a matter of substance, not merely form. Regulatory independence may be compromised by structural arrangements (such as the location of the regulator within a ministry responsible for promoting nuclear energy), by financial dependence, by the movement of personnel between regulator and industry, and by a culture of deference. The **IAEA Integrated Regulatory Review Service** (IRRS) provides peer reviews of national regulatory infrastructure against IAEA standards, and IRRS missions frequently comment on the independence of regulators.

::: casebox {title="The Fukushima Daiichi Accident (2011) and Regulatory Capture"}
**Facts.** On 11 March 2011, the Great East Japan Earthquake and the tsunami that followed it struck the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Station, operated by the Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO). The tsunami, which exceeded the height of the plant's sea wall, flooded the plant and disabled the emergency diesel generators and electrical systems. The resulting loss of cooling led to core meltdowns in three reactors, hydrogen explosions, and the release of large quantities of radioactive material. More than one hundred thousand people were evacuated from the surrounding area. The accident was rated at Level 7, the highest level, on the International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale — the only accident other than Chernobyl to receive that rating.

**Findings.** The Fukushima Nuclear Accident Independent Investigation Commission established by the National Diet of Japan concluded in its 2012 report that the accident was "a profoundly manmade disaster — that could and should have been foreseen and prevented". It found that TEPCO and the regulators had been aware of the risk of a tsunami exceeding the design basis but had failed to take action, and that the relationship between the regulators and TEPCO amounted to "regulatory capture": the regulators had lacked independence from the industry and from the ministry that promoted nuclear power, and had deferred to the operator's expertise and interests. The Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency, the principal regulator, had been located within the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, which was responsible for promoting nuclear energy.

**Legal reforms.** Japan abolished the Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency and in 2012 established the **Nuclear Regulation Authority** (NRA) as an independent commission attached to the Ministry of the Environment, adopted new regulatory requirements incorporating severe accident measures and stricter tsunami and seismic standards, and required all reactors to undergo review against the new standards before restarting. Internationally, the accident led to the IAEA Action Plan on Nuclear Safety (2011), "stress tests" of nuclear power plants in the European Union, the 2014 amendment of the Euratom Nuclear Safety Directive, and the Vienna Declaration on Nuclear Safety.

**Litigation.** The accident generated extensive litigation in Japan. In June 2022, the Supreme Court of Japan held in cases brought by evacuees that the State was not liable for damages, because the tsunami was far larger than anticipated and the accident could not have been prevented even if the State had exercised its regulatory powers to require TEPCO to take countermeasures. In July 2022, the Tokyo District Court, in a shareholders' derivative action, ordered four former TEPCO executives to pay the company around 13 trillion yen in damages for failing to take measures against the foreseeable risk of a tsunami, a judgment subject to appeal. Compensation for victims under the Act on Compensation for Nuclear Damage is examined in section 11.6.

**Significance.** Fukushima demonstrated that regulatory independence is a substantive safety requirement and not a formality, and that the assumptions underlying the design basis of nuclear installations must be continuously reassessed in the light of new knowledge about external hazards.

### Licensing and Safety Assessment

National nuclear safety law establishes a system of licensing that governs each stage in the life of a nuclear installation: site evaluation, design, construction, commissioning, operation, and decommissioning. Licensing decisions are based on the regulator's review of the applicant's safety case — a comprehensive set of documents and analyses demonstrating that the installation will meet safety requirements, including a deterministic safety analysis of design basis accidents and a probabilistic safety assessment estimating the frequency of core damage and large releases. Many legal systems also require **periodic safety reviews**, typically every ten years, in which the operator reassesses the safety of the installation against current standards and identifies reasonably practicable improvements.

Courts generally defer to the technical judgments of nuclear regulators, while scrutinising the legality of their procedures. In *Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp v Natural Resources Defense Council* 435 US 519 (1978), the United States Supreme Court held that courts may not impose procedural requirements on agencies, including the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, beyond those required by the Administrative Procedure Act and other statutes. In *Baltimore Gas & Electric Co v Natural Resources Defense Council* 462 US 87 (1983), it upheld the Commission's assumption, for the purposes of licensing decisions, that the permanent storage of nuclear waste would have no significant environmental impact, emphasising that a reviewing court must be at its most deferential when an agency makes predictions within its area of special expertise at the frontiers of science. In India, the Supreme Court in *G Sundarrajan v Union of India* (2013) declined to halt the commissioning of the Kudankulam nuclear power plant, holding that the safety of the plant was a matter for the expert regulator, but issued directions on safety, the disposal of spent fuel, and the welfare of the local population. In Japan, district courts have on several occasions issued injunctions suspending the operation of reactors on safety grounds, which have generally been overturned on appeal, illustrating both the potential and the limits of judicial supervision of nuclear safety.

The law of nuclear safety also intersects with the law of nuisance and transboundary harm. In *Land Oberösterreich v ČEZ as* (Case C-115/08, 2009), the Court of Justice held that Austria could not refuse to take into account, in proceedings brought by the Austrian province of Upper Austria seeking an injunction against nuisance from the Temelín nuclear power plant in the Czech Republic, the official authorisation granted to the plant in another member state, because to do so would discriminate on grounds of nationality contrary to the Euratom Treaty.

### Nuclear Safety in Armed Conflict: Zaporizhzhia

The Russian occupation of the Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant in Ukraine — the largest nuclear power plant in Europe — in March 2022 confronted nuclear safety law with an unprecedented situation: a nuclear power plant operated under military occupation in an active war zone, subject to shelling, repeated losses of off-site power, and the detention and intimidation of its staff. The IAEA Director General articulated "seven indispensable pillars" for ensuring nuclear safety and security during armed conflict, derived from IAEA safety standards and security guidance, including physical integrity, the functioning of safety and security systems, the ability of staff to fulfil their duties without undue pressure, secure off-site power supply, uninterrupted supply chains, effective radiation monitoring and emergency arrangements, and reliable communication with the regulator. In May 2023, he set out to the United Nations Security Council five concrete principles for the protection of the plant, including that there should be no attack from or against the plant and that it should not be used as a base for heavy weapons. The IAEA established a continuous presence of its experts at the plant from September 2022. Under international humanitarian law, Article 56 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977) provides special protection for works and installations containing dangerous forces, including nuclear electrical generating stations, which are not to be made the object of attack, even where they are military objectives, if such attack may cause the release of dangerous forces and consequent severe losses among the civilian population, subject to limited exceptions. The protection of energy infrastructure in armed conflict is examined further in Chapter 25.

## 11.4 Safeguards and Non-Proliferation Obligations

### The Concept of Safeguards

IAEA safeguards are a system of technical measures through which the IAEA verifies that states are honouring their international legal obligations to use nuclear material and technology only for peaceful purposes. Safeguards comprise nuclear material accountancy (the maintenance of records and reports of nuclear material quantities and movements), containment and surveillance (seals and cameras), and inspections and other verification activities at facilities and other locations. The IAEA's safeguards conclusions, reported annually in the Safeguards Implementation Report, state whether the IAEA has found any indication of the diversion of declared nuclear material and, for states with additional protocols, whether it has found any indication of undeclared nuclear material or activities.

### Types of Safeguards Agreements

Safeguards are implemented under agreements concluded between the IAEA and states. There are three principal types.

**Comprehensive safeguards agreements** (CSAs), concluded by non-nuclear-weapon states party to the NPT in accordance with Article III, are based on the IAEA document *The Structure and Content of Agreements between the Agency and States Required in Connection with the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons* (INFCIRC/153 (Corrected), 1972). Under a CSA, the state undertakes to accept safeguards on all source or special fissionable material in all peaceful nuclear activities within its territory, under its jurisdiction, or carried out under its control anywhere, for the exclusive purpose of verifying that such material is not diverted to nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices. The state must establish a State System of Accounting for and Control of nuclear material (SSAC), declare its nuclear material and facilities, and accept inspections. Many states with minimal or no nuclear material have concluded **small quantities protocols** suspending most of the detailed provisions of their CSAs; in 2005, the IAEA Board modified the standard text of the protocol to reinstate certain requirements, including the provision of design information on new facilities and the possibility of inspections.

**Item-specific safeguards agreements**, based on INFCIRC/66/Rev.2, apply to specified nuclear material, facilities, or equipment, and are concluded with states not party to the NPT — India, Pakistan, and Israel.

**Voluntary offer agreements** have been concluded by the five nuclear-weapon states, under which they offer some or all of their civil nuclear facilities for safeguards.

### The Additional Protocol

The discovery after the 1991 Gulf War of an extensive clandestine nuclear weapons programme in Iraq — a party to the NPT with a CSA in force — revealed that safeguards focused on the verification of declared material were inadequate to detect undeclared activities. The IAEA responded with a programme of strengthened safeguards, culminating in the **Model Additional Protocol** (INFCIRC/540 (Corrected)), approved by the Board of Governors in 1997. Under an additional protocol, a state must provide an expanded declaration of its nuclear-related activities, including nuclear fuel cycle-related research and development, uranium mines and concentration plants, the manufacture of specified equipment, and the export of specified equipment and material; and must grant the IAEA "complementary access" to any location on the site of a facility, to other locations listed in the declaration, and to other locations specified by the IAEA to resolve questions or inconsistencies, on short notice. The additional protocol enables the IAEA to provide credible assurance of the absence of undeclared nuclear material and activities. Although a large majority of states with CSAs have brought additional protocols into force, the additional protocol is not legally required by the NPT, and some states have declined to conclude one. The NSG and some supplier states have debated whether the additional protocol should be a condition of supply.

### Non-Compliance and the Security Council

Where the IAEA Board finds that a state is in non-compliance with its safeguards agreement, it may, under Article XII(C) of the Statute, report the non-compliance to the Security Council and the General Assembly. The Security Council may then take action under Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter, as it did in respect of Iraq, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK), and Iran. The DPRK announced its withdrawal from the NPT in 2003 under Article X, which permits withdrawal on three months' notice if extraordinary events related to the subject matter of the Treaty have jeopardised the supreme interests of the withdrawing state, and subsequently conducted nuclear weapons tests; the legal validity of the withdrawal remains contested, and the Security Council has imposed sanctions under Chapter VII.

### Iran and the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action

The case of Iran is the most extensively litigated and negotiated safeguards controversy. In 2003, the IAEA reported that Iran had failed to declare certain nuclear material and activities, including enrichment-related activities, in breach of its CSA. In 2005, the Board found Iran in non-compliance and in 2006 reported the matter to the Security Council, which adopted a series of resolutions under Chapter VII demanding that Iran suspend enrichment and imposing sanctions. In 2015, Iran concluded the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action** (JCPOA) with China, France, Germany, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the European Union, under which Iran accepted limits on its enrichment activities and stockpile, provisional application of its additional protocol, and enhanced monitoring, in exchange for the lifting of nuclear-related sanctions. The Security Council endorsed the JCPOA in Resolution 2231 (2015), which terminated the earlier resolutions while providing a "snapback" mechanism by which a JCPOA participant could trigger the reimposition of the previous sanctions.

The United States withdrew from the JCPOA in 2018 and reimposed its own sanctions, and Iran subsequently exceeded the JCPOA's limits, enriched uranium to sixty per cent, and reduced its cooperation with the IAEA. In June 2025, the IAEA Board found that Iran was in non-compliance with its safeguards obligations, and Israel and the United States carried out military strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities, raising grave questions under the law on the use of force; Iran subsequently suspended cooperation with the IAEA. In August 2025, France, Germany, and the United Kingdom invoked the snapback mechanism, and the previous United Nations sanctions were reimposed at the end of September 2025, although Russia and China contested the legality of the snapback. The Iranian case illustrates the dependence of the safeguards system on political support and the limits of verification without cooperation, and it is a case study in the interaction between technical verification, treaty interpretation, and the use of force.

### Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zones and the Prohibition Treaty

Regional nuclear-weapon-free zones complement the NPT. The **Treaty of Pelindaba**, which established the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone, prohibits the research, development, manufacture, stockpiling, acquisition, possession, and testing of nuclear explosive devices in the zone and the dumping of radioactive waste; requires parties to conclude CSAs with the IAEA; requires the maintenance of the highest standards of physical protection; and prohibits armed attack on nuclear installations. It established the **African Commission on Nuclear Energy** (AFCONE), headquartered in South Africa, to ensure compliance and promote the peaceful uses of nuclear energy in Africa. Other zones cover Latin America and the Caribbean (Treaty of Tlatelolco, 1967), the South Pacific (Treaty of Rarotonga, 1985), South-East Asia (Treaty of Bangkok, 1995), and Central Asia (Treaty of Semipalatinsk, 2006).

The **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons** (TPNW), adopted in 2017 and in force since January 2021, prohibits states parties from developing, testing, producing, acquiring, possessing, stockpiling, using, or threatening to use nuclear weapons. None of the nuclear-armed states is a party, and the relationship between the TPNW and the NPT is contested. Article 3 of the TPNW requires states parties to maintain, at a minimum, their existing safeguards obligations, and Article 4 provides for safeguards for states that eliminate their nuclear weapons. The legality of the threat or use of nuclear weapons under general international law was addressed by the International Court of Justice in its Advisory Opinion on the *Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons* (1996), in which it held, among other things, that there exists an obligation to pursue in good faith and bring to a conclusion negotiations leading to nuclear disarmament in all its aspects under strict and effective international control.
