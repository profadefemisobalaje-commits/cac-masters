## 29.2 Regulatory Impact Assessment

### Purpose and Development

**Regulatory impact assessment** (RIA) — also called regulatory impact analysis — is a systematic process for identifying and assessing the expected effects of proposed regulation, and of alternative options, before decisions are taken. Its purposes are to improve the quality of regulation by ensuring that it is based on evidence, that alternatives are considered, and that benefits justify costs; to enhance transparency and accountability by documenting the rationale for regulatory decisions; and to provide a basis for consultation and later evaluation. RIA originated in the United States, where executive orders since the 1970s and 1980s, notably **Executive Order 12291** (1981) and **Executive Order 12866** (1993), required federal agencies to analyse the costs and benefits of significant regulations, subject to review by the **Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs** (OIRA), and has been adopted by OECD members and many developing countries, promoted by the **OECD Recommendation of the Council on Regulatory Policy and Governance** (2012). The European Commission conducts **impact assessments** of significant legislative proposals under its Better Regulation guidelines, scrutinised by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board, and the United Kingdom requires impact assessments for government regulatory proposals, scrutinised by the Regulatory Policy Committee.

### The Stages of RIA

A standard RIA involves the following stages:

1. **Problem definition**: identifying the problem to be addressed, its causes, its magnitude, and why government intervention is needed — for example, market failure, regulatory failure, or a distributional concern. A clear problem definition is the foundation of good regulation; many poor regulations result from poorly defined problems.
2. **Objectives**: specifying the objectives of intervention, ideally in measurable terms, such as a target for electricity access, a reduction in gas flaring, or an increase in investment.
3. **Options**: identifying a range of options, including the **"do nothing"** or baseline option, non-regulatory options (such as information, voluntary agreements, or market-based instruments), and different regulatory designs. The consideration of genuine alternatives is the core of RIA.
4. **Impact analysis**: assessing the expected costs, benefits, and distributional effects of each option, including economic, social, and environmental impacts, using quantitative methods such as **cost-benefit analysis** and **cost-effectiveness analysis** where feasible, and qualitative assessment where not. Impacts on particular groups, such as small businesses, low-income households, women, and affected communities, and on competition, should be considered.
5. **Comparison and recommendation**: comparing the options against the objectives and recommending a preferred option, with reasons.
6. **Implementation and compliance**: considering how the preferred option will be implemented, enforced, and complied with, and its administrative costs.
7. **Monitoring and evaluation**: specifying how the regulation's effects will be monitored and when it will be reviewed.
8. **Consultation**: consulting stakeholders on the problem, options, and impacts, examined in section 29.5.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis and its Limits

**Cost-benefit analysis** (CBA) monetises the expected costs and benefits of a regulatory option, discounts them to present values, and compares them. In the energy sector, CBA is used to assess, for example, the benefits of network investments, the costs and benefits of renewable support schemes, and the health benefits of air pollution regulation. CBA raises difficult methodological and ethical questions. The valuation of non-market goods — human health and life (through the **value of a statistical life**), ecosystem services, biodiversity, and cultural heritage — is contested. The choice of **discount rate** profoundly affects the valuation of long-term effects such as climate change: a high discount rate reduces the present value of future climate damages, while a low rate gives greater weight to future generations, as the debate between the *Stern Review* (2006) and its critics illustrated. The **social cost of carbon** — the monetised value of the damages caused by emitting an additional tonne of carbon dioxide — is a key parameter in the assessment of energy and climate regulation; in the United States, its value has been revised repeatedly by successive administrations, rising substantially in 2023 before being withdrawn from federal regulatory analysis in 2025, illustrating the political sensitivity of the parameter. CBA may also neglect **distributional** effects, since it aggregates costs and benefits regardless of who bears them, and may therefore need to be complemented by distributional analysis and consideration of energy justice, examined in Chapter 23.

### RIA in Developing Countries and Nigeria

Many developing countries have adopted RIA requirements, often with the support of international organisations, but their implementation has frequently been limited by capacity constraints, lack of data, political resistance, and the treatment of RIA as a formality conducted after decisions have been taken. In **Nigeria**, there is no general statutory requirement of RIA for legislation, although the **National Policy on Regulatory Reform** and the work of the Presidential Enabling Business Environment Council have promoted better regulation, and some regulators, including NERC, conduct consultations and publish analyses supporting their regulatory decisions, such as the Multi-Year Tariff Order. The Electricity Act 2023 and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 require regulators to consult stakeholders before making regulations, which provides an opportunity for evidence-based analysis. The institutionalisation of RIA for significant energy legislation and regulations, proportionate to the importance of the measure and supported by capacity-building, would improve the quality of energy regulation in Nigeria and other African states.

### Climate and Sustainability Assessments

Increasingly, RIA is complemented by specialised assessments of the effects of regulation on climate change and sustainability. The European Union applies a **"do no significant harm"** principle and a **climate consistency** assessment to legislative proposals under the European Climate Law. The United Kingdom's Climate Change Act 2008 and the advisory role of the Climate Change Committee encourage the assessment of policies against carbon budgets. Nigeria's **Climate Change Act 2021**, examined in Chapter 18, requires ministries and agencies to integrate climate considerations into their plans and established the National Council on Climate Change, whose role could include reviewing the climate implications of energy legislation. **Strategic environmental assessment**, examined in Chapter 17, extends environmental assessment from projects to policies, plans, and programmes, and is particularly relevant to energy sector plans, such as the integrated resource plans that determine the future mix of generation.

## 29.3 Legislative Drafting for Energy Governance

### The Importance of Drafting

The quality of legislation depends not only on the policy it embodies but on the clarity, coherence, and effectiveness of its drafting. Poorly drafted legislation creates uncertainty, invites litigation, and may fail to achieve its purposes. Energy legislation poses particular drafting challenges: it must establish complex institutional arrangements, allocate powers among multiple bodies and levels of government, regulate technical matters, create fiscal regimes with precise definitions and formulae, provide for transitions from earlier regimes, and remain adaptable to technological and market change. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021, with its five chapters, hundreds of sections, and multiple schedules, and the Electricity Act 2023, illustrate the scale and complexity of modern energy legislation.

### Principles of Good Legislative Drafting

The principles of good legislative drafting, as articulated in the guidance of drafting offices and the scholarship of drafting experts such as Helen Xanthaki, include:

1. **Clarity**: legislation should be expressed in clear language that conveys the intended meaning to its users, avoiding ambiguity, unnecessary complexity, and archaic expressions. The **plain language** movement has promoted the use of shorter sentences, active voice, everyday words, and logical structure.
2. **Precision**: legislation must be sufficiently precise to determine rights and obligations and to be applied consistently, particularly in fiscal provisions, where small differences in definitions can have large revenue consequences.
3. **Unambiguity**: terms should be used consistently, and key terms defined where necessary, avoiding the use of different words for the same concept or the same word for different concepts.
4. **Coherence**: legislation should be internally consistent and consistent with the wider legal system, including the constitution, other legislation, and international obligations, with explicit provisions for repeals, amendments, and the relationship with other laws.
5. **Effectiveness**: legislation should be capable of achieving its objectives, which requires attention to implementation, enforcement, and institutional capacity — what Xanthaki describes as the ultimate goal of drafting, with clarity, precision, and unambiguity as means to effectiveness.
6. **Accessibility**: legislation should be organised logically, with clear headings, explanatory notes, and consolidated versions available to the public.

### Structure of Energy Legislation

A typical energy statute is structured as follows: a **preliminary part** stating the title, commencement, objectives, scope, and application, and sometimes guiding principles; **definitions**, either at the beginning or in a schedule; **institutional provisions** establishing the ministry's policy role, the regulator, and other bodies, and their functions, powers, governance, funding, and accountability; **substantive provisions** regulating the activities within its scope — licensing, technical and safety standards, market rules, tariffs, consumer protection, environmental obligations, community obligations, and fiscal terms; **enforcement provisions** setting out inspection powers, offences, penalties, and administrative sanctions; **dispute resolution and appeals**; **transitional provisions** governing the conversion of existing rights, the transfer of assets and staff, and the continuation of existing proceedings; **regulations-making powers** delegating detailed rules to the minister or regulator; and **repeals and consequential amendments**.

### Framework Legislation and Delegated Rule-Making

A key design choice is the balance between **primary legislation** and **delegated** or **subsidiary legislation**. Energy statutes increasingly take the form of **framework legislation** that establishes institutions, principles, and broad powers, delegating detailed rules to regulators through regulations, codes, licence conditions, and guidelines, which can be adapted more readily to technical and market change. The Petroleum Industry Act and the Electricity Act both confer extensive regulation-making powers on the NUPRC, the NMDPRA, and NERC. Delegation enhances flexibility and draws on regulatory expertise, but raises concerns about democratic accountability and legal certainty, which can be addressed through clear statutory objectives and limits on delegated powers, mandatory consultation, parliamentary scrutiny of delegated legislation, publication requirements, and judicial review. The US Supreme Court's major questions doctrine and the overruling of *Chevron* deference, examined in Chapter 27, illustrate the constitutional tensions that delegation can create, and suggest that legislatures should express clearly any intention to confer broad regulatory powers on agencies.

### Drafting for the Energy Transition

Drafting energy legislation for the energy transition requires particular techniques: **technology neutrality**, defining obligations by reference to outcomes (such as emissions or reliability) rather than particular technologies, so that legislation does not become obsolete as technologies evolve; **adaptive provisions**, such as periodic review clauses, sunset clauses, and powers to adjust parameters (such as targets and support levels) within defined limits; **targets and pathways**, as in climate framework laws that set long-term targets and require periodic plans and budgets; **grandfathering and transitional arrangements** that balance the protection of existing investments with the need for change, examined in Chapter 14 in relation to investment law; and **integration** across sectors, since the transition links electricity, transport, heating, industry, and land use. The drafting of **definitions** of emerging technologies — such as "green hydrogen", "energy storage", "carbon capture and storage", and "critical minerals" — has significant consequences for the scope of regulation and incentives, as the debates over the definition of "clean hydrogen" in the United States and "renewable fuels of non-biological origin" in the European Union illustrate.

### The Drafting Process

Legislative drafting is a collaborative process involving policy officials, who define the policy; **legislative drafters** (in Nigeria, the Legal Drafting Department of the Federal Ministry of Justice and the legal departments of the National Assembly), who translate policy into legal text; subject-matter experts; and, increasingly, external stakeholders through consultation on draft bills. Drafting instructions should set out the policy objectives, the intended legal effect, and the relevant background clearly. In Nigeria, many energy bills have originated as private members' bills or executive bills subject to extensive amendment in the National Assembly, and the coordination of drafting across the executive and legislature has been a recurrent challenge, as the protracted history of the Petroleum Industry Bill, which was split into several bills in 2016–2018 before being reconsolidated, illustrates. Pre-legislative scrutiny of draft bills and public hearings by legislative committees can improve the quality of legislation before enactment.
