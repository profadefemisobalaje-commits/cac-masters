### Quantitative Restrictions and State Trading

Article XI:1 of the GATT prohibits prohibitions or restrictions, other than duties, taxes, or other charges, on the importation or exportation of products, whether made effective through quotas, import or export licences, or other measures. The prohibition applies equally to imports and exports, and is therefore of particular importance in the energy sector, where export restrictions are common. Its application to export restrictions on energy and minerals is examined in section 13.4.

Article XVII of the GATT requires members that establish or maintain **state trading enterprises** — enterprises granted exclusive or special privileges — to ensure that they act, in their purchases or sales involving imports or exports, in a manner consistent with the general principles of non-discriminatory treatment, and make such purchases or sales solely in accordance with commercial considerations. The application of Article XVII to national oil companies and state-owned utilities is uncertain, and in *Canada — Wheat Exports and Grain Imports* (2004), the Appellate Body held that Article XVII:1 does not impose a broad obligation on state trading enterprises to act commercially, but rather requires them to act in a non-discriminatory manner, with the commercial considerations requirement serving to define the content of non-discrimination.

### Freedom of Transit

Article V of the GATT establishes the principle of **freedom of transit**. Article V:2 provides that there shall be freedom of transit through the territory of each member, via the routes most convenient for international transit, for traffic in transit to or from the territory of other members, and that no distinction shall be made based on the flag of vessels, the place of origin, departure, entry, exit, or destination, or on any circumstances relating to the ownership of goods, of vessels, or of other means of transport. Article V:3 prohibits unnecessary delays or restrictions on traffic in transit and exempts it from customs duties and transit duties, except charges for transportation or commensurate with administrative expenses or the cost of services rendered, and Article V:4 requires charges on traffic in transit to be reasonable.

The application of Article V to fixed infrastructure such as pipelines and electricity networks has been debated. The text refers to "routes most convenient for international transit", and it has been argued that the provision was designed for transport by road, rail, and waterway and does not require members to grant access to pipelines or to construct new transit infrastructure. In *Colombia — Ports of Entry* (2009), the panel held that Article V:2 requires members to extend freedom of transit via the routes most convenient for international transit, but did not address fixed infrastructure. In *Russia — Traffic in Transit* (2019), examined below, the panel considered measures affecting road and rail transit. The Energy Charter Treaty's transit provision, Article 7, examined in Chapter 6, was designed to address the specific features of energy transit through fixed infrastructure, and the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement (2013, in force 2017) contains further provisions on freedom of transit.

### The General Exceptions: Article XX

Article XX of the GATT permits members to adopt measures that would otherwise violate GATT obligations, provided that the measures fall within one of the listed exceptions and satisfy the requirements of the introductory clause ("chapeau"). The exceptions most relevant to energy and environmental measures are those for measures:

- (b) necessary to protect human, animal, or plant life or health;
- (d) necessary to secure compliance with laws or regulations which are not inconsistent with the provisions of the GATT;
- (g) relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources, if such measures are made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption;
- (i) involving restrictions on exports of domestic materials necessary to ensure essential quantities of such materials to a domestic processing industry during periods when the domestic price of such materials is held below the world price as part of a governmental stabilisation plan;
- (j) essential to the acquisition or distribution of products in general or local short supply.

The chapeau requires that measures not be applied in a manner which would constitute a means of arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination between countries where the same conditions prevail, or a disguised restriction on international trade.

The Appellate Body has established a **two-tier test** for the application of Article XX: first, the measure must be provisionally justified under one of the specific exceptions; second, it must satisfy the requirements of the chapeau. In *US — Gasoline* (1996), the first dispute decided by the Appellate Body, the United States had adopted regulations under the Clean Air Act establishing baselines for the cleanliness of reformulated and conventional gasoline. Domestic refiners could use individual baselines, while foreign refiners were generally required to use a statutory baseline. The Appellate Body held that clean air was an exhaustible natural resource within the meaning of Article XX(g), and that the baseline rules "related to" its conservation because they bore a substantial relationship to that objective and were made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic gasoline. However, it found that the measures failed the chapeau because the United States had not pursued cooperative arrangements with the governments of foreign refiners and had not considered the costs imposed on foreign refiners, resulting in unjustifiable discrimination and a disguised restriction on international trade. The Appellate Body also emphasised that the GATT is "not to be read in clinical isolation from public international law", a statement of considerable significance for the interaction of trade law with other branches of international law, including environmental and energy law.

In *US — Shrimp* (1998), the Appellate Body held that living species, including sea turtles, could be exhaustible natural resources, interpreting the term in an evolutionary manner by reference to contemporary international environmental law, including UNCLOS, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the Rio Declaration. It held that measures conditioning market access on whether exporting members had adopted policies comparable to those of the importing member could fall within Article XX, but found that the US measure failed the chapeau because of its rigid application and the failure to negotiate with all affected exporters. Following modifications, the revised measure was found to comply in the compliance proceedings (2001). In *Brazil — Retreaded Tyres* (2007), the Appellate Body held that a measure is "necessary" under Article XX(b) where it makes a material contribution to the achievement of its objective, considering the importance of the interests at stake and the trade-restrictiveness of the measure in comparison with reasonably available alternatives, and that measures addressing climate change and other environmental problems may contribute to their objectives over time.

### The Security Exception: Article XXI

Article XXI(b) of the GATT provides that nothing in the Agreement shall be construed to prevent any member from taking any action "which it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests" relating to fissionable materials or the materials from which they are derived (sub-paragraph (i)), relating to the traffic in arms, ammunition, and implements of war and to such traffic in other goods and materials as is carried on directly or indirectly for the purpose of supplying a military establishment (sub-paragraph (ii)), or "taken in time of war or other emergency in international relations" (sub-paragraph (iii)). Article XXI is of particular relevance to energy, which is widely regarded as a matter of national security; sub-paragraph (i) expressly refers to fissionable materials, and energy security measures may be justified as measures taken in an emergency in international relations.

For decades, it was debated whether Article XXI was "self-judging" — that is, whether the invoking member's determination was beyond review by WTO panels. In *Russia — Measures Concerning Traffic in Transit* (2019), Ukraine challenged Russian restrictions on the transit of goods from Ukraine across Russia to Kazakhstan and the Kyrgyz Republic. The panel held that Article XXI is not entirely self-judging: a panel has jurisdiction to determine whether the circumstances listed in the sub-paragraphs objectively exist, including whether there is an "emergency in international relations", and whether the member has articulated its essential security interests and acted in good faith, in the sense that the measures are not implausible as measures protective of those interests. It found that the situation between Russia and Ukraine since 2014 constituted an emergency in international relations and that Russia's measures were justified. Subsequent panels, including in *US — Steel and Aluminium Products* (2022) concerning the United States' tariffs under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, applied the same approach and rejected the United States' invocation of Article XXI; the United States, which maintains that Article XXI is self-judging, appealed into the void. The security exception and energy is examined further in Chapter 25.

### Other WTO Agreements

Several other WTO agreements are relevant to energy trade. The **Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures** (SCM Agreement) disciplines subsidies, including those for renewable energy and fossil fuels, examined in section 13.3. The **Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures** (TRIMs Agreement) prohibits investment measures inconsistent with Articles III and XI of the GATT, including local content requirements, which are common in the energy sector. The **Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade** (TBT Agreement) governs technical regulations, standards, and conformity assessment procedures, examined in section 13.5. The **Agreement on Safeguards** and the **Anti-Dumping Agreement** govern trade remedies. The **GATS** governs trade in energy services. The plurilateral **Agreement on Government Procurement** (GPA), which applies only to its parties, governs procurement by covered entities, including in some cases energy utilities. The **Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights** (TRIPS) governs intellectual property, including in clean energy technologies, the transfer of which has been the subject of debate in the climate negotiations.

### Dispute Settlement and the Appellate Body Crisis

WTO disputes are resolved under the **Dispute Settlement Understanding** (DSU), which provides for consultations, adjudication by panels, appellate review by the Appellate Body, and the authorisation of countermeasures (the suspension of concessions) where a member fails to comply with adopted rulings. From December 2019, the Appellate Body ceased to function because the United States blocked the appointment of new members, citing concerns about the Appellate Body's alleged overreach, disregard of procedural deadlines, and treatment of its reports as precedent. As a result, a party that loses before a panel may "appeal into the void", preventing the adoption of the panel report and its enforcement. In response, a group of members established the **Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement** (MPIA) in 2020, under Article 25 of the DSU, which provides for appeal arbitration between participating members. The Appellate Body crisis has significantly weakened the enforcement of WTO rules, including in disputes concerning energy and natural resources, and several important energy-related panel reports — including in *EU — Energy Package* and *Indonesia — Raw Materials* — have been appealed into the void.

## 13.3 Tariffs, Subsidies, and Trade Remedies

### Tariffs on Energy Goods

Tariffs on crude oil and natural gas are generally low or zero in most major importing states, because these states depend on imports and seek to minimise energy costs. Tariffs on refined petroleum products, coal, and energy-related equipment vary more widely. Tariffs on clean energy goods — solar panels, wind turbines, and their components — have been the subject of negotiations aimed at liberalisation. The **APEC List of Environmental Goods**, agreed in 2012, committed APEC economies to reduce applied tariffs on fifty-four environmental goods, including solar panels and wind turbines, to five per cent or less by 2015. Plurilateral negotiations on an **Environmental Goods Agreement** among a group of WTO members, launched in 2014, stalled in 2016. Tariffs on clean energy goods have, however, increased in some major markets in recent years as a result of trade remedies and industrial policy, including the substantial increases in US tariffs on Chinese solar cells, batteries, and electric vehicles announced in 2024.

### The Law of Subsidies

The SCM Agreement defines a **subsidy** in Article 1 as a financial contribution by a government or public body (including direct transfers of funds, forgone revenue, the provision of goods or services other than general infrastructure, or the purchase of goods), or any form of income or price support, which confers a **benefit**. A subsidy is subject to the Agreement only if it is **specific** to an enterprise or industry or group of enterprises or industries (Article 2). The Agreement classifies subsidies into two categories: **prohibited subsidies** (Article 3) — subsidies contingent on export performance or on the use of domestic over imported goods — which are prohibited outright; and **actionable subsidies** (Article 5), which are challengeable if they cause adverse effects to the interests of other members, including injury to their domestic industry, nullification or impairment of benefits, or serious prejudice. A category of non-actionable subsidies, which included certain environmental subsidies, lapsed at the end of 1999.

**Renewable energy subsidies.** Renewable energy support schemes, including feed-in tariffs, tax credits, and grants, may constitute subsidies within the meaning of the SCM Agreement. The most significant questions concern the identification of a "benefit", the legality of local content requirements, and the treatment of support conditioned on domestic production.

In *Canada — Renewable Energy / Canada — Feed-in Tariff Program* (2013), Japan and the European Union challenged the feed-in tariff programme of the Canadian province of Ontario, which offered twenty-year contracts at guaranteed prices to generators of renewable electricity, subject to minimum requirements for domestic content in the equipment used. The Appellate Body upheld the panel's finding that the domestic content requirements violated Article III:4 of the GATT and Article 2.1 of the TRIMs Agreement, rejecting Canada's argument that the programme fell within the government procurement derogation in Article III:8(a) of the GATT, because the product procured by the government (electricity) was not in a competitive relationship with the product discriminated against (generation equipment). On the subsidy claim, the Appellate Body reversed the panel's finding that the complainants had failed to establish a benefit, holding that the benchmark for determining whether the government's purchase of electricity conferred a benefit must be a market that the government itself had created — a competitive wholesale market for electricity generated from wind and solar technologies, reflecting the government's legitimate choice of energy supply mix — rather than the overall wholesale electricity market, but was unable to complete the analysis for lack of factual findings. The Appellate Body's approach was regarded as recognising governments' freedom to create markets for renewable energy without automatically conferring a benefit, while preserving the discipline on local content requirements.

In *India — Certain Measures Relating to Solar Cells and Solar Modules* (2016), the United States challenged domestic content requirements in India's Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission. The Appellate Body upheld the panel's findings that the requirements violated Article III:4 of the GATT and Article 2.1 of the TRIMs Agreement, that the government procurement derogation did not apply, and that the requirements were not justified under Article XX(j) (products in general or local short supply), because solar cells and modules were not in short supply given the availability of imports, or under Article XX(d), because India had not identified a domestic or international legal obligation with which the measures secured compliance, India's climate obligations under the UNFCCC not being "laws or regulations" with direct effect in Indian law. In *United States — Certain Measures Relating to the Renewable Energy Sector* (2019), brought by India, the panel found that local content requirements and incentives in renewable energy programmes of several US states violated Article III:4 of the GATT; the United States appealed into the void.

These disputes established that local content requirements in renewable energy support schemes are inconsistent with WTO law, notwithstanding the climate objectives of the schemes. They have been criticised for impeding the use of industrial policy to build domestic political support for renewable energy, and the extensive local content and domestic manufacturing incentives adopted since 2022, including in the United States' Inflation Reduction Act — which conditioned the clean vehicle credit on the sourcing of battery components and critical minerals from the United States or its free trade agreement partners — have raised questions about the continuing effectiveness of these disciplines. In March 2024, China requested consultations with the United States concerning certain IRA tax credits (*United States — Certain Tax Credits under the Inflation Reduction Act*).

**Fossil fuel subsidies.** Governments provide extensive subsidies for the production and consumption of fossil fuels, estimated by the International Monetary Fund and the International Energy Agency at hundreds of billions of US dollars annually in explicit subsidies, and substantially more if unpriced externalities are included. Consumption subsidies, which keep domestic fuel prices below international market levels, are common in oil-producing and developing states, including, until 2023, Nigeria, where the petrol subsidy consumed a substantial share of government revenue before its removal in May 2023, examined in Chapter 24. The SCM Agreement has rarely been applied to fossil fuel subsidies. Consumption subsidies available to all consumers may not be "specific", and production subsidies have rarely been challenged, in part because many members provide them. A group of WTO members issued a ministerial statement on fossil fuel subsidy reform at the Eleventh Ministerial Conference in 2017, and an initiative on fossil fuel subsidy reform continues, but no binding disciplines have been agreed. The WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies, adopted in 2022 and in force since September 2025, demonstrates that sector-specific subsidy disciplines driven by environmental objectives can be negotiated, and has been cited as a potential model. At COP26 in 2021, the parties to the Glasgow Climate Pact called for the phase-out of "inefficient fossil fuel subsidies", a commitment reaffirmed at COP28.

### Trade Remedies in the Energy Sector

**Trade remedies** — anti-dumping duties, countervailing duties, and safeguard measures — have been used extensively in the clean energy sector. Under the Anti-Dumping Agreement, a member may impose anti-dumping duties on imports sold at less than normal value (dumping) which cause material injury to the domestic industry. Under Part V of the SCM Agreement, a member may impose countervailing duties to offset subsidies on imports causing injury. Under the Agreement on Safeguards, a member may temporarily restrict imports of a product whose increased imports cause or threaten serious injury to the domestic industry.

The solar industry has been the subject of numerous trade remedy actions. In 2013, the European Union imposed anti-dumping and countervailing duties on imports of solar panels and cells from China, combined with a price undertaking accepted from Chinese exporters; the measures expired in 2018. The United States has imposed anti-dumping and countervailing duties on solar cells and modules from China since 2012, extended to imports from other countries, including in South-East Asia, found to be used to circumvent the duties, and imposed a global safeguard measure on solar cells and modules in 2018 under Section 201 of the Trade Act of 1974. In October 2024, the European Union imposed definitive countervailing duties on imports of battery electric vehicles from China, following an investigation that found that the Chinese industry benefited from subsidies; China challenged the duties before the WTO.

Trade remedies have also been applied to biofuels. In *EU — Biodiesel (Argentina)* (2016), the Appellate Body held that the European Union had acted inconsistently with the Anti-Dumping Agreement by adjusting the costs of Argentine biodiesel producers to reflect international prices of soybeans rather than the lower domestic prices resulting from Argentina's export taxes on soybeans, because the producers' records reasonably reflected the costs associated with production, and the Anti-Dumping Agreement did not permit the investigating authority to disregard costs merely because they were lower than international prices owing to government intervention. The decision has implications for the treatment of distortions arising from export restrictions and dual pricing, examined in section 13.4.
