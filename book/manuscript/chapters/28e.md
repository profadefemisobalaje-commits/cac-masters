## 28.8 Developing Original Doctoral Contributions

### What is an Original Contribution?

The defining requirement of a doctorate is an **original contribution to knowledge**. University regulations typically require that a doctoral thesis demonstrate the creation and interpretation of new knowledge through original research or other advanced scholarship, of a quality to satisfy peer review, extend the forefront of the discipline, and merit publication. Originality in legal research does not require a revolutionary discovery. Estelle Phillips and Derek Pugh, in their widely used guide to doctoral study, identify many forms of originality, including: setting down a major piece of new information in writing for the first time; continuing a previously original piece of work; carrying out original work designed by others; providing a single original technique, observation, or result in an otherwise unoriginal but competent piece of research; having many original ideas, methods, and interpretations under the guidance of a supervisor; showing originality in testing someone else's idea; carrying out empirical work that has not been done before; making a synthesis that has not been made before; using already known material but with a new interpretation; trying out something in one country that has previously only been done in another; applying a particular technique to a new area; bringing new evidence to bear on an old issue; being cross-disciplinary and using different methodologies; and looking at areas that people in the discipline have not looked at before.

In energy and natural resources law, original contributions may take many forms: the first comprehensive doctrinal analysis of a new statute, such as the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 or the Electricity Act 2023, or of a body of arbitral awards; a new interpretation of an established doctrine, such as permanent sovereignty or stabilisation, in the light of the energy transition; a comparative study yielding new insights into the design of resource legal frameworks; an empirical study of the implementation of a legal regime in a particular context, such as host community trusts in a specific state; a theoretical framework explaining patterns of compliance or reform; the application of methods from another discipline, such as economics or political science, to a legal question; or a reasoned proposal for legal reform grounded in rigorous analysis.

### Identifying a Research Gap

An original contribution begins with the identification of a **research gap** — a question that has not been answered, or not answered adequately, in the existing literature. Gaps may be **empirical** (a phenomenon that has not been studied), **theoretical** (a phenomenon that existing theories fail to explain), **methodological** (a question that has not been approached with appropriate methods), **contextual** (a question studied in some jurisdictions but not others, such as an issue studied extensively in Europe but not in Africa), or **practical** (a problem in practice for which the law provides no adequate solution). Identifying a gap requires a thorough and critical **literature review**, which maps the state of knowledge, identifies debates and limitations, and situates the proposed research. The literature review is not a summary of everything written on a topic but a critical analysis organised around the research question, demonstrating why the question matters and how the research will advance knowledge.

African energy and resource law offers abundant opportunities for original research. Many legal regimes in African states have been studied only superficially or not at all; recent reforms — Nigeria's PIA and Electricity Act, the mining codes of the DRC and other states, the petroleum laws of new producers, the AfCFTA and its investment protocol — await rigorous analysis; and the perspectives of African states, communities, and scholars remain under-represented in the international literature. Doctoral research by African scholars, grounded in local knowledge and access, can make distinctive contributions to both African and global debates.

### Formulating the Research Question and Thesis

The **research question** is the foundation of the doctoral project. A good research question is **significant** (addressing an important problem), **original** (addressing a gap), **focused** (narrow enough to be answered thoroughly within the constraints of a doctorate), **researchable** (capable of being answered with available sources and methods), and **clear**. Many doctoral projects begin with questions that are too broad — "how should Africa govern its natural resources?" — and must be narrowed through successive refinement to a question that can be answered in depth — "to what extent does the legal framework for host community development trusts under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 conform to international standards of benefit-sharing, and how can it be reformed?" The main research question is typically supported by **sub-questions**, each addressed in one or more chapters of the thesis.

A doctoral thesis is not merely a report of research but an **argument**: it advances a **thesis** in the sense of a central claim or position, which the research supports and which the work defends against counter-arguments. The thesis statement should be capable of being stated concisely, and the structure of the dissertation should be organised to develop and support it. Appendix O sets out a model structure.

### The Research Proposal

Most doctoral programmes require a **research proposal**, which sets out the research question, its significance and originality, the literature review, the theoretical framework, the methodology, the ethical considerations, the expected contribution, a timeline, and a preliminary bibliography. The proposal is both a planning document and a test of the feasibility of the project. Appendix N provides a template. A strong proposal demonstrates a clear and focused question, a command of the literature, a justified methodology appropriate to the question, awareness of limitations and risks, and a realistic plan.

### Writing, Supervision, and the Viva

Doctoral research is a **process** of several years, during which the research question, arguments, and structure typically evolve. Effective doctoral researchers write regularly from the outset, rather than leaving writing until the end, since writing is itself a means of thinking and clarifying arguments. The relationship with the **supervisor** or supervisory team is central: supervisors provide guidance on the direction, methodology, and quality of the research, and doctoral researchers should seek regular feedback, agree expectations, and take responsibility for their own progress. Presenting work at **seminars and conferences** and publishing articles during the doctorate test ideas, build networks, and develop a scholarly profile.

The doctorate is typically examined through the submission of the thesis and an **oral examination** (*viva voce*), in which examiners — including at least one external examiner — question the candidate on the thesis. The examiners assess whether the thesis makes an original contribution, demonstrates a command of the field and of appropriate methods, presents a coherent and well-supported argument, and is presented to a satisfactory standard. The candidate should be able to explain and defend the research question, methodology, findings, and contribution, acknowledge limitations, and discuss implications and future research. Common outcomes include award without corrections, award with minor or major corrections, resubmission, or the award of a lower degree.

### Impact and Dissemination

Doctoral research in energy and natural resources law can have significant **impact** beyond academia — informing legislative reform, regulatory practice, judicial decisions, corporate policy, and civil society advocacy. Researchers can enhance the impact of their work by publishing in accessible forms, including policy briefs and articles in practitioner and general media, as well as in peer-reviewed journals and monographs; engaging with policymakers, regulators, and practitioners; contributing to consultations on legislative and regulatory reform, examined in Chapter 29; and sharing findings with the communities studied. The conversion of a doctoral thesis into a published monograph or a series of journal articles is an important step in an academic career. In all these activities, the scholar's credibility depends on the rigour, honesty, and independence of the underlying research.

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 28: Designing a Doctoral Research Project (Comparative Legal Research Exercise)"}
**This exercise concerns the design of research and does not involve any hypothetical parties. Where the candidate uses illustrative scenarios, they must be identified as hypothetical.**

(a) Select one of the doctoral research questions listed at the end of any chapter of this book, or formulate your own question in energy and natural resources law.

(b) Refine the question into a focused main research question and three to five sub-questions, explaining the type of each question (descriptive, explanatory, evaluative, normative, comparative, or historical).

(c) Write a literature review outline (of no more than 1,500 words) identifying the main bodies of literature relevant to your question, the principal debates, and the gap your research will address.

(d) Prepare a methodology chapter outline (of no more than 2,000 words) justifying your choice of methods — doctrinal, comparative, socio-legal, empirical, or mixed — explaining how each sub-question will be answered, and, for a comparative study, justifying the selection of jurisdictions.

(e) Identify the ethical issues your research raises, including any involving human participants, data protection, conflicts of interest, and the use of artificial intelligence tools, and explain how you will address them.

(f) State, in no more than 300 words, the original contribution your research will make.

(g) Using Appendix N, prepare a complete research proposal and present it to a peer group for critique, as in a doctoral progression review.

## Conclusion

Research methodology is the foundation of doctoral scholarship. The questions a researcher asks entail epistemological commitments — to law as a normative system, as a social phenomenon, or as an object of critique — that must be recognised and matched by appropriate methods. Doctrinal research, the traditional method of legal scholarship, remains indispensable for understanding the content and coherence of energy law, but its focus on law in the books must often be complemented by comparative, transnational, socio-legal, and empirical research that investigates the law in action, its effects, and its context. Qualitative and quantitative methods offer complementary tools, and mixed methods designs can combine their strengths. All research must be conducted with integrity — honesty in the use of sources and data, respect for participants, transparency about interests, and, in the age of artificial intelligence, rigorous verification of every authority. Effective use of legal databases and accurate citation underpin credible scholarship. And the ultimate goal — an original contribution to knowledge — requires the identification of a genuine gap, a focused question, a coherent argument, and a sustained process of research, writing, and critical engagement. For researchers in African energy and resource law, the opportunities for original and impactful scholarship are exceptional. Chapter 29 considers how such scholarship can inform the reform of energy law and the design of regulatory institutions.

::: summary
- Legal research rests on epistemological assumptions: internal perspectives (positivism, natural law, interpretivism) treat law as a normative system; external perspectives (realism, socio-legal studies, law and economics) treat it as a social phenomenon; critical perspectives (CLS, feminist theory, TWAIL, ecological approaches) expose power and bias; research questions may be descriptive, explanatory, evaluative, normative, comparative, or historical, and methods must fit the question.
- Doctrinal research identifies, interprets, and synthesises authoritative sources using source-specific interpretive conventions; it is indispensable but limited in addressing law in action, and faces distinctive challenges in multi-level, confidential, technical, and rapidly changing energy law.
- Comparative research requires a functional question, principled selection of jurisdictions, a clear *tertium comparationis*, and attention to context and transferability; transnational research studies norms crossing public–private and national–international boundaries.
- Socio-legal and empirical research investigates the implementation and effects of law through case studies, process tracing, surveys, quasi-experiments, and ethnography, using documents, interviews, observation, statistics, and remote sensing, subject to practical challenges of access, security, and data quality.
- Qualitative methods (interviews, focus groups, documentary, thematic, and discourse analysis) and quantitative methods (descriptive statistics, content analysis, econometrics, computational analysis) can be combined in mixed methods designs, and doctrinal and empirical methods are often productively combined.
- Research integrity requires honesty, accountability, and respect; fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism are misconduct; AI tools must be used transparently and every authority verified; research with human participants requires ethics approval, informed consent, confidentiality, harm avoidance, and data protection.
- Legal research relies on international, arbitral, national, and contract databases, critical use of secondary sources, systematic search strategies, and consistent, accurate citation, such as OSCOLA.
- An original contribution may take many forms; it requires the identification of a research gap, a focused research question, a defensible thesis, a sound proposal, sustained writing and supervision, and a successful oral examination, with impact achieved through dissemination and engagement.
:::
