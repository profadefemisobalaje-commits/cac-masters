## 11.7 Radioactive Waste and Decommissioning

### The Challenge of Radioactive Waste

Radioactive waste is generated at every stage of the nuclear fuel cycle and by the use of radioactive materials in medicine, industry, and research. It is classified according to its radioactivity and the duration of its hazard: very low level and low level waste, which may be disposed of in near-surface facilities; intermediate level waste, which requires greater containment; and high level waste, principally spent nuclear fuel (where it is treated as waste rather than reprocessed) and the residues of reprocessing, which generates heat and remains hazardous for many thousands of years. The management of high level waste is the most intractable problem of nuclear energy, both technically and politically, and the long timescales involved raise distinctive questions of intergenerational equity.

### The International Framework

The **Joint Convention on the Safety of Spent Fuel Management and on the Safety of Radioactive Waste Management** (1997) is the only legally binding international instrument addressing the safety of spent fuel and radioactive waste management on a global scale. It applies to spent fuel and radioactive waste resulting from civilian applications, and requires contracting parties to ensure that at all stages of management individuals, society, and the environment are adequately protected against radiological hazards, including by taking into account interdependencies among the different steps of management, providing for effective protection of individuals, society, and the environment, taking into account biological, chemical, and other hazards, striving to avoid actions that impose reasonably predictable impacts on future generations greater than those permitted for the current generation, and aiming to avoid imposing undue burdens on future generations (Articles 4 and 11). The preamble affirms that radioactive waste should, as far as is compatible with the safety of the management of such material, be disposed of in the state in which it was generated, while recognising that in certain circumstances safe and efficient management might be fostered through agreements among parties to use facilities in one of them.

Article 27 of the Joint Convention regulates transboundary movements of spent fuel and radioactive waste, requiring prior notification to and consent of the state of destination, and prohibiting shipments to destinations south of latitude 60 degrees South for storage or disposal. The Bamako Convention on the Ban of the Import into Africa and the Control of Transboundary Movement and Management of Hazardous Wastes within Africa (1991) prohibits the import of hazardous wastes, including radioactive wastes, into Africa from non-parties, and the Treaty of Pelindaba prohibits the dumping of radioactive wastes in the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone. The dumping of radioactive waste at sea is prohibited by the 1996 Protocol to the London Convention on the Prevention of Marine Pollution by Dumping of Wastes and Other Matter (1972). The Joint Convention reflects the principle of national responsibility for radioactive waste, which is also expressed in Article 4 of the Euratom Radioactive Waste and Spent Fuel Directive (Council Directive 2011/70/Euratom), under which each member state has ultimate responsibility for management of the spent fuel and radioactive waste generated in it, and must establish a national policy and national programme. Article 4(4) of the Directive provides that radioactive waste shall be disposed of in the member state in which it was generated, unless an agreement has been concluded for the use of a disposal facility in another member state or a third country, subject to strict conditions.

### Deep Geological Disposal

There is a broad scientific consensus that deep geological disposal — the emplacement of high level waste and spent fuel in engineered repositories several hundred metres underground in stable geological formations, relying on a combination of engineered and natural barriers to isolate the waste for the required period — is the most appropriate long-term solution for high level waste. Progress in implementing geological disposal has nevertheless been slow, owing principally to public opposition and the difficulty of securing the consent of host communities.

**Finland** is the first country to construct a deep geological repository for spent fuel. The Onkalo repository at Olkiluoto, developed by Posiva, received a construction licence in 2015 following a decision-in-principle ratified by Parliament in 2001, and commenced trial operations in 2024. The Finnish process has been regarded as a model because it combined a staged decision-making process, the right of the host municipality (Eurajoki) to veto the decision-in-principle, an independent and trusted regulator (the Radiation and Nuclear Safety Authority, STUK), and the long-term commitment of the waste producers. **Sweden** approved the construction of a repository at Forsmark in 2022, following a process in which the host municipality consented. **France** has pursued the Cigéo project at Bure under legislation adopted in 1991, 2006, and 2016, which introduced the concept of reversibility — the ability to retrieve waste for a period after emplacement — into the legal framework. The emphasis in these processes on voluntarism, consent-based siting, and staged decision-making reflects lessons learned from earlier failures.

**The United States** illustrates the consequences of a failed siting process. The **Nuclear Waste Policy Act** of 1982 established a federal responsibility for the disposal of spent fuel and required utilities to pay a fee into a Nuclear Waste Fund in exchange for a federal commitment to begin accepting spent fuel by 31 January 1998. The 1987 amendments to the Act designated Yucca Mountain in Nevada as the sole site to be characterised, over Nevada's opposition. The Department of Energy applied for a construction licence in 2008, but the Obama administration sought to withdraw the application in 2010 and ceased funding. In *In re Aiken County* 725 F.3d 255 (DC Cir 2013), the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit issued a writ of mandamus requiring the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to continue the licensing process with the funds available, holding that the Commission could not decline to comply with the statutory mandate on policy grounds. The project nevertheless remained stalled. The federal government's failure to accept spent fuel by 1998 has resulted in extensive litigation by utilities for breach of contract, in which the government has been held liable for billions of dollars in damages for the costs of on-site storage, and the fee was suspended in 2014 following a ruling of the D.C. Circuit. Efforts to license private interim storage facilities were addressed by the Supreme Court in *Nuclear Regulatory Commission v Texas* (2025), which held that the State of Texas and other challengers, which had not been parties to the licensing proceeding, were not entitled to seek judicial review of a licence granted for an interim storage facility.

### Decommissioning

Decommissioning comprises the administrative and technical actions taken to allow the removal of some or all regulatory controls from a nuclear facility at the end of its life, including decontamination, dismantling, and the management of resulting waste. Strategies include **immediate dismantling**, in which decommissioning commences shortly after permanent shutdown, and **deferred dismantling** (safe enclosure), in which the facility is maintained in a safe condition for several decades to allow radioactive decay before dismantling. IAEA safety standards and the Euratom Directives increasingly favour immediate dismantling, on grounds including the availability of staff familiar with the facility and the avoidance of transferring burdens to future generations.

**Financing.** The legal regulation of decommissioning financing is critical, because decommissioning costs are incurred decades after the revenue from operation has been earned, and there is a risk that operators will become insolvent or that funds will be inadequate. The principal models are segregated external funds, in which operators make contributions during operation to an independently managed fund; internal provisions on the operator's balance sheet; and state funding. Article 9 of the Euratom Radioactive Waste Directive requires member states to ensure that the national framework requires that adequate financial resources be available for the implementation of national programmes, with due regard to the responsibility of waste generators. The United Kingdom's arrangements for new nuclear power stations, under the Energy Act 2008, require operators to have an approved Funded Decommissioning Programme before construction may begin.

### The German Nuclear Phase-Out

Germany's phase-out of nuclear energy illustrates the legal consequences of the political decision to end nuclear power. In 2002, the government and the nuclear operators reached a consensus, implemented by legislation, under which each plant was allocated a residual quantity of electricity it could produce. In 2010, the government extended the operating lives of the plants, but following the Fukushima accident in 2011, the Thirteenth Amendment to the Atomic Energy Act revoked the extension and required all plants to close by the end of 2022. The operators challenged the amendment before the Federal Constitutional Court, and the Swedish state-owned company Vattenfall also brought a claim under the Energy Charter Treaty (*Vattenfall AB and others v Germany*, ICSID Case No ARB/12/12).

In its judgment of 6 December 2016, the Federal Constitutional Court held that the accelerated phase-out was largely compatible with the Basic Law: it did not constitute an expropriation, and it was, in principle, a permissible determination of the content and limits of property under Article 14 of the Basic Law, justified by the legislature's reassessment of the risks of nuclear energy after Fukushima. However, the Court held that the amendment was unconstitutional in so far as it failed to provide compensation for the residual electricity quantities allocated in 2002 that certain operators could not use, and for investments made in reliance on the 2010 extension. Germany enacted compensation legislation, and in 2021 reached a settlement with the operators, under which they received approximately EUR 2.4 billion in compensation, including around EUR 1.4 billion for Vattenfall, which discontinued its ECT claim. The last three German nuclear power plants closed in April 2023, after a brief extension due to the energy crisis.

Separately, the **Act on the Reorganisation of Responsibility in Nuclear Waste Management** (2017) transferred responsibility for the interim and final storage of radioactive waste from the operators to the state, in exchange for the payment by the operators of approximately EUR 24 billion into a public fund (KENFO), while the operators remained responsible for decommissioning and dismantling their plants and for packaging the waste. This arrangement illustrates a model for the allocation of long-term liability between private operators and the state.

## 11.8 Small Modular Reactors and Emerging Legal Issues

### The Promise of Small Modular and Advanced Reactors

**Small modular reactors** (SMRs) are nuclear reactors with a power capacity of up to around 300 MW(e) per unit, designed to be manufactured in factories and transported to sites for installation, in contrast to large reactors constructed on-site. Proponents claim that SMRs offer lower upfront capital costs, shorter construction times, enhanced safety through passive safety features and smaller radioactive inventories, siting flexibility (including at the sites of retiring coal plants and in remote locations), and suitability for smaller electricity grids, including those of many developing countries. **Advanced reactors** — including high-temperature gas-cooled reactors, molten salt reactors, sodium-cooled fast reactors, and microreactors of a few megawatts — employ non-light-water technologies with potential advantages in safety, efficiency, waste management, and industrial heat applications. Several SMR designs have progressed towards deployment: the BWRX-300 at Darlington in Ontario, Canada, received a licence to construct from the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission in 2025; the United Kingdom selected a Rolls-Royce SMR design for deployment in 2025; and the floating nuclear power plant *Akademik Lomonosov*, comprising two small reactors mounted on a barge, has operated in the Russian Arctic since 2019–2020. Technology companies have concluded agreements to purchase electricity from SMRs and other advanced reactors to power data centres.

### Licensing Challenges

Existing licensing frameworks were developed for large light-water reactors and may not be well suited to SMRs and advanced reactors, whose designs, safety features, and fuel cycles differ substantially. Regulators have responded with technology-inclusive and risk-informed approaches. In the United States, the **Nuclear Energy Innovation and Modernization Act** of 2019 required the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to develop a technology-inclusive regulatory framework for advanced reactors, and the **ADVANCE Act** of 2024 directed the Commission to modernise its mission and licensing processes, reduce fees for advanced reactor applicants, and expedite reviews. The NRC approved the design of NuScale's SMR in 2020 and an uprated version in 2025, the first SMR designs certified or approved in the United States. In the United Kingdom, the Generic Design Assessment process permits the assessment of reactor designs independently of specific sites.

Key licensing issues include the scope and size of **emergency planning zones** (which proponents argue can be reduced for SMRs, given their smaller inventories and passive safety, enabling siting closer to population centres and industrial users); **staffing requirements** and remote or autonomous operation; the treatment of multi-module plants; **security requirements** proportionate to the risk; and the qualification of **novel fuels**, such as high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU), which raises safeguards and security considerations.

### International Harmonisation

The economic case for SMRs depends on serial factory production of standardised designs, which in turn depends on the ability to deploy the same design in multiple countries without extensive redesign to meet different national requirements. Licensing has traditionally been an exclusively national prerogative, and differences in national requirements are a significant barrier to SMR deployment. The IAEA launched the **Nuclear Harmonization and Standardization Initiative** (NHSI) in 2022 to promote regulatory collaboration and industrial standardisation for SMRs, and regulators have entered into cooperation arrangements, including agreements between the United States and Canadian regulators on joint reviews of advanced reactor designs. Proposals for mutual recognition of design assessments, or for an international design certification, raise questions of sovereignty and regulatory accountability: under the CNS and IAEA standards, responsibility for safety cannot be delegated by the host state, which must retain an independent and competent regulatory body capable of making its own judgments.

### Transportable and Floating Reactors

Transportable reactors — including floating nuclear power plants and microreactors designed to be moved between sites — raise novel legal questions under international nuclear law, which was developed for stationary installations. These include the determination of the "installation state" for the purposes of safety, safeguards, and liability; the application of the liability conventions to reactors in transit or on the high seas or in the territorial sea of a foreign state; the application of the law of the sea to floating reactors (including innocent passage and the rights of coastal states); the allocation of safeguards responsibilities where a reactor is fuelled in one state and operated in another; and the security of reactors in transit. The IAEA has studied these questions, but the international legal framework remains incomplete.

### Safeguards by Design and Proliferation Considerations

SMRs and advanced reactors raise safeguards considerations relating to the use of higher-enriched fuels, novel fuel forms (such as pebbles or molten salt fuels, which complicate item counting), long-life cores that are not refuelled for many years, and deployment in large numbers in many locations, including in states with limited nuclear infrastructure. The IAEA promotes "safeguards by design", the integration of safeguards considerations into the design of nuclear facilities from the earliest stages, and the participation of the IAEA in the design process.

### Newcomer States and Nuclear Governance in Africa

SMRs are frequently marketed to newcomer states, including in Africa, as a means of introducing nuclear power at a scale compatible with smaller grids and financing capacities. However, the IAEA Milestones Approach applies equally to SMRs: a newcomer state must establish a comprehensive legal framework covering safety, security, safeguards, and liability; an independent and competent regulator; human resources; and arrangements for waste management, decommissioning, and financing, regardless of the size of the reactor. The vendor-financed "build-own-operate" model, under which a foreign state-owned vendor finances, builds, owns, and operates a nuclear plant in the host state, as in Rosatom's Akkuyu project in Turkey, may reduce the burden on the host state but raises questions of long-term dependence, regulatory independence from a powerful foreign operator, and the allocation of liability for waste and decommissioning. Egypt's El Dabaa project, under construction with Rosatom since 2022 with four VVER-1200 reactors and financed largely by a Russian state loan, will be the largest nuclear project in Africa. South Africa's Koeberg plant, the only operating nuclear power plant in Africa, received a twenty-year extension of the operating licence of its first unit in 2024. The development of nuclear governance in Africa, including the role of AFCONE and regional cooperation in regulation, is a significant subject for doctoral research.

### Financing, Taxonomy, and Market Design

The cost and financing of nuclear power are as important as its regulation. Large nuclear projects have high capital costs, long construction times, and a history of cost overruns, which make them difficult to finance in liberalised markets without state support. Governments have adopted a range of support mechanisms: CfDs, as used for Hinkley Point C in the United Kingdom; the **regulated asset base** (RAB) model, introduced in the United Kingdom by the Nuclear Energy (Financing) Act 2022 and applied to the Sizewell C project, under which investors receive a regulated return during construction funded by consumers; direct state ownership, as in France's full nationalisation of EDF in 2023; and state loans and guarantees. The inclusion of nuclear energy in the EU taxonomy, the removal of the World Bank's longstanding restriction on financing nuclear projects in 2025, and the pledges made at COP28 to triple nuclear capacity have improved the prospects of access to sustainable finance.
