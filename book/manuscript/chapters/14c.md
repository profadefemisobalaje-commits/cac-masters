## 14.4 National Treatment and Most-Favoured-Nation Treatment

### The Relative Standards

National treatment and MFN treatment are **relative** standards: they do not prescribe an absolute level of treatment, but require that foreign investors and their investments be treated no less favourably than domestic investors (national treatment) or investors of third states (MFN treatment) in like circumstances. They protect against discrimination on grounds of nationality. In the energy sector, discrimination claims arise from measures favouring national oil companies or state-owned utilities, from local content requirements, from preferential fiscal or regulatory treatment of domestic or third-state investors, and from the differential application of regulatory measures.

### National Treatment

A typical national treatment clause requires each party to accord to investors of the other party and their investments treatment no less favourable than that which it accords, in like circumstances, to its own investors and their investments with respect to the establishment, acquisition, expansion, management, conduct, operation, and sale or other disposition of investments. The analysis typically involves three steps: identifying the appropriate comparator in "like circumstances"; determining whether the foreign investor was accorded less favourable treatment; and determining whether the difference in treatment is justified by a legitimate, non-discriminatory policy rationale.

The identification of **like circumstances** has been contested. In *SD Myers Inc v Canada* (UNCITRAL, 2000), concerning Canada's ban on the export of PCB waste, which prevented a US company from processing Canadian waste in the United States, the tribunal held that the assessment of like circumstances must take into account circumstances that would justify governmental regulations that treat investors differently in order to protect the public interest, and that the US investor was in like circumstances with Canadian waste processors because they were in the same economic or business sector and competed for the same business. It found a breach of national treatment, holding that Canada's measure was designed to protect the Canadian industry rather than to protect the environment. In *Occidental Exploration and Production Co v Ecuador* (LCIA, 2004), concerning the refusal of value added tax refunds to oil exporters, the tribunal adopted a broad comparison, holding that "in like situations" in the US–Ecuador BIT did not refer to the oil sector alone but to exporters generally, and that Occidental had been treated less favourably than exporters of other products, such as flowers and seafood, who received refunds; Ecuador's subsequent challenge to the award in the English courts was unsuccessful. In *Methanex Corp v United States* (UNCITRAL, 2005), concerning California's ban on the gasoline additive MTBE, the tribunal held that the appropriate comparator was domestic producers of methanol, not producers of the competing additive ethanol, and found no discrimination.

The jurisprudence suggests that tribunals will examine whether a difference in treatment between foreign and domestic investors is reasonably related to a legitimate public policy objective that does not distinguish, on its face or de facto, between foreign and domestic investors, and that the burden lies initially on the claimant to establish less favourable treatment of an investor in like circumstances, and then shifts to the state to justify the difference.

### National Oil Companies and State Enterprises

The privileged position of national oil companies and state-owned utilities in many host states raises questions of national treatment. Host states commonly reserve exclusive rights or preferential terms to NOCs — for example, the right to participate in all licences on a carried basis, preferential access to acreage, or exemption from regulatory requirements. Most investment treaties permit such reservations, either through exceptions in the treaty text, as in the annexes of non-conforming measures in US and Canadian treaties, or because the NOC is not in like circumstances with private investors given its public functions. Under the USMCA, Mexico reserved its constitutional ownership of hydrocarbons. Under the ECT, contracting parties could list exceptions to national treatment in the pre-investment phase. Where no exception applies, discriminatory treatment favouring NOCs may breach national treatment, unless justified by a legitimate public policy. The prohibition of **performance requirements** in some treaties, such as Article 5 of the ECT (which incorporates the TRIMs Agreement) and the performance requirements articles of US and Canadian treaties, further constrains local content requirements imposed on foreign energy investors.

### Most-Favoured-Nation Treatment

The MFN clause requires each party to accord to investors of the other party treatment no less favourable than that which it accords, in like circumstances, to investors of any third state. Its most significant and contested application concerns not discrimination in fact but the **importation of more favourable provisions** from other treaties concluded by the host state.

**Procedural provisions.** In *Emilio Agustín Maffezini v Spain* (ICSID, 2000), the tribunal held that the MFN clause in the Argentina–Spain BIT allowed the claimant to rely on the more favourable dispute settlement provisions of the Chile–Spain BIT, which did not contain the eighteen-month domestic litigation requirement of the Argentina–Spain BIT, while noting that the MFN clause could not be used to override fundamental public policy considerations that the parties had envisaged as conditions of their acceptance of arbitration. In *Plama Consortium Ltd v Bulgaria* (ICSID, 2005), the tribunal took the opposite view, holding that an MFN provision in a basic treaty does not incorporate by reference dispute settlement provisions set forth in another treaty, in whole or in part, unless the MFN provision leaves no doubt that the contracting parties intended to incorporate them. Arbitral practice has remained divided, and many recent treaties expressly exclude dispute settlement provisions from the scope of the MFN clause.

**Substantive provisions.** Tribunals have more readily permitted the use of MFN clauses to import substantive standards of protection from other treaties. In *White Industries Australia Ltd v India* (UNCITRAL, 2011), the tribunal allowed the claimant to rely on the MFN clause in the Australia–India BIT to import the obligation to provide "effective means of asserting claims and enforcing rights" from the India–Kuwait BIT, and found that India had breached that obligation through the nine-year delay of its courts in dealing with the enforcement of an arbitral award. Some recent treaties exclude the importation of substantive standards from other treaties, providing that substantive obligations in other treaties do not in themselves constitute "treatment" for the purposes of MFN.

## 14.5 Direct and Indirect Expropriation

### Expropriation in Treaty Law and Custom

The customary international law of expropriation, including the historical controversy over the standard of compensation and the nationalisations of the twentieth century, has been examined in Chapter 3. This section examines the treaty standard of protection against expropriation, focusing on the distinction between direct and indirect expropriation and the boundary between compensable expropriation and non-compensable regulation, which is the central issue in modern energy disputes.

Investment treaties typically prohibit expropriation or nationalisation of investments, directly or indirectly through measures having an effect equivalent to expropriation, except for a public purpose, on a non-discriminatory basis, in accordance with due process of law, and against the payment of compensation. Most treaties specify that compensation shall be prompt, adequate, and effective, and shall be equivalent to the fair market value of the investment immediately before the expropriation or before the impending expropriation became public knowledge — effectively incorporating the "Hull formula". An expropriation that satisfies all the conditions is lawful, and the state must pay the compensation specified in the treaty. An expropriation that fails to satisfy them — for example, because no compensation is paid, or because it is discriminatory — is unlawful, and the state's obligation is to make full reparation under customary international law, as articulated by the Permanent Court of International Justice in *Factory at Chorzów* (1928), which may result in higher compensation, including consequential losses and the value of the investment at the date of the award where that is higher than its value at the date of the expropriation, as held in *ADC Affiliate Ltd v Hungary* (ICSID, 2006). The valuation of damages is examined in Chapter 26.

### Direct Expropriation

**Direct expropriation** involves the formal transfer of title to the investment to the state or a third party, or its outright physical seizure. Direct expropriations of energy investments were common in the era of the great nationalisations and have recurred in the twenty-first century. In Venezuela, the government of President Chávez required foreign investors in the heavy oil projects of the Orinoco Belt to convert their interests into minority stakes in joint ventures controlled by the state oil company PDVSA in 2007, and expropriated the interests of those that refused, including ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips. In *ConocoPhillips Petrozuata BV and others v Venezuela* (ICSID, 2019), the tribunal held that Venezuela had unlawfully expropriated ConocoPhillips' investments, having failed to negotiate compensation in good faith, and awarded around USD 8.7 billion; an annulment committee upheld the award in 2025. In *Venezuela Holdings BV (Mobil) v Venezuela* (ICSID, 2014), the tribunal found that the expropriation was not unlawful merely because the compensation offered was inadequate, and awarded around USD 1.6 billion, an amount substantially reduced on annulment in 2017. The enforcement of these and other awards against Venezuela, including through the court-ordered sale of shares in CITGO, a US refining subsidiary of PDVSA, is examined in Chapter 26.

### Indirect Expropriation

**Indirect expropriation** occurs where the state, without formally transferring title, takes measures that substantially deprive the investor of the use, enjoyment, or value of its investment. Indirect expropriation may result from a single measure or from a series of measures ("creeping expropriation"). Its identification raises the central question of where the line is to be drawn between compensable expropriation and the non-compensable exercise of the state's regulatory powers.

Tribunals have adopted several approaches. Under the **sole effect doctrine**, the decisive criterion is the effect of the measure on the investor — whether it results in a substantial deprivation of the economic value or control of the investment — irrespective of the state's intention or purpose. In *Metalclad Corp v Mexico* (ICSID, 2000), the tribunal held that expropriation includes covert or incidental interference with the use of property which has the effect of depriving the owner, in whole or in significant part, of the use or reasonably expected economic benefit of property, even if not necessarily to the obvious benefit of the host state, and that it need not consider the motivation or intent of the measure. Under the **police powers doctrine**, by contrast, the state is not liable to pay compensation for the bona fide exercise of its regulatory powers in the public interest, such as the protection of health, safety, or the environment, even if the measure causes substantial economic harm. In *Methanex Corp v United States* (UNCITRAL, 2005), the tribunal held that, as a matter of general international law, a non-discriminatory regulation for a public purpose, which is enacted in accordance with due process and which affects, among others, a foreign investor or investment, is not deemed expropriatory and compensable unless specific commitments had been given by the regulating government to the investor contemplating investment that the government would refrain from such regulation. In *Philip Morris Brands Sàrl v Uruguay* (ICSID, 2016), the tribunal held that Uruguay's tobacco control measures — a single presentation requirement and enlarged health warnings — were a valid exercise of the state's police powers for the protection of public health and did not constitute an expropriation.

Between these poles, many tribunals adopt a **balancing** or proportionality approach, considering the economic impact of the measure, its interference with the investor's reasonable investment-backed expectations, and its character, including its purpose and proportionality. In *Tecmed v Mexico* (ICSID, 2003), the tribunal held that, in determining whether regulatory measures are expropriatory, it must consider whether such measures are proportional to the public interest presumably protected thereby and to the protection legally granted to investments, taking into account that the significance of the impact has a key role in deciding the proportionality.

Modern treaties increasingly codify the analysis of indirect expropriation. The US Model BIT (2012), the Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement between Canada and the European Union (CETA), and many other treaties contain annexes providing that the determination of whether a measure constitutes an indirect expropriation requires a case-by-case, fact-based inquiry considering the economic impact of the measure, the extent to which it interferes with distinct, reasonable investment-backed expectations, and its character, and that, except in rare circumstances, non-discriminatory regulatory actions designed and applied to protect legitimate public welfare objectives, such as public health, safety, and the environment, do not constitute indirect expropriations. CETA adds that the rare circumstances include those where the impact of a measure is so severe in light of its purpose that it appears manifestly excessive.

### Fiscal Measures and Expropriation

Fiscal measures — taxes, royalties, and windfall levies — are a common source of energy investment disputes, given the volatility of commodity prices and the pressure on host states to capture windfall profits. States are generally entitled to impose taxes, and the imposition of taxation is not, in itself, expropriatory. However, taxation may amount to expropriation where it is confiscatory, discriminatory, or an abuse of the taxing power. Many treaties, including Article 21 of the ECT, carve out taxation measures from most treaty obligations, while preserving the expropriation provision, often subject to a referral of the question whether the tax is expropriatory to the competent tax authorities of the parties.

In *Burlington Resources Inc v Ecuador* (ICSID, 2012), Ecuador had enacted Law 42, imposing a levy of first fifty and then ninety-nine per cent on oil companies' "extraordinary revenues" — revenues resulting from oil prices above the reference price in their production sharing contracts. The tribunal held that the windfall levy at fifty per cent, and even at ninety-nine per cent, did not in itself constitute an expropriation, because the investment continued to be profitable and the investor retained control of it, although the subsequent physical takeover of the oil blocks constituted an unlawful expropriation. In *Perenco Ecuador Ltd v Ecuador* (ICSID, 2014), a tribunal found that the ninety-nine per cent levy breached the FET standard, given the specific commitments in Perenco's contracts. Both tribunals subsequently considered Ecuador's counterclaims for environmental damage, examined in section 14.7.

### The Yukos Arbitrations

The *Yukos* arbitrations are the largest expropriation cases in the history of investment arbitration. Yukos Oil Company, Russia's largest oil producer in the early 2000s, was subjected from 2003 to a series of tax reassessments, asset freezes, and criminal proceedings against its senior executives, including its chief executive, Mikhail Khodorkovsky, culminating in the auction of its principal production subsidiary, Yuganskneftegaz, which was acquired by the state-owned Rosneft, and the company's bankruptcy in 2006. The former majority shareholders brought claims under the ECT, which Russia had signed and applied provisionally under Article 45 pending ratification. In 2014, tribunals constituted under the UNCITRAL Rules and administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration held that Russia's measures, taken as a whole, had the effect of an expropriation of Yukos and that their primary objective was not to collect taxes but to bankrupt Yukos and appropriate its assets, and awarded around USD 50 billion in damages.

In 2016, the District Court of The Hague set aside the awards, holding that the tribunals lacked jurisdiction because Russia's provisional application of the ECT under Article 45 was limited to provisions consistent with its constitution, laws, and regulations, which did not permit investor–state arbitration. The Hague Court of Appeal reversed that judgment in 2020, holding that Article 45 required an assessment of whether the principle of provisional application as such was consistent with Russian law, rather than whether each provision was consistent. The Supreme Court of the Netherlands upheld the Court of Appeal's ruling on jurisdiction in 2021, but remitted the case for consideration of Russia's argument that the awards were procured by fraud, which the Amsterdam Court of Appeal rejected in 2024, subject to Russia's further appeal. The shareholders have pursued enforcement in several jurisdictions. The *Yukos* saga illustrates the interaction of investment arbitration with the provisional application of treaties under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, the role of national courts at the seat of arbitration, and the challenges of enforcing awards against a non-compliant state.
