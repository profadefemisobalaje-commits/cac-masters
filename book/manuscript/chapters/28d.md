## 28.6 Research Ethics and Academic Integrity

### The Foundations of Research Integrity

**Research integrity** refers to the adherence to ethical principles and professional standards in the conduct of research. Its core values, as articulated in instruments such as the **Singapore Statement on Research Integrity** (2010) and the **European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity** (revised 2023), include **honesty** in all aspects of research; **accountability** for the conduct of research; **professional courtesy and fairness** in working with others; **good stewardship** of research on behalf of others; **reliability** in ensuring the quality of research; and **respect** for colleagues, research participants, society, ecosystems, and the environment. Universities and research funders typically require doctoral researchers to comply with institutional codes of research integrity and to complete training in research ethics.

### Research Misconduct

**Research misconduct** is conventionally defined as **fabrication**, **falsification**, and **plagiarism** in proposing, performing, reviewing, or reporting research. **Fabrication** is making up data or results; **falsification** is manipulating research materials, processes, or data, or changing or omitting results, so that the research is not accurately represented. In legal research, falsification may take subtler forms, such as misrepresenting the holding of a case, quoting selectively from a source so as to distort its meaning, citing a source for a proposition it does not support, or ignoring contrary authority. **Plagiarism** is the appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results, or words without giving appropriate credit. It includes verbatim copying without quotation marks and attribution, close paraphrasing without attribution, the use of another's ideas or structure without acknowledgement, and **self-plagiarism** — the reuse of one's own previously published or submitted work without disclosure. Other unacceptable practices include the misrepresentation of credentials, the manipulation of authorship, and the citation of sources one has not read.

The consequences of misconduct for a doctoral researcher can be severe, including failure of the thesis, revocation of the degree, and lasting damage to professional reputation; for legal practitioners, misrepresentation of authorities to a court is also a serious breach of professional duty. Universities use text-matching software to detect plagiarism, but integrity depends ultimately on the researcher's own practices: keeping careful records of sources, distinguishing clearly in notes between quotation, paraphrase, and one's own ideas, and citing every source on which the work relies.

### Artificial Intelligence and Research Integrity

The emergence of **generative artificial intelligence** tools capable of producing fluent text, summarising sources, and answering legal questions has created new challenges for research integrity. These tools can assist researchers in searching, summarising, translating, and editing, but they also present serious risks. They may generate **fabricated authorities** — plausible but non-existent cases, statutes, and articles — and misstate the content of real sources, as several courts have discovered when lawyers submitted AI-generated briefs citing fictitious cases: in ***Mata v Avianca*** (2023), a US federal court sanctioned lawyers for submitting a brief containing fictitious citations generated by an AI tool, and in ***R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey*** (2025), the Divisional Court of the High Court of England and Wales warned that lawyers who cite fictitious AI-generated authorities may face regulatory and even criminal consequences. The use of AI to generate text presented as one's own may constitute plagiarism or misrepresentation under institutional policies, and the use of AI tools may raise issues of confidentiality, copyright, and bias.

Universities, publishers, and courts have issued **guidance** on the use of AI. Common principles include that researchers remain fully responsible for the accuracy and integrity of their work; that AI tools cannot be credited as authors; that the use of AI in research and writing must be disclosed in accordance with institutional and publisher policies; that all authorities and factual claims must be verified against primary sources; and that confidential or personal data must not be entered into AI tools without appropriate safeguards. The doctoral researcher should consult and comply with the policies of his or her institution, which vary considerably, and should treat AI outputs as unverified leads rather than authorities. The principle that every proposition of law must be checked against the authoritative source is the foundation of reliable legal research, whatever tools are used.

### Ethics of Research Involving Human Participants

Research involving **human participants** — interviews, surveys, focus groups, observation — is subject to ethical principles derived from instruments such as the **Belmont Report** (1979), which identified the principles of **respect for persons**, **beneficence**, and **justice**, and the **Declaration of Helsinki** (in medical research). Universities typically require such research to be approved by a **research ethics committee** or institutional review board before it begins. Key ethical requirements include:

1. **Informed consent**: participants must be informed of the purpose of the research, what participation involves, any risks, how the data will be used and stored, and their right to withdraw, and must consent freely. In communities with low literacy or different cultural norms, consent processes must be adapted, for example by oral consent and explanation in local languages, and community-level consent may be appropriate in addition to individual consent, particularly in research with indigenous peoples, consistent with the principle of free, prior, and informed consent examined in Chapter 21.
2. **Confidentiality and anonymity**: researchers must protect the identity of participants where promised, which may be crucial for officials, employees, and community members who could face retaliation for speaking about corruption, pollution, or conflict.
3. **Avoidance of harm**: researchers must assess and minimise risks to participants, including physical, psychological, legal, social, and economic risks, and risks to themselves, particularly in conflict-affected areas.
4. **Data protection**: personal data must be collected, stored, and processed in accordance with data protection law — such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation and Nigeria's **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023** — and institutional data management plans.
5. **Fairness and reciprocity**: researchers should consider how the research can benefit the communities studied, share findings with participants in accessible forms, and avoid "extractive" research that takes knowledge from communities without return — a concern that parallels the critique of extractive industries themselves.

### Conflicts of Interest and Funding

Research in energy law is often funded by, or conducted in association with, governments, companies, international organisations, or NGOs with interests in the outcome. Researchers must **disclose conflicts of interest**, including funding sources, consultancy relationships, and personal interests, and must preserve their **independence** in designing, conducting, and reporting research. The influence of industry funding on research on fossil fuels and climate change has been the subject of scholarly and public concern, and researchers should be transparent about funding and resist pressure to suppress or shape findings.

### Normative Commitments and Objectivity

Energy and resource law raises questions on which researchers may hold strong normative commitments — to climate action, to resource sovereignty, to community rights, or to investment protection. Such commitments are not incompatible with rigorous scholarship, and critical scholarship explicitly embraces normative goals. But the researcher must distinguish between advocacy and scholarship: scholarly work must engage fairly with opposing arguments and evidence, acknowledge uncertainty and limitations, and allow conclusions to be determined by the analysis rather than by prior commitment. Being explicit about one's normative framework is a mark of rigour, not bias.

## 28.7 Legal Databases, Primary Authorities, and Citation Methods

### The Research Environment

Legal research has been transformed by digital technology. The doctoral researcher in energy law now has access to vast collections of primary and secondary materials through commercial databases, free public resources, and institutional repositories. The challenge is less access than **navigation**: identifying the authoritative and relevant sources efficiently, evaluating their reliability, and keeping up to date in a rapidly changing field.

### Primary Sources

**International law sources** include: the **United Nations Treaty Collection**, which publishes the texts and status of treaties registered with the UN; the websites of treaty bodies and international organisations, such as the UNFCCC, the International Seabed Authority, the IEA, IRENA, and the Energy Charter Secretariat; the judgments, advisory opinions, and pleadings of the **International Court of Justice**, the **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea**, and the **Permanent Court of Arbitration**; the documents of the **International Law Commission**; the WTO's dispute settlement database; and the decisions of regional human rights bodies, such as the HUDOC database of the European Court of Human Rights and the decisions of the African Commission and Court on Human and Peoples' Rights.

**Investment arbitration sources** include the **ICSID** website, which publishes cases and many awards; **italaw**, a free repository of investment treaty awards and documents; the **UNCTAD Investment Policy Hub**, which provides databases of investment treaties and investor–state cases; and commercial services such as Investment Arbitration Reporter and Global Arbitration Review. **Commercial arbitration** awards are less accessible, but the ICC and other institutions publish redacted extracts, and the Kluwer Arbitration database provides extensive materials.

**National law sources** vary by jurisdiction. For **Nigeria**, sources include the official **Federal Government Gazette**; the **Laws of the Federation of Nigeria** (2004 revision, with subsequent legislation); the websites of the National Assembly, regulators (NUPRC, NMDPRA, NERC, NEITI), and ministries; and law reports including the **Nigerian Weekly Law Reports** (NWLR), the **Supreme Court of Nigeria Judgments** (SCJ), and the **Law Pavilion** and **LawNigeria** electronic databases. For the **United Kingdom**, **legislation.gov.uk** and the **National Archives' Find Case Law** service provide free access to legislation and judgments, and **BAILII** provides free access to judgments of the UK and Ireland, with Westlaw and LexisNexis offering comprehensive commercial coverage. For the **United States**, sources include the US Code, the Code of Federal Regulations, the Federal Register, and court decisions on PACER, CourtListener, and commercial databases. The **EUR-Lex** database provides EU legislation and case law, and the **CURIA** website the judgments of the Court of Justice. **ResourceContracts.org**, developed by the Natural Resource Governance Institute, the World Bank, and the Columbia Center on Sustainable Investment, provides a repository of publicly available oil, gas, and mining contracts, with annotations; the **EITI** publishes country reports and contract disclosures.

### Secondary Sources

Secondary sources include **treatises and textbooks**; **journals**, including specialised energy and resource law journals such as the *Journal of Energy & Natural Resources Law*, the *Journal of World Energy Law & Business*, *Oil, Gas & Energy Law Intelligence* (OGEL), the *Journal of World Investment & Trade*, *ICSID Review*, *Transnational Environmental Law*, and the *Review of European, Comparative & International Environmental Law*, as well as general international law and law and development journals; **working papers** on SSRN and institutional repositories; **reports** of international organisations, think tanks, and NGOs; and **news and specialist services**. Researchers should evaluate secondary sources critically, considering the author's expertise and potential interests, the quality of peer review, and the currency of the analysis.

### Search Strategies

Effective legal research requires systematic **search strategies**: defining key concepts and synonyms; using Boolean operators and proximity searches; following citation trails backwards (to sources cited by a key work) and forwards (to later works citing it, using citator services); monitoring new developments through alerts; and maintaining a **research log** recording searches and sources. For literature reviews, some researchers adopt **systematic review** methods, which use explicit, reproducible procedures for identifying, selecting, and appraising literature, adapted from the health and social sciences. **Reference management software** (such as Zotero, EndNote, or Mendeley) assists in organising sources and formatting citations.

### Citation Methods

Accurate **citation** serves several purposes: it acknowledges the work of others, allows readers to verify claims and locate sources, demonstrates the scholarly foundations of the research, and avoids plagiarism. Legal citation follows specialised conventions. The **Oxford University Standard for the Citation of Legal Authorities** (**OSCOLA**), which is revised periodically by the Oxford Law Faculty, is widely used in the United Kingdom and many Commonwealth jurisdictions, including in Nigerian universities, and is the style used in this book; it uses footnotes, minimal punctuation, and specific formats for cases, legislation, treaties, and secondary sources. **The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation** is the standard in the United States; the **Australian Guide to Legal Citation** (AGLC) in Australia; and the **McGill Guide** in Canada. Nigerian legal writing commonly cites cases by party names, year, and law report series (for example, NWLR with part and page numbers). The doctoral researcher should follow the style required by his or her institution consistently, cite primary sources wherever possible, provide pinpoint references to specific pages or paragraphs, and verify every citation against the original source. Appendix P provides a guide to citation and case analysis.

### Stating the Law as at a Date

Because energy law changes rapidly, a thesis or publication should state the **date at which the law is stated**, and the researcher should update the analysis as close as possible to submission, noting significant developments after that date where feasible. Pending legislation, ongoing litigation, and recent awards should be described with appropriate caution, distinguishing between what has been decided and what is proposed or contested.
