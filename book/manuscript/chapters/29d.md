## 29.7 Governance Reform in Resource-Rich Economies

### The Governance Challenge

The **resource curse** literature, examined in Chapter 23, has established that the economic and political outcomes of resource wealth depend critically on the quality of governance. States with strong institutions — accountable governments, effective bureaucracies, the rule of law, and control of corruption — have generally converted resource wealth into development, as Norway, Botswana, Chile, and Malaysia illustrate, while states with weak institutions have often suffered from volatility, corruption, conflict, and stagnation. Governance reform is therefore at the heart of the resource development agenda. Yet it is also especially difficult in resource-rich states, because resource rents create powerful incentives to preserve the status quo.

### Frameworks for Resource Governance

Several frameworks articulate the components of good resource governance. The **Natural Resource Charter**, developed by an independent group of experts and hosted by the Natural Resource Governance Institute (NRGI), sets out twelve precepts covering the **decision chain** of resource management: strategy, consultation, and institutions; accountability; the exploration and award of rights; taxation; local effects; state-owned enterprises; investing for sustained development; revenue smoothing; government spending; private sector development; the roles of companies; and international cooperation. The NRGI's **Resource Governance Index**, which assesses the quality of resource governance in many countries across value realisation, revenue management, and the enabling environment, has consistently found significant governance deficits in many resource-rich states, including in Africa. The **Africa Mining Vision** (2009) and its **Country Mining Vision** guidelines, the **EITI Standard**, examined in Chapter 22, and the **IMF's Fiscal Transparency Code** with its resource revenue pillar provide further frameworks. The World Bank's **Extractive Industries Value Chain** framework describes the sequence from the award of contracts and licences through regulation and monitoring of operations, collection of taxes and royalties, and revenue distribution and management, to the implementation of sustainable development policies.

### Key Areas of Reform

Drawing on these frameworks and the analysis throughout this book, the key areas of governance reform in resource-rich economies include:

1. **The award of rights**: replacing discretionary allocation of licences and contracts with transparent, competitive procedures, published criteria, and disclosure of contracts and beneficial owners, as examined in Chapters 5 and 22.
2. **Fiscal regimes**: designing fiscal regimes that capture a fair share of resource rents through progressive instruments, minimise discretionary incentives, and are administered by capable tax authorities with powers to audit costs and address transfer pricing, as examined in Chapter 16.
3. **State-owned enterprises**: clarifying the mandates of national oil and mining companies, separating their commercial and regulatory functions, subjecting them to commercial governance, transparency, and audit, and limiting quasi-fiscal activities, as the PIA sought to do for NNPC Limited.
4. **Revenue management**: adopting fiscal rules, stabilisation and savings funds, and transparent budgeting to manage volatility and ensure that revenues benefit current and future generations, as examined in Chapter 23.
5. **Regulatory institutions**: establishing independent, capable, and accountable regulators, as discussed in section 29.4.
6. **Local benefits and community rights**: ensuring that host communities share in benefits and that their rights are respected, through benefit-sharing mechanisms, consultation, and grievance mechanisms, as examined in Chapters 21 and 24.
7. **Environmental protection**: effective environmental regulation, remediation of legacy pollution, and decommissioning security.
8. **Transparency and accountability**: disclosure of payments, contracts, and beneficial owners; independent audit; parliamentary oversight; freedom of information; and space for civil society and the media.
9. **Anti-corruption**: effective investigation and prosecution of corruption, international cooperation on asset recovery, and integrity in public procurement, as examined in Chapter 22.

### Reform in Nigeria: Lessons and Challenges

**Nigeria**'s experience offers rich material for the study of governance reform in a resource-rich economy. The **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** addressed long-standing governance problems: the conflict of roles within the old NNPC, the opacity of the fiscal regime, the absence of a legal framework for host community benefits, and the fragmentation of regulation. The **Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative Act 2007** made Nigeria the first EITI implementing country to give the initiative a statutory basis, and NEITI's audits have exposed significant revenue losses and governance failures. The **removal of the petrol subsidy** in 2023 and the **unification of exchange rates** addressed major fiscal distortions. The **Electricity Act 2023** opened the electricity sector to state-level markets. The **tax reform legislation of 2025** sought to modernise and harmonise the tax system.

Significant challenges remain: the implementation of the PIA, including the effectiveness of host community trusts and the commercial transformation of NNPC Limited; **crude oil theft** and pipeline vandalism, which have at times severely reduced production; the **remediation** of legacy pollution in the Niger Delta; the **financial viability** of the electricity sector; the management of **resource revenues** across the federation, including the depletion of the Excess Crude Account and the limited size of the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority's funds; the **energy transition**, including the role of gas, the development of renewable energy, and the implications of global decarbonisation for oil revenues; and the broader challenges of corruption and institutional capacity. The Nigerian experience demonstrates that legislative reform is a necessary but not sufficient condition of governance reform, and that implementation, enforcement, and the sustained engagement of citizens and institutions are equally important.

### International Support and Its Limits

International actors — multilateral development banks, the IMF, bilateral donors, the EITI, and international NGOs — have supported governance reform in resource-rich states through technical assistance, conditionality, transparency standards, and advocacy. Their contributions have included model laws and contracts, capacity-building for regulators and tax authorities, support for revenue management frameworks, and pressure for transparency. However, externally driven reforms may lack domestic ownership, reflect inappropriate models, or produce isomorphic mimicry. The lesson of the governance literature is that sustainable reform depends on **domestic coalitions** for reform — reformist officials, legislators, civil society, the media, business, and citizens — and that international support is most effective when it strengthens such coalitions rather than substituting for them. The approach of **problem-driven iterative adaptation** (PDIA), developed by Matt Andrews, Lant Pritchett, and Michael Woolcock, emphasises locally defined problems, iterative experimentation, and broad engagement, rather than the imposition of best-practice solutions.

## 29.8 Designing Future-Ready Energy Legal Systems

### The Challenge of Change

Energy legal systems designed for the twentieth century — centralised, fossil-fuel-based, and organised around large utilities and national oil companies — must be redesigned for a twenty-first century characterised by decarbonisation, decentralisation, digitalisation, and deepening geopolitical uncertainty. A **future-ready** energy legal system is one capable of guiding and accommodating this transformation: providing the stability investors need while adapting to rapid change, protecting the vulnerable while enabling innovation, and integrating energy, climate, environmental, and social objectives. This section identifies the design principles of such systems, drawing on the analysis throughout this book.

### Design Principles

1. **Clear long-term direction.** Future-ready systems establish a clear, legally anchored long-term direction — such as a net zero target and pathway in a climate framework law, with interim targets and carbon budgets — which provides a framework for investment and policy, as in the United Kingdom's Climate Change Act 2008 and Nigeria's Climate Change Act 2021. Long-term direction reduces policy uncertainty and the risk of stranded assets.
2. **Adaptive regulation.** Systems must be able to adapt to technological and market change without constant legislative amendment, through framework legislation, delegated rule-making with accountability, periodic review, **regulatory sandboxes** that allow innovators to test new business models under relaxed rules and close supervision (as introduced by Ofgem, the Australian Energy Market Commission, and regulators in Kenya and other African states), and **experimental** approaches that learn from evaluation.
3. **Technology neutrality with strategic choice.** Regulation should generally be technology-neutral, setting outcomes rather than prescribing technologies, while allowing targeted support for strategic technologies at early stages of development.
4. **Integrated governance.** The transition requires integration across sectors (electricity, transport, heating, industry), levels of government (international, national, subnational), and policy domains (energy, climate, environment, industry, trade, and social policy), through coordinating institutions and integrated planning, such as the integrated national energy and climate plans required of EU member states and Nigeria's Energy Transition Plan.
5. **Investment-enabling stability.** Future-ready systems balance adaptability with the stability investors require, through credible and independent institutions, transparent rules, protection against arbitrary change, and mechanisms for managing unavoidable change fairly, such as transitional arrangements and compensation for early closure where appropriate — lessons drawn from the investment disputes examined in Chapters 14 and 26.
6. **Just transition.** Systems must protect workers, communities, and consumers affected by the transition, through social protection, retraining, regional development, affordable energy, and the participation of affected groups in transition planning, as examined in Chapter 23.
7. **Access and affordability.** In Africa, a future-ready energy legal system must above all achieve universal access to modern energy, through frameworks for grid extension, mini-grids, off-grid solutions, and clean cooking, and through tariff and subsidy designs that protect the poor without undermining financial viability.
8. **Resilience and security.** Systems must be resilient to physical, cyber, climate, and geopolitical shocks, through diversification, reserves, critical infrastructure protection, and emergency frameworks, as examined in Chapter 25.
9. **Digital and data governance.** As energy systems become digitalised, legal systems must govern data ownership, access, privacy, cyber security, and the use of artificial intelligence, as examined in Chapter 30.
10. **Accountability and participation.** Future-ready systems are transparent, accountable, and participatory, enabling citizens, communities, and civil society to hold governments and companies to account and to shape the transition.

### The Role of Legal Leaders

The design and reform of energy legal systems requires **leadership** — from legislators, regulators, judges, practitioners, academics, and civil society. Legal leaders in the energy sector need not only technical legal expertise but also an understanding of economics, technology, and politics; the ability to communicate across disciplines and with the public; the integrity to resist capture and corruption; and the vision to see beyond immediate interests to long-term public goods. Doctoral graduates in energy and natural resources law are well placed to exercise such leadership — as advisers to governments and regulators, as drafters of legislation, as judges and arbitrators, as counsel to companies and communities, as officials of international organisations, and as scholars whose research informs reform. Chapter 30 considers the challenges that this generation of legal leaders will face.
