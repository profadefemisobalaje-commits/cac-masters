## 10.7 Renewable Energy Certificates and Attribute Markets

### The Concept of Environmental Attributes

Once electricity enters the grid, electrons from renewable and fossil sources are physically indistinguishable, and it is impossible to trace the electricity consumed by a particular customer to a particular generator. Renewable energy certificates address this problem by separating the "environmental attributes" of renewable generation — its renewable origin and associated characteristics — from the physical electricity, and embodying them in a tradable instrument. A certificate typically represents one megawatt-hour of electricity generated from a renewable source and records information about the source, location, technology, date of generation, and the installation. Certificates enable consumers, suppliers, and companies to substantiate claims that their electricity is renewable, to comply with disclosure or quota obligations, and to account for the emissions associated with their electricity consumption.

Certificates serve different legal functions in different systems, and it is important to distinguish between them. **Compliance certificates** are used to demonstrate compliance with quota obligations (such as renewable portfolio standards and the former UK Renewables Obligation, examined in section 10.6). **Disclosure certificates** — notably guarantees of origin in the European Union — are used to substantiate the information that suppliers must give consumers about the sources of their electricity. **Voluntary certificates** are purchased by companies and individuals to support voluntary claims of renewable energy use, for example under corporate sustainability commitments.

### Guarantees of Origin in the European Union

Article 19 of the Renewable Energy Directive requires member states to ensure that the origin of electricity from renewable sources can be guaranteed, for the purpose of proving to final customers the share or quantity of renewable energy in a supplier's energy mix, by issuing **guarantees of origin** (GOs) in response to a request from a producer. Each guarantee of origin has a standard size of one megawatt-hour, may be used only once, and must be used within twelve months of the production of the corresponding unit of energy; GOs that have not been cancelled expire no later than eighteen months after production. Member states must recognise GOs issued by other member states, and may refuse recognition only where they have well-founded doubts about their accuracy, reliability, or veracity. RED III extended the GO system to renewable gases, including hydrogen. Under the Electricity Directive, suppliers must disclose the share of each energy source in their overall fuel mix, and must use GOs to demonstrate the share of renewable energy. The technical standard for GOs is the European Energy Certificate System (EECS), administered by the Association of Issuing Bodies, which links national registries through a hub enabling cross-border transfers.

Article 19(2) of the Directive, as amended, also provides that member states may decide not to issue GOs to producers that receive financial support from a support scheme, or to issue them and transfer their value to the market, to avoid double compensation — an issue that has generated debate about whether certificates from subsidised plants should be sold to consumers who might believe that their purchases contribute to new renewable investment.

### Certificate Systems Elsewhere

In the United States, **renewable energy certificates** (RECs) are used both for compliance with state renewable portfolio standards and for voluntary claims, tracked through regional tracking systems. Australia's large-scale generation certificates, created under the Renewable Energy (Electricity) Act 2000, are used for compliance with the Renewable Energy Target. In countries without national certificate systems, including most of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, the **I-REC Standard**, administered by a non-profit foundation, provides an international framework for issuing and tracking certificates through local issuers, and has been used in a growing number of African countries. The development of certificate systems in developing countries enables domestic and foreign companies to procure renewable electricity credibly, and may support corporate renewable procurement and investment, but requires robust registries, independent verification, and safeguards against double counting.

### Corporate Renewable Procurement and Claims

Corporate demand has become an important driver of renewable energy investment. Companies with commitments to use renewable electricity — many of them members of the **RE100** initiative, which brings together companies committed to sourcing one hundred per cent renewable electricity — procure renewable electricity through several means: on-site generation; **corporate PPAs**, under which the company contracts directly with a renewable generator for its output and associated certificates; green tariffs offered by utilities; and the purchase of "unbundled" certificates separately from electricity. Corporate PPAs may be **physical** (involving the delivery of electricity to the buyer through the grid) or **virtual** or **financial** (a contract for difference settled financially against the market price, with the transfer of certificates). RED II required member states to remove unjustified barriers to corporate renewable PPAs, and the EU's 2024 electricity market reform requires member states to facilitate PPAs, including through state-backed guarantee schemes addressing the credit risk of buyers.

The accounting rules for corporate claims are set by the **Greenhouse Gas Protocol**, a private standard-setting initiative of the World Resources Institute and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development. Its Scope 2 Guidance (2015) requires companies to report emissions from purchased electricity using two methods: a **location-based method**, reflecting the average emissions intensity of the grid in the locations where consumption occurs, and a **market-based method**, reflecting the emissions associated with contractual instruments such as certificates and PPAs, subject to quality criteria. The market-based method has been criticised for allowing companies to claim zero emissions from electricity by purchasing cheap unbundled certificates from existing plants, which may have no effect on actual emissions or on investment in new renewable capacity ("additionality"), and from distant locations or different times of day from the company's consumption. In response, some companies have adopted "24/7 carbon-free energy" commitments, matching their consumption on an hourly basis with carbon-free generation in the same grid region, and the Greenhouse Gas Protocol has consulted on revisions to the Scope 2 Guidance, including proposals for hourly matching and requirements that certificates be sourced from the same market as consumption.

The integrity of corporate claims has become a legal as well as a reputational issue. In the European Union, Directive (EU) 2024/825 on empowering consumers for the green transition, which applies from September 2026, prohibits generic environmental claims that cannot be substantiated and claims, based on the offsetting of greenhouse gas emissions, that a product has a neutral, reduced, or positive impact on the environment. Consumer protection authorities and courts in several jurisdictions have taken action against misleading "green" claims, and the use of renewable energy certificates to support claims may be scrutinised under consumer protection and unfair competition law. The relationship between renewable certificates and carbon credits, which are distinct instruments representing emission reductions or removals and are governed by the carbon market rules examined in Chapter 18, is a recurrent source of confusion: a renewable energy certificate attests to the renewable origin of a unit of electricity, while a carbon credit represents a tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent reduced or removed relative to a baseline, and the same project should not generate both instruments for the same attribute without safeguards against double counting.

## 10.8 Renewable Energy Investment Disputes

### The Context: Retroactive Changes to Support Schemes

The renewable energy investment disputes arose from a common pattern. In the mid-2000s, several European states — notably Spain, Italy, and the Czech Republic — introduced generous feed-in tariffs to attract investment in renewable energy, particularly solar PV. The tariffs, combined with rapidly falling costs of solar panels, generated investment booms far exceeding government projections, and the cost of support, borne by consumers or the state, escalated rapidly. Following the global financial crisis of 2008, and facing large deficits in their electricity systems, these governments enacted measures reducing the support available to existing installations, including caps on the hours of production eligible for support, levies on revenues, and, in Spain's case, the replacement of the feed-in tariff regime by a new regime guaranteeing a "reasonable return" on investment calculated over the regulatory life of installations. Investors, many of them foreign, brought claims under the Energy Charter Treaty and bilateral investment treaties, arguing that the measures breached the obligation to accord fair and equitable treatment (FET), including by frustrating their legitimate expectations of regulatory stability, and constituted indirect expropriation.

### The Spanish Arbitrations

Spain's original regime, set out principally in Royal Decree 661/2007, offered renewable installations registered by specified deadlines a regulated tariff for their entire operational life, with tariffs for solar PV substantially above market prices. In 2010, Spain introduced initial measures limiting the hours of production eligible for the tariff and imposing a grid access charge. Between 2012 and 2014, it enacted more radical measures: a seven per cent tax on the value of electricity production (Law 15/2012); the elimination of the inflation adjustment; and, by Royal Decree-law 9/2013, Law 24/2013, Royal Decree 413/2014, and Ministerial Order IET/1045/2014, the abolition of the RD 661/2007 regime and its replacement by a "specific remuneration" regime designed to provide a "reasonable return" for a "standard installation", initially set at around 7.4 per cent pre-tax, over the regulatory life of the installation, taking into account the remuneration already received.

Spain faced around fifty arbitration claims, making it one of the most frequently sued states in the world. The outcomes varied, but investors prevailed in the majority of cases decided on the merits in respect of the 2013–2014 measures.

**Claims dismissed.** In *Charanne BV and Construction Investments SARL v Spain* (SCC, 2016), the first award, the tribunal dismissed claims concerning the 2010 measures, holding that the investors could not have had a legitimate expectation that the regulatory framework would remain unchanged in the absence of a specific commitment, that RD 661/2007 was a general regulation, and that the 2010 measures were not unreasonable or disproportionate. In *Isolux Infrastructure Netherlands BV v Spain* (SCC, 2016), the tribunal dismissed claims concerning the 2013–2014 measures, holding that the investor, which had invested in 2012 after the first changes and when further reform was foreseeable, could not have had a legitimate expectation that the regime would remain unchanged. In *Stadtwerke München GmbH and others v Spain* (ICSID, 2019), the tribunal similarly found that a diligent investor would have known that the regime could be modified, and that the new regime was not unreasonable.

**Claims upheld.** In *Eiser Infrastructure Ltd and Energía Solar Luxembourg SÀRL v Spain* (ICSID, 2017), examined in the case box below, the tribunal found that Spain had breached the FET standard by fundamentally and abruptly altering the regulatory regime, and awarded around EUR 128 million. Subsequent tribunals, including in *Novenergia II v Spain* (SCC, 2018), *Masdar Solar & Wind Cooperatief UA v Spain* (ICSID, 2018), *Antin Infrastructure Services Luxembourg SARL v Spain* (ICSID, 2018), *RREEF Infrastructure v Spain* (ICSID, 2019), *NextEra Energy Global Holdings BV v Spain* (ICSID, 2019), and *Cube Infrastructure Fund SICAV v Spain* (ICSID, 2019), found breaches of the FET standard, with awards ranging from tens to hundreds of millions of euros. The reasoning varied. Some tribunals, such as *Masdar*, found that specific assurances — including letters from the Spanish authorities confirming that the installations qualified for the RD 661/2007 tariff for their operational life — created legitimate expectations. Others, such as *RREEF*, found no legitimate expectation that the specific tariff would be preserved, but held that the investors were entitled to expect a reasonable return, and that Spain had breached the FET standard to the extent that the new regime failed to provide it, or applied retroactively by "clawing back" past remuneration.

**The tax carve-out.** Tribunals generally held that the seven per cent tax on the value of electricity production was a "taxation measure" within the meaning of Article 21 of the ECT, which excludes taxation measures from the scope of the treaty's protections (subject to exceptions for expropriation and certain non-discrimination obligations), and therefore declined jurisdiction over claims relating to it, notwithstanding the investors' argument that the tax was in substance a disguised reduction of the tariff.
