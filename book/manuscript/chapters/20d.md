## 20.7 Marine Environmental Protection

### Scope of this Section

Chapter 17 examined the regulation of marine pollution from offshore petroleum operations and shipping, including the MARPOL Convention, the civil liability and compensation regime for oil pollution damage, the regional seas conventions applicable to offshore operations, and national liability regimes. This section focuses on the general framework of **Part XII of UNCLOS** as the overarching treaty basis for the protection of the marine environment, its interpretation by international courts and tribunals — in particular in the ITLOS advisory opinion on climate change — and the **BBNJ Agreement**, with particular attention to their implications for offshore energy activities and the energy transition.

### The General Obligation and its Structure

Article 192 provides, in deceptively simple terms, that "States have the obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment". This is the first provision of any global treaty to impose a general obligation of environmental protection on states, and it applies to all maritime zones and to all activities, including those within national jurisdiction. Article 193 affirms the sovereign right of states to exploit their natural resources pursuant to their environmental policies, but "in accordance with their duty to protect and preserve the marine environment" — formulating sovereignty over resources as subject to environmental obligations.

Article 194 requires states to take, individually or jointly as appropriate, all measures consistent with the Convention that are necessary to prevent, reduce, and control pollution of the marine environment from any source, using for this purpose the best practicable means at their disposal and in accordance with their capabilities (Article 194(1)), and to take all measures necessary to ensure that activities under their jurisdiction or control are so conducted as not to cause damage by pollution to other states and their environment (Article 194(2)). The measures must deal with all sources of pollution, including pollution from installations and devices used in the exploration or exploitation of the natural resources of the seabed and subsoil, in particular measures for preventing accidents and dealing with emergencies, ensuring the safety of operations at sea, and regulating the design, construction, equipment, operation, and manning of such installations (Article 194(3)(c)). They must include measures necessary to protect and preserve rare or fragile ecosystems and the habitat of depleted, threatened, or endangered species (Article 194(5)).

"Pollution of the marine environment" is defined broadly in Article 1(1)(4) as the introduction by man, directly or indirectly, of substances or energy into the marine environment which results or is likely to result in deleterious effects such as harm to living resources and marine life, hazards to human health, hindrance to marine activities, impairment of the quality of sea water, and reduction of amenities. The inclusion of "energy" covers noise and heat, relevant to seismic surveys, pile-driving for offshore wind foundations, and thermal discharges.

Part XII then addresses particular sources of pollution: land-based sources (Article 207); pollution from seabed activities subject to national jurisdiction (Article 208), which requires coastal states to adopt laws no less effective than international rules and standards; activities in the Area (Article 209); dumping (Article 210); vessels (Article 211); and pollution from or through the atmosphere (Article 212). It establishes enforcement jurisdiction of flag states, port states, and coastal states (Articles 213–222), provides safeguards (Articles 223–233), and contains a special provision for ice-covered areas (Article 234). Article 235 provides that states are responsible for the fulfilment of their international obligations concerning the protection and preservation of the marine environment and are liable in accordance with international law, and requires them to ensure that recourse is available under their legal systems for prompt and adequate compensation for damage caused by pollution by persons under their jurisdiction.

### Cooperation, Monitoring, and Environmental Impact Assessment

Section 2 of Part XII requires states to cooperate on a global and regional basis in formulating international rules and standards (Article 197), to notify other states of imminent or actual damage (Article 198), and to develop contingency plans against pollution (Article 199). Section 4 requires states to monitor the risks or effects of pollution (Article 204) and, under **Article 206**, when they have reasonable grounds for believing that planned activities under their jurisdiction or control may cause substantial pollution of or significant and harmful changes to the marine environment, to assess, as far as practicable, the potential effects of such activities and to communicate reports of the results. Article 206 is the treaty basis for **environmental impact assessment** of offshore energy projects with potential transboundary or high-seas effects, and international courts have treated the obligation as one of due diligence requiring an assessment before activities are authorised.

### Judicial Interpretation

International courts and tribunals have interpreted Part XII expansively. In the *South China Sea Arbitration* (2016), the tribunal held that Article 192 imposes a positive obligation to take active measures to protect and preserve the marine environment and a negative obligation not to degrade it, that Article 194(5) extends to the protection of fragile ecosystems from harvesting of endangered species, and that China had breached Part XII through its land reclamation and construction of artificial islands, which caused severe harm to coral reef ecosystems, and through its toleration of harmful harvesting by its fishing vessels. In the *Chagos Marine Protected Area Arbitration* (*Mauritius v United Kingdom*, 2015), the tribunal held that the United Kingdom had breached its obligation to have due regard to the rights of Mauritius in declaring a marine protected area. In the *Seabed Disputes Chamber's* advisory opinion of 2011, examined in section 20.6, the Chamber described the obligation to conduct an environmental impact assessment as a general obligation under customary international law.

### The ITLOS Advisory Opinion on Climate Change

In its unanimous advisory opinion of **21 May 2024**, requested by the Commission of Small Island States on Climate Change and International Law, ITLOS addressed the obligations of states parties to UNCLOS to prevent, reduce, and control pollution of the marine environment in relation to the deleterious effects that result or are likely to result from climate change and ocean acidification caused by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, and to protect and preserve the marine environment in relation to climate change impacts. The Tribunal held, in summary, that:

- anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions into the atmosphere constitute **pollution of the marine environment** within the meaning of Article 1(1)(4);
- under Article 194(1), states have a specific obligation to take all necessary measures to prevent, reduce, and control marine pollution from such emissions, and to endeavour to harmonise their policies; this is an obligation of **due diligence**, whose standard is **stringent** given the high risks of serious and irreversible harm, and which may vary according to states' capabilities and available resources;
- the obligation under UNCLOS is not satisfied simply by compliance with the obligations and commitments under the Paris Agreement, although the temperature goal and the timelines of the IPCC are relevant to determining the necessary measures;
- under Article 194(2), states must take all measures necessary to ensure that emissions under their jurisdiction or control do not cause damage by pollution to other states, an obligation applicable in a transboundary context;
- the obligations to conduct environmental impact assessments under Article 206 and to monitor under Article 204 apply to planned activities that may cause substantial pollution through greenhouse gas emissions; and
- Articles 192 and 194(5) require states to protect and preserve the marine environment from climate change impacts and ocean acidification, including by restoring degraded habitats and ecosystems.

The opinion, together with the advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice of 23 July 2025, examined in Chapter 18, has potential implications for offshore petroleum development: it suggests that the authorisation of new offshore fields and the conduct of environmental impact assessments under Article 206 must take account of greenhouse gas emissions, including potentially downstream emissions, a question also raised in domestic litigation such as *Finch*, examined in Chapter 17. The opinion also supports offshore renewable energy as a means of fulfilling the obligation to reduce marine pollution, while reminding states that such projects are themselves subject to the obligations of Part XII.

### Environmental Dimensions of Offshore Renewable Energy

The expansion of offshore wind and other marine renewable energy raises environmental issues that must be addressed consistently with Part XII and regional and national law: underwater noise from pile-driving during construction, which can harm marine mammals; collision risks for birds and the displacement of bird and marine species; electromagnetic fields from cables; changes to seabed habitats; and cumulative effects. Wind farm foundations may also create artificial reefs and exclusion areas that benefit some species. Regulatory responses include environmental impact assessments, seasonal restrictions on piling, noise mitigation measures such as bubble curtains, marine spatial planning, and monitoring. The balance between accelerating offshore renewable deployment, as required by climate objectives, and protecting marine biodiversity is a recurring theme in the permitting of projects, reflected in the European Union's provisions for renewables acceleration areas under the revised Renewable Energy Directive, examined in Chapter 10.

### The BBNJ Agreement

The **Agreement under UNCLOS on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Marine Biological Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction** was adopted on 19 June 2023 after nearly two decades of discussion, opened for signature in September 2023, and entered into force in January 2026, following the deposit of the sixtieth instrument of ratification in September 2025. It applies to areas beyond national jurisdiction — the high seas and the Area — which cover nearly two-thirds of the ocean. The Agreement addresses four elements:

- **marine genetic resources**, including digital sequence information, with obligations of notification and fair and equitable sharing of monetary and non-monetary benefits;
- **area-based management tools, including marine protected areas**, which the Conference of the Parties may establish in areas beyond national jurisdiction, providing a mechanism to contribute to the global target of protecting thirty per cent of the ocean by 2030 under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework;
- **environmental impact assessments**, requiring parties to assess planned activities under their jurisdiction or control in areas beyond national jurisdiction that may cause more than minor or transitory effects, and to consider cumulative impacts and impacts in areas within national jurisdiction; and
- **capacity building and the transfer of marine technology** to developing states.

The Agreement establishes a Conference of the Parties, a Scientific and Technical Body, a Secretariat, a clearing-house mechanism, and a funding mechanism. It must be interpreted and applied in a manner that does not undermine relevant legal instruments and frameworks and relevant global, regional, subregional, and sectoral bodies — a provision designed to address its relationship with the ISA, regional fisheries management organisations, and the International Maritime Organization. For the energy sector, the Agreement is relevant to the environmental assessment of activities in areas beyond national jurisdiction, such as the laying of cables and pipelines on the high seas, offshore installations beyond national jurisdiction, and potentially marine geoengineering and carbon dioxide removal techniques, and the establishment of marine protected areas on the high seas may constrain such activities. Its relationship with deep-seabed mining, which remains within the competence of the ISA, will be a significant issue in its implementation.

### Marine Geoengineering and Ocean-Based Carbon Removal

Ocean-based approaches to carbon dioxide removal, such as ocean fertilisation, ocean alkalinity enhancement, and the sinking of biomass, are attracting commercial and research interest. The London Protocol was amended in 2013 to regulate marine geoengineering, prohibiting ocean fertilisation other than legitimate scientific research authorised under a permit — an amendment that has not yet entered into force — and the parties to the London Convention and Protocol have continued to evaluate other techniques. The regulation of such activities under Part XII, the London Protocol, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the BBNJ Agreement is an emerging area of law at the intersection of climate and marine environmental governance.

## 20.8 Maritime Boundaries and Offshore Resource Disputes

### The Problem of Overlapping Entitlements

Because coastal states are entitled to territorial seas of twelve nautical miles and EEZs and continental shelves of at least two hundred nautical miles, their entitlements overlap wherever their coasts are opposite or adjacent at distances less than twice these breadths. Hundreds of potential maritime boundaries exist worldwide, of which a substantial proportion remain undelimited. Since many of the world's offshore petroleum provinces lie in areas of overlapping entitlement, maritime boundary disputes have been closely connected with offshore resources: disputes often arise or intensify when one state licenses exploration in an area claimed by another, and the prospect of discoveries can either motivate agreement or entrench disagreement.

### The Law of Delimitation

UNCLOS provides that, failing agreement, neither state may extend its **territorial sea** beyond the median line every point of which is equidistant from the nearest points on the baselines, except where it is necessary by reason of historic title or other special circumstances to delimit in a different way (Article 15). For the **EEZ** and **continental shelf**, Articles 74(1) and 83(1) provide that delimitation between states with opposite or adjacent coasts is to be effected by agreement on the basis of international law, as referred to in Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, **in order to achieve an equitable solution**. These provisions, deliberately vague as a result of the division at the Third Conference between proponents of equidistance and proponents of equitable principles, have been given content by international jurisprudence.

In the *North Sea Continental Shelf* cases (1969), the International Court of Justice rejected the argument that the equidistance principle of the 1958 Convention was binding as customary law and held that delimitation was to be effected by agreement in accordance with equitable principles, taking account of all relevant circumstances. Subsequent cases, including *Tunisia/Libya* (1982), *Gulf of Maine* (*Canada/United States*, Chamber, 1984), *Libya/Malta* (1985), and *Jan Mayen* (*Denmark v Norway*, 1993), refined the approach, and in *Maritime Delimitation in the Black Sea* (*Romania v Ukraine*, 2009), the Court consolidated a **three-stage methodology**:

1. the construction of a **provisional equidistance line** (or median line for opposite coasts), using appropriate base points on the relevant coasts;
2. the consideration of whether **relevant circumstances** call for the adjustment or shifting of the provisional line to achieve an equitable result — such as the configuration of the coasts, the presence of islands, and significant disparities in coastal lengths; and
3. a **disproportionality test**, to verify that the line does not lead to an inequitable result by reason of a marked disproportion between the ratio of the respective coastal lengths and the ratio of the maritime areas allocated to each state.

This methodology has been followed by ITLOS and Annex VII tribunals, including in *Bangladesh/Myanmar* (2012), *Bangladesh v India* (2014), *Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire* (2017), and *Mauritius/Maldives* (2023), and by the International Court of Justice in *Nicaragua v Colombia* (2012), *Peru v Chile* (2014), and *Somalia v Kenya* (2021). In some cases of unstable or concave coasts, tribunals have used an angle-bisector method instead of equidistance, as in *Nicaragua v Honduras* (2007) and in part of *Bangladesh v India*.

A consistent theme of the jurisprudence is that **the location of natural resources** is generally not a relevant circumstance, except in exceptional cases. In *Barbados v Trinidad and Tobago* (Annex VII, 2006), the tribunal observed that resource-related criteria have been treated more cautiously and have not generally been applied as relevant circumstances, and in *Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire*, the Special Chamber declined to adjust the line to take account of the location of oil fields. Similarly, **oil practice** — the pattern of concessions granted by the parties — has been relevant only where it evidences a tacit agreement between the parties on a boundary, which tribunals have been reluctant to find, as in *Cameroon v Nigeria* (2002) and *Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire*.

::: casebox {title="Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire: Oil Fields in a Disputed Area"}
**Dispute concerning Delimitation of the Maritime Boundary between Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in the Atlantic Ocean (ITLOS Special Chamber, Judgment of 23 September 2017).**

**Facts.** Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire had not delimited their maritime boundary. Ghana granted petroleum licences in waters that it regarded as being on its side of an equidistance line that it claimed had been tacitly accepted by both states through decades of oil practice, and the TEN (Tweneboa, Enyenra, Ntomme) fields were discovered and developed close to the claimed boundary. Côte d'Ivoire contested this boundary, claiming a line based on the angle-bisector method that would have placed the TEN fields on its side. In 2014, the parties submitted the dispute to a Special Chamber of ITLOS. In 2015, at the request of Côte d'Ivoire, the Special Chamber prescribed provisional measures requiring Ghana to ensure that no new drilling took place in the disputed area, while declining to suspend ongoing activities, given the risk of irreparable prejudice to Ghana and the environment.

**Judgment.** The Special Chamber rejected Ghana's claim that the parties had tacitly agreed on a boundary based on their oil practice, holding that the evidence of a tacit agreement must be compelling and that oil practice, however consistent, did not by itself establish a maritime boundary. It applied the three-stage methodology, constructed an equidistance line, found no relevant circumstances justifying its adjustment, and confirmed the absence of disproportionality. The resulting boundary placed the TEN fields on Ghana's side. The Chamber also rejected Côte d'Ivoire's claim that Ghana's activities in the disputed area had violated its sovereign rights, holding that, since the area was found to belong to Ghana, its activities had not breached Côte d'Ivoire's rights; that Ghana had not violated the obligation to negotiate in good faith; and that Ghana had not breached the obligation under Article 83(3) not to jeopardise or hamper the reaching of an agreement, since that obligation was triggered only by activities after the claim had been made known, and Ghana had suspended new drilling following the provisional measures order.

**Significance.** The case is the leading African maritime delimitation decision with direct consequences for petroleum operations. It confirms that oil practice is not a reliable substitute for a delimitation agreement, illustrates the use of provisional measures to manage petroleum activities in disputed areas, and leaves open the extent of liability for unilateral activities in areas that are ultimately found to belong to the other party. It also illustrates that boundary uncertainty imposes costs on investors and states, which are best avoided by early agreement or joint development.

### Cameroon v Nigeria

The *Land and Maritime Boundary between Cameroon and Nigeria* case (ICJ, 2002) is of particular significance for Nigeria. Cameroon brought proceedings in 1994 following armed clashes on the **Bakassi Peninsula**, a potentially oil-rich area administered by Nigeria. The Court held, on the basis of the Anglo-German Agreement of 1913, that sovereignty over Bakassi lay with Cameroon, and it delimited the maritime boundary, confirming the effect of the Maroua Declaration of 1975 and drawing an equidistance line further offshore, while declining to adjust it for the configuration of the coast or the parties' oil practice. Nigeria's oil concessions on Cameroon's side of the line did not constitute evidence of an agreed boundary. Following the judgment, a Cameroon–Nigeria Mixed Commission, established under UN auspices, oversaw implementation, and in the **Greentree Agreement** of 2006, Nigeria agreed to withdraw from the Bakassi Peninsula, which it completed in 2008, with protections for the Nigerian population remaining in the area. The peaceful implementation of the judgment is often cited as a model, although the resettlement and livelihoods of affected communities have remained contested.

### Activities in Disputed Areas: Articles 74(3) and 83(3)

Pending agreement on delimitation, Articles 74(3) and 83(3) require the states concerned, in a spirit of understanding and cooperation, to **make every effort to enter into provisional arrangements of a practical nature** and, during this transitional period, **not to jeopardise or hamper the reaching of the final agreement**. In *Guyana v Suriname* (Annex VII, 2007), which arose from the forcible removal by Surinamese naval vessels of a drilling rig licensed by Guyana from a disputed area in 2000, the tribunal held that both states had breached their obligations under these provisions: Guyana by authorising exploratory drilling, which could cause permanent physical change to the marine environment, without consulting Suriname; and Suriname by threatening force. The tribunal distinguished activities that cause permanent physical change, such as exploratory drilling and exploitation, which jeopardise or hamper a final agreement, from activities such as seismic surveys, which do not. Suriname's threat of force was also held to violate the Convention and the UN Charter. The tribunal's distinction has influenced the practice of states and companies in managing petroleum activities in disputed areas, although it has been criticised as both too permissive and too restrictive.

### Joint Development Agreements

Where states cannot agree on a boundary, or where resources straddle a boundary, they may conclude **joint development agreements**, under which they jointly manage the exploration and exploitation of resources in a defined area and share the revenues, without prejudice to their boundary claims. Examples include the Malaysia–Thailand Joint Development Area established by a memorandum of understanding of 1979 and an agreement of 1990; the Timor Gap Treaty between Australia and Indonesia (1989) and its successor, the Timor Sea Treaty between Australia and Timor-Leste (2002); and the arrangements between Saudi Arabia and Kuwait for the former Neutral Zone.

The **Nigeria–São Tomé and Príncipe Joint Development Zone**, established by a **Treaty** concluded in 2001, is the leading African example. Rather than delimit their overlapping EEZ claims, the two states established a Joint Development Zone, managed by a **Joint Ministerial Council** and a **Joint Development Authority**, in which resources are shared in the proportion of sixty per cent to Nigeria and forty per cent to São Tomé and Príncipe. Licensing rounds were held, and exploration wells were drilled in the 2000s, but no commercial discoveries were made, and the Zone's activities have been limited. The JDZ has been studied for its institutional design and for the governance challenges arising from the disparity in size and capacity between the partners, including concerns over the transparency of licence allocations.

The **Timor Sea** illustrates the evolution from joint development to delimitation. Following the first-ever compulsory conciliation under Annex V of UNCLOS, initiated by Timor-Leste in 2016, Australia and Timor-Leste concluded the **Treaty Establishing their Maritime Boundaries in the Timor Sea** in March 2018, which delimited permanent boundaries and established a special regime for the Greater Sunrise gas fields, under which revenues are shared seventy to thirty or eighty to twenty in favour of Timor-Leste, depending on the location of processing. The development of Greater Sunrise remains under negotiation.

### Unitisation of Cross-Border Fields

Where a petroleum deposit straddles an agreed boundary, international practice favours **unitisation** — the joint development of the field as a single unit, with production allocated between the states and their licensees according to an agreed formula, subject to periodic redetermination. Unitisation prevents wasteful competitive drilling and maximises recovery, and its obligations may reflect a duty of cooperation in respect of shared resources under customary law, although the existence of a customary obligation to unitise is debated. Unitisation clauses are common in boundary agreements: the United Kingdom–Norway agreement of 1965 on the delimitation of the continental shelf provided for agreement on the exploitation of straddling fields, implemented for the Frigg, Statfjord, and Murchison fields, and the **Framework Agreement concerning Cross-Boundary Petroleum Co-operation** of 2005 provides a general regime. Nigeria and Equatorial Guinea concluded a maritime boundary treaty in 2000 and subsequently agreed on the unitised development of the Ekanga–Zafiro field straddling their boundary. Within national systems, unitisation is required by petroleum legislation, including Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021.

### Other Resource-Related Boundary Disputes

Other disputes with significant energy dimensions include *Somalia v Kenya* (ICJ, 2021), in which the Court rejected Kenya's claim that Somalia had acquiesced in a boundary along a parallel of latitude and drew an adjusted equidistance line, with consequences for offshore blocks licensed by both states, while rejecting Somalia's claim that Kenya had violated its sovereign rights; the dispute between **Guyana and Venezuela** over the Essequibo region, before the International Court of Justice since 2018, which has intensified since major oil discoveries offshore Guyana from 2015, and in which the Court has indicated provisional measures requiring Venezuela to refrain from altering the existing situation; the **Israel–Lebanon** maritime boundary agreement of October 2022, mediated by the United States, which resolved a dispute over gas prospects including the Qana prospect; and disputes in the **Eastern Mediterranean**, including those involving Cyprus, Turkey, and Greece, in which gas discoveries have intersected with unresolved questions of sovereignty and the entitlement of islands. The interaction between maritime boundary disputes and geopolitics is examined further in Chapter 25, and the procedural aspects of inter-state litigation in Chapter 27.

## Conclusion

The law of the sea provides the jurisdictional architecture within which offshore energy and marine resources are developed. UNCLOS, building on the Truman Proclamation and the *North Sea Continental Shelf* cases, grants coastal states sovereign rights over the resources of the continental shelf and EEZ — including, prophetically, the production of energy from water, currents, and winds — while preserving the freedoms of other states, including the freedom to lay submarine cables and pipelines. The Convention's institutions and its compulsory dispute settlement system have generated a rich jurisprudence on maritime delimitation, activities in disputed areas, and the protection of the marine environment, culminating in the ITLOS advisory opinion of 2024, which brought greenhouse gas emissions within the scope of Part XII.

The energy transition is reshaping the law of the sea. Offshore wind is turning EEZs into spaces of intensive industrial use, requiring marine spatial planning and new cooperative arrangements for hybrid projects; HVDC interconnectors and pipelines are becoming targets in hybrid conflicts, exposing gaps in the legal protection of submarine infrastructure; the demand for critical minerals has brought deep-seabed mining to the threshold of commercial exploitation, testing the common heritage principle and the authority of the ISA; and the BBNJ Agreement extends environmental governance to areas beyond national jurisdiction. For coastal states such as Nigeria, the law of the sea offers both opportunities — in deepwater petroleum, offshore renewable energy, and an extended continental shelf — and responsibilities, in maritime security, environmental protection, and the peaceful resolution of disputes. For the doctoral researcher, these developments offer fertile ground for examining how a treaty regime negotiated in the 1970s can adapt to the demands of the twenty-first century.
