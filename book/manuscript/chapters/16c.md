## 16.5 Royalties, Resource Rent Taxes, and Production Levies

### Royalties

A **royalty** is a payment to the resource owner — usually the state — calculated by reference to the volume or value of the resource produced, regardless of the profitability of production. Royalties are the oldest and most widespread extractive fiscal instrument, reflecting the owner's entitlement to compensation for the depletion of a non-renewable asset. They may be **specific** (a fixed amount per unit of production, such as per tonne or per barrel) or **ad valorem** (a percentage of the value of production). Ad valorem royalties are more common because they automatically adjust for price changes.

From the state's perspective, royalties have the advantages of providing revenue from the start of production, being relatively simple to administer, and being less vulnerable than profit-based taxes to the manipulation of costs. From the investor's perspective, royalties are regressive, because they are payable regardless of profitability and therefore represent a higher proportion of profits for marginal projects, and may cause premature abandonment of fields or mines when costs rise towards revenue. To address these effects, many regimes use **sliding-scale royalties**, whose rates vary with production volume, water depth, or price. Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021 combines production-based royalty rates that vary by terrain with an additional price-based royalty applicable when oil prices exceed specified thresholds, and provides lower rates for gas and for frontier and deep offshore acreage. Chile's 2023 mining royalty and Zambia's sliding-scale mineral royalty illustrate price-sensitive designs in mining.

The **valuation** of production for royalty purposes is a frequent source of dispute. Royalties may be calculated on the value at the wellhead or mine gate, requiring the deduction of transportation, processing, and marketing costs from the sale price — the **netback** method — or on the value at the point of sale. Where production is sold to affiliates, the sale price may not reflect market value, and royalty legislation commonly provides for valuation by reference to benchmark or reference prices, such as Brent or other published crude prices adjusted for quality and location, or the London Metal Exchange price for metals. In the history of the petroleum industry, the disputes between producing states and the international oil companies over **posted prices** — the reference prices used for royalty and tax purposes — were central to the shift of power to OPEC in the early 1970s, as examined in Chapter 4.

### Resource Rent Taxes

A **resource rent tax** (RRT) is a tax on the economic rent generated by a resource project, levied on cash flow after the investor has recovered its costs together with a threshold rate of return. The concept was proposed by the economists Ross Garnaut and Anthony Clunies Ross in the 1970s as a means of capturing a high share of rent from rich projects without deterring marginal ones. Because the tax applies only once the investor has earned a threshold return, it is in principle neutral and highly progressive, and allocates more of the risk of poor outcomes to the investor, who bears the full cost of failure until the threshold is reached, while sharing the upside with the state.

Australia's **Petroleum Resource Rent Tax**, examined in section 16.4, is the best-known example. The attempt to extend the resource rent concept to Australian mining illustrates the political difficulties of such taxes: the Resource Super Profits Tax proposed in 2010 following the Henry Tax Review provoked an intense campaign by mining companies and contributed to a change of Prime Minister; it was replaced by a narrower **Minerals Resource Rent Tax**, applicable to iron ore and coal, which took effect in 2012, raised far less revenue than projected, and was repealed in 2014. RRTs have been adopted in several developing countries, including in petroleum legislation in Ghana and Timor-Leste, but their effectiveness depends on the capacity of tax administrations to audit costs, since the tax base is vulnerable to the inflation of costs through transfer pricing and other means.

PSCs perform a similar function through **R-factor** or rate-of-return-based profit oil splits, examined in Chapter 15, under which the state's share of profit oil increases as the contractor recovers its investment and earns specified returns.

### Windfall Taxes

**Windfall taxes** are taxes imposed on profits that are regarded as unexpectedly high, usually as a result of a sharp increase in commodity prices. They are typically introduced on a temporary basis, outside the ordinary fiscal regime, and frequently apply to existing projects. Examples include the 2006 Mongolian windfall profits tax on copper and gold, subsequently repealed; the Ecuadorian law of 2006, increased in 2007, which claimed fifty and then ninety-nine per cent of oil revenues above a reference price, generating the investment claims examined in Chapter 14; the United Kingdom's Energy Profits Levy of 2022; and the European Union's **solidarity contribution**, introduced by Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854 on an emergency intervention to address high energy prices, which required Member States to levy a temporary contribution of at least thirty-three per cent on surplus taxable profits of companies active in the crude petroleum, natural gas, coal, and refinery sectors, and imposed a cap on the market revenues of inframarginal electricity generators. The regulation, adopted under Article 122 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union as an emergency measure, was challenged before the General Court by several energy companies on the ground, among others, that Article 122 did not permit the adoption of fiscal measures by qualified majority.

Windfall taxes are popular politically, particularly when consumers face high energy prices, but raise significant legal and economic questions. They may undermine fiscal stability and investor confidence, breach stabilisation clauses, give rise to investment treaty claims, and discourage the investment needed to increase supply. Economists generally prefer permanent progressive fiscal regimes, which capture a rising share of rent automatically when prices rise, over ad hoc windfall taxes.

### Production Levies and Earmarked Charges

In addition to royalties and taxes, extractive companies frequently pay levies earmarked for particular purposes. In Nigeria, petroleum companies are subject to contributions to the **Niger Delta Development Commission**, established in 2000 to promote development in the oil-producing region; the **Tertiary Education Trust Fund**; and, under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, contributions of three per cent of their actual annual operating expenditure to **Host Communities Development Trusts**, established for the benefit of communities in the areas of operations, examined in Chapters 21 and 24. The Act also established a Midstream and Downstream Gas Infrastructure Fund and a Frontier Exploration Fund. Earmarked levies respond to legitimate concerns about the distribution of resource benefits to affected regions and communities, but they fragment public revenues, complicate the assessment of the overall fiscal burden, and raise questions of governance and accountability in the management of the funds.

### State Participation and Carried Interests

Many states take an equity interest in extractive projects, either through a national resource company or directly. **Carried interests**, under which the investor funds the state's share of costs — wholly (a "free" carried interest) or until a specified stage, with the costs recoverable from the state's share of production — are common in mining and petroleum. Ghana's mining law provides for a free carried interest of ten per cent in mining companies, and Tanzania's 2017 legislation provided for a non-dilutable free carried interest of not less than sixteen per cent in mining operations. State participation is a fiscal instrument — since it entitles the state to dividends — but also an instrument of control, information, and technology transfer, and its value depends on the governance of the state company.

### Export Taxes, Export Restrictions, and Local Processing

Some resource-rich states impose export taxes or restrictions on unprocessed minerals to promote domestic processing and capture a larger share of the value chain. Indonesia's ban on the export of nickel ore, reinstated in 2020, contributed to large-scale investment in domestic smelting, principally by Chinese companies, and was found inconsistent with the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade by a WTO panel in 2022 in a dispute brought by the European Union, a decision appealed by Indonesia "into the void" in the absence of a functioning Appellate Body, as examined in Chapter 13. Zimbabwe banned the export of unprocessed lithium ore in 2022. Such measures raise questions under international trade law, investment treaties, and stabilisation clauses, and their effectiveness depends on the competitiveness of domestic processing.

### Revenue Management and Sovereign Wealth Funds

The capture of resource revenues is only the first step; their management determines whether they contribute to sustainable development. The volatility of resource revenues, the risk of "Dutch disease" — the appreciation of the real exchange rate and the decline of other tradable sectors — and the exhaustibility of resources have led many states to adopt fiscal rules and to establish **sovereign wealth funds**. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, financed from petroleum revenues and governed by a fiscal rule limiting the use of fund returns in the budget, is the largest sovereign wealth fund in the world. Ghana's Petroleum Revenue Management Act 2011 allocates petroleum revenues among the national budget, a Ghana Stabilisation Fund, and a Ghana Heritage Fund, with oversight by a Public Interest and Accountability Committee. Nigeria established the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority in 2011, with a stabilisation fund, a future generations fund, and an infrastructure fund, succeeding the Excess Crude Account, whose management had been controversial. Timor-Leste's Petroleum Fund, established in 2005, finances a large proportion of government spending. The **Santiago Principles** — the Generally Accepted Principles and Practices for sovereign wealth funds, adopted in 2008 by the International Working Group of Sovereign Wealth Funds and promoted by its successor, the International Forum of Sovereign Wealth Funds — provide voluntary standards on governance, accountability, and investment practices.

## 16.6 Transfer Pricing and International Taxation

### The International Tax Framework

The taxation of multinational energy and mining enterprises operating across borders is governed by national tax laws and a network of more than three thousand bilateral **double taxation treaties**, most of which are based on the **OECD Model Tax Convention on Income and on Capital** or the **United Nations Model Double Taxation Convention between Developed and Developing Countries**. The treaties allocate taxing rights between the state of residence of the taxpayer and the state of source of the income, and provide mechanisms for relieving double taxation. The UN Model generally preserves greater taxing rights for source states, which is significant for capital-importing resource-rich states. Both models permit the source state to tax the business profits of a non-resident enterprise only if the enterprise has a **permanent establishment** in the source state, and both treat a mine, an oil or gas well, a quarry, or any other place of extraction of natural resources as a permanent establishment. Offshore activities and short-duration service contracts raise specific issues, which some treaties address through special provisions for offshore exploration and exploitation. The United Nations Committee of Experts on International Cooperation in Tax Matters published a **Handbook on Selected Issues for Taxation of the Extractive Industries by Developing Countries** in 2017, which addresses treaty, transfer pricing, and other issues specific to the sector.

### The Arm's Length Principle

Multinational enterprises conduct a large proportion of their transactions with related parties — affiliates within the same group — in the course of selling production, purchasing equipment and services, licensing technology, providing management services, and lending money. The prices charged in these transactions, known as **transfer prices**, determine how the group's profits are allocated among the jurisdictions in which it operates, and therefore how much tax is paid in each. Article 9 of the OECD and UN Models establishes the **arm's length principle**: the profits of associated enterprises may be adjusted if the conditions of their commercial or financial relations differ from those that would be made between independent enterprises. The **OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and Tax Administrations**, most recently consolidated in 2022, set out the methods for applying the principle: the comparable uncontrolled price method, the resale price method, the cost plus method, the transactional net margin method, and the transactional profit split method.

Transfer pricing is especially significant in the extractive sector because of the scale of related-party sales of commodities, the use of marketing and trading hubs in low-tax jurisdictions such as Switzerland, Singapore, and Dubai, the prevalence of intra-group services and financing, and the limited capacity of many developing country tax administrations to audit complex transactions. Studies by international organisations and civil society groups have suggested that resource-rich developing countries lose significant revenue through mispricing of commodity exports and inflated costs, although estimates vary widely and are contested.

### Commodity Transactions

For commodity sales, the comparable uncontrolled price method, using quoted prices on commodity exchanges or published price reporting agencies, is generally the most appropriate. Following the BEPS project, the OECD added guidance to the Transfer Pricing Guidelines in 2015 confirming that quoted prices may be used as a reference for commodity transactions between associated enterprises, subject to comparability adjustments, and addressing the **pricing date** — since the ability to select the pricing date with hindsight would permit the manipulation of prices — by allowing tax administrations to impute the shipment date where the taxpayer cannot prove the agreed pricing date. Several Latin American states, beginning with Argentina in 2003, adopted a so-called "sixth method", under which commodity exports to related parties are priced by reference to quoted market prices on the date of shipment, an approach that influenced the OECD guidance. The Platform for Collaboration on Tax — a joint initiative of the IMF, OECD, UN, and World Bank — and the Intergovernmental Forum on Mining have produced additional guidance and toolkits on the pricing of minerals.

### Intra-Group Financing and Interest Deductions

Multinational groups may reduce their taxable profits in high-tax resource jurisdictions by financing operations with intra-group loans carrying high interest rates, the interest on which is deductible in the source state, while the corresponding income is received in a low-tax jurisdiction. States respond through **thin capitalisation** rules, which limit the deduction of interest where debt exceeds a specified ratio to equity, and through **earnings stripping** rules, which limit net interest deductions to a percentage of earnings. BEPS Action 4 recommended a fixed ratio rule limiting net interest deductions to between ten and thirty per cent of earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation (EBITDA), which has been implemented in the European Union by the Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (Directive (EU) 2016/1164), and in many other states. Nigeria's tax law introduced a limit on the deductibility of interest on related-party debt at thirty per cent of EBITDA, following the BEPS recommendation. The application of the arm's length principle to intra-group loans was tested in a leading Australian case concerning an energy multinational.

::: casebox {title="Chevron Australia Holdings Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [2017] FCAFC 62"}
**Facts.** In 2003, Chevron Australia Holdings Pty Ltd (CAHPL), the Australian holding company of the Chevron group's Australian operations, which included interests in major LNG projects, borrowed approximately USD 2.5 billion (in Australian dollar equivalent) from a newly established United States subsidiary, Chevron Texaco Funding Corporation, under a credit facility agreement. The funding company raised the money in the United States commercial paper market at an interest rate of approximately 1.2 per cent, with the benefit of a guarantee from the ultimate parent company, and lent it to CAHPL at an interest rate of approximately nine per cent, without security or a parent guarantee. The interest paid by CAHPL was deductible in Australia, while the funding company's interest income was effectively not taxed in the United States, and was returned to CAHPL as dividends that were exempt from Australian tax.

**The dispute.** The Commissioner of Taxation issued amended assessments under the Australian transfer pricing provisions, disallowing a substantial part of the interest deductions on the ground that the interest exceeded an arm's length amount, with resulting tax and penalties of approximately AUD 340 million.

**The decision.** The Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia unanimously upheld the assessments. The Court held that the arm's length price had to be determined by reference to what an independent borrower in CAHPL's position, as a member of a multinational group, would have paid to an independent lender for the same loan, and that in determining this, it was appropriate to take into account that an independent borrower in CAHPL's position would ordinarily have provided security and obtained a guarantee from its parent company in order to secure a lower rate. The Court rejected Chevron's argument that the loan should be priced as if CAHPL were a stand-alone company without the support of the group, and held that the absence of security and covenants, which would have been present in an arm's length transaction, could not be used to justify a higher interest rate. An application for special leave to appeal to the High Court of Australia was subsequently discontinued.

**Significance.** The case is a leading authority on the pricing of intra-group loans in the extractive sector and on the relevance of group membership and implicit parental support in applying the arm's length principle. It has been cited in the OECD's 2020 guidance on financial transactions and in transfer pricing disputes in other jurisdictions, and illustrates the scale of revenue at stake in the financing structures of major energy projects.

### Indirect Transfers of Interests

A distinctive issue in the extractive sector is the taxation of **indirect transfers** — sales of shares in a company, often incorporated in a low-tax jurisdiction, that directly or indirectly holds a licence, concession, or other interest in a resource project in the host state. Because the asset sold is a share in a foreign company rather than the resource interest itself, the host state may not have the right under its domestic law or its tax treaties to tax the gain, although the value of the shares derives from the resource interest located in its territory.

The issue has generated major disputes. In **India**, the Supreme Court held in *Vodafone International Holdings BV v Union of India* (2012) that the gain on the sale of shares of a Cayman Islands company that indirectly held an Indian telecommunications business was not taxable in India. The government responded by amending the Income Tax Act with retrospective effect to tax such indirect transfers. The retrospective tax was applied to Vodafone and to the British energy company Cairn Energy in respect of an internal reorganisation of its Indian oil and gas business, leading to investment treaty arbitrations in which tribunals ruled in favour of Vodafone in 2020 and of Cairn in December 2020, awarding Cairn approximately USD 1.2 billion plus interest and costs. Cairn commenced enforcement proceedings against Indian state assets in several jurisdictions, and India subsequently enacted the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act 2021, which withdrew the retrospective demands in return for the withdrawal of claims, and refunded the taxes collected.

In **Uganda**, the sale by Heritage Oil and Gas of its interests in oil exploration areas in the Lake Albert basin to Tullow Oil in 2010, for approximately USD 1.45 billion, led the Uganda Revenue Authority to assess capital gains tax of approximately USD 404 million. Tullow paid a portion of the tax to the government as a condition of obtaining approval of the transaction, and successfully claimed an indemnity from Heritage in the English courts in *Tullow Uganda Operations Pty Ltd v Heritage Oil and Gas Ltd* [2014] EWCA Civ 1048. The case illustrates the leverage that host governments derive from the requirement of consent to the transfer of licences, and the importance of tax indemnities in acquisition agreements.

Many resource-rich states have since amended their laws to tax indirect transfers of interests in domestic resource assets, and the Platform for Collaboration on Tax published a toolkit on the taxation of offshore indirect transfers in 2020. The UN Model was amended in 2017 to permit source state taxation of gains from the alienation of shares deriving their value principally from immovable property, including resource rights, a provision also reflected in Article 13(4) of the OECD Model.

### BEPS and the Global Minimum Tax

The OECD/G20 **Base Erosion and Profit Shifting** (BEPS) project, whose fifteen action reports were delivered in 2015, addressed the strategies by which multinational enterprises shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions, including hybrid mismatches, treaty abuse, the artificial avoidance of permanent establishment status, transfer pricing of intangibles and risk, and interest deductions. The BEPS measures were implemented through national legislation, revisions to the OECD Model and Guidelines, and the **Multilateral Convention to Implement Tax Treaty Related Measures to Prevent BEPS** (the MLI), signed in 2017, which introduced a **principal purpose test** to deny treaty benefits where obtaining them was one of the principal purposes of an arrangement. These measures have restricted the use of treaty shopping through conduit jurisdictions, such as Mauritius and the Netherlands, which had been widely used to structure investment in African resource projects.

The **Two-Pillar Solution**, agreed by the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework in October 2021, comprises Pillar One, which would reallocate a portion of the residual profits of the largest and most profitable multinational enterprises to market jurisdictions, and from which extractive activities are excluded, and **Pillar Two**, the Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) rules, which establish a global minimum effective tax rate of fifteen per cent for multinational groups with annual revenues of at least EUR 750 million. Pillar Two has been implemented in the European Union by Council Directive (EU) 2022/2523 and in numerous other jurisdictions from 2024. Pillar Two has significant implications for resource-rich states: tax incentives, such as tax holidays granted under stabilised investment agreements, may result in the profits being taxed by a "top-up tax" in the parent company's jurisdiction, so that the incentive transfers revenue to the residence state rather than benefiting the investor, and host states may respond by adopting a **qualified domestic minimum top-up tax** to collect the top-up tax themselves. The interaction between Pillar Two and stabilisation clauses that freeze the tax regime is an emerging legal issue. The United States' position on the application of Pillar Two to United States-parented groups changed in 2025, creating uncertainty about the global implementation of the rules.

Developing countries have argued that the OECD-led process has not adequately reflected their interests. In December 2023, the United Nations General Assembly decided to begin work on a **United Nations Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation**, and terms of reference for the negotiation of the convention and early protocols were adopted in 2024, with negotiations scheduled to continue until 2027. The African Tax Administration Forum and initiatives such as Tax Inspectors Without Borders, launched by the OECD and UNDP in 2015, support capacity building in tax administration, including in the extractive sector.
