## 6.3 Gas Transportation and Pipeline Access

### The Gas Supply Chain and Its Regulation

The physical supply chain for pipeline gas comprises upstream gathering pipelines, which collect gas from wells and convey it to processing facilities; high-pressure transmission pipelines, which carry processed gas over long distances; storage facilities, including depleted reservoirs, salt caverns, and aquifers, which balance seasonal and short-term fluctuations in demand; and low-pressure distribution networks, which deliver gas to end-users. LNG terminals, considered in section 6.4, perform an analogous function at the interface between maritime and pipeline transport. Each element raises distinct regulatory issues, but the central questions are the same: who may own and operate the infrastructure; on what terms must the owner grant access to third parties; how are tariffs determined; and how is new investment encouraged.

### Models of Industry Structure

Historically, gas industries in most countries were organised as vertically integrated monopolies, whether state-owned (as in France, Italy, and much of the developing world) or privately owned but regulated (as in the United States). A single company, or a small number of regional companies, purchased gas from producers, transported it, and sold it to distributors or end-users. Liberalisation sought to introduce competition in the potentially competitive segments of the chain by separating them from the monopoly networks and requiring network owners to provide access to competing suppliers on non-discriminatory terms.

The United States led this process. Under the Natural Gas Act of 1938, interstate pipelines were regulated by the Federal Power Commission (now the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, FERC) as merchants that bought and resold gas. Following the partial decontrol of wellhead prices under the Natural Gas Policy Act of 1978, FERC Order No 436 (1985) encouraged pipelines to offer open-access transportation services, and Order No 636 (1992) required interstate pipelines to unbundle their sales and transportation services, transforming them into pure transporters and creating a competitive wholesale gas market. The United Kingdom followed with the privatisation of British Gas in 1986, the Gas Act 1995, and the separation of transportation from supply, producing one of the most liquid gas markets in the world. The European Union adopted a sequence of liberalisation directives, culminating in the Third Energy Package of 2009, comprising Directive 2009/73/EC concerning common rules for the internal market in natural gas and Regulation (EC) No 715/2009 on conditions for access to the natural gas transmission networks. In 2024, the EU adopted the Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package — Directive (EU) 2024/1788 and Regulation (EU) 2024/1789 — which recasts the gas market rules and extends them to the emerging hydrogen sector.

### Third-Party Access

Third-party access (TPA) is the right of a person other than the owner of a pipeline or other facility to use its capacity to transport gas. TPA may be negotiated or regulated.

Under **negotiated TPA**, the owner and the access seeker negotiate the terms of access commercially, subject to a general obligation to negotiate in good faith and not to discriminate, and to the oversight of a regulator or competition authority in the event of a dispute. Negotiated TPA is common for upstream pipelines and processing facilities, where the circumstances of each access request are highly specific. In the United Kingdom, for example, the Energy Act 2011 and the Infrastructure Code of Practice govern access to upstream oil and gas infrastructure on the UK Continental Shelf, and the North Sea Transition Authority may determine the terms of access in default of agreement.

Under **regulated TPA**, the terms of access, including tariffs, are published in advance and approved or set by an independent regulator. Regulated TPA is the norm for transmission and distribution networks in the European Union, where Directive 2009/73/EC (now the recast Directive) requires member states to ensure a system of third-party access to transmission and distribution systems and LNG facilities based on published tariffs, applicable to all eligible customers and applied objectively and without discrimination. Access to storage may be either negotiated or regulated, at the option of the member state.

Access may be refused on limited grounds. The EU directive permits a natural gas undertaking to refuse access on the basis of lack of capacity, or where access would prevent it from carrying out public service obligations, or on the basis of serious economic and financial difficulties with take-or-pay contracts. Refusals must be duly substantiated.

### Unbundling

Third-party access is unlikely to be effective if the network owner is also a supplier of gas in competition with the access seekers, because it will have both the incentive and the means to discriminate against them — for example, by underinvesting in capacity, manipulating information, or delaying connections. Liberalisation has therefore been accompanied by requirements for the *unbundling* of network activities from supply and production activities. Unbundling may take several forms, of increasing stringency:

1. **Accounting unbundling**, requiring separate accounts for network and supply activities, to prevent cross-subsidisation.
2. **Functional or legal unbundling**, requiring network activities to be carried out by a separate legal entity with independent management, although it may remain within a vertically integrated group.
3. **Independent system operator (ISO)**, under which the integrated group retains ownership of the network but its operation is entrusted to an independent operator.
4. **Independent transmission operator (ITO)**, under which the network remains within the integrated group but is subject to detailed rules ensuring its independence in management and decision-making.
5. **Ownership unbundling**, requiring the separation of ownership of transmission networks from production and supply interests.

The Third Energy Package required member states to choose among ownership unbundling, the ISO model, and the ITO model for transmission system operators, and the choice was the subject of intense political controversy, with France and Germany defending the ITO model to protect their integrated incumbents. The "third country clause" in Article 11 of the Directive required certification of transmission system operators controlled by persons from third countries, permitting refusal of certification where the grant would put at risk the security of energy supply of the member state or the Union — a provision directed in large part at Gazprom.

### Tariff Regulation

The regulation of transportation tariffs seeks to allow network owners to recover their efficiently incurred costs, including a reasonable return on capital, while preventing monopoly pricing. The principal methodologies are *cost-of-service* or *rate-of-return* regulation, which sets tariffs to recover a defined revenue requirement based on operating costs, depreciation, and a return on the regulated asset base; and *incentive regulation*, such as price caps or revenue caps of the "RPI–X" type pioneered in the United Kingdom, under which tariffs are set for multi-year periods and the network owner retains the benefits of efficiency improvements. Tariff structures may be *distance-related* (point-to-point), *postage stamp* (uniform irrespective of distance), or *entry–exit*, under which users book capacity separately at entry and exit points and gas can flow freely within the system between them. The EU Network Code on Harmonised Transmission Tariff Structures for Gas (Regulation (EU) 2017/460) requires entry–exit systems and sets out detailed rules for reference price methodologies.

### Pipeline Regulation in Nigeria

Nigeria's gas transportation infrastructure has historically been owned and operated by the Nigerian Gas Company, a subsidiary of NNPC, now succeeded within NNPC Ltd's corporate structure. The PIA establishes a framework for midstream gas regulation by the NMDPRA, including licensing of gas transportation pipelines, gas distribution systems, gas processing facilities, and gas storage; the establishment of a Gas Transportation Network Code governing access to the transportation network; open access to pipelines and facilities on a non-discriminatory basis, subject to available capacity; and the regulation of tariffs. The Act requires the separation of gas transportation network operation from gas wholesale and supply activities, reflecting the European unbundling model. Major infrastructure projects, including the Ajaokuta–Kaduna–Kano (AKK) pipeline and the Obiafu–Obrikom–Oben (OB3) pipeline, are intended to connect gas resources in the south with demand centres in the north, but have faced delays associated with financing, security, and construction challenges.

## 6.4 Liquefied Natural Gas Project Structures

### The LNG Value Chain

The LNG value chain comprises upstream production of feed gas; liquefaction, in which the gas is treated and cooled to approximately minus 162 degrees Celsius, reducing its volume by a factor of around six hundred; shipping in specialised LNG carriers; and regasification at import terminals, either onshore or on floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs). Each stage requires very large capital investments: a large liquefaction plant may cost tens of billions of United States dollars, a modern LNG carrier more than two hundred million dollars, and a regasification terminal several hundred million dollars or more. The capital intensity of the chain, together with its specificity and long life, means that LNG projects have historically required long-term contracts covering most of their output before financing can be secured.

### Principal Project Structures

LNG export projects have been organised according to several structures, distinguished by the relationship between ownership of the upstream gas, the liquefaction facilities, and the LNG product.

**The integrated structure.** Under the integrated structure, the same parties (in the same proportions) own the upstream gas reserves and the liquefaction plant, and sell LNG directly to buyers. The upstream and liquefaction activities are treated as a single project for fiscal and commercial purposes. The structure aligns the interests of upstream and midstream investors and avoids the need for a transfer price between them, but it requires all investors to participate in both segments. Many projects in Qatar, Australia, and elsewhere have used integrated structures.

**The merchant structure.** Under the merchant structure, the liquefaction company is owned separately from the upstream producers (or by them in different proportions). It purchases feed gas from the producers under gas supply agreements, liquefies it, and sells LNG to buyers on its own account, taking the margin between its purchase and sales prices. Nigeria LNG Limited (NLNG), owned by NNPC (49 per cent), Shell (25.6 per cent), TotalEnergies (15 per cent), and Eni (10.4 per cent), operates on a broadly merchant basis, purchasing feed gas from upstream joint ventures and selling LNG under long-term contracts to buyers in Europe and elsewhere. The merchant structure requires a negotiated feed gas price, which allocates value between upstream and midstream, and it may raise fiscal issues where the liquefaction company is subject to a different tax regime from the upstream producers.

**The tolling structure.** Under the tolling structure, the liquefaction company does not take title to the gas. It provides liquefaction services to the gas owners (or to LNG offtakers) in return for a tolling fee, which is typically fixed and payable regardless of whether capacity is used, providing a utility-like return to the plant owners. The gas owners retain title through the liquefaction process and sell the LNG themselves. The tolling model was used for later trains of Atlantic LNG in Trinidad and Tobago and for several of the export projects developed in the United States from the mid-2010s, such as Freeport LNG, whose capacity is contracted to offtakers under liquefaction tolling agreements. Other United States projects, such as Cheniere's Sabine Pass, sell LNG under sale and purchase agreements priced at a percentage of Henry Hub plus a fixed fee, which replicate the economics of tolling. Under the United States model, buyers enter into long-term "liquefaction services" or sale and purchase agreements under which they pay a fixed liquefaction fee for capacity and purchase feed gas at Henry Hub prices plus a percentage, giving them flexibility to lift cargoes or cancel them (paying the fixed fee) and to resell LNG anywhere in the world.

**Hybrid structures.** Many projects combine elements of these structures, and the choice depends on the fiscal regime, the identity and objectives of the investors, the availability of financing, and the requirements of the host state.

### The Host State Framework

LNG projects typically require a dedicated legal framework, often in the form of a project-specific statute or host government agreement, providing fiscal terms, guarantees against expropriation, stability of the legal regime, rights to export LNG, and rights to retain export proceeds offshore. The Nigeria LNG (Fiscal Incentives, Guarantees and Assurances) Act 1990 is an example: it conferred on NLNG a package of fiscal incentives, guarantees, and assurances, including exemption from certain taxes for an initial period, a guarantee against nationalisation, the right to maintain offshore accounts, and a commitment that the Act could not be amended without the consent of the company's shareholders. The NLNG Act illustrates both the attractions and the controversies of project-specific legislation. When the Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency (NIMASA) sought to impose levies on NLNG's shipping activities, and when the Federal Government sought to subject NLNG to contributions to the Niger Delta Development Commission, the company invoked the protections of the Act, generating litigation concerning the extent to which a statute can bind future legislatures. The question whether a legislature may validly restrict its own future legislative competence by statute raises fundamental constitutional issues; in common law systems influenced by the doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty, such entrenchment is generally ineffective as a matter of domestic law, although it may still give rise to liability under contract or investment treaties.

### Shipping

LNG shipping arrangements depend on the delivery terms of the sales contract. Under a *free on board* (FOB) contract, the buyer takes delivery at the loading port and is responsible for shipping; under a *delivered ex-ship* (DES) or *cost, insurance and freight* (CIF) contract, the seller is responsible for shipping and delivers the LNG at the discharge port. LNG carriers are chartered under long-term time charters, voyage charters, or, increasingly, short-term charters for spot cargoes. The legal issues include the compatibility of vessels with terminals (ship–shore compatibility), the allocation of boil-off gas (LNG that evaporates during the voyage), demurrage, and the application of maritime conventions.

### Regasification and FSRUs

The legal structure of regasification terminals varies. Some terminals are owned by the buyers that use them; others are owned by independent operators that offer capacity to multiple users under terminal use agreements, subject to regulated or negotiated third-party access. In the European Union, LNG terminals are subject to regulated third-party access, but major new infrastructure may obtain an exemption under Article 36 of Directive 2009/73/EC (now carried forward in the recast) where the investment enhances competition and security of supply and would not take place without the exemption. Many European terminals, including the Adriatic LNG terminal in Italy and the Gate terminal in Rotterdam, have obtained exemptions.

Floating storage and regasification units have dramatically reduced the time and cost required to establish LNG import capacity, enabling countries such as Egypt, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and, following the 2022 crisis, Germany to begin importing LNG within months rather than years. Germany's LNG Acceleration Act (LNG-Beschleunigungsgesetz) of May 2022 expedited permitting for FSRUs and onshore terminals by, among other things, temporarily exempting projects from certain environmental impact assessment requirements, illustrating the use of emergency legislation to accelerate energy infrastructure.

### LNG Export Authorisation

Some states require specific authorisation for the export of LNG. In the United States, section 3 of the Natural Gas Act requires authorisation by the Department of Energy for exports of natural gas. Exports to countries with which the United States has a free trade agreement requiring national treatment for trade in natural gas are deemed to be consistent with the public interest and must be granted without modification or delay; exports to non-FTA countries must be authorised unless the Department finds that they would not be consistent with the public interest. In January 2024, the Department announced a temporary pause on pending decisions on non-FTA exports while it updated the economic and environmental analyses underlying its public interest determinations; the pause was lifted in January 2025 by the incoming administration. The episode illustrates the regulatory risk facing LNG projects, whose commercial viability depends on export authorisation, and the growing incorporation of climate considerations into export licensing.
