## 12.6 Offshore Renewable Energy Technologies

### The Expansion of Offshore Energy

The ocean offers vast renewable energy resources: offshore wind, which is stronger and more consistent than onshore wind; wave energy; tidal energy, derived from tidal ranges and tidal currents; ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC), which exploits temperature differences between surface and deep waters in tropical regions; and salinity gradient energy. Offshore wind is the only one of these technologies that has achieved commercial maturity at scale, with tens of gigawatts installed, predominantly in the North Sea, the Baltic Sea, and the coastal waters of China. **Floating offshore wind**, in which turbines are mounted on floating platforms moored to the seabed, extends the potential of offshore wind to deep waters, which account for most of the global offshore wind resource, including off the coasts of Japan, Korea, the western United States, Norway, and parts of Africa. Wave and tidal energy remain at the demonstration and early commercial stage, with projects such as the MeyGen tidal stream array in Scotland.

This section examines the legal framework governing offshore renewable technologies — their installation, the allocation of marine space, offshore grids, and decommissioning. The leasing and permitting of offshore wind have been introduced in Chapter 10, and the law of the sea relating to maritime zones, submarine cables, and seabed resources more generally is examined in Chapter 20.

### The Law of the Sea Framework

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) provides the international legal framework for offshore renewable energy. In the **territorial sea**, extending up to twelve nautical miles from the baselines, the coastal state exercises sovereignty, subject to the right of innocent passage of foreign ships, and may authorise offshore renewable installations under its domestic law. In the **exclusive economic zone**, extending up to two hundred nautical miles, Article 56(1)(a) grants the coastal state sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring and exploiting, conserving, and managing the natural resources of the zone, "and with regard to other activities for the economic exploitation and exploration of the zone, such as the production of energy from the water, currents and winds". Article 60 grants the coastal state the exclusive right to construct and to authorise and regulate the construction, operation, and use of artificial islands, installations, and structures for these purposes, and exclusive jurisdiction over them, including with regard to customs, fiscal, health, safety, and immigration laws and regulations.

Article 60 imposes obligations on the coastal state in respect of installations in the EEZ. Due notice must be given of their construction, and permanent means for giving warning of their presence must be maintained. Installations which are abandoned or disused must be removed to ensure safety of navigation, taking into account generally accepted international standards (the IMO's 1989 *Guidelines and Standards for the Removal of Offshore Installations and Structures on the Continental Shelf and in the Exclusive Economic Zone*). The coastal state may establish reasonable safety zones around installations, which shall not exceed a distance of five hundred metres around them, except as authorised by generally accepted international standards or as recommended by the competent international organisation. Installations may not be established where interference may be caused to the use of recognised sea lanes essential to international navigation. In exercising its rights in the EEZ, the coastal state must have due regard to the rights and duties of other states, including the freedoms of navigation, overflight, and the laying of submarine cables and pipelines (Article 56(2) and Article 58).

The growth of large offshore wind farms raises questions about the adequacy of the five-hundred-metre limit on safety zones, the effect of large arrays on navigation and on the rights of other states, the extension of offshore renewable development beyond the EEZ onto the extended continental shelf (where the coastal state's rights under Part VI of UNCLOS relate to natural resources of the seabed and subsoil and may not extend to wind energy in the superjacent waters), and the protection of marine biodiversity. The **Agreement under UNCLOS on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Marine Biological Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction** (BBNJ Agreement, 2023), which provides for environmental impact assessments of activities with potential effects in areas beyond national jurisdiction, may apply to the transboundary impacts of large offshore energy installations.

### Maritime Spatial Planning

The allocation of marine space among competing uses — offshore energy, shipping, fishing, aquaculture, conservation, defence, cables and pipelines, and tourism — is increasingly governed by **maritime spatial planning**. The EU Maritime Spatial Planning Directive (Directive 2014/89/EU) required member states to establish maritime spatial plans by 2021, taking into account land-sea interactions, environmental, economic, social, and safety aspects, and cooperation with neighbouring states. Maritime spatial plans designate areas suitable for offshore renewable energy, and RED III requires member states to designate renewables acceleration areas, including offshore. The integration of offshore wind with conservation objectives has generated debate about the co-location of wind farms with marine protected areas and the potential for offshore wind foundations to act as artificial reefs.

### Offshore Grids and Hybrid Projects

The scale of planned offshore wind development, particularly in the North Sea, has led to a shift from the radial connection of individual wind farms to shore towards **offshore grids** — networks of offshore transmission infrastructure that connect multiple wind farms and interconnect national electricity systems. **Hybrid projects** combine offshore wind connection and cross-border interconnection, allowing offshore wind to be exported to more than one country. These projects raise complex legal questions: the classification of hybrid assets as transmission or interconnection; the allocation of cross-border capacity (under EU rules requiring that at least seventy per cent of interconnection capacity be made available for cross-border trade, examined in Chapter 9); the definition of **offshore bidding zones**, in which offshore wind farms connected to a hybrid asset receive the price of the offshore zone, rather than that of the onshore zone, which may reduce their revenues; the allocation of costs and benefits between states; and the governance of cross-border infrastructure.

In the **Esbjerg Declaration** of May 2022, Denmark, Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands committed to developing the North Sea as a "green power plant of Europe", with a combined target of at least 65 GW of offshore wind by 2030 and 150 GW by 2050. In the **Ostend Declaration** of April 2023, nine North Seas states set targets of 120 GW by 2030 and 300 GW by 2050. The **North Seas Energy Cooperation**, an intergovernmental cooperation framework, supports coordination of offshore grid planning. The EU's TEN-E Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2022/869) provides for priority offshore grid corridors and for cooperation among member states in the development of offshore network development plans, and the 2024 electricity market reform addresses cost sharing and revenue arrangements for offshore hybrid projects. Denmark's plans for artificial "energy islands" in the North Sea and on Bornholm in the Baltic, intended as hubs for offshore wind and interconnectors, illustrate both the ambition and the difficulties of offshore grid development: Denmark put the North Sea energy island on hold after cost estimates rose substantially.

### Wave, Tidal, and Ocean Thermal Energy

Wave and tidal energy technologies require legal frameworks for seabed leasing, consenting, and support. The United Kingdom's Contracts for Difference scheme has included ring-fenced budgets for tidal stream energy in recent allocation rounds, supporting deployment. The proposed Swansea Bay tidal lagoon, which would have used a seawall to harness the tidal range, was rejected by the United Kingdom government in 2018 on the ground that it did not offer value for money, illustrating the significance of support mechanisms and cost for novel technologies. Tidal range and tidal stream projects raise environmental issues relating to marine mammals, fish, and estuarine ecosystems, and require appropriate assessment under nature conservation law. OTEC, which is of interest to tropical island states, raises issues relating to the discharge of cold deep water and the location of installations in deep waters.

### Offshore Renewables in Africa

Offshore renewable energy development in Africa is at an early stage. South Africa has identified significant offshore wind potential, and the World Bank's Offshore Wind Development Program has supported assessments of offshore wind resources in several African countries, including Morocco, Mauritania, South Africa, and Nigeria, and the development of roadmaps. Nigeria does not have a legal framework specifically addressing offshore renewable energy; offshore activities in its territorial sea and EEZ are governed principally by petroleum legislation, the **Exclusive Economic Zone Act 1978**, and the **Territorial Waters Act 1967**, as amended, and the allocation of offshore areas for renewable energy would require consideration of the interaction with petroleum licensing under the PIA, coastal state jurisdiction, and federal–state relations. The development of offshore renewable frameworks in African states offers an opportunity to learn from the experience of other jurisdictions in the design of leasing, consenting, local content, and decommissioning rules.

### Decommissioning of Offshore Renewable Installations

The decommissioning of offshore renewable installations is governed by Article 60(3) of UNCLOS, the IMO Guidelines of 1989, and, in the North-East Atlantic, the OSPAR Convention, together with domestic law. Domestic regimes typically require developers to prepare decommissioning programmes and to provide financial security. In the United Kingdom, the Energy Act 2004 requires persons responsible for offshore renewable energy installations to submit decommissioning programmes for approval and empowers the Secretary of State to require financial security. The decommissioning of offshore wind farms raises questions similar to those of offshore petroleum installations, examined in Chapter 5, including the extent of removal required (particularly of foundations, scour protection, and cables), the potential for "rigs-to-reefs" approaches, and the recycling of turbine blades.

## 12.7 Smart Grids, Digital Infrastructure, and Cybersecurity

### The Digitalisation of Energy Systems

The electricity system is being transformed by digitalisation: smart meters that measure consumption at short intervals and communicate data to suppliers and network operators; sensors and automated controls on transmission and distribution networks; distributed energy resources — rooftop solar, batteries, electric vehicles, and heat pumps — that can be controlled remotely and aggregated; digital platforms for peer-to-peer trading and flexibility markets; and the use of artificial intelligence to forecast demand and renewable output and to optimise system operation. A **smart grid** is an electricity network that uses digital technologies to monitor and manage the flow of electricity from all generation sources to meet the varying demands of end users efficiently, reliably, and sustainably. At the same time, the rapid growth of electricity demand from data centres, driven by cloud computing and artificial intelligence, has created new pressures on electricity systems and new questions of grid access, cost allocation, and planning, examined in Chapter 9.

Digitalisation raises legal issues in four principal areas: the regulation of smart metering; the protection of energy data and privacy; the governance of data access and interoperability; and cybersecurity.

### Smart Metering

The deployment of smart meters has been mandated or encouraged in many jurisdictions. In the European Union, the Third Energy Package required member states to ensure the implementation of intelligent metering systems where a cost-benefit assessment was positive, with a target of rolling out smart meters to at least eighty per cent of consumers by 2020. Articles 19 to 21 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 set out requirements for smart metering systems, including functionality, the right of consumers to access their data, data protection, cybersecurity, and interoperability, and the right of every consumer to request a smart meter where the roll-out is not positively assessed. In Great Britain, energy suppliers were required by licence conditions to take all reasonable steps to roll out smart meters to their domestic and small business customers, and the Data Communications Company, a licensed body, provides a national communications infrastructure for smart meters.

In Nigeria, metering is a critical issue for the electricity sector, because a large proportion of customers have been unmetered and charged through "estimated billing", which has been a major source of consumer grievance and revenue leakage. NERC's **Meter Asset Provider Regulations** (2018, subsequently revised) enabled third-party meter asset providers to finance, supply, and install meters for distribution companies, and the **National Mass Metering Programme**, launched in 2020, sought to provide meters free of charge to unmetered customers. NERC has issued orders capping estimated bills for unmetered customers, and the transition to prepayment and smart meters raises issues of consumer protection, data, and the security of revenue examined in Chapter 9.

### Energy Data and Privacy

Smart meters and connected devices generate granular data on energy consumption that can reveal intimate details of private life, including occupancy patterns, daily routines, and the use of particular appliances. Energy data is therefore personal data subject to data protection law where it relates to identified or identifiable individuals. In the European Union, the **General Data Protection Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) applies to the processing of energy data, requiring a lawful basis for processing, purpose limitation, data minimisation, and security, and the Electricity Directive requires member states to ensure that the processing of consumers' metering data complies with data protection law. In the Netherlands, the initial proposal for a mandatory smart meter roll-out was withdrawn in 2009 after the Senate expressed concerns that compulsory installation would infringe the right to respect for private life under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, and the roll-out proceeded on a voluntary basis with privacy safeguards. In Nigeria, the **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**, which established the Nigeria Data Protection Commission, governs the processing of personal data, including by electricity distribution companies and meter providers.

### Data Access and Interoperability

The value of energy data for innovation — enabling new services such as demand response, energy efficiency advice, and the aggregation of flexibility — depends on the ability of consumers and authorised third parties to access data in standardised, interoperable formats. Article 23 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires member states to specify rules on access to data of the final customer by eligible parties, ensuring efficient and secure data access and exchange, and Article 24 provides for the adoption of interoperability requirements and non-discriminatory and transparent procedures for access to data. The EU's **Data Act** (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) establishes general rules on access to data generated by connected products, including smart energy devices, giving users the right to access and share such data with third parties. The governance of energy data raises tensions between innovation, competition, privacy, and security.

### Cybersecurity

The digitalisation of energy systems has increased their exposure to cyberattacks, which can disrupt supply, damage equipment, and endanger safety. In December 2015, a cyberattack on distribution companies in western Ukraine caused power outages affecting around two hundred and twenty-five thousand customers — the first publicly confirmed cyberattack causing a power outage — and a further attack in December 2016, using malware designed specifically to attack electricity grid control systems, caused an outage in part of Kyiv. In May 2021, a ransomware attack on the information technology systems of **Colonial Pipeline**, which operates the largest refined products pipeline system in the United States, led the company to shut down the pipeline for several days, causing fuel shortages on the east coast; the company paid a ransom, part of which was subsequently recovered by the Department of Justice. In response, the Transportation Security Administration issued security directives imposing mandatory cybersecurity requirements on pipeline operators, illustrating the shift from voluntary to mandatory cybersecurity regulation in the energy sector.

**Regulatory frameworks.** In the United States, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, the electric reliability organisation certified by FERC under section 215 of the Federal Power Act (added by the Energy Policy Act of 2005), develops mandatory **Critical Infrastructure Protection** (CIP) reliability standards, approved and enforced by FERC, which require owners and operators of the bulk electric system to identify critical cyber assets and implement security controls, with substantial penalties for violations. In the European Union, the **NIS2 Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) imposes cybersecurity risk management and incident reporting obligations on essential and important entities, including in the energy sector (electricity, district heating, oil, gas, and hydrogen); the **Critical Entities Resilience Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2557) addresses the physical and other resilience of critical entities; and the **Network Code on Cybersecurity** for cross-border electricity flows (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1366) establishes sector-specific rules for cybersecurity risk assessment and management in the electricity sector. In Nigeria, the **Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) Act 2015**, as amended in 2024, provides for the designation and protection of critical national information infrastructure and criminalises attacks on it.

**International law.** Cyberattacks on energy infrastructure by states or state-sponsored actors raise questions of international law, including the prohibition of the use of force, the principle of non-intervention, state responsibility, and, in armed conflict, international humanitarian law. The reports of the United Nations Group of Governmental Experts on Developments in the Field of Information and Telecommunications in the Context of International Security, endorsed by the General Assembly, affirmed in 2013 that international law, and in particular the United Nations Charter, applies to cyberspace, and in 2015 set out voluntary norms of responsible state behaviour, including that a state should not conduct or knowingly support activity contrary to its obligations under international law that intentionally damages critical infrastructure or otherwise impairs the use and operation of critical infrastructure to provide services to the public. The *Tallinn Manual 2.0 on the International Law Applicable to Cyber Operations* (2017), prepared by an international group of experts, offers a scholarly restatement of the application of international law to cyber operations, including the protection of critical infrastructure. These questions are examined further in Chapter 25.
