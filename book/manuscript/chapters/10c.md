## 10.5 Geothermal and Biomass Energy Regulation

### Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy — heat derived from the earth's interior — can be used to generate electricity, where high-temperature resources are available, typically in tectonically active regions, and for direct heating and cooling. Geothermal power provides firm, dispatchable, low-carbon electricity, and is a major source of supply in Kenya, Iceland, El Salvador, New Zealand, the Philippines, and Indonesia. Kenya, which has developed the Olkaria geothermal fields in the East African Rift, generates a substantial share of its electricity from geothermal sources and is among the world's leading geothermal producers. Emerging technologies, including enhanced geothermal systems and closed-loop systems, may extend geothermal development beyond traditional high-temperature regions.

**Legal classification.** The legal classification of geothermal resources is a threshold question with significant consequences. Geothermal resources may be classified as minerals (and governed by mining law), as water (and governed by water law), or as a sui generis resource (governed by specific geothermal legislation). Classification determines who owns the resource, which authority regulates it, and which legal regime applies. In the United States, the Geothermal Steam Act of 1970 governs geothermal leasing on federal lands, and state laws variously classify geothermal resources as minerals, water, or a distinct resource. In Indonesia, geothermal resources were historically regulated as a mining activity, which meant that geothermal exploration was prohibited in conservation forests under the forestry laws governing mining; Law No 21 of 2014 on Geothermal reclassified geothermal energy as a non-mining activity, enabling development in conservation forest areas subject to environmental permits, and transferred licensing authority for geothermal power from regional governments to the central government. Kenya's Energy Act 2019, which replaced the Geothermal Resources Act 1982, vests geothermal resources in the national government and provides for licensing by the Cabinet Secretary.

**Licensing and risk.** Geothermal development involves high upfront exploration risk: drilling exploratory wells is expensive, and the characteristics of the resource cannot be confirmed until wells are drilled. Many governments therefore undertake or finance early-stage exploration through state geothermal companies (such as Kenya's Geothermal Development Company) or risk mitigation facilities (such as the Geothermal Risk Mitigation Facility for Eastern Africa, supported by the African Union Commission and development partners), and then offer proven steam fields to private developers. Geothermal licences typically follow a sequence of exploration and exploitation permits similar to that in mining and petroleum law.

**Environmental issues.** Geothermal development can cause induced seismicity (particularly with enhanced geothermal systems), land subsidence, emissions of hydrogen sulphide and other gases, and impacts on surface thermal features. The suspension of an enhanced geothermal project in Pohang, South Korea, after it was found by a government commission to have triggered a magnitude 5.4 earthquake in 2017, and the abandonment of a geothermal project in Basel, Switzerland, after induced earthquakes in 2006, illustrate the risks. Geothermal projects in Kenya's Rift Valley have also raised issues of land acquisition and resettlement affecting Maasai communities, which were the subject of complaints to the World Bank's Inspection Panel concerning the Olkaria IV project.

### Biomass and Bioenergy

Bioenergy — energy derived from biomass, including wood, agricultural residues, energy crops, and organic waste — is the largest source of renewable energy globally when traditional uses of biomass for cooking and heating are included. Modern bioenergy includes the combustion of biomass for electricity and heat, the production of liquid biofuels (ethanol and biodiesel) for transport, and the production of biogas and biomethane from anaerobic digestion. Traditional biomass use — the burning of wood, charcoal, crop residues, and dung for cooking in inefficient stoves — remains the primary source of cooking energy for around two billion people, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, causing household air pollution that the World Health Organization estimates contributes to millions of premature deaths annually, and contributing to deforestation. The transition to clean cooking is examined in Chapter 23.

**Sustainability concerns.** The renewable and low-carbon credentials of bioenergy depend critically on how the biomass is produced. The combustion of wood releases carbon dioxide at the point of combustion, and the carbon is reabsorbed only as replacement trees grow, which may take decades; if biomass is sourced from primary forests or causes deforestation, its net climate impact may be negative over relevant time horizons. Biofuels produced from food crops may compete with food production and drive land use change, including the conversion of forests and grasslands to agriculture elsewhere ("indirect land use change", or ILUC), which may negate their emissions savings. These concerns have led to the development of sustainability criteria in law.

**The EU sustainability criteria.** The EU Renewable Energy Directive establishes sustainability and greenhouse gas emissions saving criteria that biofuels, bioliquids, and biomass fuels must satisfy in order to count towards renewable energy targets, to be eligible for financial support, and to count towards renewable energy obligations. Under Article 29 of Directive (EU) 2018/2001, as amended by RED III, the criteria prohibit the sourcing of agricultural biomass from land with high biodiversity value (primary forests, highly biodiverse grasslands, and protected areas) or high carbon stock (wetlands, continuously forested areas, and peatland); impose requirements on forest biomass to ensure legal harvesting, regeneration, protection of biodiversity and soil, and maintenance of carbon stocks; and require minimum greenhouse gas emissions savings compared with fossil fuels. The ILUC Directive (Directive (EU) 2015/1513) capped the contribution of biofuels produced from food and feed crops towards transport targets, and the Commission's Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/807 identified palm oil as a high-ILUC-risk feedstock, to be phased out from counting towards targets by 2030. The phase-out of palm oil biofuels was challenged by Malaysia and Indonesia before the WTO in *EU — Palm Oil (Malaysia)* (DS600) and *EU — Palm Oil (Indonesia)* (DS593); in March 2024, the panel in the Malaysian case accepted the legitimacy of the EU's objective of addressing ILUC, while finding deficiencies in certain aspects of the design and application of the measures, and a panel report in the Indonesian case followed in early 2025.

**Biofuel mandates.** Many countries mandate the blending of biofuels with petrol and diesel. The United States Renewable Fuel Standard, established under the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and expanded by the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007, requires the blending of specified volumes of renewable fuels, administered through a system of tradable Renewable Identification Numbers. Brazil's long-standing ethanol programme and blending mandates have made it a world leader in sugarcane ethanol. Nigeria's **Official Gazette of the Nigerian Bio-fuel Policy and Incentives** (2007) set targets for blending ethanol and biodiesel with petroleum products, but implementation has been limited.

**Waste-to-energy and biogas.** The conversion of municipal solid waste, agricultural residues, and sewage into energy through incineration, gasification, or anaerobic digestion is regulated by waste law, air quality law, and energy law. EU law establishes a waste hierarchy, under Directive 2008/98/EC, that prioritises prevention, reuse, and recycling over energy recovery, and waste-to-energy plants are subject to emission limits under the Industrial Emissions Directive.

## 10.6 Renewable Energy Incentives and Support Mechanisms

### The Rationale for Support

Public support for renewable energy has been justified on several grounds: the failure of markets to price the environmental costs of fossil fuels, particularly greenhouse gas emissions, which places renewable energy at an artificial disadvantage; the learning-by-doing benefits of deployment, which drive down costs for all future users but cannot be fully captured by early adopters; energy security and diversification; industrial policy and job creation; and the need to accelerate the transition to meet climate targets. As the costs of wind and solar have fallen, the rationale for direct subsidies has weakened in many markets, and the focus of support has shifted from subsidising generation costs to reducing investment risk and the cost of capital, which is the principal determinant of the cost of renewable electricity.

### Feed-in Tariffs

A **feed-in tariff** (FiT) guarantees eligible renewable generators a fixed price per unit of electricity fed into the grid for a long period (typically fifteen to twenty years), often combined with an obligation on network operators or suppliers to purchase the electricity. The German Electricity Feed-in Act (*Stromeinspeisungsgesetz*) of 1990 and Renewable Energy Sources Act (*Erneuerbare-Energien-Gesetz*, EEG) of 2000 established the model, which was adopted by numerous countries and is credited with driving the early growth of wind and solar energy. FiTs provide high investment certainty, but they require the regulator to set the tariff administratively, with the risk of overcompensation if costs fall faster than anticipated — as occurred with solar PV in Spain, Italy, the Czech Republic, and Germany in the late 2000s, leading to "solar booms" and escalating costs for consumers, and ultimately to retroactive tariff reductions that generated the investment disputes examined in section 10.8.

A **feed-in premium** (FiP) pays generators a premium on top of the market price, either fixed or sliding (varying with the market price to provide a target level of revenue). Feed-in premiums expose generators to market signals while providing revenue support, and EU State aid guidelines from 2014 required member states to move from feed-in tariffs to market-based premiums for all but small installations.

### Quota Obligations and Tradable Certificates

Under a **quota obligation** — known in the United States as a **renewable portfolio standard** (RPS) and in the United Kingdom, until 2017, as the **Renewables Obligation** — electricity suppliers or other obligated parties must source a specified and generally rising proportion of their electricity from renewable sources, demonstrating compliance by surrendering tradable green certificates issued to renewable generators. The price of certificates is determined by the market, and generators receive revenue from the sale of both electricity and certificates. Quota systems leave the quantity of renewable energy to be set by the regulator and the price to be determined by the market, inverting the logic of feed-in tariffs. They have been criticised for exposing generators to volatile certificate prices, increasing the cost of capital. More than half of the states of the United States, together with the District of Columbia, have adopted RPS or clean energy standards, many with targets of one hundred per cent clean electricity by 2040 or 2050.

### Auctions and Contracts for Difference

**Competitive auctions** (or tenders) have become the dominant mechanism for allocating support to utility-scale renewable energy worldwide. Under an auction, the government or a designated agency invites bids for the right to build a specified quantity of renewable capacity or generation, and awards support — usually a long-term PPA or contract for difference — to the bidders offering the lowest price, sometimes combined with other criteria. Auctions combine the investment certainty of long-term contracts with competitive price discovery, and have driven dramatic reductions in the prices of wind and solar electricity in countries including India, Brazil, Mexico, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Morocco.

South Africa's **Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme** (REIPPPP), launched in 2011, is widely regarded as one of the most successful renewable procurement programmes in a developing country. Through successive bid windows, it procured several gigawatts of wind, solar, and other renewable capacity at falling prices, combining price competition with economic development criteria (local content, job creation, and community ownership) and a robust contractual framework of twenty-year PPAs with Eskom backed by government support agreements. Its success was attributed to transparent procurement, clear rules, credible institutions (the IPP Office, a unit established jointly by the Department of Energy, the National Treasury, and the Development Bank of Southern Africa), and bankable documentation, although its momentum was interrupted between 2015 and 2018 when Eskom delayed signing PPAs.

In the United Kingdom, the **Contracts for Difference** (CfD) scheme, established under the Energy Act 2013, awards fifteen-year (now twenty-year for some technologies) private law contracts between generators and the Low Carbon Contracts Company, a government-owned counterparty, through competitive allocation rounds. Under a two-way CfD, the generator receives the difference between a "strike price" and a market reference price when the market price is below the strike price, and pays the difference when the market price is above it. During the 2022 crisis, generators with CfDs paid substantial sums back to the counterparty, reducing consumer bills. As noted in Chapter 9, the EU's 2024 electricity market design reform requires direct price support for new renewable and nuclear investment to take the form of two-way CfDs, subject to exceptions.

### Fiscal Incentives

Tax incentives are a major instrument of renewable energy support, particularly in the United States, where the federal **investment tax credit** (ITC) and **production tax credit** (PTC) were the principal instruments of support for decades. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 extended and restructured these credits, replacing them from 2025 with technology-neutral clean electricity investment and production credits (sections 48E and 45Y of the Internal Revenue Code), with bonus credits for meeting domestic content, energy community, and prevailing wage and apprenticeship requirements, and introduced "transferability", permitting credits to be sold to unrelated taxpayers. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025 significantly curtailed these incentives for wind and solar energy, terminating the credits for wind and solar facilities placed in service after 2027 unless construction began within a short period after enactment, and imposing restrictions related to "prohibited foreign entities" — an illustration of the vulnerability of fiscal incentives to political change. Developing countries commonly use import duty and VAT exemptions for renewable energy equipment, tax holidays, and accelerated depreciation; Nigeria has granted pioneer status incentives and import duty waivers for certain renewable energy equipment.

### State Aid Control in the European Union

In the European Union, renewable energy support schemes funded through state resources are subject to the State aid rules of Articles 107 and 108 TFEU. The Court of Justice's jurisprudence on whether support schemes constitute State aid has been complex. In *PreussenElektra* (Case C-379/98, 2001), the Court held that the German obligation on electricity suppliers to purchase renewable electricity at minimum prices did not involve the transfer of state resources and therefore did not constitute State aid. In *Vent De Colère* (Case C-262/12, 2013), it held that the French scheme, under which the additional costs of purchase obligations were financed by a levy on consumers collected and administered under state control, involved state resources. In *Germany v Commission* (Case C-405/16 P, 2019), the Court set aside the General Court's judgment and annulled the Commission's decision classifying the EEG 2012 as State aid, holding that the sums generated by the EEG surcharge were not under public control and therefore did not constitute state resources. Where schemes do constitute State aid, they must be notified and approved by the Commission under the **Guidelines on State aid for climate, environmental protection and energy** (CEEAG), adopted in 2022, which require, among other things, competitive allocation of aid and a demonstrated incentive effect.

The interaction of national support schemes with the free movement of goods was considered in *Ålands Vindkraft AB v Energimyndigheten* (Case C-573/12, 2014), in which the Court held that a Swedish green certificate scheme that awarded certificates only to producers located in Sweden constituted a measure having equivalent effect to a quantitative restriction on imports, but was justified by the objective of promoting renewable energy, given that the Renewable Energy Directive expressly permitted member states to limit support to domestic production. The WTO compatibility of renewable support schemes with local content requirements, considered in *Canada — Renewable Energy/Feed-in Tariff Program* (2013) and *India — Solar Cells* (2016), is examined in Chapter 13.
