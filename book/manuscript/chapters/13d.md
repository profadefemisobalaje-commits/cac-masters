## 13.6 Regional Trade Agreements and Energy Integration

### Regional Trade Agreements in WTO Law

Regional trade agreements (RTAs) — free trade agreements and customs unions — are an exception to the MFN principle, permitted under Article XXIV of the GATT for trade in goods and Article V of the GATS for trade in services, provided that they meet specified conditions. Article XXIV:8 requires that duties and other restrictive regulations of commerce be eliminated with respect to "substantially all the trade" between the constituent territories, and Article XXIV:5 requires that the duties and other regulations of commerce applied to third parties not be higher or more restrictive than those applicable before the formation of the agreement. In *Turkey — Textiles* (1999), the Appellate Body held that Article XXIV may justify a measure inconsistent with other GATT provisions only if the measure is introduced upon the formation of an RTA that fully meets the requirements of Article XXIV, and the formation of the RTA would be prevented if the measure were not allowed. Hundreds of RTAs are in force, and many contain provisions on energy that go beyond WTO rules, addressing export duties, dual pricing, transit, access to networks, state enterprises, investment, and regulatory cooperation.

### Energy in North American Trade Agreements

The **North American Free Trade Agreement** (NAFTA, 1994) contained a dedicated chapter on energy and basic petrochemicals (Chapter 6), which incorporated GATT disciplines on import and export restrictions and, controversially, a "proportionality" clause (Article 605) under which a party that restricted exports of an energy good to another party for reasons of conservation, shortage, or price stabilisation was required to maintain the proportion of total supply exported to that party over the previous thirty-six months. Mexico was exempted from the proportionality clause, and reserved its constitutional monopoly over hydrocarbons and electricity. The proportionality clause was criticised in Canada as restricting its ability to control its energy resources.

The **United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement** (USMCA, in force 2020), which replaced NAFTA, contains no dedicated energy chapter and no proportionality clause. Mexico's commitments on energy reflect its 2013 constitutional reform, which opened the hydrocarbons and electricity sectors to private investment, but the USMCA includes provisions recognising Mexico's direct, inalienable, and imprescriptible ownership of hydrocarbons in the subsoil. The USMCA's investment chapter substantially narrowed the availability of investor–state arbitration, except for investors with "covered government contracts" in specified sectors, including oil and natural gas and power generation, who retain broader access to arbitration under Annex 14-E. In July 2022, the United States and Canada requested consultations with Mexico under the USMCA regarding Mexican energy policies alleged to favour the state-owned companies Petróleos Mexicanos (Pemex) and the Comisión Federal de Electricidad (CFE) over private and foreign energy companies, including measures under the 2021 amendments to Mexico's Electricity Industry Law. The episode illustrates the role of RTAs in disciplining energy nationalism.

### European Union Agreements

The European Union's trade agreements increasingly contain dedicated chapters on energy and raw materials. The **EU–Ukraine Association Agreement** (2014), including its Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area, contains provisions on trade-related energy, including on transit, the prohibition of dual pricing, and regulatory approximation with EU energy law. The **EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement** (2020) contains a heading on energy and raw materials, which addresses pricing, export monopolies, third-party access to networks, unbundling, and cooperation in electricity trading and offshore energy in the North Seas. The **EU–Chile Advanced Framework Agreement** (2023) contains a chapter on energy and raw materials that prohibits dual pricing and export monopolies, addresses access to energy infrastructure, and promotes cooperation in renewable energy, hydrogen, and critical raw materials, including lithium and copper.

The **Treaty establishing the Energy Community** (2005, in force 2006) extends the EU's internal energy market acquis to non-EU states in South-East Europe and the Black Sea region, including the Western Balkans, Ukraine, Moldova, and Georgia. Contracting parties undertake to implement EU legislation on electricity, gas, renewable energy, energy efficiency, environment, and competition, subject to adaptation and monitoring by the Energy Community Secretariat, with a dispute settlement procedure that may result in a finding of breach by the Ministerial Council. The Energy Community is a distinctive model of regional energy integration through the export of a regulatory acquis.

### The African Continental Free Trade Area

The **Agreement Establishing the African Continental Free Trade Area** (AfCFTA), signed in Kigali in March 2018 and in force since May 2019, with trading under its preferential arrangements commencing in January 2021, aims to create a single continental market for goods and services. It is implemented through protocols on trade in goods, trade in services, rules and procedures on the settlement of disputes, investment (adopted in 2023), competition policy, intellectual property rights, and digital trade, among others. Nigeria is a party.

The AfCFTA has potential significance for energy trade and investment in Africa. Intra-African trade in energy is limited by inadequate infrastructure, but the progressive elimination of tariffs on energy goods, including petroleum products and renewable energy equipment, and the liberalisation of energy-related services could support the development of regional value chains — for example, in the refining of crude oil, the production of fertilisers from natural gas, the manufacture of batteries using African critical minerals, and the assembly of solar equipment. The AfCFTA's rules of origin will determine whether products incorporating imported components qualify for preferential treatment. Energy services were not among the initial priority sectors for services negotiations, but may be addressed in subsequent rounds. The Protocol on Investment includes provisions on the right to regulate and on sustainable development, and the relationship between the AfCFTA and regional energy integration through power pools and the African Single Electricity Market (AfSEM), examined in Chapter 9, is an important area of development.

### ECOWAS Instruments

In West Africa, the **Revised Treaty of the Economic Community of West African States** (1993) provides in Article 28 for cooperation in energy, including the development of energy resources and the establishment of appropriate mechanisms for the collective resolution of the energy development problems of the Community. The **ECOWAS Energy Protocol** (A/P4/1/03), adopted in 2003 and modelled substantially on the Energy Charter Treaty, provides for the promotion and protection of investment in energy, freedom of energy transit, and access to energy infrastructure, and contains a dispute settlement provision that includes investor–state arbitration. The Protocol's practical application has been limited, but it represents an important regional legal framework for energy investment and trade. ECOWAS has also established a common external tariff (in force since 2015) and the ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme, which applies to trade in energy goods among member states.

The **Treaty on the West African Gas Pipeline Project** (2003), concluded among Benin, Ghana, Nigeria, and Togo, established the legal framework for the West African Gas Pipeline, which transports Nigerian gas to the three other states. The Treaty and the associated International Project Agreement established the West African Gas Pipeline Authority as the regulator of the pipeline, and provide for the harmonisation of the legal and fiscal frameworks applicable to it, transit, and dispute settlement. The pipeline has been affected by supply interruptions and payment disputes, which illustrate the challenges of regional energy infrastructure examined in Chapter 6. Proposals for the Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline, which would traverse the territories of numerous West African states, would require an extensive intergovernmental legal framework.

### Other Regional Arrangements

In Southern Africa, the **SADC Protocol on Energy** (1996) provides for cooperation in the development of energy and energy pooling, and established the framework for the Southern African Power Pool. In South-East Asia, the **ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation** supports the ASEAN Power Grid and the Trans-ASEAN Gas Pipeline. In South America, regional integration of energy infrastructure has been pursued through bilateral treaties, such as those governing the Itaipu hydroelectric project between Brazil and Paraguay. These arrangements demonstrate a range of approaches to regional energy integration, from binding market integration (the Energy Community) to project-specific treaties (the West African Gas Pipeline) and cooperation frameworks (SADC and ASEAN).

## 13.7 Carbon Border Measures and Trade Compatibility

### The Problem of Carbon Leakage

Where states adopt climate policies of differing stringency, carbon-intensive industries in states with stringent policies may face higher costs than competitors in states with less stringent policies, with two potential consequences: a loss of competitiveness for domestic industries, and **carbon leakage** — the relocation of production, investment, or emissions to states with less stringent policies, undermining the environmental effectiveness of climate policy. Governments have addressed leakage risk principally through the free allocation of emission allowances to industries at risk, as under the EU Emissions Trading System, but free allocation weakens the incentive to reduce emissions and reduces revenues. **Carbon border adjustments** — measures that impose a carbon price on imports equivalent to that borne by domestic producers, and possibly rebate carbon costs on exports — offer an alternative approach.

### The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

The **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** (CBAM), established by Regulation (EU) 2023/956, is the first comprehensive carbon border adjustment in force. It applies to imports into the European Union of goods in sectors at high risk of carbon leakage: cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen, together with certain precursors and downstream products. During a **transitional period** from 1 October 2023 to 31 December 2025, importers were required to report the embedded emissions of imported goods, without financial obligations. From the beginning of the **definitive period** in 2026, importers must be authorised as CBAM declarants and must purchase and surrender CBAM certificates corresponding to the embedded emissions of the goods they import, with the price of certificates linked to the price of allowances under the EU ETS. Importers may deduct the carbon price effectively paid in the country of production, and the number of certificates to be surrendered is adjusted to reflect the free allocation still received by EU producers of the same goods. The free allocation for the CBAM sectors is to be phased out progressively between 2026 and 2034, as CBAM is phased in. Embedded emissions are to be calculated on the basis of actual emissions data, verified by accredited verifiers, or, where adequate data is unavailable, default values. Amendments adopted in 2025 as part of a simplification package introduced a de minimis threshold based on the mass of imported goods, exempting importers of small quantities, and deferred the sale of CBAM certificates.

### Compatibility with WTO Law

The compatibility of CBAM with WTO law has been extensively debated. The analysis depends on how the measure is characterised.

**Border tax adjustment or regulation.** If CBAM is characterised as a border adjustment of an internal tax, it may be permitted under Article II:2(a) of the GATT, which permits members to impose on imports a charge equivalent to an internal tax imposed in respect of the like domestic product or an article from which the imported product has been manufactured, subject to the national treatment obligation of Article III:2. Whether the EU ETS, a cap-and-trade system, constitutes an "internal tax" for this purpose is uncertain. If CBAM is characterised instead as an internal regulation, it would be assessed under Article III:4. Either way, the national treatment analysis turns on whether imported goods are treated less favourably than like domestic goods. The EU designed CBAM to mirror the ETS, so that importers bear a carbon cost equivalent to that borne by EU producers, net of free allocation. Critics argue that differences in the methodologies for calculating embedded emissions, the use of default values, and the administrative burdens imposed on importers may result in less favourable treatment.

**Most-favoured-nation treatment.** The deduction of carbon prices paid in the country of origin results in different treatment of imports according to the climate policies of exporting states, which may raise issues under Article I:1. The EU argues that the measure treats all imports according to the same objective criterion — embedded emissions net of carbon prices paid — and that differences in treatment reflect differences in products' carbon intensity and carbon costs rather than their origin.

**Article XX.** If CBAM is found inconsistent with Articles I, II, or III, the EU would seek to justify it under Article XX(g), as a measure relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources (the global atmosphere and climate system) made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption (the EU ETS), or Article XX(b), as necessary to protect human, animal, or plant life or health. The provisional justification of a well-designed carbon border adjustment under Article XX(g) appears plausible in the light of *US — Gasoline* and *US — Shrimp*. The more difficult question is the chapeau, which requires that the measure not be applied in a manner constituting arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination between countries where the same conditions prevail. Following *US — Shrimp*, a measure that conditions market access on exporting countries' policies must allow sufficient flexibility to take account of different conditions in different countries, and should be preceded by serious good faith efforts to negotiate. Questions arise as to whether CBAM adequately accounts for the different circumstances of developing countries, whether it recognises climate policies other than explicit carbon pricing, and whether the EU engaged in sufficient negotiation.

In 2025, Russia requested consultations with the European Union under the DSU concerning CBAM, initiating the first WTO dispute concerning the measure. Other states, including India, China, and South Africa, have expressed strong objections to CBAM, characterising it as a unilateral and discriminatory measure.

### Compatibility with the Climate Regime

CBAM also raises questions under the international climate regime. Article 3(5) of the UNFCCC provides that measures taken to combat climate change, "including unilateral ones", should not constitute a means of arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination or a disguised restriction on international trade — language that mirrors the chapeau of Article XX. The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, embodied in the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement and examined in Chapter 18, has been invoked by developing states to argue that CBAM imposes the EU's climate policy on states that bear lesser responsibility for climate change and have fewer resources to decarbonise. The Paris Agreement's bottom-up architecture, under which each party determines its own contribution, is said to be in tension with a measure that effectively requires exporters to meet the carbon price of the importing state. The EU has responded that CBAM applies to embedded emissions irrespective of the policies of the exporting state, that it is necessary to maintain the integrity of its own climate policy, and that it has committed to support for decarbonisation in developing countries. CBAM revenues have not, however, been earmarked for climate finance for developing countries, a point of criticism.

### Impact on Developing and African Countries

The economic impact of CBAM on developing countries depends on their exports of covered goods to the European Union and the carbon intensity of their production. Several African countries have significant exposure, including Mozambique (aluminium), South Africa (iron, steel, and aluminium), Egypt, Algeria, and Morocco (fertilisers and steel), and Zimbabwe (iron and steel). Studies have estimated that CBAM could reduce exports and GDP in affected African economies, while also creating incentives for investment in low-carbon production, including through Africa's renewable energy potential. Nigeria's exports of covered goods to the European Union are relatively limited, but CBAM may affect its fertiliser exports and its potential exports of hydrogen and ammonia, examined in Chapter 12. The application of CBAM to electricity imports affects states connected to the EU grid, including the Western Balkans, Ukraine, and potentially North African states planning to export renewable electricity to Europe by submarine cable.

### Other Carbon Border Measures and Climate Clubs

The United Kingdom has legislated for its own CBAM, to take effect from January 2027, covering a similar range of sectors. Carbon border adjustments have been proposed in the United States, Canada, and elsewhere, but not adopted. The European Union and the United States negotiated from 2021 on a Global Arrangement on Sustainable Steel and Aluminium, intended to address both carbon intensity and non-market excess capacity, without agreement. The **Climate Club**, launched by the G7 in 2022, provides a forum for cooperation on industrial decarbonisation, including the development of common methodologies for measuring the carbon intensity of products, which might reduce the fragmentation of carbon border measures. The relationship between carbon border measures and the multilateral trading system has been discussed in the WTO Committee on Trade and Environment and in the Trade and Environmental Sustainability Structured Discussions, but no multilateral framework has been agreed.
