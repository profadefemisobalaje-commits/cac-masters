## 30.2 Decarbonisation and the Transformation of Resource Law

### From Scarcity to Stranding

For most of the twentieth century, the law of natural resources was shaped by a paradigm of **scarcity**: the assumption that resources were finite and valuable, that demand would grow, and that the central legal questions concerned the allocation of rights to exploit them, the division of rents between states and investors, and the protection of investments. Decarbonisation is challenging this paradigm for fossil fuels. If the world is to meet the temperature goals of the Paris Agreement, a large share of known fossil fuel reserves must remain unburned — studies have estimated that, for a fifty per cent chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C, nearly sixty per cent of oil and fossil methane gas and ninety per cent of coal reserves would need to remain unextracted by 2050 — and the IEA's Net Zero Emissions scenario envisaged no new long-lead-time oil and gas fields approved for development beyond those already committed as of 2021. Whether or not these pathways are followed, the prospect of declining demand transforms the legal questions surrounding fossil fuel resources: from how to allocate scarce resources to how to manage **stranded assets**, decline, and exit.

### The Law of Managed Decline

A body of law is emerging to govern the **managed decline** of fossil fuel production. Its elements include: **restrictions on new exploration and production**, such as the bans on new oil and gas licensing adopted by Denmark (which set an end date of 2050 for production), France, Ireland, Costa Rica, and New Zealand (which subsequently reversed its ban on new offshore exploration), and the United Kingdom's policy, examined in Chapter 24, of not issuing new licences to explore new fields; **coal phase-out laws**, such as Germany's Coal Phase-out Act 2020, which sets an end date of 2038 at the latest and provides compensation to operators, and the Netherlands' ban on coal-fired electricity from 2030; **phase-out of fossil fuel subsidies**, a long-standing G20 commitment rarely fulfilled; **decommissioning** obligations and financial security, examined in Chapter 7, whose importance grows as fields reach the end of their lives; **just transition** frameworks for affected workers and regions, examined in Chapter 23; and the treatment of **compensation** for early closure, which raises questions under investment law, as the RWE and Uniper claims against the Netherlands and the Westmoreland claim against Canada's coal phase-out, examined in Chapter 26, illustrate.

### Producer Economies and the Transition

The managed decline of fossil fuels poses profound challenges for **producer economies**, particularly developing countries such as Nigeria, Angola, Mozambique, Senegal, and Uganda, which depend on, or hope to benefit from, fossil fuel revenues. These states argue that they bear little historical responsibility for climate change, that their populations lack access to modern energy, and that they should be permitted to develop their resources, particularly gas, as a **transition fuel** — a position reflected in the African Union's common position on energy access and just transition (2022) and in Nigeria's Energy Transition Plan, which envisages gas as a bridge to net zero by 2060. Others argue that new fossil fuel investments risk becoming stranded assets, locking producer states into dependence on declining revenues, and that the principle of **common but differentiated responsibilities** requires that wealthy producers phase out first and finance the transition of developing producers. The legal questions include how international law should allocate the remaining carbon budget among producers, whether there is an emerging obligation to limit production — a question to which the ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion has given new salience — and how transition finance can be mobilised to support economic diversification. Proposals for an equitable **production phase-out**, in which wealthy, diversified producers phase out first, have been advanced by scholars and civil society, but have not been accepted by major producers.

### The Rise of Transition Minerals

While the transition threatens fossil fuel resources with stranding, it creates soaring demand for **transition minerals** — lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, copper, manganese, and rare earth elements — used in batteries, electric vehicles, wind turbines, solar panels, and electricity networks, as examined in Chapter 8. The IEA has projected that demand for some of these minerals could increase several-fold by 2040 in scenarios consistent with climate goals. The law of transition minerals is being shaped by the same questions that once shaped the law of oil — sovereignty, rent capture, value addition, investment protection, and the rights of affected communities — but in a new context characterised by heightened geopolitical competition, stronger human rights and environmental norms, and the assertiveness of producer states seeking to avoid the mistakes of the past. The **export bans** of Indonesia and Zimbabwe, the **strategic minerals** regimes of the DRC and Chile, the **local processing** requirements of several African states, and the **critical minerals** strategies of consumer states, examined in Chapters 8, 24, and 25, are elements of this emerging law.

A central question is whether the rush for transition minerals will repeat the patterns of the resource curse — enclave development, environmental devastation, human rights abuses, and the capture of rents by elites and foreign investors — or whether it can be governed to promote broad-based development. The UN Secretary-General's Panel on Critical Energy Transition Minerals, the OECD due diligence guidance, the EU's Battery Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), with its due diligence and battery passport requirements, the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (whose obligations were scaled back and postponed by the "Omnibus" simplification package agreed in 2025), and the **Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance** standard are among the instruments that seek to embed responsible practices. The legal frameworks of producer states — their mining codes, fiscal regimes, community benefit-sharing, and environmental regulation — will be decisive.

### Deep Seabed Minerals

The **deep seabed** has become a new frontier of transition minerals. Polymetallic nodules on the abyssal plains of the Pacific Ocean contain nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese, and the **International Seabed Authority**, examined in Chapter 20, has been negotiating a **Mining Code** of exploitation regulations since 2014. The invocation by Nauru in 2021 of the "two-year rule" in the 1994 Implementation Agreement, which was intended to require the Authority to complete the regulations within two years, did not result in their adoption, and a growing number of states have called for a **moratorium or precautionary pause** on deep seabed mining pending better scientific understanding of its environmental effects. In 2025, the United States, which is not a party to UNCLOS, issued an executive order seeking to accelerate deep seabed mining under its **Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act 1980**, including in areas beyond national jurisdiction, and a company sought a commercial recovery permit from the US authorities — prompting objections from the ISA and many states that such unilateral action would violate the principle that the resources of the Area are the **common heritage of mankind**. The outcome of this contest will test the resilience of the UNCLOS regime and shape the law of resources beyond national jurisdiction.

### New Resources: Hydrogen, Carbon Storage, and Beyond

Decarbonisation is also creating **new categories of resources** requiring legal frameworks: **geological storage space** for captured carbon dioxide and hydrogen, which raises questions of ownership of pore space, long-term liability, and cross-border transport, examined in Chapter 12; **natural (geological) hydrogen**, whose discovery in several countries has prompted questions about whether it falls within existing mining or petroleum legislation; **offshore wind** sites, allocated through seabed leasing rounds; **geothermal** resources; and the **radio spectrum** and **data** on which digital energy systems depend. Each requires the law to define property rights, allocate access, capture public value, and manage environmental risks — the classic tasks of resource law, applied to novel resources.

## 30.3 Artificial Intelligence and Automated Energy Regulation

### AI in Energy Systems

**Artificial intelligence** is transforming energy systems. Machine learning algorithms are used to forecast renewable generation and demand, optimise the dispatch of generation and storage, detect faults and predict maintenance needs in grids and power plants, manage distributed energy resources and electric vehicle charging, trade electricity in wholesale markets, explore for oil, gas, and minerals by analysing seismic and geological data, and detect methane leaks and illegal activities through satellite imagery. At the same time, AI is becoming a major source of **energy demand**: the rapid expansion of data centres to train and run AI models has increased electricity demand in several countries, prompting concerns about grid capacity, emissions, and water consumption, and leading technology companies to contract for new nuclear, gas, and renewable capacity. The IEA has estimated that electricity consumption by data centres could roughly double by 2030, with AI the most important driver.

### Legal Questions Raised by AI in Energy

The use of AI in energy systems raises several legal questions. **Liability**: who is liable when an AI system causes harm — for example, a blackout caused by an erroneous algorithmic dispatch decision, or a trading loss caused by an autonomous trading algorithm? Existing liability rules, based on fault and causation, may be difficult to apply to opaque, adaptive systems; the European Union's revised **Product Liability Directive** (Directive (EU) 2024/2853) extends product liability to software, including AI systems. **Market conduct**: algorithmic trading in energy markets raises risks of market manipulation and **tacit algorithmic collusion**, in which pricing algorithms learn to coordinate on supra-competitive prices without explicit agreement, challenging competition law's reliance on proof of agreement. The EU's **REMIT** regime, revised in 2024, and FERC's anti-manipulation rules apply to algorithmic trading, but their enforcement against AI-driven strategies is untested. **Safety and reliability**: AI systems controlling critical infrastructure must be safe, robust, and secure against cyber attack, raising questions of certification and standards. **Transparency and accountability**: the opacity of complex AI models ("black boxes") may make it difficult for regulators and affected persons to understand and challenge decisions. **Discrimination and fairness**: AI systems used in customer management — for example, to assess creditworthiness, target disconnections, or set personalised tariffs — may produce discriminatory outcomes.

### Regulating AI

The European Union's **Artificial Intelligence Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), the first comprehensive legal framework for AI, adopts a risk-based approach, imposing strict requirements on **high-risk** AI systems, which include AI systems intended to be used as safety components in the management and operation of critical infrastructure, including the supply of electricity, gas, and heating. Providers of such systems must implement risk management systems, ensure data quality, maintain technical documentation and logs, ensure transparency and human oversight, and meet standards of accuracy, robustness, and cyber security, with obligations phased in from 2025 to 2027. Other jurisdictions have adopted different approaches: the United States has relied principally on sectoral regulation and executive policy, which shifted in 2025 towards promoting AI development and infrastructure, including the acceleration of energy supply for data centres; China has adopted regulations on algorithmic recommendations, deep synthesis, and generative AI; and the **Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law** (2024) is the first international treaty on AI. In Africa, the African Union adopted a **Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy** in 2024, and Nigeria has developed a National AI Strategy.

### AI in Regulation: "RegTech" and Automated Regulation

AI is also being used **by regulators**: to monitor compliance through the analysis of large datasets, satellite imagery, and smart meter data; to detect market manipulation; to process licence applications; and to draft and analyse regulations. Such "**RegTech**" and "**SupTech**" applications can enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of regulation, particularly for under-resourced regulators. They also raise concerns about **automated decision-making** in public administration: the requirements of administrative law — that decisions be made by the authorised decision-maker, on relevant considerations, with reasons, and subject to review — may be difficult to satisfy where decisions are made or heavily influenced by opaque algorithms. Data protection law, including Article 22 of the EU's General Data Protection Regulation, which gives individuals a right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, and Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023, which contains a similar provision, impose limits. The idea of "**regulation by code**", in which compliance is built into the technical architecture of energy systems — for example, through smart contracts that automatically settle peer-to-peer energy trades in accordance with market rules — raises deeper questions about the relationship between law and technology, which Lawrence Lessig anticipated in his observation that "code is law".
