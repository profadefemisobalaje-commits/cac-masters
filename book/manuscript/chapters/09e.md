## 9.8 Market Abuse, Competition, and Regulatory Enforcement

### Vulnerability of Electricity Markets to Abuse

Electricity markets are unusually vulnerable to the exercise of market power and to manipulation. Because electricity cannot be stored economically at scale and demand is inelastic in the short term, a generator that withholds capacity at times of tight supply can raise prices dramatically; because the marginal generator sets the price for all, even a small generator may have significant market power at peak times; and because markets are governed by complex rules, traders may exploit flaws in market design to profit at the expense of other participants and consumers. The California electricity crisis of 2000–2001, examined in the casebox below, demonstrated the scale of harm that market abuse can inflict, and led to the development of specialised regimes for the prevention and sanctioning of market manipulation in energy markets.

### Market Manipulation and Insider Trading: REMIT and United States Law

In the European Union, the **Regulation on Wholesale Energy Market Integrity and Transparency** (REMIT) — Regulation (EU) No 1227/2011, amended by Regulation (EU) 2024/1106 — prohibits insider trading and market manipulation in wholesale energy markets, requires market participants to publish inside information in a timely manner, and requires the reporting of transactions and orders to ACER. Market manipulation includes transactions or orders that give false or misleading signals as to supply, demand, or price, that secure the price at an artificial level, or that employ fictitious devices or any other form of deception. ACER monitors trading activity, and national regulatory authorities investigate and enforce the prohibitions, imposing penalties under national law. The 2024 amendments strengthened ACER's investigatory powers in cross-border cases, extended REMIT to algorithmic trading, and harmonised penalties. Enforcement decisions have included substantial fines for wash trades, layering, and the withholding of capacity, such as the fine imposed by the French Energy Regulatory Commission's dispute settlement and sanctions committee (CoRDiS) on VITOL in 2018 for manipulation of gas markets, and decisions by Ofgem against generators for charging excessive prices in the balancing mechanism.

In the United States, the **Energy Policy Act of 2005** amended the Federal Power Act and the Natural Gas Act to prohibit the use of any manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance in connection with the purchase or sale of electricity or natural gas subject to FERC's jurisdiction, and gave FERC authority to impose civil penalties of up to USD 1 million per day per violation (adjusted for inflation). FERC's Anti-Manipulation Rule, modelled on Rule 10b-5 under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, has been the basis of numerous enforcement actions. In *FERC v Barclays Bank plc*, FERC assessed a civil penalty of USD 435 million against Barclays and four of its traders for manipulating electricity prices in western United States markets between 2006 and 2008 by trading physical products to benefit financial swap positions; the matter was settled in 2017 for USD 105 million. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has parallel jurisdiction over manipulation in energy futures and swaps.

### Competition Law in Electricity Markets

General competition law applies to electricity markets alongside sector-specific regulation. In the European Union, Article 102 TFEU, which prohibits the abuse of a dominant position, has been applied to incumbent electricity companies in a series of cases. In 2008, the European Commission accepted commitments from E.ON to divest generation capacity and its extra-high-voltage transmission network in Germany, following an investigation into suspected strategic withholding of generation capacity to raise prices and discrimination in the procurement of balancing services. In 2010, the Commission accepted commitments from EDF to release capacity tied up in long-term supply contracts with large industrial customers in France, which allegedly foreclosed the market to competing suppliers. In *DEI v Commission*, concerning the Greek incumbent's exclusive access to lignite deposits, the Commission found that Greece had infringed Article 106 in conjunction with Article 102 by granting the state-owned Public Power Corporation privileged access to lignite, and the Court of Justice in 2014 upheld the Commission's approach, holding that it was not necessary to prove actual abuse where state measures created a situation of inequality of opportunity between economic operators.[^dei] The **State aid rules** of Articles 107 and 108 TFEU are also of central importance, governing the compatibility of renewable support schemes, capacity mechanisms, and emergency interventions, as examined in Chapter 10.

[^dei]: *European Commission v DEI*, Case C-553/12 P, EU:C:2014:2083.

In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority's **Energy Market Investigation** (2014–2016) found that a combination of features of the retail market, including weak customer engagement, gave suppliers a position of unilateral market power over their inactive customers, resulting in detriment of around £1.4 billion per year, and recommended remedies including a temporary price cap for customers on prepayment meters. The subsequent legislative price cap for default tariffs, described in section 9.7, went further than the CMA's recommendations.

In Nigeria, the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018 established the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) with powers over anti-competitive conduct and consumer protection across the economy, while the Electricity Act 2023 confers on NERC responsibility for promoting competition and preventing abuse of market power in the electricity industry. The coordination of sector regulators and competition authorities, examined in Chapter 4, is a recurrent challenge.

### Regulatory Enforcement Powers

Effective regulation depends on effective enforcement. Electricity regulators typically possess powers to require information, to investigate suspected breaches of licence conditions, codes, and legislation, to issue compliance orders, to impose financial penalties, to require the payment of compensation to consumers, and to modify, suspend, or revoke licences. In Great Britain, Ofgem may impose penalties of up to ten per cent of a licensee's turnover for breaches of licence conditions or relevant requirements, and has frequently agreed settlements under which licensees make payments to consumer redress funds. In Nigeria, the Electricity Act 2023 empowers NERC to impose fines and sanctions on licensees for breaches of the Act, regulations, codes, and licence conditions, and NERC has periodically imposed fines on DisCos for violations such as failure to meet metering obligations, overbilling, and failure to deliver required hours of supply.

The exercise of enforcement powers is subject to administrative law principles of legality, procedural fairness, rationality, and proportionality, and enforcement decisions are subject to appeal or judicial review. The design of enforcement — the balance between deterrence and compliance-oriented approaches, the use of settlements and consumer redress, and the transparency of enforcement decisions — has been the subject of extensive regulatory scholarship, including the theory of "responsive regulation" developed by Ian Ayres and John Braithwaite, which advocates an "enforcement pyramid" in which regulators escalate from persuasion to increasingly severe sanctions in response to non-compliance. Regulatory enforcement and judicial review are examined further in Chapter 27.

::: casebox
**The California Electricity Crisis (2000–2001) and the Law of Market Manipulation**

*Background.* California's restructuring legislation of 1996 (Assembly Bill 1890) required the state's investor-owned utilities to divest much of their generation and to buy electricity in a spot market operated by the California Power Exchange, while capping retail prices for a transitional period and discouraging long-term contracts. In 2000, a combination of rising demand, drought reducing hydroelectric imports, rising gas prices, and capacity withdrawals caused wholesale prices to rise to many times their historical levels, while retail rates remained frozen.

*Manipulation.* Investigations by FERC and others subsequently revealed that traders, notably at Enron, had exploited flaws in market rules through strategies given names such as "Death Star", "Get Shorty", and "Ricochet", which involved scheduling fictitious congestion to collect congestion relief payments, selling ancillary services they did not have, and moving electricity out of and back into California to evade price caps. Generators were also found to have withheld capacity to raise prices.

*Consequences.* Pacific Gas and Electric filed for bankruptcy in April 2001, Southern California Edison came close to insolvency, and the state government purchased power on behalf of the utilities at very high prices under long-term contracts. Rolling blackouts affected millions of customers. FERC ordered refunds, and extensive litigation followed. Enron collapsed in December 2001 amid accounting fraud, and several of its executives and traders were convicted of criminal offences.

*Legal significance.* The crisis led directly to the anti-manipulation provisions of the Energy Policy Act of 2005, to reforms of market design in California and elsewhere, including the introduction of locational marginal pricing and market power mitigation measures, and to a lasting scepticism about retail price caps combined with spot market exposure. Its lessons informed the design of REMIT in the European Union. It remains the paradigmatic illustration of the importance of market design and enforcement in electricity markets.
:::

::: summary
- The physical characteristics of electricity — the need for continuous balancing, flows governed by physics rather than contract, inelastic demand, and natural monopoly networks — shape its legal structure.
- Electricity industries range from vertically integrated monopolies through single-buyer and wholesale competition models to full retail competition; Nigeria's industry has moved from the NEPA monopoly through privatisation to constitutional and statutory decentralisation under the Electricity Act 2023.
- Regulation of generation, transmission, and distribution involves licensing, unbundling, tariff regulation, planning, system operation, and reliability standards, with significant federal–state allocation issues in the United States and now Nigeria.
- Liberalisation has produced diverse market designs; the EU's 2024 reform retains marginal pricing while promoting long-term contracts and two-way contracts for difference.
- Grid access law governs third-party access, connection procedures, cost allocation, priority dispatch, and access for distributed resources; connection queues have become a major bottleneck for the energy transition.
- Power purchase agreements allocate risk through capacity payments, currency indexation, change in law, force majeure, and termination provisions, supported by sovereign and multilateral credit enhancement; their renegotiation under economic pressure is a recurrent source of disputes.
- Cross-border trade is governed by the EU internal market, the WAPP and ERERA in West Africa, other regional power pools, and the emerging African Single Electricity Market.
- Consumer protection addresses billing, metering, switching, price regulation, disconnection, and energy poverty, with constitutional litigation emerging as a means of enforcement.
- Market abuse is addressed through REMIT, United States anti-manipulation law, competition law, and regulatory enforcement powers.
:::
