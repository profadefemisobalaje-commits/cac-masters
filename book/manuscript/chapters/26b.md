## 26.3 Investment Treaty Arbitration

### Scope of this Section

Chapter 14 examined the substantive standards of investment protection and gave an institutional overview of investor–state dispute settlement (ISDS), including the concept of "arbitration without privity", the available forums, state counterclaims, transparency, and the reform debate. This section addresses investment treaty arbitration from the procedural perspective of the parties and counsel, following the life cycle of a typical energy investment arbitration, shown in Figure 26.1, and highlighting the strategic decisions that arise at each stage.

### Pre-Arbitral Stage: Notice and Cooling-Off

Most investment treaties require the investor to notify the host state of the dispute and to attempt to settle it amicably for a specified **cooling-off period** — typically three to six months — before commencing arbitration. Article 26 of the **Energy Charter Treaty**, for example, permits the investor to submit the dispute to arbitration if it has not been settled amicably within three months of a request for amicable settlement. Some treaties also require the investor to pursue local remedies for a specified period, typically eighteen months, before resorting to arbitration. Tribunals have differed on the consequences of non-compliance with cooling-off periods: some have treated them as jurisdictional requirements whose breach precludes the claim (as in *Burlington Resources v Ecuador*, 2010, in relation to the specific claim not notified), while others have regarded them as procedural requirements whose non-observance may be excused where negotiation would be futile (as in *SGS v Pakistan*, 2003). The prudent course is to comply strictly with the notice requirements and to document the attempts at settlement, which may also lead to a negotiated outcome, examined in section 26.8.

The pre-arbitral stage also involves strategic decisions on **structuring and standing**: whether the claimant is a protected investor under the treaty, examined in section 26.5; which treaty and forum to invoke where several are available; whether to bring contract claims, treaty claims, or both; whether to seek **third-party funding**; and how to preserve evidence and manage the continuing relationship with the host state. Investors must also consider the effect of **fork-in-the-road** clauses, under which the choice of domestic courts may preclude subsequent treaty arbitration, and **waiver** requirements, such as Article 1121 of the former NAFTA and its successors, which require the investor to waive the right to pursue domestic proceedings concerning the same measure.

### Commencement and Constitution of the Tribunal

Arbitration is commenced by a **request for arbitration** under Article 36 of the ICSID Convention, which the Secretary-General of ICSID registers unless the dispute is manifestly outside the jurisdiction of the Centre, or by a **notice of arbitration** under Article 3 of the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules. Tribunals usually comprise three arbitrators, one appointed by each party and the president by agreement or by an appointing authority — the Chair of the ICSID Administrative Council in ICSID cases, or the Secretary-General of the PCA or an authority designated by him in UNCITRAL cases.

The **independence and impartiality** of arbitrators are fundamental. Arbitrators must disclose circumstances that may give rise to justifiable doubts about their independence or impartiality, and parties may challenge arbitrators on such grounds. The **IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration** (2014, revised 2024) classify circumstances into red, orange, and green lists according to their seriousness, and are widely used as a reference. Concerns about **repeat appointments** of the same arbitrators by the same parties or law firms, and about **"double-hatting"** — the practice of individuals acting as arbitrators in some cases and as counsel in others raising similar issues — have prompted the development of the **Code of Conduct for Arbitrators in International Investment Dispute Resolution**, adopted by UNCITRAL in 2023, which limits double-hatting and strengthens disclosure obligations. Under the ICSID Convention, a challenge must show a "manifest lack" of the qualities required by Article 14(1), which has been interpreted as requiring the lack to be evident or obvious, and is decided by the unchallenged arbitrators or, if they are equally divided or the challenge concerns a majority, by the Chair of the Administrative Council.

### The First Session and Procedural Framework

Following its constitution, the tribunal holds a **first session** or case management conference, typically by video conference, to establish the procedural framework, recorded in **Procedural Order No. 1**. The order addresses the applicable rules, the language and place of proceedings, the timetable for written submissions, the rules on document production and evidence (usually by reference to the **IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence in International Arbitration**, as guidelines), confidentiality and transparency, the organisation of hearings, and the allocation of costs. A key early decision is whether to **bifurcate** the proceedings — addressing jurisdiction, or liability, separately from quantum — or to hear all issues together. Bifurcation may save costs if a preliminary issue disposes of the case, but may lengthen the proceedings if it does not; under Rule 44 of the 2022 ICSID Arbitration Rules, the tribunal considers all relevant circumstances, including whether bifurcation would materially reduce time and cost and whether the issues are so intertwined as to make bifurcation impractical.

States may also file **preliminary objections** that a claim is manifestly without legal merit, under Rule 41 of the 2022 ICSID Arbitration Rules (formerly Rule 41(5)), which must be decided expeditiously, and many modern treaties provide for similar expedited objections. **Security for costs** — an order requiring the claimant to provide security for the respondent's potential costs — has become an important issue in cases funded by third parties, where an impecunious claimant may be unable to pay an adverse costs award; Rule 53 of the 2022 ICSID Rules expressly empowers tribunals to order security for costs, taking into account the party's ability to comply with an adverse costs decision, the existence of third-party funding, and other relevant circumstances.

### Provisional Measures

Tribunals may recommend or order **provisional measures** to preserve the rights of the parties pending the award. Under Article 47 of the ICSID Convention, a tribunal may "recommend" provisional measures, but tribunals have consistently held that such recommendations are binding. Provisional measures in energy disputes have included orders to preserve the status quo, to refrain from enforcing tax assessments or criminal proceedings that would aggravate the dispute, and to preserve evidence. In ***Perenco Ecuador v Ecuador*** (2009), the tribunal recommended that Ecuador refrain from enforcing a windfall levy against the investor's oil assets pending the arbitration; Ecuador's subsequent seizure of the investor's oil production in disregard of the recommendation was later taken into account in the tribunal's assessment. In ***Burlington v Ecuador***, the tribunal similarly recommended that Ecuador refrain from seizing the investor's oil. Tribunals have also considered requests to suspend domestic criminal proceedings against investors' personnel, granting such measures only in exceptional circumstances, out of respect for the state's sovereign prerogative to prosecute crimes.

### The Written and Oral Phases

The written phase typically involves two rounds of submissions — the claimant's **memorial** and the respondent's **counter-memorial**, followed by a **reply** and a **rejoinder** — each accompanied by witness statements, expert reports, legal authorities, and documentary exhibits. Between the rounds, the parties exchange requests for the production of documents, usually in the form of a **Redfern schedule** setting out the documents requested, the justification for each request, the objections, and the tribunal's decision. The oral **hearing**, which in an energy arbitration may last two or three weeks, involves opening statements, the examination and cross-examination of fact and expert witnesses, and closing statements, followed by post-hearing briefs and submissions on costs. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, **virtual and hybrid hearings** have become common, and tribunals have generally rejected objections that virtual hearings violate the right to be heard.

### Non-Disputing Parties and Transparency

Investment treaty arbitration differs from commercial arbitration in its increasing **transparency**. The **UNCITRAL Rules on Transparency in Treaty-based Investor-State Arbitration** (2014), which apply to UNCITRAL arbitrations under treaties concluded after April 2014 and to earlier treaties under the **Mauritius Convention on Transparency** (2014), require the publication of key documents and awards, open hearings, and the possibility of submissions by non-disputing parties. The 2022 ICSID Rules provide for the publication of awards (with the parties' consent deemed given unless they object within a specified period), the participation of **non-disputing parties** such as NGOs and community representatives through *amicus curiae* submissions, and submissions by the non-disputing treaty party. In energy disputes with significant environmental or community dimensions, such as disputes involving mining projects in Latin America, *amicus* submissions by affected communities and NGOs have been admitted, though their influence on outcomes is debated.

### Third-Party Funding

**Third-party funding**, in which a funder with no other interest in the dispute finances the claimant's costs in return for a share of any recovery, has become common in investment arbitration, including in energy disputes, enabling claimants to pursue claims they could not otherwise afford and allowing companies to manage the financial risk of litigation. Funding raises concerns about conflicts of interest between funders and arbitrators, the encouragement of speculative claims, and the claimant's ability to pay an adverse costs award. Rule 14 of the 2022 ICSID Rules requires parties to disclose the name and address of any third-party funder, and many treaties and institutional rules now contain similar provisions; Nigeria's Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 also requires disclosure of funding arrangements. The P&ID case, examined in section 26.7, illustrates the role of funders in high-value disputes involving states.

## 26.4 ICSID and UNCITRAL Arbitration Frameworks

### The ICSID Convention

The **Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States** (ICSID Convention), concluded in 1965 under the auspices of the World Bank and in force since 1966, established the **International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes** as an autonomous international institution. It has more than 150 contracting states, including Nigeria, which signed in 1965 and ratified in the same year, and which gave the Convention force of law through the **International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (Enforcement of Awards) Act**. The Convention provides a self-contained, **delocalised** system of arbitration, insulated from the law and courts of any national seat: ICSID awards are subject only to the remedies provided in the Convention itself, and contracting states must recognise and enforce the pecuniary obligations imposed by awards as if they were final judgments of their own courts (Article 54). The Convention's procedural framework is supplemented by the **ICSID Arbitration Rules**, comprehensively revised in 2022 to improve efficiency and transparency, with timelines for procedural steps and awards, provisions on expedited arbitration, disclosure of third-party funding, and security for costs.

### Jurisdiction of the Centre

Under **Article 25(1)** of the ICSID Convention, the jurisdiction of the Centre extends to any **legal dispute arising directly out of an investment**, between a **contracting state** (or a constituent subdivision or agency designated to the Centre) and a **national of another contracting state**, which the parties have **consented in writing** to submit to the Centre. Each element has generated jurisprudence. The term "investment" is not defined in the Convention, and tribunals have debated whether it has an objective meaning; the **Salini test**, derived from *Salini Costruttori v Morocco* (2001), identifies the characteristics of an investment as a contribution, a certain duration, the assumption of risk, and, in some formulations, a contribution to the host state's development, while other tribunals have treated these as indicative features rather than jurisdictional requirements. Energy projects generally satisfy any formulation of the test, though questions have arisen concerning pre-investment expenditures, sale contracts, and financial instruments. The requirement that the investor be a **national of another contracting state** excludes nationals of the host state, subject to Article 25(2)(b), which allows a locally incorporated company under foreign control to be treated as a foreign national if the parties so agree — a provision of particular importance in the energy sector, where investments are often made through local subsidiaries, and which states may agree to in the investment contract. Consent may be given in an investment contract, in domestic investment legislation, or in a treaty; once given, it cannot be withdrawn unilaterally (Article 25(1)).

### Annulment

ICSID awards are not subject to appeal or to setting aside by national courts, but may be **annulled** by an *ad hoc* committee of three persons appointed by the Chair of the Administrative Council, on the exhaustive grounds in **Article 52(1)**: that the tribunal was not properly constituted; that it **manifestly exceeded its powers**; that there was corruption on the part of a member; that there has been a **serious departure from a fundamental rule of procedure**; or that the award has **failed to state the reasons** on which it is based. Annulment is not an appeal: a committee may not review the merits of the award or substitute its own view of the law or facts. Early annulment decisions, including *Klöckner v Cameroon* (1985) and *Amco v Indonesia* (1986), were criticised for intrusive review, but subsequent committees have generally adopted a more restrained approach. Failure to apply the applicable law may constitute a manifest excess of powers, but an error in its application does not. Notable energy-related annulment decisions include the partial annulment of the award in *Occidental v Ecuador* (2015), in which the committee reduced the damages of around USD 1.8 billion by around forty per cent on the ground that the tribunal had manifestly exceeded its powers by awarding compensation for a share of the investment that had been transferred to a third party in breach of Ecuadorian law, and the annulment of awards in several Spanish renewable energy cases, including *Eiser v Spain* (2020), on the ground that an arbitrator had failed to disclose a relationship with the claimant's damages experts, while most other Spanish awards survived annulment. Where an award is annulled, either party may resubmit the dispute to a new tribunal. Annulment proceedings may result in a **stay of enforcement** of the award, which the committee may condition on the provision of security.

### Recognition and Enforcement under ICSID

Article 54 requires all contracting states to recognise ICSID awards as binding and to enforce their pecuniary obligations as if they were final judgments of their courts, with no grounds for refusal comparable to those in Article V of the New York Convention. Article 53 provides that awards are binding and not subject to appeal or any other remedy except those provided for in the Convention. However, **Article 55** preserves the law of each contracting state on the immunity of states from execution, so that a successful investor may face obstacles in executing an award against state assets, examined in section 26.7. The European Commission's position that intra-EU awards are contrary to EU law, following *Achmea* (2018) and *Komstroy* (2021), examined in Chapter 14, has led to conflicts between the obligation to enforce ICSID awards and EU law, as courts in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia have enforced intra-EU awards against Spain while EU courts have refused to do so. In ***Spain v Infrastructure Services Luxembourg*** (2023), the High Court of Australia held that Spain's agreement to Article 54 amounted to a waiver of its immunity from recognition and enforcement proceedings, though not from execution, and in 2024 the English Court of Appeal reached a similar conclusion in *Infrastructure Services Luxembourg v Spain*, a question subsequently taken to the UK Supreme Court.

### The ICSID Additional Facility

The **ICSID Additional Facility Rules**, revised in 2022, provide for the administration by ICSID of proceedings that fall outside the scope of the Convention, including disputes where either the host state or the investor's home state is not a party to the Convention, and disputes that do not arise directly out of an investment. Additional Facility awards are not governed by the Convention's annulment and enforcement regime, but are subject to the law of the seat and to the New York Convention. The Additional Facility was used extensively in disputes against Mexico and Canada under NAFTA, since neither was a party to the ICSID Convention at the time.

### The UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules

The **UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules**, adopted in 1976 and revised in 2010, 2013 (to incorporate the transparency rules), and 2021 (adding expedited arbitration rules), provide a framework for ad hoc arbitration that may be used in both commercial and investor–state disputes. Many investment treaties, including the Energy Charter Treaty, give investors a choice between ICSID and UNCITRAL arbitration. UNCITRAL arbitrations are often administered by the PCA, which also acts as appointing authority. Unlike ICSID arbitrations, UNCITRAL arbitrations have a **legal seat**, whose law and courts govern the procedure and supervise the award; the award may be set aside by the courts of the seat and is enforced under the New York Convention. The choice of seat in investor–state UNCITRAL arbitration therefore has important consequences, as the *Yukos* litigation in the Dutch courts — in which the Hague District Court set aside the awards in 2016, the Hague Court of Appeal reinstated them in 2020, and, after the Dutch Supreme Court remitted the fraud allegation in 2021, the Amsterdam Court of Appeal rejected Russia's remaining arguments in 2024 — illustrates.

### Choosing Between ICSID and UNCITRAL

Investors and states choosing between ICSID and UNCITRAL consider several factors: the **delocalised** character of ICSID arbitration, which excludes review by national courts and provides for enforcement without the New York Convention defences, against the possibility of annulment by an ICSID committee; the **transparency** of ICSID proceedings and the publication of awards; the **institutional support** and fixed fee structure of ICSID; the **jurisdictional requirements** of Article 25, which do not apply in UNCITRAL arbitration; the **supervisory role of the seat** in UNCITRAL arbitration, which may be advantageous or disadvantageous depending on the seat; and the **enforcement** strategy, including the location of the state's assets and the attitude of the courts there to sovereign immunity.
