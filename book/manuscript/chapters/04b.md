## 4.4 The International Renewable Energy Agency

### Establishment and Legal Foundation

The International Renewable Energy Agency was established by the Statute of IRENA, opened for signature in Bonn on 26 January 2009 and entering into force on 8 July 2010. Its headquarters are in Masdar City, Abu Dhabi, in the United Arab Emirates — a symbolically significant choice, locating the principal international institution for renewable energy in a major oil-exporting state. Membership is open to all states members of the United Nations and to regional intergovernmental economic integration organisations; the European Union is a member. With approximately 170 members, IRENA has one of the broadest memberships of any energy institution.

Article II of the Statute defines IRENA's objective as promoting "the widespread and increased adoption and the sustainable use of all forms of renewable energy", taking into account national and domestic priorities and benefits derived from a combined approach of renewable energy and energy efficiency measures, and the contribution of renewable energy to environmental preservation, climate protection, economic growth and social cohesion, access to and security of energy supply, regional development, and intergenerational responsibility. Article III defines renewable energy to include bioenergy, geothermal energy, hydropower, ocean energy (including tidal, wave, and ocean thermal energy), solar energy, and wind energy.

### Functions and Activities

IRENA's functions, set out in Article IV, are advisory and facilitative. They include analysing and systematising renewable energy practices, policy instruments, investment mechanisms, and best practices; providing policy advice and assistance to members on request; promoting knowledge and technology transfer; providing capacity building and education; and stimulating research and networking. IRENA publishes influential reports, including the *World Energy Transitions Outlook*, annual *Renewable Capacity Statistics*, and *Renewable Power Generation Costs*, the last of which has documented the dramatic decline in the costs of solar photovoltaic and wind power over the past decade.

IRENA has no power to adopt binding decisions. Its influence derives from its analytical work, its convening role, and its support for member states in designing renewable energy policies. In the context of COP28 in 2023, IRENA played a central role in tracking progress towards the pledge, endorsed in the global stocktake decision, to triple global renewable energy capacity and double the global average annual rate of energy efficiency improvements by 2030.

### Assessment

IRENA represents a distinctive model of international energy governance: a universal-membership institution with a promotional rather than regulatory mandate. Its strengths lie in its legitimacy, its expertise, and its capacity to support developing states. Its limitations lie in the absence of binding powers and of mechanisms for addressing the legal barriers to renewable energy deployment — such as trade disputes over local content requirements and investment disputes over the withdrawal of incentives — which are governed by other regimes.

## 4.5 The International Atomic Energy Agency

### Establishment and Mandate

The International Atomic Energy Agency was established by its Statute, approved on 23 October 1956 and entering into force on 29 July 1957. Its creation followed President Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" address to the United Nations General Assembly in December 1953, which proposed an international agency to promote the peaceful uses of atomic energy while preventing the diversion of nuclear materials to military purposes. The IAEA is headquartered in Vienna and is an autonomous organisation within the United Nations system, reporting annually to the General Assembly and, where appropriate, to the Security Council.

Article II of the Statute defines the Agency's dual objective: to "seek to accelerate and enlarge the contribution of atomic energy to peace, health and prosperity throughout the world" and to "ensure, so far as it is able, that assistance provided by it or at its request or under its supervision or control is not used in such a way as to further any military purpose". This dual mandate — promotion and control — has shaped the Agency's work and generated tensions between its developmental and verification roles.

### Safeguards

The IAEA's most significant legal function is the application of safeguards to verify that nuclear material is not diverted from peaceful uses to nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices. Under Article III of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons 1968 (NPT), each non-nuclear-weapon state party undertakes to accept safeguards, as set forth in an agreement to be negotiated and concluded with the IAEA, for the exclusive purpose of verification of the fulfilment of its obligations under the Treaty. These comprehensive safeguards agreements, based on a model document known as INFCIRC/153 (Corrected), apply to all source or special fissionable material in all peaceful nuclear activities within the territory of the state.

Following the discovery in 1991 of Iraq's clandestine nuclear weapons programme, which comprehensive safeguards had failed to detect, the IAEA Board of Governors approved in 1997 the Model Additional Protocol (INFCIRC/540), which significantly expands the information that states must provide and the access that inspectors enjoy, including complementary access to sites. The Additional Protocol is voluntary but has been concluded by a large majority of states with comprehensive safeguards agreements.

Where the Agency finds that a state is in non-compliance with its safeguards agreement, the Board of Governors may report the non-compliance to all members and to the Security Council and General Assembly (Statute, Article XII.C). The Board found Iran in non-compliance in 2005, leading to referral to the Security Council and the adoption of sanctions resolutions, and later to the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action of 2015, under which the IAEA was entrusted with extensive monitoring and verification responsibilities. The Board again found Iran in breach of its safeguards obligations in June 2025.

### Safety Standards

Article III.A.6 of the Statute authorises the Agency "to establish or adopt ... standards of safety for protection of health and minimization of danger to life and property". The IAEA Safety Standards Series — comprising Safety Fundamentals, Safety Requirements, and Safety Guides — is not binding in itself, but the standards are binding on the Agency in relation to its own operations and on states in relation to operations assisted by the Agency, and they are widely incorporated into national regulation. The Convention on Nuclear Safety 1994 and the Joint Convention on the Safety of Spent Fuel Management and on the Safety of Radioactive Waste Management 1997, both adopted under IAEA auspices, establish binding obligations reinforced by peer review mechanisms. These instruments are examined in Chapter 11.

### Nuclear Newcomers and the Milestones Approach

A growing number of states without existing nuclear power programmes — including several in Africa — have expressed interest in developing nuclear energy. The IAEA supports such "newcomer" states through its Milestones Approach, which identifies nineteen infrastructure issues (including the national position, nuclear safety, management, funding and financing, the legal framework, safeguards, the regulatory framework, radiation protection, the electrical grid, human resource development, stakeholder involvement, site and supporting facilities, environmental protection, emergency planning, nuclear security, the nuclear fuel cycle, radioactive waste management, industrial involvement, and procurement) that must be addressed across three phases of programme development, each concluding with a milestone. The Agency conducts Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review (INIR) missions to assess progress.

The legal framework is an explicit component of the Milestones Approach. A newcomer state is expected to adhere to the principal international nuclear instruments and to enact comprehensive national nuclear legislation establishing an independent regulatory body, licensing requirements, nuclear liability rules, and provisions on safeguards, security, and waste management. In Nigeria, the Nuclear Safety and Radiation Protection Act 1995 established the Nigerian Nuclear Regulatory Authority as the regulator for nuclear safety and radiological protection, while the Nigeria Atomic Energy Commission, established under the Nigeria Atomic Energy Commission Act 1976, is responsible for promoting the development of atomic energy. Ghana, Kenya, and Uganda are among the other African states that have engaged extensively with the IAEA on nuclear power infrastructure. The interaction between IAEA guidance and national legislation is a good illustration of how the soft law outputs of an international organisation shape domestic legal frameworks.

### Assessment

The IAEA is the most legally powerful of the energy-specific international organisations, by virtue of its safeguards function and its role in the non-proliferation regime. Its experience demonstrates both the possibilities and the limits of international verification: safeguards have been strengthened in response to failures, but the Agency depends on the cooperation of states and on the Security Council for enforcement. Its promotional role has expanded with renewed interest in nuclear energy as a low-carbon source, including small modular reactors.

## 4.6 Regional Energy Regulatory Institutions

### The European Union

The European Union possesses the most developed regional energy governance system in the world. Article 194 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, introduced by the Treaty of Lisbon in 2009, provides that Union policy on energy shall aim, in a spirit of solidarity between member states, to ensure the functioning of the energy market, ensure security of energy supply, promote energy efficiency and energy saving and the development of new and renewable forms of energy, and promote the interconnection of energy networks. Article 194(2) preserves "a Member State's right to determine the conditions for exploiting its energy resources, its choice between different energy sources and the general structure of its energy supply", a significant limitation on Union competence that reflects the sovereignty sensitivities discussed above.

EU energy law has developed through successive legislative packages. The Third Energy Package of 2009 required the unbundling of transmission system operators from generation and supply activities, strengthened the independence of national regulatory authorities, and established the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) and the European Networks of Transmission System Operators for Electricity and Gas (ENTSO-E and ENTSOG). The Clean Energy for All Europeans Package of 2019 updated the framework for electricity markets, renewable energy, energy efficiency, and governance, including the Regulation on the Governance of the Energy Union and Climate Action (EU) 2018/1999, which requires member states to adopt integrated national energy and climate plans. The "Fit for 55" package, adopted between 2021 and 2023, aligned EU energy law with the target of reducing net greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55 per cent by 2030 relative to 1990 levels, established by the European Climate Law, Regulation (EU) 2021/1119, which also enshrines the objective of climate neutrality by 2050. The REPowerEU plan of 2022 sought to end dependence on Russian fossil fuels, and in 2024 the Union adopted reforms of electricity market design and a hydrogen and decarbonised gas market package.

The Court of Justice of the European Union has played a major role in shaping EU energy law, including through cases on unbundling, the independence of regulators, state aid for renewable energy (*PreussenElektra*, 2001), and the principle of energy solidarity. In *Germany v Poland* (2021), the Court held that the principle of energy solidarity in Article 194(1) is a legally binding principle that requires the EU institutions and member states to take into account the interests of other member states in energy security, annulling a Commission decision on the OPAL gas pipeline that had failed to examine the effects on Poland's security of supply.[^opal]

[^opal]: Case C-848/19 P, *Federal Republic of Germany v Republic of Poland*, EU:C:2021:598 (CJEU, 15 July 2021).

### The Energy Community

The Energy Community, established by the Treaty Establishing the Energy Community signed in Athens on 25 October 2005, extends the EU's internal energy market rules to contracting parties in South-East Europe and the Black Sea region, currently comprising Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Kosovo (designation without prejudice to positions on status), Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, and Ukraine. Contracting parties undertake to implement the relevant EU *acquis* in energy, environment, competition, and renewables. The Energy Community has a Secretariat in Vienna and a dispute settlement procedure under which the Ministerial Council may declare that a contracting party has failed to comply with its obligations. The Energy Community has been a significant vehicle for the integration of Ukraine's energy system with that of the European Union, which culminated in the synchronisation of the Ukrainian and Moldovan electricity grids with the continental European grid in March 2022.

### African Regional Institutions

Africa has developed a growing network of regional energy institutions, reflecting both the continent's vast energy resources and the imperative of expanding energy access.

**The African Union.** The AU's Agenda 2063 identifies energy as a priority, and the African Energy Commission (AFREC), established by a Convention adopted in 2001 and in force since 2008, is the AU's specialised agency for energy. The Programme for Infrastructure Development in Africa (PIDA) identifies priority regional energy projects. The African Single Electricity Market (AfSEM), launched in 2021, aims to create the world's largest electricity market by integrating Africa's regional power pools. The Agreement Establishing the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), signed in 2018 and in force since 2019, has implications for trade in energy goods and services.

**ECOWAS.** In West Africa, the Economic Community of West African States has established the ECOWAS Energy Protocol (2003), modelled on the ECT, which provides for investment protection, trade, and transit in the energy sector; the ECOWAS Regional Electricity Regulatory Authority (ERERA), established in 2008 and based in Accra, which regulates cross-border electricity exchanges; the West African Power Pool (WAPP), a specialised institution of ECOWAS established in 1999 and given its current status in 2006, which coordinates the development of regional transmission infrastructure and the regional electricity market; and the ECOWAS Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency (ECREEE), based in Praia. The West African Gas Pipeline, which transports Nigerian gas to Benin, Togo, and Ghana, is governed by a treaty concluded among the four states in 2003 and by an International Project Agreement with the pipeline company. The proposed Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline and the Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline would extend this infrastructure substantially.

**Southern and Eastern Africa.** The Southern African Power Pool (SAPP), established in 1995, is the most mature of the African power pools and operates a competitive regional electricity market. The Regional Electricity Regulators Association of Southern Africa (RERA) supports regulatory cooperation. The Eastern Africa Power Pool (EAPP) and the Central African Power Pool (CAPP) pursue similar objectives in their regions.

### Development Finance Institutions as Energy Governors

Multilateral development banks and other development finance institutions are among the most influential actors in energy governance in developing countries, although they are rarely described as energy institutions. Their influence operates through several channels: the conditions attached to policy-based lending, which have historically encouraged power sector unbundling, privatisation, and the establishment of independent regulators; the environmental and social standards attached to project finance, notably the World Bank's Environmental and Social Framework (2018) and the IFC Performance Standards; the provision of guarantees and political risk insurance, including through the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency; and the provision of technical assistance for the drafting of legislation and model contracts.

Development banks have also become central to the financing of energy access. Mission 300, launched by the World Bank Group and the African Development Bank in 2024, aims to connect 300 million people in Africa to electricity by 2030 through a combination of grid extension, mini-grids, and off-grid solutions. At the Mission 300 Africa Energy Summit held in Dar es Salaam in January 2025, participating governments presented national energy compacts setting out policy and regulatory reforms intended to attract investment. The African Development Bank's Desert to Power initiative seeks to develop solar generation across the Sahel. Such initiatives blend finance with policy conditionality, and their legal dimension — the reforms to tariff regulation, licensing, and procurement that they require — is a significant but understudied aspect of energy law in Africa.

The energy lending policies of development banks have also become a site of contestation over fossil fuel finance. The World Bank Group announced in 2017 that it would cease financing upstream oil and gas after 2019, save in exceptional circumstances, and the European Investment Bank's 2019 energy lending policy ended financing for most unabated fossil fuel projects. Developing-country governments, including Nigeria, have argued that such policies unfairly constrain the use of natural gas as a transition fuel in countries with low per capita emissions and large energy access deficits, an argument that connects institutional policy to the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities discussed in Chapter 18.

### Other Regional Arrangements

Elsewhere, regional cooperation in energy takes a variety of forms. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) has pursued the ASEAN Power Grid and the Trans-ASEAN Gas Pipeline projects through memoranda of understanding. In Latin America, the Central American Electrical Interconnection System (SIEPAC) is underpinned by the Framework Treaty for the Electricity Market of Central America (1996), which established a regional regulator, the Comisión Regional de Interconexión Eléctrica (CRIE). The Gulf Cooperation Council Interconnection Authority operates a regional transmission grid linking the six GCC states.

**Regional integration models.** The European Union represents a *supranational* model, in which binding legislation, an independent regional regulator (ACER), and a supreme court (the CJEU) drive deep market integration. ECOWAS and SADC represent *intergovernmental* models, in which regional regulators and power pools coordinate national systems with more limited binding authority, and in which the pace of integration depends heavily on the construction of physical infrastructure and on the financial health of national utilities.
