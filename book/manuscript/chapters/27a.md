## Introduction

Although arbitration predominates in transnational commercial and investor–state energy disputes, as examined in Chapter 26, **courts** remain central to energy law. National courts review the legality of licensing decisions, regulatory determinations, and environmental approvals; they adjudicate contractual disputes not subject to arbitration and supervise arbitration; they provide remedies for communities harmed by oil spills, gas flaring, and mining pollution; they hear climate cases that have reshaped the obligations of governments and companies; they enforce regulatory requirements through civil and criminal proceedings; and they determine questions of jurisdiction and enforcement in transnational litigation. Regional and international courts — the International Court of Justice, the European Court of Human Rights, the Court of Justice of the European Union, the ECOWAS Community Court of Justice, and the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights — add further layers of adjudication.

Energy litigation is distinctive in several respects. It frequently involves the review of complex technical and economic judgments by regulators, raising questions about the appropriate intensity of judicial scrutiny. It engages fundamental questions of the separation of powers, as courts are asked to adjudicate matters of energy and climate policy traditionally reserved to the executive and legislature. It often involves claimants with limited resources confronting well-resourced companies and governments, raising questions of access to justice, standing, costs, and collective redress. And it increasingly crosses borders, as claimants harmed by the activities of multinational companies in one country seek remedies in the courts of the companies' home states.

This chapter examines domestic courts and energy disputes (section 27.1), administrative and regulatory litigation (section 27.2), judicial review of licensing decisions (section 27.3), environmental and climate litigation (section 27.4), contractual and commercial litigation (section 27.5), class actions and collective redress (section 27.6), injunctions, remedies, and enforcement orders (section 27.7), and cross-border litigation and jurisdictional conflicts (section 27.8). The substantive principles of climate law and litigation have been examined in Chapter 18 and environmental liability in Chapter 17; this chapter focuses on litigation as a process and on the procedural and remedial questions that shape its outcomes.

## 27.1 Domestic Courts and Energy Disputes

### The Functions of Courts in Energy Governance

Domestic courts perform several functions in energy governance. They **resolve disputes** between private parties, between private parties and the state, and among public authorities. They **review the legality** of governmental and regulatory action, ensuring that authorities act within their powers, follow fair procedures, and respect fundamental rights. They **interpret** constitutional provisions on resource ownership, the division of powers in federal states, and environmental rights, as in the Nigerian Supreme Court's decisions on the ownership of offshore resources and the Indian Supreme Court's decision on coal block allocations, examined in Chapter 24. They **enforce** regulatory requirements through civil and criminal proceedings. And they **develop the law**, particularly in common law systems, through the elaboration of principles of tort, contract, and administrative law in the energy context — as in the development of the law of nuisance and negligence in oil pollution cases and the duty of care of parent companies, examined in Chapter 17.

### Jurisdiction and Specialised Courts

The allocation of jurisdiction over energy disputes varies among legal systems. In **Nigeria**, section 251(1) of the Constitution of 1999 confers on the **Federal High Court** exclusive jurisdiction in civil causes and matters relating to, among other things, the revenue of the federal government, the operation of companies, and "mines and minerals (including oil fields, oil mining, geological surveys and natural gas)", as well as actions for declarations or injunctions affecting the validity of executive or administrative actions of the federal government or its agencies. The Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have interpreted this provision as conferring on the Federal High Court exclusive jurisdiction over disputes relating to oil and gas operations, including, in many cases, claims for compensation for oil pollution against oil companies — though the precise scope of the provision, particularly its application to simple contract and tort claims between private parties connected with the oil industry, has generated inconsistent decisions. In ***Shell Petroleum Development Co v Isaiah*** and later cases, the courts addressed compensation claims for oil spills, and the question whether State High Courts have jurisdiction over such claims has been the subject of extensive litigation, with the trend favouring the exclusive jurisdiction of the Federal High Court where the claim arises from oil mining operations.

Several jurisdictions have established **specialised courts** for environmental and energy matters. **Environmental courts and tribunals** exist in more than fifty countries, including the **Land and Environment Court of New South Wales**, established in 1980; India's **National Green Tribunal**, established in 2010, which has decided many cases concerning power plants, mining, and pollution; Kenya's **Environment and Land Court**; and the environmental courts of Chile, Brazil, and other Latin American states. Specialised courts can develop expertise in technical matters, apply more flexible procedures, and provide faster and more accessible justice, but may also be criticised for their limited powers or capture by particular interests. In the United Kingdom, the **Planning Court** and the **Competition Appeal Tribunal** hear energy-related planning and competition matters, and the **Commercial Court** and **Technology and Construction Court** are leading fora for energy contract disputes.

### Justiciability and the Separation of Powers

A recurring question in energy litigation is whether disputes involving energy and climate policy are **justiciable** — that is, suitable for judicial resolution — or whether they raise **political questions** that should be left to the elected branches. In the **United States**, the political question doctrine and standing requirements have been invoked to dismiss climate cases against the federal government. In ***Juliana v United States***, in which youth plaintiffs alleged that the federal government's promotion of fossil fuels violated their constitutional rights to life, liberty, and property, the Ninth Circuit held in 2020 that the plaintiffs lacked standing because the relief sought — an order requiring the government to prepare and implement a plan to phase out fossil fuel emissions — was beyond the power of an Article III court to provide, and the case was finally dismissed, with the Supreme Court declining review in 2025. In ***American Electric Power v Connecticut*** (2011), the Supreme Court held that the Clean Air Act and the EPA's authority to regulate greenhouse gas emissions displaced federal common law nuisance claims against power companies.

Courts in other jurisdictions have been more willing to adjudicate climate and energy policy. In ***Urgenda Foundation v State of the Netherlands*** (2019), the Dutch Supreme Court held that the state's duty of care under Articles 2 and 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights required it to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by at least twenty-five per cent from 1990 levels by 2020, rejecting the argument that the case raised political questions beyond judicial competence: the courts' task was to protect human rights, and the determination of the minimum required reduction did not intrude on the political choice of the means. In ***Neubauer v Germany*** (2021), the German Federal Constitutional Court held that the Federal Climate Change Act violated the fundamental rights of young people by failing to specify emission reductions after 2030, thereby placing a disproportionate burden of future reductions on the younger generation. In ***Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz v Switzerland*** (2024), the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held that Article 8 of the Convention encompasses a right to effective protection by the state from the serious adverse effects of climate change, and that Switzerland had failed to comply with its positive obligations, while recognising the standing of an association but not of individual applicants who did not meet a high threshold of victim status. These cases, examined in Chapter 18, demonstrate the varying approaches of courts to the separation of powers in climate matters.

### Courts in Nigeria's Energy Sector

Nigerian courts have played an important role in the energy sector, though their effectiveness has been constrained by delays, the complexity of jurisdictional rules, and enforcement difficulties. Landmark decisions include ***Gbemre v Shell Petroleum Development Co*** (Federal High Court, Benin, 2005), in which the court held that gas flaring in the claimant's community violated the constitutional rights to life and dignity and the right to a healthy environment under the African Charter, as domesticated by the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (Ratification and Enforcement) Act, and ordered that flaring cease — an order not implemented; ***Centre for Oil Pollution Watch v NNPC*** (Supreme Court, 2018), in which the Supreme Court held that a non-governmental organisation had standing to bring an action to compel the remediation of oil pollution in the public interest, liberalising the restrictive approach to standing in environmental matters; and the many decisions awarding compensation for oil spills under statute and the common law. The **National Industrial Court**, the **Investments and Securities Tribunal**, and the tribunals established under the Petroleum Industry Act and the Electricity Act add further fora. The **Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules 2009** provide an expedited procedure for the enforcement of fundamental rights, which has been used in environmental cases, and expressly encourage public interest litigation by providing that human rights activists, advocates, and NGOs may institute proceedings on behalf of applicants.

## 27.2 Administrative and Regulatory Litigation

### Regulatory Decisions and the Courts

Energy regulators make decisions with significant economic consequences: determining tariffs for network services and regulated supply, granting and revoking licences, approving mergers and acquisitions, imposing penalties, and setting market rules. The legal mechanisms for challenging such decisions include **appeals** on the merits, available where legislation provides for them, and **judicial review** of the lawfulness of the decision. The design of these mechanisms involves a trade-off between **accountability** and **regulatory certainty**: broad rights of appeal enhance the accountability of regulators and protect the rights of regulated entities, but may undermine regulatory independence and predictability, encourage strategic litigation, and transfer technical decisions to generalist judges.

### Appeals Against Regulatory Decisions

In the **United Kingdom**, decisions of the Gas and Electricity Markets Authority (**Ofgem**) on price controls under the RIIO framework may be appealed to the **Competition and Markets Authority** (CMA), which reviews whether the decision was wrong on specified grounds, including errors of fact, law, or the exercise of discretion. In its decision on the appeals against the **RIIO-2** price control in 2021, the CMA upheld most of Ofgem's decisions but found that Ofgem had erred in its approach to the "outperformance wedge", which reduced the allowed return of network companies. Appeals against Ofgem's licence modification decisions and penalties are also available. In the **United States**, decisions of the **Federal Energy Regulatory Commission** are subject to rehearing before the Commission and then to review in the federal courts of appeals, particularly the D.C. Circuit, under the **Administrative Procedure Act** standard of whether the decision was "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law", and whether the Commission's findings of fact are supported by substantial evidence. In ***Allegheny Defense Project v FERC*** (D.C. Circuit en banc, 2020), the court held that FERC could not indefinitely delay rehearing requests through "tolling orders" while allowing pipeline construction to proceed, protecting landowners' access to judicial review. In the **European Union**, decisions of the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) may be appealed to the ACER Board of Appeal and then to the EU General Court.

In **Nigeria**, the **Electricity Act 2023** provides for the resolution of disputes and the review of NERC's decisions, including through NERC's own review processes and appeals to the courts, and for the establishment of state electricity regulatory commissions, whose decisions will raise new questions of jurisdiction and review in the decentralised regime examined in Chapter 24. Under the **Petroleum Industry Act 2021**, the decisions of the NUPRC and the NMDPRA are subject to internal review and judicial review, and the Act provides for the resolution of certain disputes through arbitration or expert determination. The **Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018** established the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission and the **Competition and Consumer Protection Tribunal**, which hears appeals against the Commission's decisions, including in the energy sector, where the Commission has investigated pricing practices of electricity distribution companies and fuel marketers.

### The Intensity of Review and Regulatory Deference

The intensity of judicial review of regulatory decisions — the degree of **deference** courts accord to the expertise of regulators — varies among jurisdictions and is the subject of continuing debate. In the **United States**, the ***Chevron*** doctrine (*Chevron USA v Natural Resources Defense Council*, 1984), which required courts to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes, was **overruled** in ***Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo*** (2024), in which the Supreme Court held that the Administrative Procedure Act requires courts to exercise their independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority. Together with the **major questions doctrine** articulated in *West Virginia v EPA* (2022), examined in Chapter 24, *Loper Bright* has significantly expanded the scope of judicial scrutiny of energy and environmental regulation in the United States, though courts continue to defer to agencies' technical and factual judgments under the arbitrary and capricious standard. In the **United Kingdom**, courts accord considerable respect to the expertise of regulators on technical and economic matters, intervening on the grounds of illegality, irrationality, or procedural impropriety, while the CMA's appellate jurisdiction allows more intensive review of price control decisions. The approach of courts in other common law jurisdictions, including Nigeria, generally follows the English model, though the extent of deference to specialist regulators is less developed.

### Litigation Between Public Authorities

Energy litigation also arises between public authorities, particularly in federal systems. In **Nigeria**, the federal government and the states have litigated over the ownership of offshore resources in ***Attorney-General of the Federation v Attorney-General of Abia State*** (2001 and 2002), in which the Supreme Court held that, for the purposes of the derivation principle, the seaward boundary of littoral states is the low-water mark, so that revenues from offshore resources were not subject to derivation — a decision reversed by the **Allocation of Revenue (Abolition of Dichotomy in the Application of the Principle of Derivation) Act 2004**. States have also challenged federal laws on electricity, environmental regulation, and value added tax, and the Electricity Act 2023 may generate further intergovernmental litigation over the boundaries of federal and state competence. In the **United States**, states have repeatedly sued federal agencies over energy and environmental regulation, as in *Massachusetts v EPA* (2007), in which the Supreme Court held that Massachusetts had standing to challenge the EPA's refusal to regulate greenhouse gas emissions from vehicles and that greenhouse gases were air pollutants under the Clean Air Act, and in the numerous challenges by states to federal power plant and methane regulations and to federal efforts to curtail renewable energy.
