::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 23: An Energy Justice Review of a State Electrification Law (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The state, legislation, institutions, and data described are fictitious and do not refer to any real jurisdiction, statute, or body.**

Following the devolution of electricity regulation to the states of the Federal Republic of Amara, the government of Riverbend State — a state with a population of eight million, of whom forty-five per cent lack access to grid electricity, concentrated in rural and riverine areas — has published a draft **Riverbend State Electricity and Universal Access Bill** for public comment. The Bill's principal features are as follows:

- It establishes the Riverbend Electricity Regulatory Commission, whose members are appointed by the Governor and may be removed "at the Governor's pleasure".
- It requires all tariffs to be fully cost-reflective within twelve months, with no provision for lifeline or social tariffs, but authorises the Governor to direct "such subsidies as may be appropriate from time to time".
- It grants an exclusive twenty-year distribution franchise over the whole state to a single company, which has no obligation to connect customers more than two kilometres from its existing network.
- It permits mini-grids only in areas that the franchisee certifies it does not intend to serve within five years, and provides that when the grid arrives, mini-grid assets transfer to the franchisee without compensation.
- It establishes a Rural Electrification Fund financed by a levy of two per cent on the bills of all customers, to be administered by the Ministry of Energy, without provision for reporting.
- It requires consultation on tariff changes "where the Commission considers it appropriate" and provides no dedicated consumer complaints mechanism.
- It contains no provisions on gender, disability, or vulnerable customers, and no data collection or reporting obligations.

Riverbend State is also an oil-producing state, receiving derivation revenues, and hosts several oil fields whose host communities lack electricity. A coalition of women's cooperatives, mini-grid developers, and community organisations has asked for your assistance.

**Tasks.**

(a) Using the distributional, procedural, recognition, and restorative tenets of energy justice, prepare a written submission (of no more than 3,000 words) to the State House of Assembly evaluating the Bill and identifying its principal deficiencies.

(b) Propose specific amendments to the Bill, drafted in legislative language, addressing at least: the independence and accountability of the regulator; tariff design and protection of poor and vulnerable customers; universal service obligations; the framework for mini-grids and off-grid solutions, including the arrival of the grid; the governance and transparency of the Rural Electrification Fund; consumer participation and complaints; gender and social inclusion; and data and reporting.

(c) Prepare a short note on how the state could use a portion of its derivation revenues to accelerate electrification in host communities, consistently with the federal constitutional framework and principles of sound revenue management.

(d) In a group discussion, debate whether the Bill's emphasis on rapid cost-reflectivity is compatible with energy justice, and how the tension between financial sustainability and affordability should be resolved.
:::

::: summary
- Energy justice, which emerged in the 2010s, applies principles of justice to energy systems, drawing on distributive justice, the capabilities approach, the politics of recognition, and environmental and climate justice; it functions as a conceptual, analytical, and decision-making tool.
- Its core tenets are distributional justice (the fair allocation of benefits and burdens), procedural justice (fair, transparent, and participatory decision-making with access to remedies), and recognition justice (respect for the identities and needs of all groups), supplemented by restorative, intergenerational, and cosmopolitan dimensions.
- Around 666 million people lack electricity and around 2.1 billion lack clean cooking, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa; Nigeria has the world's largest electricity access deficit. Instruments for universal access include rural electrification agencies and funds, mini-grid and off-grid frameworks, clean cooking programmes, and initiatives such as Mission 300; subsidy reform must be accompanied by protection for the poor.
- Energy systems are gendered: women bear disproportionate burdens of energy poverty and are underrepresented in the energy workforce and decision-making; CEDAW, the Maputo Protocol, and ECOWAS instruments on gender in energy provide a legal framework.
- The resource curse highlights the importance of institutions for revenue management; Nigeria's revenue allocation system, including the thirteen per cent derivation principle and the onshore/offshore dichotomy, has been deeply contested; revenue management laws and sovereign wealth funds, such as those of Norway and Ghana, provide models.
- Intergenerational equity requires conservation of options, quality, and access for future generations, is increasingly recognised by courts, and has implications both for saving resource revenues and for limiting fossil fuel extraction in the energy transition.
- SDG 7 provides a widely accepted but non-binding framework for universal access, renewable energy, and efficiency; progress is off track, and the SDGs are integrated with commitments made at COP28 and in African initiatives.
- Equitable regulatory systems require universal service, tariffs that balance cost-reflectivity with protection of the poor, fair allocation of transition costs, community energy and benefit-sharing, just transition mechanisms, and independent and accountable regulators.
:::

::: research
1. Can energy justice serve as a legally operative principle, rather than merely an analytical framework? Evaluate proposals to incorporate energy justice into energy legislation and regulatory mandates, with reference to Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023.
2. Is there, or should there be, a human right to energy? Assess the legal basis for such a right in international and African human rights law and in national constitutional jurisprudence.
3. Evaluate the service-based tariff system adopted in the Nigerian electricity sector from the perspective of distributional and procedural justice.
4. Has the thirteen per cent derivation principle, together with the NDDC and host communities development trusts, delivered distributional justice to oil-producing communities in the Niger Delta? Propose reforms.
5. How should resource-rich developing countries reconcile intergenerational equity in the management of resource wealth with the imperative of limiting fossil fuel extraction to protect future generations from climate change?
6. Assess the effectiveness of regional instruments on gender and energy, such as those of ECOWAS, and identify the legal measures required to implement them in national law.
:::
