## 8.3 Strategic Mineral Ownership and Export Controls

### State Ownership and Control of Strategic Minerals

As examined in Chapter 3, almost all states assert ownership or control over subsurface minerals. In relation to minerals classified as strategic, however, many states go further, reserving exploitation to the state or to state enterprises, requiring majority state participation, or subjecting the minerals to special regimes. The Chilean, Bolivian, and Mexican regimes for lithium, examined in section 8.2, are examples, as are the DRC's regime for strategic substances and the reservation of radioactive minerals (uranium and thorium) to the state in many jurisdictions. Such reservations are an exercise of permanent sovereignty over natural resources and are, in principle, consistent with international law, provided that they are applied prospectively or, where they affect existing rights, in compliance with the requirements for lawful expropriation, including compensation (examined in Chapters 3 and 14).

State ownership of strategic minerals is often justified on grounds of national security, the capture of resource rents, and the promotion of downstream industrialisation. Its effectiveness depends on the capacity and governance of state enterprises, which have frequently struggled to attract the capital and technology necessary to develop complex projects. Bolivia's experience with lithium, where state control has not yet produced significant output despite immense resources, illustrates the difficulties; Chile's model of public-private partnerships under state control represents an attempt to combine state ownership with private capital and expertise.

### Export Restrictions by Producing States

Producing states have increasingly used export restrictions — bans, quotas, export taxes, and licensing requirements — to promote domestic processing, conserve resources, manage prices, or raise revenue. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which maintains an inventory of export restrictions on industrial raw materials, has documented a more than five-fold increase in such restrictions between 2009 and 2020, with the largest increases in critical minerals. Examples include Indonesia's nickel ore export ban, Zimbabwe's and Namibia's restrictions on unprocessed lithium, the DRC's cobalt export suspension and quotas, and the restrictions on the export of unprocessed bauxite in Guinea and other states.

### International Trade Law Disciplines

Export restrictions are subject to the disciplines of international trade law, examined generally in Chapter 13. The central provision is **Article XI:1 of the GATT 1994**, which prohibits "prohibitions or restrictions other than duties, taxes or other charges, whether made effective through quotas, import or export licences or other measures" on the exportation of any product destined for the territory of another WTO member. Export *duties*, by contrast, are not prohibited by the GATT, although some members, notably China, accepted commitments to eliminate export duties in their accession protocols.

Export restrictions may be justified under several exceptions:

- **Article XI:2(a)** permits export prohibitions or restrictions "temporarily applied to prevent or relieve critical shortages of foodstuffs or other products essential to the exporting contracting party".
- **Article XX(g)** permits measures "relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources if such measures are made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption", subject to the requirements of the chapeau of Article XX that measures not be applied in a manner that would constitute arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination or a disguised restriction on international trade.
- **Article XX(i)** permits measures "involving restrictions on exports of domestic materials necessary to ensure essential quantities of such materials to a domestic processing industry during periods when the domestic price of such materials is held below the world price as part of a governmental stabilization plan", subject to conditions.
- **Article XXI(b)** permits measures that a member "considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests", including those "taken in time of war or other emergency in international relations".

The WTO dispute settlement bodies have interpreted these exceptions narrowly in the context of mineral export restrictions. In *China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Various Raw Materials* (2012), concerning Chinese export duties and quotas on bauxite, coke, fluorspar, magnesium, manganese, silicon carbide, silicon metal, yellow phosphorus, and zinc, the Appellate Body held that China could not invoke Article XX to justify export duties inconsistent with paragraph 11.3 of its Accession Protocol, because that paragraph contained no reference to the GATT exceptions. It also held that a measure is "temporarily applied" under Article XI:2(a) only if it is applied for a limited time to bridge a passing need, and that a "critical shortage" refers to deficiencies in quantity that are crucial or that reach a vitally important or decisive stage. In *China — Rare Earths* (2014), the Appellate Body upheld the panel's findings that China's export quotas on rare earths, tungsten, and molybdenum were not justified under Article XX(g), because China had not demonstrated that the quotas were made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption in an even-handed manner; on the contrary, the quotas appeared designed to secure supply for domestic users at the expense of foreign users. China removed the export duties and quotas in 2015.

In *Indonesia — Raw Materials* (DS592), discussed in section 8.2, the panel held that Indonesia's nickel ore export ban was a prohibition contrary to Article XI:1, that it was not "temporarily applied" to relieve a "critical shortage" within Article XI:2(a), since it was intended to continue indefinitely, and that it was not justified under Article XX(d). The panel's report has not been adopted owing to Indonesia's appeal to the non-functioning Appellate Body.

These decisions establish that WTO law significantly constrains the use of export restrictions to promote downstream processing. Critics argue that the rules are asymmetric: they constrain the industrial policy tools available to resource-rich developing countries, which historically were used by today's industrialised countries during their own development, while importing states are relatively free to use subsidies and other measures to build processing capacity. The legitimacy of the WTO disciplines on export restrictions, and the scope for reform, are significant themes for doctoral research, explored in Chapter 13.

### Export Controls on Minerals and Technologies by China

China's use of export controls on critical minerals and processing technologies has transformed the landscape. The Export Control Law of the People's Republic of China (2020) established a comprehensive framework for controlling exports of dual-use items, military products, nuclear materials, and other items related to national security and interests, and authorised countermeasures against countries that abuse export controls to endanger China's national security and interests. Under this framework and related regulations, including the Regulations on the Export Control of Dual-Use Items (2024), China has imposed licensing requirements on exports of:

- **gallium and germanium** and their compounds, from August 2023;
- **graphite**, including certain high-purity natural and synthetic graphite products, from December 2023;
- **rare earth extraction, separation, and magnet manufacturing technologies**, added to the Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited or Restricted from Export in December 2023;
- **antimony** and superhard materials, from September 2024;
- **seven medium and heavy rare earth elements** (samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium) and related magnets, from April 2025.

In December 2024, China prohibited exports of gallium, germanium, antimony, and superhard materials to the United States in response to United States export controls on semiconductors. In October 2025, China announced a substantial expansion of its rare earth export controls, including provisions asserting jurisdiction over foreign-made products containing Chinese-origin rare earths or produced with Chinese technology; following a meeting between the presidents of China and the United States at the end of October 2025, China announced that it would suspend the implementation of the expanded measures for one year. These measures, and their partial suspension as part of a broader trade negotiation, illustrate the use of critical minerals as instruments of economic statecraft and the vulnerability of importing states to concentrated supply chains. China has justified the measures as necessary for the protection of national security and the fulfilment of non-proliferation obligations; importing states have characterised them as economic coercion. Their consistency with WTO law would turn largely on the interpretation of Article XXI, examined in Chapters 13 and 25.

### Export Controls by Importing States and Investment Screening

Importing states also use export controls and investment screening in relation to critical minerals. The United States, the European Union, Japan, and other states control exports of technologies related to semiconductor manufacturing, advanced batteries, and other strategic sectors, and the United States' foreign direct product rules extend these controls extraterritorially. Investment screening regimes — such as the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) under the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act of 2018, the EU Foreign Direct Investment Screening Regulation (EU) 2019/452 (the revision of which was under negotiation in 2025), the United Kingdom's National Security and Investment Act 2021, and the Australian Foreign Acquisitions and Takeovers Act 1975 — have been used to block or condition acquisitions of critical mineral assets by foreign investors, particularly Chinese state-linked companies. Australia has blocked several Chinese investments in rare earth and lithium companies on national interest grounds. The use of investment screening in the mineral sector raises questions about its consistency with investment treaties and with the principle of non-discrimination, and about the risk that screening becomes an instrument of geopolitical rivalry rather than a narrowly targeted security measure.

## 8.4 Resource Security and Industrial Policy

### From Market Reliance to Industrial Policy

For several decades, the dominant approach of industrialised states to mineral supply was reliance on global markets: minerals were commodities to be purchased wherever they were cheapest, and the state's role was limited to maintaining open trade and, in some cases, strategic stockpiles for defence purposes. The concentration of supply chains in China, China's demonstrated willingness to use export restrictions, the supply chain disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the Russian invasion of Ukraine have led to a fundamental shift. Industrialised states now pursue active industrial policies aimed at building domestic and allied capacity across the mineral value chain — mining, processing, component manufacturing, and recycling — using legislation, subsidies, public financing, permitting reforms, and international partnerships.

### The EU Critical Raw Materials Act

The EU Critical Raw Materials Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252) is the most comprehensive legislative framework for critical mineral security. Its principal elements are:

**Benchmarks.** Article 5 establishes benchmarks for strengthening the different stages of the strategic raw materials value chain, providing that by 2030 the Union's capacity should be sufficient for extraction to produce at least ten per cent, processing to produce at least forty per cent, and recycling to produce at least twenty-five per cent of the Union's annual consumption of strategic raw materials, and that the Union should diversify its imports so that no more than sixty-five per cent of its annual consumption of each strategic raw material at any relevant stage of processing comes from a single third country. The benchmarks are not legally binding targets for member states, but they guide the implementation of the Act.

**Strategic projects.** The Act establishes a procedure for the recognition of strategic projects — projects that contribute meaningfully to the security of the Union's supply of strategic raw materials, are technically feasible, are implemented sustainably, and, for projects in third countries, are mutually beneficial for the Union and the third country concerned. Strategic projects benefit from priority status in permitting, with maximum permit-granting periods (twenty-seven months for extraction projects and fifteen months for processing and recycling projects within the Union), a presumption that they are in the public interest for the purposes of certain environmental assessments, and support in accessing finance. In March 2025, the Commission selected the first forty-seven strategic projects within the Union, and in June 2025 it selected thirteen strategic projects in third countries, including projects in Africa and Latin America.

**Monitoring and risk preparedness.** The Act requires the Commission and member states to monitor supply chains, to conduct stress tests, and to coordinate strategic stocks, and requires large companies manufacturing strategic technologies to carry out risk assessments of their supply chains.

**Circularity and sustainability.** The Act requires member states to adopt national programmes to increase the collection and recycling of critical raw materials, and requires certain products to disclose information on the recyclability and recycled content of permanent magnets.

**International partnerships.** The Act provides a framework for strategic partnerships with third countries, building on the memoranda of understanding concluded by the EU with Canada, Ukraine, Kazakhstan, Namibia, Argentina, Chile, Zambia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Greenland, Rwanda, Norway, Uzbekistan, Australia, and others.

### United States Industrial Policy

The United States has adopted a range of measures to build domestic critical mineral supply chains. The **Inflation Reduction Act of 2022** introduced, among other incentives, a clean vehicle tax credit under section 30D of the Internal Revenue Code, part of which was conditional on a rising percentage of the value of critical minerals in the vehicle's battery being extracted or processed in the United States or in a country with which the United States has a free trade agreement, or recycled in North America, and excluded vehicles whose batteries contained critical minerals extracted, processed, or recycled by a "foreign entity of concern". The FTA requirement led the United States to conclude "critical minerals agreements" with Japan (March 2023) and to negotiate similar agreements with the European Union and the United Kingdom, raising questions about whether such limited agreements qualify as "free trade agreements". The clean vehicle tax credit was terminated for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025 by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025, illustrating the vulnerability of industrial policy to political change.

The **Defense Production Act** of 1950, Title III of which authorises the President to provide financial assistance to expand domestic productive capacity for materials essential to national defence, has been invoked to support critical mineral projects, and in 2022 President Biden authorised its use for battery minerals. In March 2025, President Trump issued an Executive Order on Immediate Measures to Increase American Mineral Production, directing agencies to expedite permitting for priority mineral projects and to expand the use of Defense Production Act authorities and federal financing. The Department of Energy's Loan Programs Office, the Development Finance Corporation, and the Export-Import Bank of the United States have provided financing for domestic and overseas critical mineral projects, and in 2025 the federal government took equity stakes in several critical mineral companies, including MP Materials and the lithium developer Lithium Americas.

### Other Jurisdictions

Japan has pursued mineral security for decades through the Japan Organization for Metals and Energy Security (JOGMEC), which provides equity financing, loan guarantees, and exploration support for overseas mineral projects and maintains national stockpiles. Following the Chinese restrictions on rare earth exports to Japan in 2010, during a territorial dispute, JOGMEC and Japanese companies invested in the Australian rare earth producer Lynas, helping to establish one of the few significant sources of rare earths outside China. Australia's Critical Minerals Strategy 2023–2030 and its Critical Minerals Facility, administered by Export Finance Australia, provide financing for critical mineral projects, and the Future Made in Australia Act 2024 introduced production tax incentives for critical mineral processing. In October 2025, Australia and the United States concluded a framework agreement on critical minerals providing for joint investment in projects. Canada's Critical Minerals Strategy (2022) and India's National Critical Mineral Mission (2025) pursue similar objectives.

### Industrial Policy in Producing States

Producing states pursue their own industrial policies, aimed at capturing a greater share of the value chain. These include requirements for domestic processing (export bans on unprocessed ores, as in Indonesia, Zimbabwe, and Namibia), local content and beneficiation requirements, state participation, and incentives for investment in processing and manufacturing. The success of Indonesia's nickel strategy in attracting downstream investment has inspired other producing states, but its replicability is uncertain: Indonesia combined its export ban with a large domestic market, abundant coal-fired power, and massive Chinese investment, and its strategy has generated significant environmental and social costs. Producing states must also navigate the constraints of WTO law and investment treaties, and the risk that export restrictions will simply divert investment to other sources.

### The Tension Between Security and Sustainability

Industrial policies aimed at accelerating mineral supply create tensions with environmental protection, indigenous rights, and community consent. Streamlined permitting, presumptions of public interest, and political pressure to approve projects quickly may compromise the rigour of environmental assessment and the quality of consultation. The EU Critical Raw Materials Act seeks to address this tension by requiring that strategic projects be implemented sustainably, including in compliance with environmental and social standards, but civil society organisations have criticised the selection of strategic projects for insufficient transparency and inadequate consideration of community opposition. The reconciliation of mineral security with sustainability and human rights is one of the defining legal challenges of the energy transition.
