## Introduction

Climate change is the defining challenge for energy law in the twenty-first century. The combustion of fossil fuels for energy accounts for roughly three-quarters of global greenhouse gas emissions, and the transformation of the energy system — from one dominated by coal, oil, and gas to one based on renewable energy, electrification, efficiency, and low-carbon fuels — is therefore at the heart of the global response to climate change. That transformation is reshaping every area of energy and natural resources law examined in this book: the licensing of petroleum and mining, the regulation of electricity markets, the design of fiscal regimes, the protection of investments, the content of contracts, the financing of projects, and the resolution of disputes.

The scientific basis for action is well established. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), in its Sixth Assessment Report completed in 2023, concluded that human activities, principally through emissions of greenhouse gases, have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature having reached about 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels in 2011–2020; that widespread and rapid changes in the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere have occurred; and that limiting warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot requires deep, rapid, and sustained reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, with global net zero carbon dioxide emissions reached in the early 2050s. The World Meteorological Organization reported that 2024 was the first calendar year in which the global average temperature exceeded 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, although the Paris Agreement's temperature goal refers to long-term averages rather than individual years.

The legal response to climate change operates at multiple levels: the international regime established by the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Paris Agreement; the general rules of international law, including the law of state responsibility, the law of the sea, and human rights law, as clarified by recent advisory opinions; regional law, notably that of the European Union; national climate legislation and energy regulation; and the increasingly important role of courts, investors, and private standards. This chapter examines the UNFCCC (section 18.1), the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement (section 18.2), nationally determined contributions (section 18.3), mitigation and adaptation obligations (section 18.4), the regulation of fossil fuels and transition policies (section 18.5), climate litigation and corporate accountability (section 18.6), just transition (section 18.7), and the reform of climate governance (section 18.8). The procedural dimensions of climate litigation in domestic courts — standing, justiciability, and remedies — are examined in Chapter 27, and the theoretical foundations of energy justice in Chapter 23.

## 18.1 United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change

### Origins

Scientific concern about the effects of carbon dioxide emissions on the climate dates to the nineteenth century, but climate change emerged as an issue of international policy only in the 1980s. In 1988, the World Meteorological Organization and UNEP established the **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change** to assess the scientific, technical, and socio-economic information relevant to understanding climate change, and the United Nations General Assembly declared in Resolution 43/53 that climate change is a "common concern of mankind". The IPCC's First Assessment Report of 1990 provided the scientific basis for negotiations, which culminated in the adoption of the **United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change** (UNFCCC) in May 1992 and its opening for signature at the Rio Conference in June 1992. The Convention entered into force in 1994 and has near-universal participation, with 198 parties.

### The Objective

Article 2 sets out the ultimate objective of the Convention: to achieve stabilisation of greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system, within a time-frame sufficient to allow ecosystems to adapt naturally to climate change, to ensure that food production is not threatened, and to enable economic development to proceed in a sustainable manner. The objective does not define the level of "dangerous" interference, a question that was subsequently addressed by the political agreement on the 2°C limit in the Copenhagen Accord of 2009 and the Cancún Agreements of 2010, and by the temperature goal of the Paris Agreement.

### The Principles

Article 3 sets out principles to guide the parties' actions. They include the principle that parties should protect the climate system for the benefit of present and future generations of humankind, on the basis of **equity** and in accordance with their **common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities** (CBDR-RC), with developed country parties taking the lead in combating climate change (Article 3(1)); the principle that the specific needs and special circumstances of developing countries, especially those particularly vulnerable to climate change, should be given full consideration (Article 3(2)); the **precautionary** principle, providing that parties should take precautionary measures and that lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason for postponing such measures (Article 3(3)); the right to and duty to promote **sustainable development** (Article 3(4)); and the principle that measures taken to combat climate change should not constitute a means of arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination or a disguised restriction on international trade (Article 3(5)).

The principle of CBDR-RC has been the most contested element of the regime. It reflects two ideas: that developed countries bear greater responsibility for the accumulated stock of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, as a result of their historical emissions since the industrial revolution; and that they have greater financial and technological capacity to respond. Developing countries have relied on the principle to resist binding emission limits and to demand finance and technology transfer, while developed countries have argued that the rapid growth of emissions from large emerging economies, notably China, which became the world's largest emitter in the mid-2000s, requires all major economies to act. The evolution of the principle — from a binary division between developed and developing countries under the Kyoto Protocol to a more nuanced formulation under the Paris Agreement, which adds the words "in the light of different national circumstances" — has been central to the development of the regime.

### Commitments and Annexes

Article 4(1) sets out general commitments applicable to all parties, including the preparation of national inventories of emissions and removals, the formulation and implementation of national programmes containing measures to mitigate climate change and facilitate adaptation, the promotion of technology transfer, the conservation of sinks, and cooperation in research, education, and public awareness. Article 4(2) imposed more specific commitments on developed country parties listed in **Annex I** — the members of the OECD in 1992 and countries with economies in transition — to adopt policies and measures to mitigate climate change, with the aim of returning their emissions to 1990 levels, a formulation that was too weak to constitute a binding target. Developed country parties listed in **Annex II** — the OECD members — undertook to provide new and additional financial resources to meet the costs incurred by developing countries in complying with their obligations, and to promote technology transfer (Articles 4(3)–(5)). Article 4(7) provides that the extent to which developing country parties will effectively implement their commitments will depend on the effective implementation by developed country parties of their commitments related to financial resources and transfer of technology. Article 4(8) and (10) require consideration of the needs and concerns of developing countries arising from the impact of response measures, including countries whose economies are highly dependent on income from fossil fuels — a provision on which oil-exporting states, including Saudi Arabia and other OPEC members, have relied.

### Institutions

The **Conference of the Parties** (COP), meeting annually, is the supreme body of the Convention, and reviews its implementation and takes decisions to promote its effective implementation. The COP is assisted by two subsidiary bodies — the Subsidiary Body for Scientific and Technological Advice (SBSTA) and the Subsidiary Body for Implementation (SBI) — and by a secretariat based in Bonn. The **financial mechanism** of the Convention is operated by the Global Environment Facility and, since 2010, the **Green Climate Fund**, headquartered in Songdo, Republic of Korea. The COP also serves as the meeting of the parties to the Kyoto Protocol (CMP) and the Paris Agreement (CMA).

A distinctive feature of the regime is that the parties have never adopted the rules of procedure of the COP, because of disagreement over the voting rule; as a result, decisions are taken by consensus, which gives each party a potential veto and has frequently led to lowest-common-denominator outcomes and protracted negotiations. The annual COPs have grown into very large events, attended by tens of thousands of delegates, observers, and representatives of business and civil society, and the hosting of recent COPs by major oil and gas producers — the United Arab Emirates in 2023 and Azerbaijan in 2024 — and the participation of large numbers of fossil fuel industry representatives have prompted debate about the integrity of the process, examined in section 18.8.

### The Convention and the Energy Sector

The Convention does not refer expressly to fossil fuels, energy, or the energy transition, reflecting the political sensitivity of these issues for fossil fuel producing states. Its obligations are framed in terms of greenhouse gas emissions generally, leaving to parties the choice of means. Only in the decisions of the COP and the CMA in recent years — the Glasgow Climate Pact of 2021, which called for the phasedown of unabated coal power and the phase-out of inefficient fossil fuel subsidies, and the outcome of the first global stocktake at Dubai in 2023, which called on parties to contribute to "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner" — has the regime addressed fossil fuels explicitly. This evolution, examined in section 18.2, reflects the growing recognition that the objectives of the Convention cannot be achieved without a fundamental transformation of the energy system.

### The Position of the United States

The participation of the United States, historically the largest cumulative emitter, has fluctuated with changes of administration. The United States signed but never ratified the Kyoto Protocol; joined the Paris Agreement in 2016; withdrew in 2020; rejoined in 2021; and, following a further change of administration, notified its withdrawal again in January 2025, effective one year later. The United States ratified the UNFCCC itself in 1992 with the advice and consent of the Senate; the extent of its continued engagement in the wider regime, and the constitutional question whether a President may withdraw from a Senate-approved treaty without congressional involvement, have since become matters of debate. The repeated reversals illustrate the vulnerability of international climate commitments to domestic politics, and the significance of sub-national governments, businesses, and courts in sustaining climate action in the absence of federal leadership.
