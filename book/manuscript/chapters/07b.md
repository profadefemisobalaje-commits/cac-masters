## 7.2 Exploration Permits and Mining Licences

### The Sequence of Mining Titles

Most modern mining codes establish a sequence of titles corresponding to the stages of the mining life cycle: reconnaissance, exploration, development and production, and closure. Each stage involves different activities, risks, and levels of investment, and the titles granted for each stage differ in their duration, area, rights, and obligations. The holder of an exploration title that discovers a commercially viable deposit typically has a right — subject to conditions — to obtain a mining title over the deposit. This "security of tenure", which links the exploration investment to the right to exploit any resulting discovery, is regarded by investors as the most important feature of a mining regime: no rational company will invest millions of dollars in exploration if the state may refuse a mining licence and award the deposit to someone else.

### Reconnaissance Permits

Reconnaissance is the preliminary search for mineral occurrences by geological, geophysical, and geochemical surveys, remote sensing, and surface sampling, without significant ground disturbance. Reconnaissance permits are typically non-exclusive, cover large areas, and are granted for short periods (one or two years). Under the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007, a reconnaissance permit confers a non-exclusive right to search for minerals by surface geological and geophysical surveys, and does not entitle the holder to engage in drilling, excavation, or other subsurface activities.

### Exploration Licences

Exploration involves detailed investigation of a defined area to locate and evaluate mineral deposits, including drilling, trenching, bulk sampling, and feasibility studies. Exploration licences are exclusive, cover defined areas (often subject to a maximum size), and are granted for initial periods of three to five years, renewable for further periods subject to the fulfilment of work obligations and the relinquishment of a proportion of the area on renewal. Work obligations may be expressed as minimum expenditure commitments or as minimum work programmes. Relinquishment requirements prevent the holding of large areas for speculative purposes and return unexplored land to the state for allocation to others.

Under the Nigerian Act, an exploration licence confers the exclusive right to conduct exploration for the minerals specified in the licence within the licence area. It is granted for an initial period of three years, renewable twice for periods of two years each, subject to the holder having complied with its obligations, and the licence area is subject to maximum size limits. The holder is required to commence exploration within a specified period, to expend minimum amounts on exploration, to submit reports, and to pay annual service fees. Upon discovery of a commercially viable deposit, the holder may apply for a mining lease or a small-scale mining lease.

Exploration licensing raises recurrent legal issues: the transferability of licences (which is usually subject to ministerial consent), the treatment of licences held by "juniors" — small exploration companies, often listed on the Toronto, Australian, or London AIM stock exchanges, that finance exploration through equity markets and seek to sell discoveries to larger companies; the extent of the holder's right to a mining licence; and the confidentiality of exploration data, which must usually be submitted to the state geological survey and may be released after a period.

### Mining Licences and Leases

A mining licence or lease confers the exclusive right to develop and operate a mine within a defined area and to sell the minerals produced. Mining licences are granted for long periods — commonly twenty to thirty years, or the estimated life of the mine — renewable for further periods. The application for a mining licence must usually be supported by a feasibility study demonstrating the technical and economic viability of the deposit, a mining plan, an environmental impact assessment and environmental management plan, a community development plan, and evidence of financial and technical capacity.

Under the Nigerian Act, a mining lease is granted for a period of up to twenty-five years, renewable every twenty-four years, over an area not exceeding a specified maximum. The holder must commence development within a specified period of grant, comply with an approved Environmental Impact Assessment and an Environmental Protection and Rehabilitation Programme, enter into a community development agreement with the host community (discussed in section 7.5), pay royalties and other fees, and comply with the Act's provisions on health, safety, and employment. The Act also provides for small-scale mining leases, quarry leases (for the extraction of construction materials such as granite, marble, and laterite), and water use permits.

### The Right to Proceed from Exploration to Mining

The extent to which an exploration licence holder is entitled to a mining licence is critical. Some regimes confer an automatic right to a mining licence upon satisfaction of objective criteria; others confer a right to apply, leaving the state a discretion to refuse on grounds such as environmental considerations, the public interest, or inadequate financial terms. A discretion to refuse undermines security of tenure but preserves the state's ability to protect the public interest.

The tension between security of tenure and regulatory discretion has generated major investment disputes. In *Crystallex International Corp v Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela* (2016), an ICSID tribunal held that Venezuela had breached the fair and equitable treatment standard and expropriated the claimant's investment in the Las Cristinas gold deposit by refusing an environmental permit required for exploitation and subsequently rescinding the mining contract, and awarded damages of approximately USD 1.2 billion plus interest.[^crystallex] In *Tethyan Copper Co Pty Ltd v Islamic Republic of Pakistan* (2019), concerning the Reko Diq copper-gold deposit in Balochistan, an ICSID tribunal held that the refusal of a mining lease to the claimant, after it had carried out extensive exploration and a feasibility study, breached the fair and equitable treatment standard of the Australia–Pakistan bilateral investment treaty and constituted an expropriation, and awarded approximately USD 5.9 billion in damages and interest — one of the largest awards in ICSID history.[^tethyan] The dispute was resolved in 2022 through a settlement under which Barrick Gold, one of the claimant's shareholders, agreed to develop the project in partnership with the federal and provincial governments.

[^crystallex]: *Crystallex International Corp v Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela*, ICSID Case No ARB(AF)/11/2, Award (4 April 2016).

[^tethyan]: *Tethyan Copper Co Pty Ltd v Islamic Republic of Pakistan*, ICSID Case No ARB/12/1, Award (12 July 2019).

These awards are frequently cited by investors as demonstrating the importance of security of tenure, and by critics of investment arbitration as demonstrating the risk that awards of speculative future profits impose enormous liabilities on developing countries. The methodology of damages in such cases, including the use of discounted cash flow valuation for projects that have never produced, is examined in Chapter 26.

### Transfer and Change of Control

Mining titles are valuable assets that are frequently bought and sold, both directly and through transactions involving the shares of title-holding companies. Most mining codes require ministerial consent to the transfer of titles, and increasingly to changes of control of title holders, in order to ensure that transferees meet the qualification requirements and to enable the state to capture a share of the gains through capital gains taxation. Indirect transfers — the sale of shares in an offshore holding company that indirectly owns a mining title — have been a particular concern, since they may allow substantial gains to escape taxation in the host state. Several states, including Uganda, Tanzania, Mozambique, and India, have introduced legislation to tax indirect transfers, and the issue is examined in Chapter 16. The Platform for Collaboration on Tax (comprising the IMF, OECD, United Nations, and World Bank) published a toolkit on the taxation of offshore indirect transfers in 2020.

## 7.3 Mining Concessions and Investment Agreements

### The Role of Mining Agreements

Many large mining projects, particularly in developing countries, are governed not only by the general mining code but also by a project-specific agreement between the state and the investor — variously called a mining agreement, mineral development agreement, mining convention, or investment agreement. Such agreements may supplement the code by providing details specific to the project, or derogate from it by granting special fiscal terms, stability guarantees, or other incentives. In some states, mining agreements must be ratified by the legislature, giving them the force of law, as in Liberia, Sierra Leone, and Ghana (where the Constitution requires parliamentary ratification of agreements relating to the grant of rights to minerals).

Mining agreements typically address: the project area and title; development obligations and timetable; fiscal terms, including royalty rates, income tax rates, depreciation, and import duty exemptions; state participation; foreign exchange and the right to maintain offshore accounts; employment and local procurement; infrastructure, including access to rail, ports, and power; community development; environmental obligations; stabilisation; and dispute resolution, usually by international arbitration.

### Stabilisation in Mining Agreements

Stabilisation clauses in mining agreements are designed to protect investors from adverse changes in the legal and fiscal regime over the long life of the project. Mining codes themselves may offer stability guarantees: the 2002 Mining Code of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, for example, guaranteed holders of mining rights that the fiscal and customs regime in force at the time of grant would remain unchanged for ten years. Ghana's Minerals and Mining Act 2006 (Act 703) permits the Minister to enter into stability agreements guaranteeing that the holder will not be adversely affected by changes in law for a period not exceeding fifteen years, and development agreements for investments exceeding a specified threshold.

The effectiveness of stabilisation in mining has been sorely tested by the "resource nationalism" of the past two decades. When commodity prices rose dramatically in the 2000s, many states concluded that stabilised fiscal terms negotiated in a period of low prices were inequitable, and sought to renegotiate or override them. The DRC's 2018 Mining Code, which increased the royalty rate for cobalt and other "strategic substances" to ten per cent, introduced a super-profits tax, and reduced the stability period, applied the new provisions to existing title holders notwithstanding the stability guarantee in the 2002 Code, provoking protests from major mining companies. Tanzania's Natural Wealth and Resources (Permanent Sovereignty) Act 2017 and the Natural Wealth and Resources Contracts (Review and Re-negotiation of Unconscionable Terms) Act 2017 required the review of agreements containing "unconscionable" terms, prohibited the submission of disputes relating to natural resources to international arbitration, and asserted the permanent sovereignty of the people of Tanzania over natural resources. In the same year, the Tanzanian government imposed an export ban on gold and copper concentrates and issued tax assessments of approximately USD 190 billion against Acacia Mining, a subsidiary of Barrick Gold. The dispute was resolved through a framework agreement in 2019 under which the government acquired a sixteen per cent free-carried interest in Barrick's Tanzanian mines and a fifty per cent share of economic benefits, and Barrick paid USD 300 million in settlement of the tax claims.

### The Renegotiation of Mining Agreements

The renegotiation of mining agreements raises fundamental questions about the balance between *pacta sunt servanda* and the state's sovereign right to regulate in the public interest. Investors argue that stability is essential to attract long-term investment and that states should be held to their bargains; states argue that agreements concluded under conditions of information asymmetry, weak negotiating capacity, or corruption may lack legitimacy, and that circumstances may change so fundamentally that the original bargain becomes inequitable. Several states have conducted systematic reviews of mining agreements: Liberia reviewed concessions concluded during its civil war; Guinea conducted a review under its 2011 Mining Code, leading to the revocation of the rights of BSG Resources over the Simandou iron ore deposit on the ground that they had been obtained through corruption; and the DRC reviewed sixty-one mining contracts in 2007–2008.

The Guinea case illustrates the relevance of corruption to the validity of mining titles. BSGR commenced ICSID arbitration against Guinea following the revocation of its rights, and Vale, which had paid BSGR USD 500 million for a fifty-one per cent interest in the project, commenced LCIA arbitration against BSGR. In 2019 an LCIA tribunal found that BSGR had obtained its rights through bribery and fraudulently misrepresented the position to Vale, and ordered BSGR to pay Vale approximately USD 2 billion. The English courts subsequently enforced the award. The ICSID proceedings were settled in 2019 through an agreement under which BSGR relinquished its claims to Simandou. The Simandou project, divided into blocks held by Rio Tinto and a consortium including Chinese interests, began exporting iron ore in late 2025, illustrating both the immense value of major mineral deposits and the decades-long delays that legal and political disputes can cause.

### Model Mining Development Agreement

In response to concerns about the imbalance of negotiating capacity in mining agreements, the International Bar Association's Mining Law Committee published a Model Mine Development Agreement (MMDA) in 2011, designed as a template that reflects a balance of the interests of states, investors, and communities. The MMDA includes provisions on community development, environmental and social impact assessment, transparency, and dispute resolution, and is accompanied by commentary explaining the policy choices involved. Other resources include the Intergovernmental Forum on Mining, Minerals, Metals and Sustainable Development (IGF) Mining Policy Framework of 2013, the African Minerals Development Centre's Country Mining Vision guidebook, and the negotiation support initiatives of the African Legal Support Facility, established by the African Development Bank to provide legal advice to African governments in complex commercial negotiations, including mining agreements.

### Mining Investment Disputes and Social Licence

A significant body of investment arbitration concerns the interaction between mining investments and community opposition. In *Bear Creek Mining Corp v Republic of Peru* (2017), the claimant had obtained rights to the Santa Ana silver project near the border with Bolivia, but the government revoked the decree authorising the project following violent protests by local Aymara communities. The tribunal held that the revocation constituted an indirect expropriation under the Canada–Peru free trade agreement, rejecting Peru's argument that the claimant's failure to obtain a "social licence to operate" should defeat or reduce its claim, although the tribunal limited damages to the amounts actually invested.[^bearcreek] A partially dissenting arbitrator, Professor Philippe Sands, would have reduced damages on the ground that the investor had contributed to the social unrest by failing to engage adequately with the affected communities. In *Copper Mesa Mining Corp v Republic of Ecuador* (2016), the tribunal reduced the damages awarded to the investor by thirty per cent on account of contributory fault, because the investor's contractors had used armed security personnel against protesters. In *Eco Oro Minerals Corp v Republic of Colombia* (2021), the tribunal held that Colombia's prohibition of mining in the Santurbán páramo, a high-altitude wetland ecosystem, breached the minimum standard of treatment under the Canada–Colombia FTA, but declined to find that the general environmental exception in the treaty precluded liability, and later awarded only limited damages. In *Gabriel Resources Ltd v Romania* (2024), concerning the Roșia Montană gold project, the tribunal dismissed the investor's claims, finding that the investor had not secured the social licence necessary for the project and that the state's conduct did not breach the treaty.

[^bearcreek]: *Bear Creek Mining Corp v Republic of Peru*, ICSID Case No ARB/14/21, Award (30 November 2017).

These cases illustrate the growing significance of community acceptance in mining investment law and the extent to which arbitral tribunals are prepared to consider the investor's own conduct in assessing liability and damages. They are examined further in Chapters 14 and 21.
