## 21.7 Business and Human Rights Due Diligence

### From Voluntary Codes to the UN Guiding Principles

The question of how international law should address the human rights impacts of business enterprises, which are not generally subjects of international human rights obligations, has been debated since the 1970s. Early efforts included the abortive UN Draft Code of Conduct on Transnational Corporations, negotiated from 1977 to the early 1990s; the **ILO Tripartite Declaration of Principles concerning Multinational Enterprises and Social Policy** (1977); and the **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises** (1976). The **UN Global Compact**, launched in 2000, invited companies to commit to ten principles on human rights, labour, environment, and anti-corruption. In 2003, the UN Sub-Commission on the Promotion and Protection of Human Rights adopted the **Norms on the Responsibilities of Transnational Corporations and Other Business Enterprises with regard to Human Rights**, which would have imposed direct human rights obligations on companies, but the Commission on Human Rights declined to adopt them.

In 2005, the UN Secretary-General appointed Professor John Ruggie as Special Representative on business and human rights. His work, informed by extensive consultations and research — including on the extractive sector, which accounted for the largest share of alleged corporate human rights abuses in his early surveys — led to the **"Protect, Respect and Remedy" Framework** (2008) and the **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights** (UNGPs), unanimously endorsed by the Human Rights Council in Resolution 17/4 in June 2011.

### The Three Pillars

The UNGPs rest on three pillars:

1. **The state duty to protect** against human rights abuse within their territory and/or jurisdiction by third parties, including business enterprises, through appropriate policies, legislation, regulations, and adjudication (Principles 1–10). This includes ensuring policy coherence, including in investment treaties and contracts with investors — a point developed in Ruggie's **Principles for Responsible Contracts** (2011), which address the integration of human rights risk management into state–investor contract negotiations, examined in Chapter 15.
2. **The corporate responsibility to respect** human rights, meaning that business enterprises should avoid infringing on the human rights of others and should address adverse human rights impacts with which they are involved (Principles 11–24). The responsibility exists independently of states' abilities or willingness to fulfil their own obligations and over and above compliance with national laws. It refers, at a minimum, to the rights in the International Bill of Human Rights and the ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work.
3. **Access to remedy** for victims of business-related human rights abuses, through judicial, state-based non-judicial, and non-state-based grievance mechanisms (Principles 25–31), examined in sections 21.6 and 21.8.

### Human Rights Due Diligence

The core of the corporate responsibility to respect is **human rights due diligence** (Principles 17–21). To identify, prevent, mitigate, and account for how they address their adverse human rights impacts, business enterprises should carry out due diligence, which should include:

- **assessing actual and potential human rights impacts**, drawing on internal and independent expertise and involving meaningful consultation with potentially affected groups and other stakeholders;
- **integrating and acting upon the findings**, assigning responsibility within the enterprise and allocating resources;
- **tracking the effectiveness of the response**, based on qualitative and quantitative indicators and feedback from affected stakeholders; and
- **communicating** how impacts are addressed, particularly when concerns are raised by or on behalf of affected stakeholders.

The UNGPs distinguish three modes of involvement in adverse impacts. Where an enterprise **causes** or may cause an impact, it should take the necessary steps to cease or prevent it and provide for remediation. Where it **contributes** or may contribute to an impact, it should cease or prevent its contribution, use its leverage to mitigate any remaining impact, and contribute to remediation. Where an impact is **directly linked** to its operations, products, or services by a business relationship, without the enterprise having caused or contributed to it, the enterprise should use its leverage to prevent or mitigate the impact and consider whether to terminate the relationship, taking into account the severity of the impact and the consequences of termination. Due diligence should be ongoing, recognising that risks change over time, and should be prioritised according to the **severity** of impacts — their scale, scope, and irremediable character.

The UNGPs have been incorporated into the **OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises**, updated in 2011 and again in 2023 as the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct, which extend the due diligence approach to environmental matters, including climate change and biodiversity, and to the use of technology. The OECD has issued sector-specific guidance, including the **Due Diligence Guidance for Meaningful Stakeholder Engagement in the Extractive Sector** (2017) and the Minerals Guidance noted in section 21.5. The IFC Performance Standards, the Equator Principles, and the standards of industry bodies such as ICMM and IPIECA, the global oil and gas industry association for advancing environmental and social performance, also reflect the UNGPs.

### Human Rights Due Diligence in the Extractive Sector

Human rights due diligence in the extractive sector must address a characteristic range of risks: impacts on land and livelihoods, including resettlement; impacts on indigenous peoples; environmental impacts on health, water, and food; the conduct of public and private security forces; labour conditions, including among contractors and suppliers; the treatment of human rights defenders; in-migration and its social effects, including on women and girls; corruption and the misuse of revenues; and operations in conflict-affected areas, where the UNGPs call for **heightened due diligence** (Principle 7 and Principle 23) and where companies risk complicity in gross human rights abuses, which the UNGPs indicate should be treated as a legal compliance issue. The energy transition adds risks in transition mineral supply chains and renewable energy projects, as examined in sections 21.3 and 21.5.

### Mandatory Human Rights Due Diligence

The UNGPs were initially implemented through voluntary measures and **national action plans** on business and human rights, adopted by around thirty states, including, in Africa, Kenya (2019) and Uganda (2021); Nigeria has developed a draft national action plan with the support of the National Human Rights Commission. However, growing recognition of the limits of voluntary approaches led to a trend towards **mandatory human rights due diligence** legislation in home states of multinational enterprises, which forms part of the "smart mix" of measures that the UNGPs recommend (Principle 3).

**Transparency and reporting laws** were the first step. The **California Transparency in Supply Chains Act** (2010), the **UK Modern Slavery Act 2015** (section 54), and the **Australian Modern Slavery Act 2018** require large companies to report on steps taken to address modern slavery in their operations and supply chains, without requiring them to take any particular steps. Section 1502 of the **Dodd-Frank Act** (2010) in the United States requires listed companies to disclose whether they use conflict minerals — tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold — originating in the DRC or adjoining countries, and the due diligence they have undertaken. The **EU Conflict Minerals Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2017/821), applicable since January 2021, requires EU importers of these minerals above specified volume thresholds to conduct supply chain due diligence in accordance with the OECD Guidance.

**General due diligence laws** followed. The **French Duty of Vigilance Law** (Loi relative au devoir de vigilance, 2017) requires large French companies to establish, publish, and effectively implement a **vigilance plan** identifying risks and preventing serious violations of human rights and fundamental freedoms, health and safety, and the environment resulting from their activities and those of companies they control, subcontractors, and suppliers, and provides for injunctions and civil liability. It has been invoked in litigation against TotalEnergies concerning its projects in Uganda and Tanzania and its climate strategy, examined in Chapter 22. The **German Supply Chain Due Diligence Act** (Lieferkettensorgfaltspflichtengesetz), in force since 2023, requires large companies to conduct due diligence in their own operations and with respect to direct suppliers and, on substantiated knowledge, indirect suppliers, enforced by a federal agency through administrative fines, but does not create a new basis for civil liability. The **Norwegian Transparency Act** (2022) requires large enterprises to carry out due diligence in accordance with the OECD Guidelines and to respond to requests for information.

The **EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive** (Directive (EU) 2024/1760), adopted in 2024 and examined in Chapter 8, requires large EU and non-EU companies meeting specified thresholds to conduct risk-based human rights and environmental due diligence across their own operations, those of their subsidiaries, and their "chain of activities", to adopt and put into effect a transition plan for climate change mitigation, and to provide for civil liability for damage caused by intentional or negligent failure to comply with prevention and remediation obligations. In 2025, as part of a broader simplification initiative, the EU postponed the Directive's application and proposed amendments to narrow its scope and requirements, reflecting political contestation over the burden of due diligence obligations. Sector-specific due diligence obligations also apply under the **EU Batteries Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), which requires economic operators placing batteries on the market to conduct supply chain due diligence on cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, and nickel, and the **EU Deforestation Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2023/1115).

### A Binding Treaty?

In 2014, the Human Rights Council adopted **Resolution 26/9**, establishing an **open-ended intergovernmental working group** with the mandate to elaborate an international legally binding instrument to regulate, in international human rights law, the activities of transnational corporations and other business enterprises. The initiative, led by Ecuador and South Africa, was opposed by most Western states, which argued that it would undermine the UNGPs. Successive drafts, beginning with a "zero draft" in 2018, have addressed the prevention of human rights abuses through mandatory due diligence, legal liability, access to remedy, jurisdiction, applicable law, and mutual legal assistance. Negotiations have continued through annual sessions, but significant disagreements remain over the scope of the instrument — whether it should apply to all business enterprises or only those with transnational activities — and over the extent of corporate liability. Whatever its outcome, the process has influenced national and regional legislation.

## 21.8 Judicial and Non-Judicial Remedies

### The Right to a Remedy and its Barriers

The third pillar of the UNGPs provides that, as part of their duty to protect against business-related human rights abuse, states must take appropriate steps to ensure that those affected have access to effective remedy through judicial, administrative, legislative, or other appropriate means (Principle 25). The right to an effective remedy is recognised in human rights treaties, including Article 2(3) of the ICCPR and Article 7 of the African Charter. Remedies may include apologies, restitution, rehabilitation, financial or non-financial compensation, punitive sanctions, and guarantees of non-repetition. In practice, victims of human rights abuses connected with resource development face formidable barriers: weak and under-resourced courts in host states; corruption and political interference; the cost of litigation and the lack of legal aid; difficulties in obtaining evidence held by companies; limitation periods; the separate legal personality of corporate group members; and, in transnational litigation, doctrines such as *forum non conveniens* and the presumption against extraterritoriality.

### Remedies in Host States

The host state is the natural forum for most claims, and Nigerian courts have adjudicated a substantial body of litigation concerning oil pollution, under the law of tort (negligence, nuisance, trespass, and the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*), the Oil Pipelines Act, and constitutional and African Charter rights, as noted in Chapter 17. The decisions in *Gbemre*, noted in section 21.1, and in cases awarding compensation for oil spills illustrate the potential of domestic litigation. Nigerian courts have also, on occasion, awarded substantial damages against oil companies, but the duration of litigation, which may take decades, difficulties of proof, problems of enforcement, and the attribution of spills to sabotage have limited the effectiveness of domestic remedies. The **Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules 2009** liberalised standing and procedure in human rights cases, allowing public interest litigation.

### Regional Human Rights Mechanisms

Regional mechanisms have provided important remedies. The African Commission's decisions in *SERAC* and *Endorois*, the African Court's judgments in *Ogiek*, and the ECOWAS Court's judgment in *SERAP v Nigeria*, noted in section 21.1, illustrate the potential of the African system, although compliance with decisions has been uneven. The Inter-American Court's judgments, including *Saramaka* and *Sarayaku*, have been accompanied by detailed reparations orders, including collective reparations and guarantees of non-repetition, and supervised through compliance proceedings.

### Home-State Litigation: The United States

For three decades, the United States **Alien Tort Statute** of 1789 (ATS), which grants federal district courts jurisdiction over civil actions by aliens for torts committed in violation of the law of nations or a treaty of the United States, was the principal vehicle for transnational human rights litigation against corporations. Following *Filártiga v Peña-Irala* (1980), plaintiffs brought numerous claims against extractive companies for complicity in human rights abuses abroad. In ***Wiwa v Royal Dutch Petroleum Co***, concerning Shell's alleged complicity in the execution of Ken Saro-Wiwa and other Ogoni leaders, Shell settled in 2009 for USD 15.5 million, without admitting liability. In *Doe v Unocal*, concerning forced labour in the construction of the Yadana gas pipeline in Myanmar, the parties settled in 2005.

The Supreme Court progressively restricted the ATS. In ***Kiobel v Royal Dutch Petroleum Co*** (2013), a claim by Ogoni plaintiffs alleging that Shell had aided and abetted human rights abuses by the Nigerian military, the Court held that the **presumption against extraterritoriality** applies to the ATS and that, where all the relevant conduct took place outside the United States, the claims must "touch and concern" the territory of the United States with sufficient force to displace the presumption; mere corporate presence was insufficient. In *Jesner v Arab Bank* (2018), the Court held that foreign corporations may not be defendants in ATS suits, and in *Nestlé USA Inc v Doe* (2021), it held that allegations of general corporate activity, such as decision-making in the United States, were insufficient to establish domestic application. The ATS is now of limited utility for claims against extractive companies concerning conduct abroad.

### Home-State Litigation: The United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Canada

As the ATS route narrowed, claimants turned to the courts of the home states of parent companies, relying on ordinary tort law and the duty of care of parent companies. In the **United Kingdom**, the Supreme Court in ***Lungowe v Vedanta Resources plc*** [2019] UKSC 20, concerning pollution from the Nchanga copper mine in Zambia, and ***Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc*** [2021] UKSC 3, concerning oil pollution in the Niger Delta, held that it was arguable that the parent company owed a duty of care to persons affected by its subsidiary's operations, where the parent had intervened in, controlled, supervised, or advised on the relevant operations, or held itself out as exercising such control, including through group-wide policies, and allowed the claims to proceed. The jurisdictional rules on which these claims rested, and the subsequent litigation, including the *Bodo* settlement of £55 million in 2015, are examined in Chapters 17 and 27. In the litigation arising from the collapse of the Fundão tailings dam in Brazil in 2015, *Município de Mariana v BHP Group*, the Court of Appeal allowed a claim by more than six hundred thousand claimants to proceed in England in 2022, and a liability trial took place in 2024 and 2025, illustrating the scale that such litigation may attain.

In the **Netherlands**, in ***Oguru, Efanga and others v Shell***, the Court of Appeal of The Hague held in January 2021 that Shell's Nigerian subsidiary was liable for oil spills from pipelines near the villages of Oruma and Goi, and that the parent company had breached a duty of care by failing to install a leak detection system on a pipeline; in 2022, Shell agreed to pay €15 million to the affected communities. The litigation is examined in Chapter 17.

::: casebox {title="Nevsun Resources Ltd v Araya: Customary International Law in Domestic Tort Claims"}
**Nevsun Resources Ltd v Araya, 2020 SCC 5 (Supreme Court of Canada, 28 February 2020).**

**Facts.** Three Eritrean workers brought proceedings in British Columbia against Nevsun Resources, a Canadian company that owned, through subsidiaries, a majority interest in the Bisha gold, copper, and zinc mine in Eritrea, in partnership with the Eritrean state mining company. They alleged that they had been conscripted under Eritrea's national service programme and forced to work at the mine in harsh and dangerous conditions, by state-controlled contractors, and that Nevsun was liable for breaches of customary international law prohibitions on forced labour, slavery, cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment, and crimes against humanity, as well as for domestic torts such as battery and unlawful confinement. Nevsun applied to strike the claims, arguing that they were barred by the act of state doctrine and that customary international law could not ground a civil claim against a corporation.

**Judgment.** A majority of the Supreme Court of Canada (five to four on the customary international law issue) dismissed Nevsun's application. It held that the act of state doctrine does not form part of Canadian law, having been subsumed into the principles of conflict of laws and judicial restraint. It further held that customary international law is automatically adopted into Canadian common law in the absence of conflicting legislation, and that it was not "plain and obvious" that the prohibitions on forced labour, slavery, and other norms invoked, some of which are *jus cogens*, could not apply to corporations or ground a civil remedy in Canadian law. The claims were allowed to proceed to trial.

**Subsequent events.** The parties settled the claims later in 2020 on undisclosed terms.

**Significance.** *Nevsun* opened the possibility, in Canada and potentially in other common law jurisdictions, of civil claims against corporations based directly on breaches of customary international law, alongside conventional tort claims. It rejected the act of state doctrine as a bar to claims concerning the conduct of foreign states involved in resource projects and is regarded as a significant development in the law of corporate accountability for human rights abuses abroad. Canada has also established a **Canadian Ombudsperson for Responsible Enterprise** (2019), with a mandate to review complaints concerning Canadian companies in the mining, oil and gas, and garment sectors abroad, although its investigative powers have been criticised as limited.

### Criminal Accountability

Criminal accountability of companies and their officers for complicity in human rights abuses abroad is emerging, though rare. In France, the cement company **Lafarge** was charged with complicity in crimes against humanity and financing terrorism in connection with payments to armed groups, including the Islamic State, to keep a plant operating in Syria; its parent company pleaded guilty in the United States in 2022 to providing material support to terrorist organisations and paid USD 778 million in penalties. In Sweden, executives of **Lundin Oil** (later Lundin Energy) were charged in 2021 with complicity in war crimes in connection with the company's operations in southern Sudan between 1999 and 2003, in one of the longest trials in Swedish history. Such proceedings, which depend on the willingness of prosecutors to pursue complex cases, may have significant deterrent effects.

### Non-Judicial Remedies and Their Interaction with Litigation

Non-judicial mechanisms — the operational-level grievance mechanisms, accountability mechanisms of international financial institutions, National Contact Points, and national human rights institutions examined in section 21.6 — complement judicial remedies. They can provide faster, cheaper, and more flexible solutions, including dialogue-based outcomes and remedies tailored to communities' needs, but they lack binding force and may be used to deflect litigation. The UNGPs envisage a "remedy ecosystem" in which judicial and non-judicial mechanisms work together, and the effectiveness of remedies depends on the interaction among them, the leverage of financial institutions and home states, and the capacity of affected communities and their advocates to use them strategically. The procedural dimensions of transnational litigation, including jurisdiction, *forum non conveniens*, applicable law, and the enforcement of judgments — including the saga of the *Lago Agrio* litigation against Chevron — are examined in Chapter 27.

## Conclusion

Human rights law has become an essential framework for the governance of energy and natural resource development. The obligations of states to respect, protect, and fulfil human rights, articulated in universal and regional instruments and given concrete content by decisions such as *SERAC*, *Endorois*, *Saramaka*, and *Sarayaku*, require states to regulate resource development so as to prevent harm to affected communities, to ensure their participation, and to remedy violations. The rights of indigenous peoples, including the principle of free, prior, and informed consent, have moved from the margins to the centre of resource law, influencing the standards of financial institutions and companies, although the scope of consent remains contested. International standards on land acquisition, resettlement, and compensation, and on labour rights and occupational safety, establish benchmarks against which national laws — including Nigeria's Land Use Act — and project practices may be measured.

The framework of business and human rights has transformed the expectations placed on companies. The UNGPs' articulation of the corporate responsibility to respect and of human rights due diligence has been embedded in the OECD Guidelines, financial institution standards, and industry practice, and is increasingly backed by mandatory legislation in Europe, although the political commitment to such legislation has been tested. Victims of abuses have pursued remedies through grievance mechanisms, regional human rights systems, and transnational litigation, with notable successes in the courts of the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Canada. Yet the gap between norms and practice remains wide, and the energy transition, with its demands for land, minerals, and water, will test whether the lessons of the fossil fuel era have been learned. For legal scholars and practitioners in resource-rich states such as Nigeria, the challenge is to translate these international norms into effective domestic law and institutions that protect the rights of affected people while enabling resource development to contribute to national development.
