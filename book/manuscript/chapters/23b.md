## 23.3 Energy Poverty and Universal Access

### The Scale of the Challenge

Access to modern energy services is a precondition for the realisation of many human rights and for economic and social development. Electricity powers lighting, communications, refrigeration of food and medicines, water pumping, health facilities, schools, and productive enterprises; clean cooking fuels and technologies protect health and save time. Yet, according to the annual *Tracking SDG 7* reports prepared by the International Energy Agency, the International Renewable Energy Agency, the UN Statistics Division, the World Bank, and the World Health Organization, around 666 million people lacked access to electricity in 2023 — a figure that had declined from more than a billion a decade earlier but remained far from the goal of universal access by 2030. Around eighty-five per cent of those without access live in **sub-Saharan Africa**, where population growth has outpaced new connections in many countries. Many more people have connections that are unreliable, of poor quality, or unaffordable, so that the binary measure of "access" understates the extent of energy deprivation.

Access to **clean cooking** is an even greater challenge. Around 2.1 billion people — more than a quarter of the world's population — continue to cook with polluting fuels and technologies, such as firewood, charcoal, crop residues, animal dung, and kerosene, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. The World Health Organization estimates that household air pollution from the use of polluting fuels and technologies for cooking causes around 3.2 million premature deaths each year, predominantly among women and children, from pneumonia, stroke, ischaemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and lung cancer. The collection of fuelwood imposes a heavy time burden, predominantly on women and girls, and contributes to deforestation.

**Nigeria** has the largest electricity access deficit of any country in the world, with an estimated eighty-five to ninety million people — around forty per cent of the population — lacking access to grid electricity, and many of those connected receiving only a few hours of supply each day, examined in Chapter 24. Most Nigerian households cook with firewood or charcoal, and Nigeria is among the countries with the highest burden of deaths attributable to household air pollution. The paradox of energy poverty in a major oil and gas producer, where many households and businesses rely on expensive and polluting petrol and diesel generators, is a central concern of Nigerian energy law and policy.

### Defining and Measuring Energy Poverty

Energy poverty has been defined in different ways. In developing countries, it is commonly understood as the lack of access to modern energy services — electricity and clean cooking — and measured by access rates. The World Bank's **Multi-Tier Framework** for measuring energy access, developed under the Sustainable Energy for All initiative, defines access along a spectrum of tiers from zero to five, based on attributes including capacity, availability (hours of supply), reliability, quality, affordability, legality, health and safety, and convenience — recognising that a solar lantern and a reliable grid connection provide very different levels of service. The **Modern Energy Minimum**, proposed by a coalition of researchers and organisations, argues for a higher threshold of around one thousand kilowatt-hours per person per year, including productive uses, as the minimum consistent with economic development.

In developed countries, energy poverty, or **fuel poverty**, is generally understood as the inability to afford adequate energy services, particularly heating and cooling, at reasonable cost, and measured by indicators such as the share of income spent on energy, the inability to keep the home adequately warm, and arrears on utility bills. The United Kingdom's Warm Homes and Energy Conservation Act 2000 required the government to adopt a strategy to end fuel poverty. In the European Union, the recast **Energy Efficiency Directive** (Directive (EU) 2023/1791) defines energy poverty as a household's lack of access to essential energy services that underpin a decent standard of living and health, including adequate warmth, cooling, lighting, and energy to power appliances, caused by a combination of factors including non-affordability, insufficient disposable income, high energy expenditure, and poor energy efficiency of homes, and requires member states to implement energy efficiency measures as a priority among people affected by energy poverty and vulnerable customers. The **Social Climate Fund** (Regulation (EU) 2023/955) will finance measures to support vulnerable households, micro-enterprises, and transport users affected by the extension of emissions trading to buildings and road transport. The energy price crisis of 2022 brought energy affordability to the forefront of policy throughout Europe, prompting emergency measures including price caps, windfall taxes, and support payments.

### Energy Access as a Human Right?

Whether there is a **human right to energy** or to electricity is debated. No international human rights treaty expressly recognises such a right, with the partial exception of Article 14(2)(h) of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, which requires states to ensure to rural women the right to enjoy adequate living conditions, particularly in relation to housing, sanitation, electricity and water supply, transport, and communications. However, the UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights has recognised that the right to adequate housing includes sustainable access to energy for cooking, heating, and lighting (General Comment No. 4, 1991), and access to energy is instrumental to the realisation of the rights to health, education, food, water, and work. Some national courts have recognised a right to electricity as a component of constitutional rights to life and dignity, notably in India and South Africa, where courts have addressed disconnections of electricity supply; in *Joseph v City of Johannesburg* (2009), the Constitutional Court of South Africa held that tenants were entitled to procedural fairness before the disconnection of electricity supply by the municipality, recognising electricity as a basic municipal service. Scholars have argued for the recognition of energy access as an emerging human right, while others caution that rights-based framing must be accompanied by the resources and institutions to deliver access.

### Instruments for Universal Access

Achieving universal access requires a combination of grid extension, mini-grids, and stand-alone systems, supported by legal and regulatory frameworks, financing, and institutions. The IEA and the World Bank estimate that, in sub-Saharan Africa, the least-cost path to universal access involves a large share of new connections through **decentralised solutions** — mini-grids and solar home systems — particularly in rural areas distant from the grid.

**Rural electrification agencies and funds.** Many countries have established dedicated agencies and funds to finance rural electrification, often supported by levies on electricity consumers or allocations from the national budget. In Nigeria, the **Rural Electrification Agency** (REA) and the **Rural Electrification Fund**, originally established under the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005 and continued under the Electricity Act 2023, promote and finance rural electrification. The REA implements the **Nigeria Electrification Project**, supported by the World Bank and the African Development Bank, which provides grants to private developers of mini-grids and solar home systems, and the **Distributed Access through Renewable Energy Scale-up** (DARES) project, approved by the World Bank in 2023 with USD 750 million in financing, which aims to provide electricity to millions of Nigerians through distributed renewable energy.

**Mini-grid regulation.** Mini-grids — small, isolated electricity networks typically powered by solar photovoltaics with battery storage, serving villages or clusters of customers — require a regulatory framework addressing licensing, tariff-setting, quality standards, and, critically, what happens when the main grid arrives. Nigeria's **NERC Regulations for Mini-Grids**, first issued in 2016, were among the first comprehensive mini-grid regulations in Africa: they distinguish between isolated and interconnected mini-grids, establish a light-touch permitting regime for small mini-grids, allow developers to charge cost-reflective tariffs agreed with communities, and provide options when the grid arrives, including conversion to a distribution franchise or compensation for the developer's assets. Under the Electricity Act 2023, which devolves power to states to regulate electricity markets within their territories, states that have established their own regulators may regulate mini-grids within the state, examined in Chapters 9 and 24. The framework has supported the development of hundreds of mini-grids, although challenges remain in affordability, demand, and the financing of the sector.

**Off-grid solar.** **Solar home systems**, ranging from small lanterns to systems capable of powering lights, phones, televisions, and fans, have been distributed to tens of millions of households in Africa, often through **pay-as-you-go** business models enabled by mobile money, under which customers pay in small instalments. Legal and regulatory issues include product quality standards (such as the *Lighting Global* quality standards), consumer protection in the financing of systems, import duties and value added tax, and electronic waste management.

**Clean cooking.** The promotion of clean cooking involves policies on liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), including subsidies, cylinder recirculation models, and safety regulation; improved biomass cookstoves; electric cooking; biogas; and ethanol. Nigeria's **National LPG Expansion Plan** sought to increase LPG penetration, and the removal of value added tax on domestic LPG was implemented to reduce costs. The IEA's **Summit on Clean Cooking in Africa**, held in Paris in May 2024, mobilised pledges of around USD 2.2 billion and proposed that clean cooking be given greater priority in national policies and international finance. Carbon finance, through the generation of carbon credits from clean cookstove projects, has been a significant source of funding, though concerns about the over-crediting of cookstove projects have prompted methodological reforms, examined in Chapter 18.

**Mission 300.** In 2024, the World Bank Group and the African Development Bank launched **Mission 300**, an initiative to connect 300 million people in Africa to electricity by 2030, through grid extension and distributed renewable energy. At the **Mission 300 Africa Energy Summit** in Dar es Salaam in January 2025, African heads of state adopted the **Dar es Salaam Energy Declaration**, and an initial group of countries, including Nigeria, presented **national energy compacts** setting out policy reforms and investment plans, including commitments on utility reform, cost-reflective tariffs with targeted support for poor consumers, private investment, and regional integration. The compacts illustrate the linkage between international finance and domestic legal reform in the pursuit of universal access.

### Affordability and Subsidies

Access is meaningless if energy is unaffordable. Many governments have sought to make energy affordable through subsidies, either on fuels — such as petrol, diesel, kerosene, and LPG — or on electricity tariffs. However, **fossil fuel subsidies** are often regressive, benefiting wealthier households that consume more energy, and fiscally costly, consuming resources that could be used for health, education, and targeted social protection. The IMF has estimated explicit fossil fuel subsidies globally at more than a trillion dollars in 2022. Nigeria's **petrol subsidy**, which had cost the federal government many trillions of naira annually and was associated with large-scale fraud and smuggling, was removed in May 2023, examined in Chapters 18 and 24. Subsidy reform raises acute questions of energy justice: while removal may free fiscal resources and improve efficiency, it imposes immediate costs on households, including through increases in transport and food prices, and requires accompanying measures — such as targeted cash transfers, investment in public transport, and the expansion of alternatives — to protect the poor. International experience, documented by the IMF and the World Bank, suggests that successful subsidy reform depends on public communication, gradual implementation, and credible compensation measures.

## 23.4 Gender and Social Equity in Energy Governance

### Why Gender Matters in Energy

Energy systems are not gender-neutral. In many developing countries, women and girls bear primary responsibility for collecting fuel and cooking, so that energy poverty imposes disproportionate burdens on them — in time spent collecting firewood (often several hours a day), in exposure to household air pollution, and in risks of violence when collecting fuel in insecure areas. Women's lack of access to electricity constrains their opportunities for education, income-generating activities, and participation in public life. At the same time, women are underrepresented in the energy workforce, especially in technical and leadership roles, and in energy decision-making: the IEA has estimated that women account for only around sixteen per cent of employment in the traditional energy sector, though their share is higher in renewable energy, which IRENA has estimated at around a third. Women's land rights are often weaker than men's, which affects their entitlement to compensation and benefits when land is acquired for energy projects, and large projects may bring increased risks of gender-based violence associated with influxes of workers.

### International Legal Framework

The international legal framework for gender equality in energy includes the **Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women** (CEDAW), whose Article 14 requires states to ensure that rural women participate in and benefit from rural development and enjoy adequate living conditions, including electricity, and whose Committee has addressed energy and climate in **General Recommendation No. 34** (2016) on the rights of rural women and **General Recommendation No. 37** (2018) on the gender-related dimensions of disaster risk reduction in a changing climate; the **Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action** (1995); **SDG 5** on gender equality, whose targets include ensuring women's full and effective participation and equal opportunities for leadership and undertaking reforms to give women equal rights to economic resources, including land; and the **Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa** (Maputo Protocol, 2003), which provides for women's right to a healthy and sustainable environment and to participate in the planning, management, and preservation of the environment and the sustainable use of natural resources (Article 18), and to sustainable development, including access to land (Article 19). Under the UNFCCC, the **Lima Work Programme on Gender** (2014) and the **Gender Action Plan**, enhanced in 2019 and renewed in subsequent years, promote gender-responsive climate policy.

### Regional Instruments: ECOWAS

The **Economic Community of West African States** has been a pioneer in addressing gender in energy. The **ECOWAS Policy for Gender Mainstreaming in Energy Access**, adopted by ECOWAS energy ministers in 2015 and endorsed by heads of state in 2017, developed by the ECOWAS Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency, aims to address barriers to the equal participation of women and men in expanding energy access, with objectives on gender-sensitive policies, women's participation in energy-sector employment and decision-making, and women's entrepreneurship. It was followed by an **ECOWAS Directive on Gender Assessments in Energy Projects**, which requires gender assessments to be conducted for energy projects in member states, making it among the first legally binding regional instruments to require gender assessment in the energy sector. The implementation of these instruments in national law, including in Nigeria, remains a work in progress.

### Gender in National Energy Law and Practice

Gender considerations are increasingly incorporated into national energy policies, project requirements, and the standards of financial institutions. The IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank's Environmental and Social Framework require consideration of gender in environmental and social assessments, resettlement, and stakeholder engagement, and the World Bank has adopted good practice notes on addressing gender-based violence in projects involving major civil works. Energy access programmes increasingly target women as customers and entrepreneurs — for example, through programmes supporting women-led clean energy enterprises and the recruitment of women as sales agents for solar products and clean cookstoves. Nigeria's **National Gender Policy**, revised in 2021, and the Nigeria Energy Transition Plan recognise the gender dimensions of energy, and the Rural Electrification Agency has implemented programmes to promote women's participation in the off-grid sector. The **Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act 2018** of Nigeria addresses the accessibility of public facilities and services, relevant to the design of energy services for people with disabilities.

### Broader Social Equity

Beyond gender, energy justice requires attention to other dimensions of social equity: age, disability, ethnicity, indigeneity, income, geographic location, and migration status. Older people and people with disabilities may be particularly vulnerable to energy poverty and to the health effects of extreme heat and cold; people dependent on electrically powered medical equipment require reliable supply and protection from disconnection; informal settlement residents may be excluded from formal connections; and young people are both disproportionately affected by unemployment in fossil fuel transitions and well placed to participate in new energy industries. **Intersectionality** — the recognition that different dimensions of identity and disadvantage interact — implies that energy law and policy should be informed by disaggregated data and by the participation of diverse groups. Legal instruments include anti-discrimination law, consumer protection rules for vulnerable customers, social tariffs, targeted support, accessibility standards, and requirements for disaggregated monitoring and reporting.
