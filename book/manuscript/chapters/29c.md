## 29.4 Institutional Capacity and Regulatory Independence

### The Rationale for Independent Regulators

The creation of **independent regulatory agencies** was a central feature of energy sector reforms from the 1990s. The rationale for independence is that regulators insulated from short-term political pressure can make credible long-term commitments to investors, apply technical expertise, and protect consumers from both monopolistic exploitation and politically motivated tariff decisions. Independence addresses the **time inconsistency** problem: once investors have sunk capital in immobile assets such as networks and power plants, governments face a temptation to reduce tariffs below cost for political gain, and investors who anticipate this will under-invest unless the government can credibly commit not to do so. An independent regulator, with a statutory mandate and secure tenure, provides such a commitment. The European Union's energy directives require member states to ensure that national regulatory authorities are legally distinct and functionally independent from any other public or private entity, that their staff and management act independently of market interests and do not seek or take instructions from any government or other public or private entity, and that they have separate annual budget allocations and adequate resources, as interpreted by the Court of Justice in ***Commission v Germany*** (2021), which held that Germany had infringed EU law by conferring on the federal government powers to determine tariff methodologies that belonged exclusively to the regulator.

### Dimensions of Independence

Regulatory independence has several dimensions: **legal independence** (a statutory basis, a clear mandate, and protection from direction by ministers on individual decisions); **appointment and tenure** (transparent, merit-based appointment of commissioners for fixed terms, with removal only for cause); **financial independence** (secure funding, often through levies on regulated entities, not dependent on annual political approval); **operational independence** (control over staff, procedures, and decisions); and **independence from regulated entities** (rules on conflicts of interest and post-employment restrictions to prevent capture). Independence is never absolute: regulators operate within the policy framework set by government and legislature, and in many systems ministers retain the power to issue general policy directions, as under Nigeria's Electricity Act and PIA. The appropriate balance between independence and political accountability is a central question of regulatory design.

### Accountability

Independence must be balanced by **accountability**, which legitimises the exercise of regulatory power by unelected bodies. Mechanisms of accountability include: **statutory objectives** that define and limit the regulator's mandate; **transparency** in decision-making, through the publication of decisions, reasons, consultation responses, and analysis; **procedural requirements**, such as consultation and RIA; **parliamentary oversight**, through annual reports, committee hearings, and budget scrutiny; **judicial review and appeals**, examined in Chapter 27; **performance evaluation**, including independent reviews; and **ombudsman** schemes for consumer complaints. The challenge is to design accountability mechanisms that ensure regulators serve the public interest without undermining their independence and capacity to make difficult decisions.

### Institutional Capacity

Independence without **capacity** is ineffective. Regulators require adequate **human resources** — economists, engineers, lawyers, accountants, and environmental and social specialists — with competitive remuneration to recruit and retain skilled staff; adequate **financial resources**; **information** about the regulated industry, including powers to require information from regulated entities and systems to analyse it; and **organisational systems** for planning, decision-making, and enforcement. Capacity constraints are acute in many developing countries, where regulators may lack skilled staff, data, and resources relative to the companies they regulate, creating information asymmetries that undermine effective regulation. Capacity-building through training, twinning arrangements with experienced regulators, regional cooperation (as through the ECOWAS Regional Electricity Regulatory Authority and the African Forum for Utility Regulators), and technical assistance can help address these constraints.

### Regulatory Institutions in Nigeria

Nigeria's energy regulatory architecture has been transformed by recent legislation. In the **electricity** sector, the **Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission**, established by the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005 and continued by the Electricity Act 2023, regulates the national market and, under the 2023 Act, transfers regulatory oversight to state regulators as states establish their own electricity markets, as examined in Chapter 24. In the **petroleum** sector, the **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** replaced the Department of Petroleum Resources and the Petroleum Products Pricing Regulatory Agency with two regulators: the **Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission** (NUPRC) and the **Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority** (NMDPRA), each governed by a board and funded by statutory fees and levies, and separated the commercial functions of NNPC Limited from regulation. The PIA's provisions on appointment, funding, and accountability of the regulators represent significant progress towards independence, though concerns persist about political influence on appointments, the capacity of the new bodies, and the relationship between the regulators, the Minister of Petroleum Resources, and NNPC Limited. The effectiveness of these institutions will be decisive for the success of the PIA.

### Multi-Level and Regional Regulation

Energy regulation increasingly operates at **multiple levels**. In federal systems, the allocation of regulatory competence between federal and state regulators raises questions of coordination and consistency, as in the United States, examined in Chapter 24, and now in Nigeria under the Electricity Act 2023, where the emergence of state regulators — beginning with states such as Enugu, Ekiti, Ondo, Imo, and Oyo — requires mechanisms for coordinating interstate transactions, the national grid, and the protection of investors' rights across jurisdictions. At the regional level, the **ECOWAS Regional Electricity Regulatory Authority** (ERERA) regulates cross-border electricity trade in the West African Power Pool, and the European Union's **ACER** coordinates national regulators. Regional regulation can promote market integration and share scarce expertise, but depends on the willingness of states to cede authority.

## 29.5 Public Consultation and Stakeholder Participation

### Why Consult?

**Public consultation** and **stakeholder participation** in the making of energy laws and regulations serve several purposes. They improve the **quality** of decisions by drawing on the knowledge and expertise of those affected, identifying problems and unintended consequences, and testing evidence and assumptions. They enhance **legitimacy** and public acceptance, by giving affected persons a voice and demonstrating that their views have been considered. They promote **transparency** and accountability, by exposing proposals and their rationale to public scrutiny. And they give effect to **rights** of participation in environmental decision-making, recognised in Principle 10 of the Rio Declaration (1992), the **Aarhus Convention** (1998), and the **Escazú Agreement** (2018), examined in Chapter 21, and to the rights of indigenous peoples to free, prior, and informed consent.

### Principles of Effective Consultation

Effective consultation should be **timely**, taking place when proposals are still at a formative stage and can genuinely be influenced; **inclusive**, reaching all affected groups, including those that are less organised or marginalised, such as host communities, women, rural consumers, and small businesses; **informed**, providing sufficient information about the proposal, its rationale, and its expected impacts to allow intelligent responses; of **adequate duration**, allowing sufficient time for consideration and response; **responsive**, with the decision-maker genuinely considering responses and explaining how they have been taken into account; and **accessible**, using languages, formats, and channels appropriate to the audiences, including public hearings, community meetings, and digital platforms. In English administrative law, the ***Gunning* principles**, endorsed by the UK Supreme Court in ***R (Moseley) v Haringey LBC*** (2014), require that consultation take place when proposals are at a formative stage, that sufficient reasons be given to permit intelligent consideration and response, that adequate time be given for consideration and response, and that the product of consultation be conscientiously taken into account. The consultation on the UK's nuclear power policy was held unlawful in ***R (Greenpeace) v Secretary of State for Trade and Industry*** (2007) because the information provided was inadequate and the process seriously flawed.

### Consultation in Energy Regulation

Energy regulators commonly use formal consultation procedures: publishing **consultation papers** on proposed regulations, tariff methodologies, and market rules; holding **public hearings**, particularly on tariff applications; establishing **stakeholder forums** and advisory panels; and publishing **responses** to consultations explaining how comments were addressed. In Nigeria, NERC holds public hearings on tariff reviews and consults on regulations, and the PIA requires the NUPRC and NMDPRA to consult before making regulations. The quality and influence of consultation vary, and consultations are sometimes criticised as formalities, conducted with short deadlines and limited responsiveness, or dominated by organised industry interests.

### Participation of Host Communities

The participation of **host communities** in decisions about resource projects and legislation affecting them is a recurring theme of this book. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 requires settlors of host community development trusts to conduct needs assessments in consultation with host communities and to involve them in the governance of the trusts, examined in Chapter 24. The effectiveness of community participation depends on the representativeness of community representatives, the inclusion of women and youth, access to information and independent advice, the capacity of communities to engage, and the willingness of companies and governments to share power. Mechanisms for meaningful participation include community consultation protocols, community development agreements, participatory monitoring, and grievance mechanisms.

### Participation in Legislative Processes

At the legislative level, participation takes place through **public hearings** by legislative committees, submissions on bills, and engagement by civil society organisations, industry associations, professional bodies, and academics. The Nigerian National Assembly holds public hearings on major energy bills, including the Petroleum Industry Bill and the Electricity Bill, at which stakeholders present memoranda. The role of **academic expertise** in legislative processes — through evidence to committees, advisory roles, and published research — offers an avenue for doctoral graduates to contribute to reform. The legitimacy and quality of legislation can be enhanced by publishing draft bills for comment before formal introduction, as many jurisdictions do through white papers and exposure drafts.

### Risks of Participation

Participation also carries risks: **capture** of consultation processes by well-resourced interests; **consultation fatigue**; the raising of expectations that cannot be met; the use of consultation to legitimise decisions already made; and the exclusion of marginalised voices. Designing participation to mitigate these risks requires attention to the representation of diffuse interests, for example through consumer advocates and funding for public interest participants, and to the transparency of how input is used.

## 29.6 Policy Evaluation and Implementation

### The Implementation Gap

The gap between the objectives of energy laws and their actual effects — the **implementation gap** — is a pervasive problem, particularly in developing countries. Laws may fail to be implemented because of inadequate institutional capacity, lack of funding, conflicting incentives among implementing agencies, resistance from affected interests, poor design that ignores implementation realities, or lack of political commitment. Implementation research, from Jeffrey Pressman and Aaron Wildavsky's classic study *Implementation* (1973) onwards, has emphasised the difficulty of translating policy into practice through complex chains of actors and decisions, the importance of "street-level" implementers (Michael Lipsky), and the need to design policies with implementation in mind.

### Planning for Implementation

Effective implementation requires planning from the outset of the reform process: identifying the institutions responsible for implementation and ensuring they have the necessary powers, resources, and capacity; developing **implementation plans** with clear milestones, responsibilities, and timelines; adopting the necessary **subsidiary legislation** promptly, since many energy laws depend on regulations for their operation; establishing **transitional arrangements**; communicating the reform to affected parties; and monitoring progress. The implementation of the Petroleum Industry Act, which required the incorporation of NNPC Limited, the establishment of the new regulators, the adoption of numerous regulations, the conversion of licences and leases, and the establishment of host community trusts within specified periods, illustrates the scale of the implementation task for major energy reforms.

### Policy Evaluation

**Policy evaluation** assesses whether laws and policies are achieving their objectives, at what cost, and with what unintended consequences, in order to support accountability, learning, and improvement. Evaluations may be **ex ante** (assessing likely effects before adoption, as in RIA), **interim** (during implementation), or **ex post** (after implementation). They may be **process evaluations**, examining how a policy is being implemented; **outcome evaluations**, examining whether intended outcomes have been achieved; or **impact evaluations**, estimating the causal effect of the policy on outcomes, using experimental or quasi-experimental methods examined in Chapter 28. The OECD's Development Assistance Committee criteria — **relevance**, **coherence**, **effectiveness**, **efficiency**, **impact**, and **sustainability** — provide a widely used framework.

Evaluation of energy laws faces methodological challenges: outcomes such as investment, prices, and emissions are influenced by many factors besides the law, making attribution difficult; data may be unavailable or unreliable; and the effects of laws may take years to materialise. Techniques for addressing these challenges include the development of a **theory of change** or **logic model**, which specifies the causal chain from inputs and activities through outputs to outcomes and impacts, and the identification of indicators for each link; the establishment of **baselines** and **counterfactuals**; and the use of **mixed methods**. Legislatures can institutionalise evaluation through **review clauses**, which require the government or regulator to review the operation of a law after a specified period and report to the legislature, and **sunset clauses**, which provide for a law to expire unless renewed. The European Union's Better Regulation agenda requires evaluations of existing legislation and "fitness checks" of policy areas, and the United Kingdom requires post-implementation reviews of significant regulations.

### Learning from Failure

Evaluation should support **learning**, including from failure. The history of energy reform is rich in instructive failures: the California electricity crisis of 2000–2001, which revealed flaws in market design; the Nigerian power sector privatisation of 2013, whose disappointing results reflected flaws in sequencing, tariffs, and the condition of the assets, as examined in Chapter 24; the retroactive cuts to renewable energy support in Spain, which generated dozens of investment arbitrations, as examined in Chapter 14; and the difficulties of many African community development schemes. A culture of evaluation and learning, in which failures are acknowledged and analysed rather than concealed, is a hallmark of effective governance.
