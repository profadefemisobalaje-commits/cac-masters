## 3.6 Resource Nationalism and Regulatory Reform

### Defining Resource Nationalism

"Resource nationalism" describes the tendency of governments to assert greater control over natural resources and a larger share of the economic benefits they generate. The term is often used pejoratively by investors and commentators to describe measures perceived as opportunistic or confiscatory, but it can also describe legitimate efforts by states to correct inequitable bargains, to capture a fair share of windfall profits, and to promote national development. A more neutral analytical definition treats resource nationalism as a spectrum of state measures designed to increase state control over, or state revenue from, the resource sector, ranging from fiscal adjustments at one end to outright expropriation at the other.

Resource nationalism is cyclical. It tends to intensify during periods of high commodity prices, when the gap between the returns earned by investors and those anticipated at the time of investment widens and governments come under political pressure to capture windfall gains. It tends to recede during periods of low prices, when states compete to attract investment and are more willing to offer favourable terms. This cyclicality reflects the obsolescing bargain described in Chapter 1.

### Forms of Resource Nationalism

Resource nationalism takes a variety of forms, which may be classified by increasing intensity:

1. **Fiscal measures**: increases in royalty rates, income tax rates, or special resource taxes; the introduction of windfall profit taxes or resource rent taxes; restrictions on cost recovery or deductions; and changes to the treatment of transfer pricing.
2. **Local content and beneficiation requirements**: obligations to employ nationals, procure goods and services locally, process minerals domestically before export, or transfer technology. Nigeria's Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act 2010 is a prominent example; Indonesia's ban on the export of unprocessed nickel ore, in force since January 2020, illustrates the beneficiation model.
3. **Export restrictions and domestic supply obligations**: requirements to supply a portion of production to the domestic market, often at below-market prices (domestic market obligations), or restrictions on exports. Section 110 of Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021 establishes a domestic crude oil supply obligation, and the Act also provides for a domestic gas delivery obligation.
4. **Increased state participation**: mandatory carried or paid interests for the state or national resource company in new or existing projects; requirements that a minimum percentage of shares be held by nationals. Tanzania's Natural Wealth and Resources (Permanent Sovereignty) Act 2017 and Natural Wealth and Resources Contracts (Review and Re-Negotiation of Unconscionable Terms) Act 2017 combined participation requirements with a statutory power to renegotiate "unconscionable" terms, and the Mining (Amendment) Act 2017 provided for a non-dilutable free carried interest of not less than sixteen per cent for the government in mining companies.
5. **Contract renegotiation and conversion**: unilateral or coerced renegotiation of contracts, or the mandatory conversion of existing contracts to new forms (for example, from concessions to production sharing contracts or service contracts).
6. **Expropriation and nationalisation**: the outright taking of investor assets, with or without compensation.

### Drivers and Justifications

Several factors drive resource nationalism: high commodity prices and perceived windfall profits; the perception that existing contracts were negotiated on unfair terms, under duress, or as a result of corruption; fiscal pressures on governments; the political salience of resource sovereignty; the desire to build national industrial capacity; and, increasingly, concerns about energy and resource security. Governments frequently justify their measures by reference to permanent sovereignty, the public interest, and the need to correct asymmetries of information and bargaining power at the time of contracting.

### Regulatory Reform: The Nigerian Petroleum Industry Act 2021

Not all reform of resource governance is properly characterised as resource nationalism. Many states have undertaken comprehensive reforms designed to modernise their legal frameworks, improve governance, and attract investment. Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021 (PIA), the product of nearly two decades of legislative effort, is a leading example. The PIA:

- restructured the institutional framework by establishing two regulators — the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission (NUPRC) and the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority (NMDPRA) — and providing for the incorporation of the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation as a limited liability company, Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited (NNPC Ltd), operating on commercial principles;
- replaced the oil prospecting licence and oil mining lease with petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases, with provisions on relinquishment, work commitments, and the conversion of existing licences;
- introduced a new fiscal regime comprising a hydrocarbon tax on upstream crude oil profits, companies income tax, and production-based royalties, with incentives for gas and frontier exploration;
- established host community development trusts;
- created frameworks for environmental remediation and decommissioning funds; and
- established the Frontier Exploration Fund and the Midstream and Downstream Gas Infrastructure Fund.

The PIA illustrates the trend towards replacing discretionary, contract-based governance with a comprehensive statutory framework. Its implementation and the conversion of existing licences to the new regime are examined in Chapters 5 and 24.

## 3.7 Expropriation, Nationalization, and Compensation

### Terminology

"Expropriation" refers to the taking of property by the state. "Nationalisation" usually refers to large-scale takings of entire industries or sectors as part of a programme of economic or social reform. "Confiscation" refers to a taking without compensation. "Indirect expropriation" or "creeping expropriation" refers to measures that do not formally transfer title but substantially deprive the owner of the use, enjoyment, or economic value of the property. The distinction between indirect expropriation and non-compensable regulation is one of the most difficult questions in international investment law and is examined in detail in Chapter 14. This section focuses on the general international law framework, with particular reference to its development through energy disputes.

### Conditions of Lawful Expropriation

Customary international law, as reflected in Resolution 1803 and in the practice of investment treaties, permits a state to expropriate foreign property provided that the expropriation is:

1. for a public purpose;
2. non-discriminatory;
3. carried out in accordance with due process of law; and
4. accompanied by the payment of compensation.

These conditions are reflected, with variations, in virtually every investment treaty. Article 13 of the ECT, for example, prohibits expropriation of investments of investors of another contracting party except where the expropriation is "(a) for a purpose which is in the public interest; (b) not discriminatory; (c) carried out under due process of law; and (d) accompanied by the payment of prompt, adequate and effective compensation".

### The Standard of Compensation

The standard of compensation has been the most contested aspect of the law of expropriation. Three formulations compete.

**The Hull standard** — "prompt, adequate and effective" compensation — requires compensation equivalent to the full market value of the expropriated property, paid without delay and in convertible currency. It was championed by capital-exporting states and is reflected in most modern investment treaties, often with the further specification that compensation shall be equivalent to the fair market value of the investment immediately before the expropriation or before it became publicly known, whichever is earlier.

**The "appropriate compensation" standard** of Resolution 1803 and the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States permits account to be taken of a range of circumstances, potentially justifying compensation below full market value. The *Aminoil* tribunal applied this standard, holding that the "legitimate expectations" of the parties, as reflected in the evolution of their contractual relationship, informed the determination of appropriate compensation, and that compensation should take account of a reasonable rate of return rather than speculative profits.

**Partial or no compensation** has been asserted in the context of large-scale nationalisations forming part of fundamental social and economic reform, on the ground that full compensation would make such reform impossible. This position has not commanded general acceptance as a rule of international law, although lump-sum settlements negotiated between states have frequently provided for compensation at substantially less than full value.

In treaty-based arbitration today, the debate has largely been resolved in favour of the full market value standard for lawful expropriation, because the treaties themselves specify it. The more significant debates now concern valuation methodology, the valuation date, and the distinction between lawful and unlawful expropriation.

### Lawful and Unlawful Expropriation

Following *Chorzów Factory*, tribunals distinguish between lawful expropriation, for which compensation is due under the treaty standard (typically fair market value at the date of taking), and unlawful expropriation, for which full reparation is due under customary international law. The distinction matters because full reparation may require valuation at the date of the award rather than the date of taking, allowing the claimant to benefit from subsequent increases in value — for example, increases in oil prices.

In *ADC Affiliate Ltd v Hungary* (2006), the tribunal held that because Hungary's expropriation was unlawful, the claimants were entitled to the value of their investment at the date of the award, which was considerably higher than at the date of the taking.[^adc] In *Yukos Universal Ltd v Russian Federation* (2014), the tribunal found that Russia's measures against Yukos amounted to an unlawful expropriation and awarded damages of approximately USD 50 billion to the former majority shareholders, using a valuation date chosen to give the claimants the benefit of the higher value.[^yukos] In *ConocoPhillips v Venezuela* (2019), the tribunal, having previously held that Venezuela's taking of ConocoPhillips' interests in heavy oil projects was unlawful because Venezuela had failed to negotiate compensation in good faith, awarded approximately USD 8.5 billion plus interest.[^conoco] By contrast, in *Venezuela Holdings BV (Mobil) v Venezuela* (2014), the tribunal found that the claimants had not established that the expropriation was unlawful merely because the compensation offered was inadequate, and accordingly applied the treaty standard of compensation for lawful expropriation, valuing the investment as of the date of the taking.

[^adc]: *ADC Affiliate Limited and ADC & ADMC Management Limited v Republic of Hungary*, ICSID Case No ARB/03/16, Award, 2 October 2006, paras 493–499.

[^yukos]: *Yukos Universal Limited (Isle of Man) v Russian Federation*, PCA Case No AA 227, Final Award, 18 July 2014.

[^conoco]: *ConocoPhillips Petrozuata BV, ConocoPhillips Hamaca BV and ConocoPhillips Gulf of Paria BV v Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela*, ICSID Case No ARB/07/30, Award, 8 March 2019.

The Yukos awards were set aside by the District Court of The Hague in 2016 on jurisdictional grounds relating to the provisional application of the ECT, reinstated by the Hague Court of Appeal in 2020, and subjected to further proceedings, with the Supreme Court of the Netherlands in 2021 remitting the question of alleged fraud in the arbitration to the Amsterdam Court of Appeal, which rejected Russia's fraud arguments in 2024. The saga illustrates the complexity and duration of enforcement proceedings against a sovereign that resists payment.

### Compensation for Natural Resource Projects: Valuation Issues

The valuation of expropriated natural resource projects presents distinctive difficulties. The value of a petroleum or mining project depends on the volume of reserves, future commodity prices, production costs, fiscal terms, and the risk profile of the host state, each of which is uncertain. Tribunals have generally accepted the discounted cash flow (DCF) method for producing assets with an established record of profitability, while being sceptical of DCF valuations for projects at an early stage of development. In *Tethyan Copper Company Pty Limited v Pakistan* (2019), the tribunal awarded approximately USD 4 billion in damages and interest for the denial of a mining lease for the Reko Diq copper-gold deposit, valuing the project by DCF despite the fact that it had not yet entered production — a decision that attracted criticism and was followed by a negotiated settlement in 2022 involving the reconstitution of the project with new participation arrangements.[^tethyan]

[^tethyan]: *Tethyan Copper Company Pty Limited v Islamic Republic of Pakistan*, ICSID Case No ARB/12/1, Award, 12 July 2019.

## 3.8 Comparative Constitutional Approaches

### A Typology of Constitutional Approaches

Constitutions address natural resources in strikingly different ways. A comparative survey reveals at least five approaches:

1. **Silence.** Some constitutions, including the Constitution of the United States, do not address natural resource ownership at all, leaving the matter to property law and legislation.
2. **Vesting clauses.** Many constitutions vest ownership of minerals and petroleum in the state, the nation, or the people (Nigeria, Mexico, Ghana).
3. **Federal allocation clauses.** Federal constitutions allocate authority between levels of government (Canada's sections 92A and 109; Nigeria's Exclusive Legislative List and derivation clause).
4. **Stewardship and sustainability clauses.** Some constitutions impose duties of sustainable management. Article 112 of the Constitution of Norway (formerly Article 110b) provides that natural resources shall be managed on the basis of comprehensive long-term considerations that will safeguard this right for future generations as well. The Supreme Court of Norway in *Greenpeace Nordic and Nature and Youth v Ministry of Petroleum and Energy* (2020) held that Article 112 is a substantive right but that the threshold for judicial intervention in parliamentary decisions is high, and it declined to invalidate Arctic petroleum licences; the subsequent *Greenpeace Nordic v Norway* proceedings before the Oslo District Court in 2024 held certain field development approvals invalid for failure to assess combustion emissions.
5. **Rights of nature.** The Constitution of Ecuador 2008 (Articles 71–74) recognises the rights of nature (*Pacha Mama*) to exist, persist, maintain, and regenerate its vital cycles. The Constitutional Court of Ecuador in the *Los Cedros* case (2021) held that mining concessions in the Los Cedros protected forest violated the rights of nature.

### Revenue-Sharing Clauses

Constitutions in resource-rich federations frequently prescribe revenue-sharing arrangements. Nigeria's thirteen per cent derivation principle has been noted. The Constitution of Iraq 2005, Article 112, provides that the federal government, with the producing governorates and regional governments, shall undertake the management of oil and gas extracted from present fields, with revenues distributed in a fair manner in proportion to the population distribution — a provision whose ambiguity has fuelled protracted disputes between Baghdad and the Kurdistan Regional Government, including the 2022 ruling of Iraq's Federal Supreme Court declaring the Kurdistan Region's oil and gas law unconstitutional, and the 2023 ICC arbitral award in Iraq's favour concerning exports through the Iraq–Türkiye pipeline. The Constitution of Kenya 2010 requires legislation providing for the sharing of benefits from natural resources between the national government, counties, and local communities, implemented in part by section 58 of the Petroleum Act 2019, which allocates 20 per cent of the national government's share of profit petroleum to the county government and 5 per cent to the local community.

### Lessons from Comparison

Comparative analysis suggests several lessons. First, the formal ownership model matters less than the quality of the institutions that administer it; state ownership can be associated with excellent governance (Norway) or with chronic mismanagement. Second, revenue-sharing arrangements that leave producing regions and communities with a visible and meaningful share of benefits tend to reduce conflict, but must be designed to avoid creating new inequalities or local governance failures. Third, constitutional entrenchment of sustainability and stewardship duties can provide a basis for judicial review of resource decisions, although courts have generally been cautious about intervening in what they regard as policy matters. Fourth, the recognition of indigenous and community rights through constitutional, judicial, and treaty mechanisms has become a defining feature of resource governance, with profound implications for project development.

::: casebox
**Government of the State of Kuwait v American Independent Oil Company (Aminoil), 1982**

*Facts.* Aminoil held a sixty-year concession granted in 1948 by the Ruler of Kuwait over the Kuwaiti share of the Neutral Zone. The concession contained stabilisation clauses providing that the Ruler would not annul the agreement by general or special legislation or administrative measures, and that no alteration should be made except by mutual agreement. Over the years, the fiscal terms were revised several times. In 1977, Kuwait terminated the concession by decree and took over Aminoil's assets.

*Holding.* The tribunal (Professor Paul Reuter, President; Professor Hamed Sultan; Sir Gerald Fitzmaurice) held that the nationalisation was lawful. The stabilisation clauses did not prohibit nationalisation because such a far-reaching limitation on sovereignty would require express stipulation and would have to cover a relatively limited period; moreover, the contract had evolved through renegotiation. However, the stabilisation clauses gave rise to legitimate expectations relevant to the assessment of compensation, which was to be "appropriate" and was calculated by reference to the value of the undertaking and a reasonable rate of return. Kuwait was ordered to pay approximately USD 179 million, including interest.

*Significance.* *Aminoil* is the leading authority for the proposition that stabilisation clauses operate primarily to affect compensation rather than to invalidate sovereign acts. Its emphasis on the evolving nature of long-term contracts and on legitimate expectations anticipated modern debates over fair and equitable treatment.

::: summary
- Resource sovereignty is an incident of territorial sovereignty, extended by UNCLOS to sovereign rights over the resources of the EEZ and continental shelf; modern instruments increasingly locate that sovereignty in the people, with the state as trustee.
- Subsurface ownership follows three broad models — regalian (state), accession (landowner), and claim-based systems — with major consequences for licensing, revenue distribution, and community attitudes.
- The public trust doctrine and related stewardship concepts impose fiduciary-type duties on states, enforceable in some jurisdictions by citizens and capable of affecting the security of investor rights.
- Federal systems allocate resource authority in widely differing ways; Nigeria's combination of federal ownership, a thirteen per cent derivation floor, the onshore/offshore dichotomy litigation, and recent electricity decentralisation illustrates the political sensitivity of the federal dilemma.
- Indigenous rights to land, consultation, consent, and benefit-sharing have been recognised through constitutional provisions, judicial decisions, and regional human rights jurisprudence, transforming the legal environment for resource projects.
- Resource nationalism is cyclical and takes forms ranging from fiscal adjustments to expropriation; it must be distinguished from good-faith regulatory reform such as Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021.
- Lawful expropriation requires public purpose, non-discrimination, due process, and compensation; the distinction between lawful and unlawful expropriation determines the measure and valuation date of compensation, with very large financial consequences in energy cases.
:::
