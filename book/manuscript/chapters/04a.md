## Introduction

Institutions matter. The rules of international energy law described in the previous chapters do not apply themselves; they are negotiated, interpreted, monitored, and enforced by institutions — international organisations, regional bodies, national regulators, courts, and tribunals. The design of these institutions shapes the content and effectiveness of the law. An institution with a narrow mandate, limited membership, and no binding powers will produce different outcomes from one with broad competence, universal membership, and the authority to adopt binding decisions.

Global energy governance is distinctive for the absence of a central institution. Unlike trade, which is governed by the WTO, or international civil aviation, which is governed by the International Civil Aviation Organization, energy has no single global organisation with comprehensive competence. Instead, governance is distributed among a constellation of institutions with specialised mandates, overlapping memberships, and divergent interests. This chapter maps that constellation, examines the principal institutions in detail, and considers the role of national regulators and the prospects for reform.

## 4.1 Global Energy Governance Architecture

### The Concept of Global Energy Governance

"Global energy governance" refers to the collective efforts of states and non-state actors to manage energy-related problems that transcend national borders through rules, institutions, and processes. The problems that global energy governance addresses include the security of supply for importing states and security of demand for exporting states, the stability of energy prices, the prevention of nuclear proliferation, the mitigation of climate change, the promotion of energy access in developing countries, the governance of transboundary infrastructure, and the protection of foreign investment.

Scholars have characterised the architecture of global energy governance as a "regime complex" — a set of partially overlapping and non-hierarchical institutions governing a particular issue area. The concept, developed by Kal Raustiala, David Victor, Robert Keohane, and others, captures the absence of a single overarching regime and the presence of multiple institutions whose rules and activities interact, sometimes reinforcing and sometimes conflicting with one another.

### Why Is Energy Governance Fragmented?

Several factors explain the fragmentation of global energy governance.

**Divergent interests.** Producers and consumers have fundamentally opposed interests regarding prices: producers seek high and stable prices, consumers seek low and stable prices. These opposing interests have produced separate institutions — OPEC for producers, the IEA for consumers — rather than a single inclusive organisation.

**Sovereignty sensitivities.** Energy resources are closely tied to national sovereignty and security. States have been reluctant to cede authority over energy policy to international institutions, preferring arrangements that preserve national discretion.

**Path dependence.** Energy institutions were created in response to specific crises — the nuclear arms race (IAEA), the oil shocks of the 1970s (IEA), the collapse of the Soviet Union (Energy Charter), the climate crisis (UNFCCC, IRENA) — and each bears the imprint of the circumstances of its creation.

**Technological diversity.** Different energy sources raise different governance challenges: nuclear energy raises proliferation and safety concerns; oil raises supply security and price stability concerns; renewables raise technology diffusion and investment concerns. Specialised institutions have emerged for each.

**Shifting centres of gravity.** The growth of energy demand in emerging economies, particularly China and India, has shifted the centre of gravity of global energy markets away from the OECD states that dominated the institutions created in the twentieth century. Existing institutions have struggled to accommodate these new actors.

### Informal Governance: G7, G20, and Clubs

In addition to formal international organisations, informal groupings play a significant role in energy governance. The Group of Seven (G7) and the Group of Twenty (G20) have repeatedly addressed energy security, fossil fuel subsidies, and clean energy transitions. At the G20 summit in Pittsburgh in 2009, leaders committed to "rationalize and phase out over the medium term inefficient fossil fuel subsidies that encourage wasteful consumption". The G7's Climate Club, launched in 2022–2023, seeks to accelerate the decarbonisation of industry. The Clean Energy Ministerial and Mission Innovation bring together energy ministers to promote clean energy technologies. Just Energy Transition Partnerships (JETPs), concluded between groups of donor states and South Africa (2021), Indonesia (2022), Vietnam (2022), and Senegal (2023), combine public and private finance to support the transition away from coal. Such informal arrangements lack legal personality and binding powers but can mobilise political commitment and finance.

## 4.2 The International Energy Agency and Its Institutional Role

### Origins and Legal Foundation

The International Energy Agency was created in November 1974 in response to the oil embargo imposed by Arab members of OPEC in October 1973 following the Yom Kippur War and the associated quadrupling of oil prices. The embargo exposed the vulnerability of industrialised oil-importing states and the absence of any mechanism for coordinating their response. At the initiative of the United States, sixteen OECD states signed the Agreement on an International Energy Program (IEP Agreement) on 18 November 1974, establishing the IEA as an autonomous agency within the framework of the OECD.

The IEP Agreement is a treaty. Membership of the IEA is open only to members of the OECD that are willing and able to meet the requirements of the emergency response mechanism, including the holding of oil stocks. This requirement has been a significant barrier to the membership of major emerging economies such as China and India, which are not OECD members. To address this limitation, the IEA launched an "association" initiative in 2015, under which China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa, and other states have become association countries participating in IEA work without assuming the obligations of full membership.

### Emergency Response Mechanism

The core legal obligation imposed by the IEP Agreement is the maintenance of emergency oil reserves. Article 2 requires each participating country to maintain emergency reserves sufficient to sustain consumption for at least sixty days with no net oil imports, a requirement subsequently increased to ninety days of net imports. The reserves may be held by governments (as in the United States Strategic Petroleum Reserve), by dedicated stockholding agencies, or by industry under obligation.

The IEP Agreement also established an emergency sharing system, triggered when the group or an individual participating country sustains or can reasonably be expected to sustain a reduction in the daily rate of its oil supplies of at least seven per cent of the average daily rate of its final consumption during a base period. Upon activation, participating countries are required to implement demand restraint measures and to share available oil. In practice, the formal emergency sharing system has never been activated. Instead, the IEA has relied on "coordinated emergency response measures" (CERM), a more flexible mechanism under which members make stocks available in coordinated releases. Coordinated stock releases were undertaken in 1991 (Gulf War), 2005 (Hurricanes Katrina and Rita), 2011 (Libyan civil war), and twice in 2022 (following the Russian invasion of Ukraine), the latter being the largest in the Agency's history.

### Evolution of the IEA's Mandate

Although established principally to address oil supply security, the IEA's mandate has broadened considerably. Its *World Energy Outlook*, published annually, is among the most influential sources of energy data and scenarios. In 2021, the IEA published *Net Zero by 2050: A Roadmap for the Global Energy Sector*, which concluded that achieving net zero emissions by 2050 would require no new oil and gas fields approved for development beyond those already committed as of 2021, and no new coal mines or mine extensions. The report was controversial among producers and some IEA members, illustrating the tensions between the Agency's historical mission of supply security and its newer role in promoting clean energy transitions. In 2022, IEA ministers mandated the Agency to help countries achieve net zero emissions, and the Agency has developed significant work programmes on critical minerals security, including the Critical Minerals Security Programme.

### The IEA and Critical Minerals Security

The IEA's recent expansion into critical minerals illustrates how institutions created for one security problem adapt to another. The Agency's 2021 special report, *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions*, and its subsequent annual *Global Critical Minerals Outlook* documented the high geographical concentration of the extraction and, especially, the processing of minerals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, and rare earth elements, and warned that supply disruptions could slow the energy transition and raise its cost. In 2024, IEA members launched the Critical Minerals Security Programme, which includes work on supply diversification, market transparency, and the feasibility of stockholding arrangements for critical minerals analogous in some respects to emergency oil stocks.

The analogy with oil is, however, imperfect. Oil is a fungible commodity traded on deep and liquid markets, whereas many critical minerals are traded in thin, opaque markets with product specifications tailored to individual buyers; stockholding is therefore more complex and potentially more expensive. Moreover, the security risks associated with critical minerals arise less from sudden supply interruptions than from structural concentration and the use of export controls as instruments of industrial and geopolitical strategy. Whether the IEA's legal toolkit, built around the IEP Agreement's oil-specific obligations, can be extended to minerals without treaty amendment is a significant institutional question. Chapter 8 examines the substantive law of critical minerals.

### Legal Assessment

The IEA is a hybrid institution. Its emergency response obligations are legally binding treaty commitments, but its broader influence derives from its analytical authority, convening power, and technical expertise rather than from legal compulsion. Its limited membership constrains its legitimacy as a global institution, and its identification with consuming states limits its acceptance by producers. Nevertheless, it remains the most important institution for consumer-country cooperation and an indispensable source of the data on which energy policy and legal analysis depend.

## 4.3 OPEC and Petroleum Market Coordination

### Origins and Legal Foundation

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries was founded at the Baghdad Conference of 10–14 September 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela. Its Statute, adopted in 1961 and subsequently amended, defines its principal aims as "the co-ordination and unification of the petroleum policies of Member Countries and the determination of the best means for safeguarding their interests, individually and collectively" and the devising of "ways and means of ensuring the stabilization of prices in international oil markets with a view to eliminating harmful and unnecessary fluctuations" (Article 2). OPEC is headquartered in Vienna. Its membership has varied over time; Nigeria joined in 1971. Qatar left in 2019, Ecuador in 2020, and Angola in 2024.

The supreme authority of OPEC is the Conference, composed of delegations of the member countries, which meets at least twice a year and takes decisions by unanimity of full members (Article 11). The Board of Governors directs the management of the Organization, and the Secretariat, headed by the Secretary General, carries out executive functions.

### Production Quotas and Market Management

OPEC's most visible function is the setting of production ceilings for its members. The system of production quotas was formally introduced in 1982 in response to falling prices and has been the principal instrument of OPEC market management since then. Quota decisions are adopted by the Conference and are politically binding on members, but the Statute provides no mechanism for enforcing compliance, and over-production by members has been a recurring feature of OPEC history.

Since December 2016, OPEC members have coordinated production with ten non-OPEC producers, including the Russian Federation, Kazakhstan, Mexico, and Oman, under the Declaration of Cooperation, in an arrangement known as "OPEC+". In July 2019, the participants adopted the Charter of Cooperation, providing a framework for continued cooperation. OPEC+ has implemented significant production cuts, including an unprecedented cut of 9.7 million barrels per day agreed in April 2020 in response to the collapse in demand caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.

### Quota Allocation and Baseline Disputes

The allocation of production quotas among members is among the most contentious aspects of OPEC's work, because the "baseline" from which each member's reductions are calculated determines its share of the collective market. Disputes over baselines have repeatedly strained the organisation. In 2023, OPEC+ participants agreed that the maximum sustainable production capacities of several members would be assessed by independent sources for the purpose of setting baselines for 2025, reflecting disagreement over whether some African producers, which had been persistently unable to produce up to their quotas, should have their baselines reduced. Angola announced its withdrawal from OPEC in December 2023, citing disagreement with its assigned production level.

For Nigeria, the quota system interacts with domestic production constraints. For extended periods in the early 2020s, Nigeria produced well below its OPEC allocation as a result of crude oil theft, pipeline vandalism, underinvestment, and the divestment of onshore assets by international oil companies. These constraints meant that the binding limit on Nigerian production was operational rather than legal, and they focused domestic policy attention on the security of pipeline infrastructure and the regulatory framework for onshore asset transfers under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021. The episode demonstrates that the legal significance of an international institution's decisions depends on the material circumstances of its members.

### The Legal Character of OPEC Decisions

The legal character of OPEC Conference resolutions has never been authoritatively determined. They are adopted by the organ of an international organisation established by treaty, and in that sense they are acts of international law. Yet the Statute does not expressly confer on the Conference the power to adopt decisions binding on members regarding production, and members have consistently treated quota decisions as political commitments rather than legal obligations enforceable by sanctions. The OPEC+ Declaration of Cooperation and Charter of Cooperation are, by their terms and the practice of the participants, non-binding instruments. The absence of legal enforcement is consistent with the general preference of states for flexibility in matters touching resource sovereignty, and it explains why OPEC's effectiveness has historically depended on the willingness of one or more members — usually Saudi Arabia — to act as "swing producer" by adjusting its own output to stabilise the market.

### Legal Issues Surrounding OPEC

OPEC's activities raise several legal questions. The first concerns competition law. OPEC's coordination of production would, if carried out by private companies, constitute a cartel prohibited by the competition laws of most states. Private plaintiffs in the United States have sought to sue OPEC and its members under the Sherman Act. In *International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers v OPEC* (1981), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal of such a suit on the basis of the act of state doctrine.[^iam] Legislation known as the "No Oil Producing and Exporting Cartels Act" (NOPEC), which would remove sovereign immunity and the act of state defence for such claims, has been introduced repeatedly in the United States Congress but has not been enacted.

[^iam]: *International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers v Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries*, 649 F 2d 1354 (9th Cir 1981).

The second concerns trade law. Production quotas are, in effect, restrictions on the production and therefore the export of oil, raising questions of compatibility with Article XI of the GATT, which prohibits quantitative restrictions on exports. Most OPEC members are members of the WTO, but no WTO challenge to OPEC production quotas has been brought. Arguments that quotas could be justified under Article XX(g) as measures relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources, or that production restrictions differ legally from export restrictions, have been advanced but not tested.

The third concerns the relationship between OPEC decisions and contractual obligations. Production quotas may require the restriction of production by joint ventures involving foreign companies, raising questions about whether quota compliance constitutes *force majeure* or a change in law under production sharing contracts and joint operating agreements. Many Nigerian production sharing contracts and joint venture arrangements contain provisions allocating the burden of OPEC-mandated production restrictions.

### OPEC and the Energy Transition

OPEC faces a fundamental strategic challenge in the energy transition. If global oil demand peaks and declines, the value of members' reserves will fall, and competition for market share may intensify. OPEC has consistently argued that oil will remain a significant part of the global energy mix for decades and has warned against underinvestment in oil supply. Within the UNFCCC process, OPEC members — particularly Saudi Arabia — have often resisted language calling for a phase-out of fossil fuels, and the formulation adopted at COP28, calling for "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems", reflected a compromise in which producer-state resistance played a central role.
