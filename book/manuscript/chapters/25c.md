## 25.5 Resource Conflicts and International Humanitarian Law

### Natural Resources and Armed Conflict

Natural resources are implicated in armed conflict in several ways. They may be a **cause** of conflict, when groups compete for control of valuable resources or when grievances over the distribution of resource revenues and environmental harm fuel rebellion, as in the Niger Delta. They may **finance** conflict, when armed groups exploit "lootable" resources such as alluvial diamonds, gold, timber, and coltan, or extract revenues from oil through theft and illicit trade, as in Sierra Leone, Liberia, Angola, the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, and the territories controlled by the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria. They may be **targets** of military operations, when energy infrastructure is attacked to weaken an adversary's military capacity or civilian morale, as in the Russian attacks on Ukraine's electricity system since 2022. And they may be **victims** of conflict, when the environment is damaged by warfare, as in the burning of Kuwait's oil wells by Iraqi forces in 1991. The United Nations Environment Programme has estimated that at least forty per cent of intrastate conflicts in the past sixty years have had a link to natural resources. International law addresses these dimensions through international humanitarian law, international criminal law, Security Council measures, and supply chain regulation.

### Pillage and the Exploitation of Resources in Occupied Territory

International humanitarian law prohibits **pillage** — the appropriation of property for private or personal use without the consent of the owner — in all circumstances. The prohibition appears in the **Hague Regulations** of 1907 (Articles 28 and 47), the **Fourth Geneva Convention** of 1949 (Article 33), and **Additional Protocol II** (Article 4(2)(g)), and is a war crime under the **Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court** (Article 8(2)(b)(xvi) and (e)(v)). After the Second World War, the Nuremberg Military Tribunals convicted German industrialists for the plunder of resources in occupied territories, including in the *Krupp* and *Flick* cases, and the Dutch courts held in the case of the *Singapore Oil Stocks* (1956) that oil resources seized by Japan during the occupation of the Dutch East Indies had been unlawfully appropriated. The **exploitation of natural resources in occupied territory** is governed by **Article 55** of the Hague Regulations, under which the occupying state is to be regarded only as **administrator and usufructuary** of public buildings, real estate, forests, and agricultural estates belonging to the hostile state, and must safeguard the capital of these properties. The usufruct principle is generally interpreted as permitting the occupant to continue existing levels of exploitation, such as production from existing oil wells, for the benefit of the occupied territory and the needs of the occupation, but not to open new mines or wells or exploit resources beyond the existing rate.

In ***Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo*** (*Democratic Republic of the Congo v Uganda*, 2005), the International Court of Justice held that Uganda was internationally responsible for acts of looting, plundering, and exploitation of the DRC's natural resources committed by members of the Ugandan armed forces in the territory of the DRC, and for failing, as an occupying power in the Ituri district, to prevent such acts. In its **reparations judgment of February 2022**, the Court awarded the DRC a total of USD 325 million, including USD 60 million for damage related to natural resources, determined as a global sum in the light of the evidence of the exploitation of gold, diamonds, coltan, and timber. The judgment illustrates both the availability of state responsibility for resource exploitation in armed conflict and the evidentiary difficulties of quantifying it. Questions concerning the exploitation of resources in occupied territory have also arisen in relation to Western Sahara, examined in Chapter 21, the occupied Palestinian territories, and the territories of Ukraine occupied by Russia, including Crimea, where Ukrainian companies have obtained investment treaty awards against Russia for the expropriation of their energy assets, including in *Naftogaz v Russia* (2023), in which a tribunal under the Ukraine–Russia BIT awarded around USD 5 billion.

### Corporate Complicity in Resource-Related Conflict

International criminal law extends to individuals, including corporate executives, who aid and abet war crimes. The trial of two former executives of **Lundin Oil** (later Lundin Energy) before the Stockholm District Court, which began in 2023, concerning alleged complicity in war crimes committed by the Sudanese government and allied militias in clearing populations from the company's oil concession area in southern Sudan between 1999 and 2003, is the first prosecution of corporate executives for complicity in war crimes since the post-Second World War trials, and illustrates the potential criminal liability of companies operating in conflict zones, as examined in Chapter 22. The **OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas**, examined in Chapter 8, and the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation provide frameworks for corporate due diligence to avoid contributing to conflict through mineral sourcing.

### Security Council Measures and Certification Schemes

The Security Council has adopted measures to sever the link between natural resources and conflict. It imposed embargoes on diamonds from areas controlled by UNITA in Angola (Resolution 1173 (1998)), on rough diamonds from Sierra Leone not certified by the government (Resolution 1306 (2000)), and on diamonds and timber from Liberia. It has established groups of experts to investigate the illegal exploitation of natural resources in the DRC, whose reports named companies and individuals involved in the trade. The concern over **"conflict diamonds"** led to the **Kimberley Process Certification Scheme**, launched in 2003 with the support of the General Assembly, under which participating states undertake to trade in rough diamonds only with other participants and to accompany shipments with certificates guaranteeing that they are conflict-free. The scheme has been credited with reducing the trade in conflict diamonds, but criticised for its narrow definition of conflict diamonds (confined to diamonds used by rebel movements to finance wars against legitimate governments), which excludes human rights abuses by state forces, as in the Marange fields of Zimbabwe, and for its consensus-based decision-making, which has blocked reform. In relation to oil, the Security Council authorised measures against vessels carrying illicitly exported crude oil from Libya (Resolution 2146 (2014)) and condemned trade with the Islamic State (Resolution 2199 (2015)), which had derived substantial revenues from oil fields under its control.

### Protection of Energy Infrastructure in Armed Conflict

The protection of energy infrastructure in armed conflict is governed by the fundamental principles of international humanitarian law: **distinction**, **proportionality**, and **precaution**. Under **Additional Protocol I** (1977), attacks may be directed only against **military objectives**, defined in Article 52(2) as objects which by their nature, location, purpose, or use make an effective contribution to military action and whose total or partial destruction, capture, or neutralisation, in the circumstances ruling at the time, offers a definite military advantage. Civilian objects may not be the object of attack. Energy infrastructure poses particular difficulties because it is typically **dual-use**: electricity grids, fuel depots, and refineries serve both civilian populations and military forces. Some military manuals and commentators have treated electricity generation and distribution facilities as military objectives when they supply military consumers, while others insist that the contribution to military action must be effective and the military advantage definite, and that the destruction of entire grids to coerce civilian populations cannot be justified. Even where a facility qualifies as a military objective, an attack is prohibited if it may be expected to cause incidental civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated (Article 51(5)(b)), and attackers must take feasible precautions to minimise civilian harm (Article 57). The **reverberating effects** of attacks on energy infrastructure — on water supply, sanitation, hospitals, and heating — must be considered in the proportionality assessment.

Additional protections apply to specific categories. **Article 54** of Additional Protocol I prohibits attacking, destroying, removing, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population, such as drinking water installations and supplies and irrigation works. **Article 56** provides special protection for **works and installations containing dangerous forces** — dams, dykes, and nuclear electrical generating stations — which shall not be made the object of attack, even where they are military objectives, if such attack may cause the release of dangerous forces and consequent severe losses among the civilian population, subject to narrow exceptions; Article 15 of Additional Protocol II contains a similar rule for non-international armed conflicts. **Articles 35(3) and 55** prohibit methods or means of warfare intended, or which may be expected, to cause widespread, long-term, and severe damage to the natural environment. In 2022, the International Law Commission adopted the **Principles on the Protection of the Environment in Relation to Armed Conflicts**, which address the protection of the environment before, during, and after armed conflict, including in situations of occupation, and the obligations of states and business enterprises.

### The War in Ukraine

The war in Ukraine has brought these rules into sharp focus. Russia's systematic missile and drone attacks on **Ukraine's electricity system** since October 2022, which destroyed or damaged a large proportion of Ukraine's generating capacity and caused prolonged blackouts in winter, have been condemned by the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission and the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine as potentially constituting war crimes and, in some assessments, crimes against humanity. In March 2024, the **International Criminal Court** issued arrest warrants for two senior Russian commanders for the war crimes of directing attacks at civilian objects and causing excessive incidental harm, and the crime against humanity of inhumane acts, in connection with strikes on Ukrainian electricity infrastructure, and in June 2024 it issued further warrants against the former Russian defence minister and the chief of the general staff on similar grounds. Ukraine has also attacked Russian oil refineries and energy infrastructure, justified by Ukraine as targeting the revenues and fuel supplies sustaining Russia's war effort.

The occupation of the **Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant**, Europe's largest, by Russian forces since March 2022 has created unprecedented nuclear safety risks. The International Atomic Energy Agency has maintained a continuous presence at the plant since September 2022, and its Director General articulated **seven indispensable pillars** of nuclear safety and security during armed conflict and, in May 2023, **five concrete principles** for the protection of the plant, presented to the Security Council, including that there should be no attack from or against the plant and that it should not be used as a base for heavy weapons. The IAEA General Conference and Board of Governors have adopted resolutions calling on Russia to withdraw. The destruction of the **Kakhovka dam** in June 2023, which caused extensive flooding and environmental damage, raised questions under Article 56 of Additional Protocol I and the prohibition of environmental damage. The **International Claims Commission for Ukraine**, established by a convention concluded under the auspices of the Council of Europe in December 2025, together with the **Register of Damage** established in 2023, is intended to address claims for damage caused by Russia's aggression, including damage to energy infrastructure.

### Conflict and Energy in Africa

In Africa, conflict and energy have been intertwined in the Niger Delta, where militant groups attacked oil installations and kidnapped workers in the 2000s, until the **Presidential Amnesty Programme** of 2009, and where crude oil theft and illegal refining continue to cause economic and environmental harm; in Libya, where competing authorities have fought over oil fields and terminals and blockaded production; in South Sudan and Sudan; in the eastern DRC, where the conflict involving the M23 armed group, which captured Goma and Bukavu in early 2025, has been linked to the control of mineral resources and trade routes, and where a peace agreement between the DRC and Rwanda was signed in Washington in June 2025, accompanied by discussions of a regional economic integration framework involving minerals; and in northern Mozambique, where the insurgency in Cabo Delgado led to the suspension of the Mozambique LNG project. The **African Union's Agenda 2063** aspiration of "silencing the guns" and the **Africa Mining Vision** both recognise the importance of breaking the link between resources and conflict.

## 25.6 Maritime Security and Energy Infrastructure

### Chokepoints and the Freedom of Navigation

A large share of the world's oil and LNG is transported by sea, through a small number of **maritime chokepoints**. The **Strait of Hormuz**, between Iran and Oman, through which around a fifth of global oil consumption and a significant share of LNG exports, notably from Qatar, pass each year, is the most important. Others include the **Strait of Malacca**, between Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia, the principal route for oil imports to East Asia; the **Bab el-Mandeb**, between Yemen and Djibouti and Eritrea, linking the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden; the **Suez Canal** and the SUMED pipeline; the **Turkish Straits**, governed by the **Montreux Convention** of 1936; and the **Danish Straits**. The **Panama Canal**, whose capacity was restricted by drought in 2023–2024, illustrates the vulnerability of chokepoints to climate change.

The legal regime of passage through straits used for international navigation is set out in **Part III of UNCLOS**, examined in Chapter 20, which establishes the right of **transit passage** — the exercise of the freedom of navigation and overflight solely for the purpose of continuous and expeditious transit — which shall not be impeded or suspended by the states bordering the strait. Iran, which has signed but not ratified UNCLOS, has maintained that the right of transit passage is a contractual right available only to parties, and that non-parties enjoy only the more limited right of innocent passage, a position contested by the United States and other maritime states, which regard transit passage as reflecting customary international law. Iran has repeatedly threatened to close the Strait of Hormuz in response to sanctions or military pressure, and has seized foreign tankers, including the British-flagged *Stena Impero* in July 2019, two weeks after British forces assisted the Gibraltar authorities in detaining the Iranian tanker *Grace 1* on suspicion of carrying oil to Syria in breach of EU sanctions.

### The Tanker War and the *Oil Platforms* Case

During the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), both belligerents attacked merchant shipping in the Persian Gulf in the so-called **"Tanker War"**, leading the United States and other states to reflag and escort Kuwaiti tankers. In ***Oil Platforms*** (*Iran v United States*, 2003), the International Court of Justice considered US attacks on Iranian offshore oil platforms in 1987 and 1988, which the United States justified as self-defence in response to attacks on US-flagged and US warships attributed to Iran. The Court held that the attacks could not be justified as measures necessary to protect the essential security interests of the United States under the 1955 Treaty of Amity, because the United States had not discharged the burden of proving that it had been the victim of armed attacks by Iran and the attacks on the platforms were not necessary or proportionate. The Court also held, however, that the attacks did not breach the treaty's provision on freedom of commerce between the parties, because oil from the platforms was not at the time being exported to the United States owing to a US embargo. The case is significant for the law of self-defence and for the treatment of energy infrastructure as a target.

### Red Sea Attacks

From November 2023, the **Houthi** movement in Yemen attacked commercial shipping in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, ostensibly in solidarity with Palestinians in Gaza, using missiles, drones, and the hijacking of the vessel *Galaxy Leader*. The attacks caused major shipping lines and many tankers to divert around the Cape of Good Hope, increasing costs and transit times and reducing traffic through the Suez Canal, a significant source of revenue for Egypt. The Security Council, in **Resolution 2722 (2024)**, condemned the attacks and demanded that they cease, noting the right of member states to defend their vessels in accordance with international law. The United States launched **Operation Prosperity Guardian** in December 2023 and, with the United Kingdom, conducted strikes on Houthi targets from January 2024, justified as self-defence; the European Union launched the defensive naval operation **EUNAVFOR Aspides** in February 2024. A ceasefire between the United States and the Houthis was announced in May 2025, though attacks on shipping linked to Israel continued. The Red Sea crisis demonstrated the vulnerability of global energy and trade flows to non-state actors and the limits of naval protection.

### Piracy and Maritime Crime in the Gulf of Guinea

The **Gulf of Guinea**, which encompasses the oil-producing states of West and Central Africa, including Nigeria, Angola, Equatorial Guinea, and Ghana, became in the 2010s the world's most dangerous area for piracy and armed robbery at sea, with attacks on tankers, kidnapping of crew for ransom, and theft of oil cargoes ("bunkering"). The legal framework for maritime security in the region, examined in Chapter 20, includes the definition of **piracy** in Article 101 of UNCLOS, which applies only on the high seas and in the exclusive economic zone, and the concept of **armed robbery at sea**, which covers equivalent acts in territorial waters. The **Yaoundé Code of Conduct** (2013), adopted by ECOWAS, ECCAS, and the Gulf of Guinea Commission, established a regional framework for cooperation in the repression of piracy, armed robbery, and illicit maritime activity, and Nigeria's **Suppression of Piracy and Other Maritime Offences Act 2019** — the first stand-alone anti-piracy law in West and Central Africa — enabled the prosecution of pirates, with the first convictions obtained in 2020. Combined with the **Deep Blue Project** (Integrated National Security and Waterways Protection Infrastructure) launched in 2021 and international naval cooperation, these measures contributed to a significant decline in incidents in the region.

### Protection of Offshore Installations

Offshore oil and gas installations, LNG terminals, and wind farms are potential targets of terrorism, sabotage, and military attack. The **Protocol for the Suppression of Unlawful Acts against the Safety of Fixed Platforms Located on the Continental Shelf** (1988), amended in 2005, requires parties to criminalise acts such as seizing or damaging fixed platforms and to prosecute or extradite offenders. Coastal states may establish **safety zones** of up to 500 metres around artificial islands, installations, and structures in their exclusive economic zones and on their continental shelves under Articles 60 and 80 of UNCLOS. The protection of **critical undersea infrastructure**, including pipelines, power cables, and data cables, has become a priority after the Nord Stream sabotage and repeated incidents of damage to cables and pipelines in the Baltic Sea, including the damage to the Balticconnector gas pipeline between Finland and Estonia in October 2023 by the anchor of a Chinese-owned vessel and to the Estlink 2 power cable in December 2024, after which NATO launched the **Baltic Sentry** mission in January 2025. The limitations of the legal framework — including the limited jurisdiction of coastal states over foreign vessels in the exclusive economic zone, the outdated 1884 Convention for the Protection of Submarine Telegraph Cables, and the difficulties of attribution — have been examined in Chapter 20.
