## 11.5 Nuclear Security and Physical Protection

### The Concept of Nuclear Security

Nuclear security is defined in IAEA guidance as the prevention and detection of, and response to, criminal or intentional unauthorised acts involving or directed at nuclear material, other radioactive material, associated facilities, or associated activities. Its concerns include the theft of nuclear material for the purpose of constructing a nuclear explosive device; the theft of radioactive sources for use in a radiological dispersal device ("dirty bomb"); the sabotage of nuclear facilities or transports with a view to causing radiological release; and cyberattacks on the digital systems of nuclear facilities. Unlike safety, which addresses accidental events, security addresses deliberate acts by adversaries, and the two are complementary: measures designed for safety and for security must be coordinated so that each supports rather than undermines the other — for example, safety requires easy egress in an emergency, while security requires controlled access.

Nuclear security rose to the top of the international agenda after the terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001, which raised concerns about the potential for terrorists to acquire nuclear or radiological material or to attack nuclear facilities. The **Nuclear Security Summits** convened between 2010 and 2016 produced national commitments to minimise the use of highly enriched uranium, strengthen physical protection, and ratify the relevant conventions, and contributed to the entry into force of the 2005 Amendment to the CPPNM.

### The Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material

The **Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material** (CPPNM), adopted in 1980 and in force since 1987, originally applied only to nuclear material used for peaceful purposes in international transport. It required parties to ensure that nuclear material in international transport within their territory, or on board ships or aircraft under their jurisdiction, was protected at specified levels; prohibited the export or import of nuclear material unless assurances of such protection had been received; and required parties to criminalise specified offences, including theft, embezzlement, and the threat to use nuclear material to cause death or serious injury or substantial property damage, and to establish jurisdiction over and either prosecute or extradite alleged offenders.

The **Amendment to the CPPNM**, adopted in 2005 and in force since 8 May 2016, substantially expanded the scope of the Convention. The amended Convention, sometimes called the Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material and Nuclear Facilities, extends to nuclear material in domestic use, storage, and transport, and to the protection of nuclear facilities against sabotage. It requires each party to establish, implement, and maintain an appropriate physical protection regime, consisting of a legislative and regulatory framework, a competent authority, and the responsibility of licence holders, and sets out twelve "fundamental principles" of physical protection, including the responsibility of the state, the responsibility of licence holders, legislative and regulatory framework, competent authority, security culture, threat evaluation (the "design basis threat"), a graded approach, defence in depth, quality assurance, contingency plans, and confidentiality. It adds new offences, including sabotage of a nuclear facility and the smuggling of nuclear material, and requires parties to cooperate in locating and recovering stolen or smuggled material.

### The International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism

The **International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism** (ICSANT), adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 2005 and in force since 2007, is one of the sectoral counter-terrorism conventions negotiated under United Nations auspices. It requires parties to criminalise the possession of radioactive material or the making or possession of a device with the intent to cause death or serious bodily injury, substantial damage to property or the environment, or to compel a natural or legal person, an international organisation, or a state to do or refrain from doing an act; the use of radioactive material or a device, or the use of or damage to a nuclear facility in a manner which releases or risks the release of radioactive material, with such intent; and threats and demands relating to such acts. It applies the "prosecute or extradite" principle and provides for international cooperation. ICSANT applies to radioactive material in general, not only nuclear material, and to acts by any person, but excludes the activities of armed forces during an armed conflict, which are governed by international humanitarian law.

### United Nations Security Council Resolution 1540

Security Council Resolution 1540 (2004), adopted under Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter, decided that all states shall refrain from providing any form of support to non-state actors that attempt to develop, acquire, manufacture, possess, transport, transfer, or use nuclear, chemical, or biological weapons and their means of delivery, and shall adopt and enforce appropriate effective laws prohibiting such activities, and appropriate effective measures to account for and secure such items, develop effective border controls, and establish export and trans-shipment controls. Resolution 1540 is notable as an instance of the Security Council imposing general legislative obligations on all states, rather than responding to a specific situation, and it has been described as "legislative" in character. Its implementation is monitored by a committee of the Security Council.

### The Security of Radioactive Sources

Radioactive sources used in medicine, industry, and research present significant security risks because they are widely distributed, often poorly controlled, and potentially usable in radiological dispersal devices. Incidents involving lost or abandoned sources — such as the incident at Goiânia, Brazil, in 1987, in which an abandoned radiotherapy source was opened by scrap metal scavengers, causing several deaths and widespread contamination — illustrate the dangers. The IAEA's **Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources** (2003), together with its supplementary Guidance on the Import and Export of Radioactive Sources and Guidance on the Management of Disused Radioactive Sources, provides non-binding guidance that a large number of states have committed politically to implement, including through national registers of sources, licensing, and the safe and secure management of disused sources.

In Nigeria, the NNRA regulates the security of radioactive sources, which are widely used in the oil and gas industry for well logging and in industrial radiography, as well as in medicine. The theft of radioactive sources from oil and gas operations has occurred in Nigeria and elsewhere, illustrating the intersection of nuclear security with the regulation of the extractive industries examined in Chapter 5.

### Nuclear Security Law in Practice

The IAEA's **Nuclear Security Series** provides guidance on the implementation of nuclear security, including the *Nuclear Security Fundamentals* (2013) and *Nuclear Security Recommendations* on physical protection, on radioactive material and associated facilities, and on material out of regulatory control. Unlike the Safety Standards, nuclear security guidance is formally treated as guidance rather than standards, reflecting the sensitivity of security matters as an aspect of national sovereignty. The IAEA's **International Physical Protection Advisory Service** (IPPAS) offers peer reviews of national physical protection regimes. The confidentiality of security information means that nuclear security law is less transparent than safety law, which complicates public scrutiny and accountability. Cybersecurity has become a growing dimension of nuclear security, as nuclear facilities increasingly rely on digital instrumentation and control systems, and is examined in the context of energy infrastructure more generally in Chapter 12.

## 11.6 Nuclear Liability and Compensation

### The Rationale for a Special Liability Regime

The potential for nuclear accidents to cause catastrophic and transboundary damage, the difficulty of proving causation for radiation-induced illnesses that may manifest decades later, and the reluctance of suppliers and insurers to participate in the nuclear industry without protection from unlimited liability led, from the late 1950s, to the development of special liability regimes for nuclear damage. These regimes were designed to serve two objectives: to ensure adequate compensation for victims of nuclear incidents, and to enable the development of the nuclear industry by limiting and channelling liability so that it could be insured.

### The Principles of Nuclear Liability Law

The international nuclear liability conventions are based on a set of common principles.

**Exclusive liability of the operator (channelling).** Liability for nuclear damage is channelled exclusively to the operator of the nuclear installation in which the nuclear incident occurs, or from which the nuclear material involved originated. No other person — including suppliers of equipment, designers, and constructors — is liable, and the operator's right of recourse against other persons is limited to cases where recourse is expressly provided for by contract or where the damage results from an act or omission done with intent to cause damage. Channelling simplifies claims for victims, who need only identify the operator, and protects suppliers, avoiding the need for every participant in the nuclear industry to insure against the same risk.

**Strict liability.** The operator's liability is strict (absolute), in the sense that it does not depend on proof of fault or negligence. Exonerations are limited — principally to damage caused by acts of armed conflict, hostilities, civil war, or insurrection, and, under the older conventions, grave natural disasters of an exceptional character (an exoneration removed by the revised conventions).

**Limitation of liability in amount.** The operator's liability may be limited in amount, in exchange for strict and exclusive liability, with the possibility of state or collective funding beyond the operator's liability.

**Limitation in time.** Claims must be brought within specified time limits, which the revised conventions have extended to thirty years from the incident for claims in respect of loss of life and personal injury, recognising the latency of radiation-induced illness.

**Compulsory financial security.** The operator must maintain insurance or other financial security covering its liability.

**Exclusive jurisdiction.** Jurisdiction over claims lies exclusively with the courts of the state in whose territory the nuclear incident occurred, ensuring a single forum for the equitable distribution of compensation among all victims.

**Non-discrimination.** Compensation must be distributed without discrimination based on nationality, domicile, or residence.

### The Paris and Vienna Systems

The **Paris Convention on Third Party Liability in the Field of Nuclear Energy** (1960), adopted under the auspices of the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency, applies principally among Western European states. It was supplemented by the **Brussels Supplementary Convention** (1963), which provides additional tiers of compensation from public funds of the installation state and from collective contributions of the parties. The **Protocols of 2004** amending the Paris and Brussels Conventions, which entered into force on 1 January 2022, raised the minimum liability of the operator to EUR 700 million, increased total compensation available under the Brussels tiers to EUR 1.5 billion, broadened the definition of nuclear damage to include economic loss, the costs of measures of reinstatement of impaired environment, loss of income deriving from an economic interest in the use or enjoyment of the environment, and the costs of preventive measures, and extended the time limit for personal injury claims to thirty years.

The **Vienna Convention on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage** (1963), adopted under the auspices of the IAEA, was intended to be of worldwide application and applies principally among states in Central and Eastern Europe, Latin America, and elsewhere. The **1997 Protocol** amending the Vienna Convention, in force since 2003, raised the minimum liability amount to not less than 300 million Special Drawing Rights, broadened the definition of nuclear damage in similar terms to the 2004 Paris Protocol, and extended the geographical scope of the Convention to damage suffered wherever, subject to exceptions.

The **Joint Protocol relating to the Application of the Vienna Convention and the Paris Convention** (1988), in force since 1992 and adopted in response to the Chernobyl accident, links the two systems, so that victims in a state party to one convention may claim against an operator in a state party to the other.

### The Convention on Supplementary Compensation

The **Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage** (CSC), adopted in 1997 and in force since 2015, was intended to establish a global liability regime capable of attracting states that are not party to either the Paris or the Vienna Convention, including the United States. It may be joined by parties to the Paris or Vienna Conventions, and by states whose national law complies with the provisions of its Annex, a "grandfather clause" designed to accommodate the economic channelling of the United States Price-Anderson Act. It requires installation states to ensure the availability of at least 300 million SDRs and provides for an international fund of contributions from all parties, calculated principally by reference to installed nuclear capacity. The CSC's parties include the United States, Japan, India, Canada, and the United Arab Emirates, as well as several other states. Japan's accession in 2015 was motivated in part by the desire to facilitate the participation of international companies in the decommissioning of Fukushima Daiichi by providing them with channelling protection.

### National Liability Regimes

The United States pioneered nuclear liability legislation with the **Price-Anderson Act** of 1957, which requires operators of nuclear power plants to maintain the maximum available private insurance and to participate in a retrospective premium scheme under which each operator contributes to the compensation of damage caused by an incident at any plant, creating a pool of more than USD 15 billion. Price-Anderson employs "economic channelling" — liability is not legally channelled to the operator, but the insurance covers any person liable — and provides for the consolidation of claims in federal courts. In *Duke Power Co v Carolina Environmental Study Group* 438 US 59 (1978), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the Act's limitation of liability, holding that it was rationally related to the legitimate objective of encouraging private participation in nuclear energy and that it provided a reasonably just substitute for the common law remedies it replaced.

Japan's **Act on Compensation for Nuclear Damage** (1961) imposes unlimited, strict, and exclusive liability on operators, with government support. Following Fukushima, TEPCO's compensation liabilities far exceeded its capacity, and the **Nuclear Damage Compensation Facilitation Corporation** (later the Nuclear Damage Compensation and Decommissioning Facilitation Corporation) was established in 2011 to provide financial assistance to TEPCO, funded by government bonds and contributions from the nuclear industry. Compensation was paid through direct claims to TEPCO, through the government's Dispute Reconciliation Committee for Nuclear Damage Compensation, which issued guidelines on the categories of compensable damage and conducted alternative dispute resolution, and through litigation. The total compensation paid by TEPCO exceeded ten trillion yen.

India's **Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act 2010** departed from the principle of exclusive channelling by granting the operator, in section 17(b), a right of recourse against the supplier where the nuclear incident resulted from the supply of equipment or material with patent or latent defects or substandard services. This provision, a response to the Bhopal gas disaster of 1984 and the inadequacy of the compensation it produced, deterred foreign suppliers from participating in the Indian nuclear market. India ratified the CSC in 2016, and the government issued explanations of the provision's scope, but the compatibility of section 17(b) with the CSC and its implications for suppliers remained controversial.

### Adequacy of the Liability Regimes

The international nuclear liability regime has been criticised on several grounds. The liability limits, even as revised, are modest in comparison with the costs of major accidents: the damage caused by Chernobyl and Fukushima has been estimated in the hundreds of billions of US dollars. Participation in the conventions is fragmented, and many states with nuclear power programmes, including China and some newcomer states, are party to none of them; the absence of a treaty relationship means that victims in one state may be unable to claim against an operator in another. Channelling has been criticised for removing the incentive for suppliers to ensure the safety of their products. And the conventions' exclusive jurisdiction provisions may leave victims in neighbouring states dependent on the courts of the installation state. For newcomer states, including those in Africa, adherence to a liability convention — commonly the revised Vienna Convention or the CSC — is a practical prerequisite for attracting foreign suppliers and financing, and the IAEA's International Expert Group on Nuclear Liability (INLEX) has recommended that states establish treaty relations among themselves through adherence to the revised conventions and the CSC.
