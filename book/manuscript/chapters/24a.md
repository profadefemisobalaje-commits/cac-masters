## Introduction

The preceding chapters have examined international energy and natural resources law and, at many points, compared the national laws through which international norms are implemented and resource activities regulated. This chapter undertakes a more systematic comparison. Comparative law is indispensable to the energy and resource lawyer for several reasons. Most resource activities are governed principally by national law, so that the practitioner advising on a transaction or dispute must understand the legal framework of the host state. Legal reform in the energy sector has been marked by extensive borrowing of legal models — production sharing contracts from Indonesia, the regulatory model of the independent utility regulator from the United States and the United Kingdom, the sovereign wealth fund model from Norway, the safety case regime from the United Kingdom, and many others — so that understanding the origins and contexts of these models is necessary to evaluate their suitability elsewhere. Comparative analysis reveals the range of choices available to legislators and the consequences of different choices, enabling evidence-based reform. And comparative research is a mainstay of doctoral scholarship in the field, examined in Chapter 28.

This chapter begins with comparative legal methodology (section 24.1). It then examines, in turn, energy and petroleum law in Nigeria (section 24.2), which receives extended treatment as the jurisdiction of primary interest to many readers of this book; United States energy regulation (section 24.3); United Kingdom and European Union energy law (section 24.4); Middle Eastern petroleum governance (section 24.5); Asian energy and mineral resource frameworks (section 24.6); Latin American resource sovereignty and regulation (section 24.7); and comparative lessons from African resource-rich states (section 24.8). The treatment of each jurisdiction is necessarily selective, emphasising features of comparative significance; many aspects have been examined in earlier chapters and are cross-referenced.

## 24.1 Comparative Legal Methodology

### Purposes of Comparative Energy Law

Comparative law serves several purposes in the energy and resource field. It is a tool of **knowledge**, deepening understanding of one's own legal system by contrasting it with others. It is a tool of **law reform**, enabling legislators and regulators to learn from the experience of other jurisdictions. It is a tool of **practice**, enabling lawyers to advise on transactions and disputes involving multiple legal systems, to draft contracts that will operate effectively in different legal environments, and to anticipate the approach of foreign courts and regulators. It contributes to **harmonisation** and the development of model laws, standards, and contracts, such as the model production sharing contracts and petroleum laws promoted by international institutions and the model contracts of the Association of International Energy Negotiators examined in Chapter 7. And it contributes to the identification of **general principles of law**, a source of international law under Article 38(1)(c) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, which arbitral tribunals have drawn upon in petroleum disputes, as examined in Chapters 1 and 15.

### Methods

Comparative law scholarship has developed several methodological approaches. The **functional method**, articulated by Konrad Zweigert and Hein Kötz in their *Introduction to Comparative Law*, proceeds from the premise that legal systems face similar social problems and solve them by different means, often with similar results; the comparatist should therefore identify a problem and examine how different systems address it, rather than comparing legal rules or institutions bearing the same name. In energy law, a functional approach might ask how different systems allocate the right to explore for and produce petroleum — through concessions, licences, production sharing contracts, or service contracts, as examined in Chapter 5 — or how they ensure third-party access to networks, rather than comparing institutions in the abstract.

The functional method has been criticised for assuming that problems are universal and for neglecting the cultural, historical, and political contexts that shape law. **Contextual** and **cultural** approaches emphasise that law is embedded in society and that legal rules cannot be understood apart from their institutional, political, economic, and cultural settings. Pierre Legrand has argued, provocatively, that legal transplants are impossible, since the meaning of a rule changes when it is transferred to a different culture. The **law and development** and **institutional economics** traditions examine how legal institutions affect economic outcomes, and the "legal origins" literature associated with Rafael La Porta, Florencio López-de-Silanes, and Andrei Shleifer argued, controversially, that common law systems are associated with stronger investor protection and financial development than civil law systems. In energy and resource law, **socio-legal** approaches examining how laws operate in practice — including the gap between law on the books and law in action — are particularly important, since many resource-rich states have sophisticated laws that are weakly implemented.

### Legal Families and Energy Law

Comparative lawyers have traditionally classified legal systems into **legal families** — the common law, the civil law (with Romanistic, Germanic, and Nordic branches), Islamic law, socialist or post-socialist law, and customary law — although the classification is increasingly regarded as simplistic, given the hybrid character of many systems. Energy and resource law is influenced by legal family in several respects: the conception of property and ownership of resources; the role of codes and statutes versus case law; the approach to administrative law and state contracts, including the French doctrine of the *contrat administratif* with its powers of unilateral variation, which influenced petroleum contracts in francophone Africa and the Middle East; and, in Islamic jurisdictions, the influence of Sharia principles, for example on interest and on certain forms of contract. Many African states have **pluralistic** legal systems in which statutory law coexists with customary law, which governs much land tenure and thereby affects the acquisition of land for resource projects, as examined in Chapter 21. Nigeria's legal system combines English common law, statute, customary law, and, in the northern states, Islamic law.

### Ownership Models

A fundamental comparative distinction concerns the **ownership of subsurface resources**. In most of the world, petroleum and minerals are owned by the state, regardless of the ownership of the surface — the **domanial** or **regalian** system, derived from Roman and Spanish colonial traditions and adopted in nearly all states in Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Asia, as well as in the United Kingdom for petroleum (under the Petroleum (Production) Act 1934 and now the Petroleum Act 1998) and Norway. In the **accession** system, the owner of the surface owns the minerals beneath it, subject to the possibility of severance; the principal example is the United States, where private landowners own most onshore petroleum on private land, which has contributed to the development of a large independent oil and gas industry and to the "shale revolution". Ownership models have consequences for the distribution of resource revenues, the bargaining relationship between companies and landowners, and the political economy of resource development.

### Legal Transplants in Energy Law

The history of energy law is in large part a history of **legal transplants** — the borrowing of rules and institutions from one system by another, a phenomenon analysed by Alan Watson. Notable examples include the production sharing contract, pioneered by Indonesia in the 1960s and adopted across Africa, Asia, and the Middle East; the independent regulatory commission, modelled on United States public utility commissions and United Kingdom regulators such as Ofgem and adopted throughout the world in the wave of electricity reforms promoted by the World Bank in the 1990s; the "standard market design" and wholesale electricity markets; the Norwegian model of NOC governance, examined in Chapter 22; the Ghana model of petroleum revenue management, examined in Chapter 23; and the safety case regime for offshore petroleum. The success of transplants depends on their fit with the institutional, economic, and political context of the receiving state. The **standard model** of electricity reform — unbundling, privatisation, independent regulation, and competitive markets — has had mixed results in developing countries, where weak institutions, low tariffs, and political interference have often frustrated reform, as the experience of the Nigerian electricity privatisation of 2013 illustrates, examined in section 24.2. Comparative energy lawyers therefore emphasise the need to adapt models to local conditions, to sequence reforms appropriately, and to build the institutions necessary for their implementation.

### Pitfalls of Comparison

Comparative energy research faces several pitfalls: the difficulty of obtaining accurate and up-to-date information on foreign law, which changes rapidly in the energy sector; the risk of comparing formal law without understanding its implementation; linguistic barriers and the risk of mistranslation; the tendency to treat Western models as benchmarks against which other systems are measured; and the danger of selecting jurisdictions for comparison without a clear rationale. A sound comparative study defines its research question clearly, justifies its selection of jurisdictions, uses primary sources wherever possible, examines law in context and in practice, and is cautious about drawing prescriptive conclusions. These methodological considerations are developed further in Chapter 28.

## 24.2 Energy and Petroleum Law in Nigeria

### The Constitutional Framework

Nigeria is a federation of thirty-six states and the Federal Capital Territory, governed by the **Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999**, as amended. The Constitution allocates legislative powers between the National Assembly and the State Houses of Assembly through the **Exclusive Legislative List** and the **Concurrent Legislative List** in the Second Schedule. Matters on the Exclusive List — on which only the National Assembly may legislate — include "mines and minerals, including oil fields, oil mining, geological surveys and natural gas" (item 39) and "nuclear energy" (item 41). Section 44(3) vests the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils, and natural gas in, under, or upon any land in Nigeria, its territorial waters, and its EEZ in the Government of the Federation. Electricity was formerly largely a federal matter, subject to limited state powers under the Concurrent List, but the **Constitution (Fifth Alteration) Act** of 2023 amended the Concurrent List to empower states to legislate on electricity generation, transmission, and distribution within the state, a change implemented by the **Electricity Act 2023**, examined below. The distribution of petroleum revenues, governed by section 162, has been examined in Chapter 23.

### The Evolution of Nigerian Petroleum Law

Commercial oil production in Nigeria began in 1958 following Shell-BP's discovery at Oloibiri in 1956. The colonial **Mineral Oils Ordinance** of 1914 governed early exploration, and after independence the **Petroleum Act 1969** became the principal statute, vesting petroleum in the state and providing for the grant of oil exploration licences, oil prospecting licences, and oil mining leases by the Minister. Nigeria joined OPEC in 1971, and the government acquired participating interests in the operations of the international oil companies, rising to sixty per cent by 1979, through **joint ventures** with the companies operating under **joint operating agreements**, examined in Chapter 7. The Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation was established in 1977. From 1993, the government granted deep offshore acreage under **production sharing contracts**, governed by the **Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contract Act 1999** (amended in 2019). Other features of the legal framework included the Petroleum Profits Tax Act, the Associated Gas Re-Injection Act 1979, which sought to end gas flaring, and, from 2010, the Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act, examined in Chapter 7.

The legal framework was widely criticised: it was fragmented and outdated; it concentrated discretionary power in the Minister; the regulator, the Department of Petroleum Resources, lacked independence; NNPC combined commercial and regulatory roles and was opaque; the fiscal regime was complex and, for deep offshore PSCs, generous to investors; the joint venture funding arrangements, under which NNPC was obliged to fund its share of costs through "cash calls", resulted in chronic underfunding; and the regime failed to address the grievances of host communities. Reform efforts began with the Oil and Gas Sector Reform Implementation Committee in 2000, and a comprehensive **Petroleum Industry Bill** was first introduced in the National Assembly in 2008. After more than a decade of failed attempts, during which the Bill was split into components and a Petroleum Industry Governance Bill was passed in 2018 but not assented to, the **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** (PIA) was signed into law in August 2021.

### The Petroleum Industry Act 2021: Structure and Institutions

The PIA is a comprehensive statute of more than three hundred sections, divided into five chapters: **governance and institutions** (Chapter 1), **administration** (Chapter 2), **host communities development** (Chapter 3), **petroleum industry fiscal framework** (Chapter 4), and **miscellaneous provisions** (Chapter 5). Its institutional architecture, illustrated in Figure 24.1, separates policy, regulation, and commercial functions.

- The **Minister of Petroleum Resources** is responsible for the formulation, monitoring, and administration of government policy in the petroleum industry, and retains powers to grant licences and leases on the recommendation of the regulator, and to exercise general supervisory functions. The President has held the petroleum portfolio in several administrations, assisted by a Minister of State.
- The **Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission** (NUPRC), a body corporate, is responsible for the technical and commercial regulation of upstream petroleum operations, including the conduct of licensing rounds, the recommendation of licences and leases, the determination of royalties, the approval of field development plans, the enforcement of health, safety, and environmental standards, and the management of the National Data Repository.
- The **Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority** (NMDPRA) regulates midstream and downstream operations, including refining, gas processing, transportation and distribution, storage, and the wholesale and retail supply of petroleum products and natural gas, and administers the Midstream and Downstream Gas Infrastructure Fund.
- **NNPC Limited**, the successor to the Corporation, operates as a commercial company under the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020, as examined in Chapter 22.

The separation of the regulators from the NOC and from each other, and the establishment of their governing boards with specified qualifications, were designed to address the conflicts of interest and lack of independence that characterised the previous regime. The regulators are funded by levies and fees, including a share of royalties and rents for the NUPRC and specified levies for the NMDPRA, rather than by direct budgetary allocations, enhancing their financial autonomy.

### Licensing under the PIA

The PIA introduced a new licensing framework for upstream operations. The NUPRC may grant **petroleum exploration licences** (non-exclusive, for data acquisition), **petroleum prospecting licences** (exclusive, for exploration drilling), and **petroleum mining leases** (for development and production), on the recommendation of the Commission and with the approval of the Minister, through a transparent competitive bid process, with exceptions specified in the Act. Petroleum prospecting licences have defined terms that vary with the terrain (onshore, shallow water, deep offshore, and frontier basins), and petroleum mining leases are granted for a term of up to twenty years, renewable. The Act introduced **"drill or drop"** provisions requiring the relinquishment of undeveloped acreage, to address the long-standing problem of acreage held without development, and provides for the conversion of existing oil prospecting licences and oil mining leases into the new licences and leases on a voluntary basis, with incentives for conversion, while existing arrangements continue to be governed by the prior law until conversion or expiry. The NUPRC has conducted licensing rounds under the new framework, including the marginal field bid round concluded in 2021 and the licensing round launched in 2024 and continued in 2025.

The PIA requires the **disclosure** of licences, leases, and contracts, and provides for the publication of information on the beneficial owners of licensees, as noted in Chapter 22. It also addresses the **divestment** of onshore and shallow water assets by international oil companies, a trend that accelerated in the 2020s as companies such as Shell, ExxonMobil, Eni, Equinor, and TotalEnergies sold onshore and shallow water interests to Nigerian independent companies, requiring the approval of the Minister on the recommendation of the NUPRC. The regulatory review of these divestments — including the assessment of the technical and financial capacity of acquirers and the treatment of decommissioning and environmental liabilities — became a significant test of the PIA's framework, given concerns raised by communities and civil society about the legacy of pollution in the Niger Delta.
