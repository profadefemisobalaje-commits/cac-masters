## Introduction

Energy and natural resources sit at the junction of almost every question that modern legal systems are asked to answer. Who owns what lies beneath the soil and the seabed? Under what conditions may a sovereign state grant rights to explore for and extract those resources, and on what terms may it later change its mind? How should the economic rent produced by a non-renewable asset be shared between the state, the investor, the host community, and future generations? What obligations attach to the transport of oil and gas across national frontiers, to the generation and trade of electricity, or to the emissions released when fossil fuels are burned? How should disputes between powerful states and powerful corporations be resolved, and by whom? Each of these questions is, at bottom, a legal question, and the body of law that has developed to answer them is the subject of this book.

International energy and natural resources law is a young discipline in name but an old one in substance. The legal instruments through which states have granted, regulated, taxed, and expropriated mineral and petroleum rights have a history that stretches back more than a century, and the doctrines that frame those transactions — sovereignty, contract, property, state responsibility, and the protection of aliens — are older still. What is new is the recognition that these instruments and doctrines constitute a coherent field of study, with its own sources, principles, institutions, and methods, and with a distinctive set of intellectual problems that cannot be adequately addressed from within any single traditional legal category.

This opening chapter lays the foundation for the remainder of the book. It begins by defining the field and identifying its boundaries. It then traces the historical development of resource governance, explains the internationalisation of energy markets and regulation, and examines the interplay between public law, private law, and transnational legal relations. It considers the relationship between energy law and international economic law, the phenomenon of legal pluralism, and the contemporary challenges confronting global resource governance. The chapter closes by setting out the foundational debates that animate doctoral research and that recur throughout the chapters that follow.

The approach throughout is analytical rather than descriptive. A doctoral candidate in this field must be able to do more than recite the content of treaties and statutes; he or she must be able to explain why the law takes the form that it does, to identify the tensions and contradictions within it, to evaluate competing interpretations, and to propose reasoned reforms. The chapter therefore emphasises the conceptual architecture of the field — the ideas that hold it together and the fault lines along which it is contested.

## 1.1 Defining Energy and Natural Resources Law

### The Problem of Definition

Any attempt to define a field of law must confront a familiar difficulty: legal categories are not natural kinds. They are constructed by scholars, practitioners, courts, and legislators for particular purposes, and their boundaries shift as those purposes change. "Energy law" and "natural resources law" are no exception. Neither phrase appears as a term of art in any multilateral treaty of general application, and neither corresponds to a single body of legislation in most domestic legal systems. Instead, each describes a cluster of rules, principles, institutions, and practices that share a common subject matter — the ownership, development, transformation, trade, and use of energy sources and natural resources — and that are united by a common set of policy concerns.

A useful working definition, adopted for the purposes of this book, is the following:

> International energy and natural resources law is the body of rules, principles, institutions, and practices — derived from public international law, international economic law, comparative domestic law, and transnational commercial practice — that governs the allocation of rights over energy sources and natural resources, the conduct of activities involving their exploration, development, transformation, transport, trade, and use, and the distribution of the benefits and burdens arising from those activities, in situations that involve more than one legal order.

Several features of this definition deserve comment. First, it is *functional* rather than formal: it defines the field by reference to its subject matter and the problems it addresses, not by reference to a particular source of law. Second, it is *pluralist*: it expressly acknowledges that the relevant rules derive from multiple sources, including domestic legislation and private contracts as well as treaties and custom. Third, it is *distributive*: it identifies the allocation of benefits and burdens as a central concern, reflecting the fact that energy and resource law is fundamentally about who gets what, when, and how. Fourth, it is *transnational*: the field is concerned with situations that involve more than one legal order, whether because a resource straddles a boundary, because an investor is foreign, because a commodity is traded internationally, or because environmental effects cross frontiers.

### Energy Law and Natural Resources Law: One Field or Two?

The conjunction "energy and natural resources" requires justification. Energy law, narrowly understood, is concerned with the production, conversion, transmission, distribution, and consumption of energy, whether derived from fossil fuels, nuclear fission, or renewable sources. Natural resources law, narrowly understood, is concerned with the ownership, management, and exploitation of resources found in nature, including minerals, petroleum, water, forests, fisheries, and biological diversity. The two fields overlap substantially — petroleum and natural gas are both natural resources and energy sources — but neither is wholly contained within the other. Renewable electricity generated from wind or solar radiation is an energy source but is not usually described as a natural resource in the extractive sense; copper and lithium are natural resources but are not themselves sources of energy, although they are indispensable to its transmission and storage.

There are strong reasons for treating the two fields together. Historically, the law governing petroleum developed out of the law governing hard-rock minerals, and many of the foundational instruments — concessions, licences, royalties, and the doctrine of permanent sovereignty — are common to both. Functionally, the energy transition is collapsing the distinction: the decarbonisation of energy systems depends on the extraction of critical minerals on an unprecedented scale, on access to land and water for renewable installations, and on the use of geological formations for the storage of carbon dioxide and hydrogen. Institutionally, the same ministries, regulators, and courts often have jurisdiction over both energy and minerals. Doctrinally, the same principles of international law — sovereignty over natural resources, the prohibition of transboundary harm, the protection of foreign investment, and the rights of indigenous peoples — govern both.

This book therefore treats energy law and natural resources law as a single integrated field, while recognising that particular chapters will emphasise one or the other. The integrated approach has the further advantage of exposing the connections between subjects that are too often studied in isolation: between petroleum fiscal regimes and renewable energy incentives, for example, or between mining law and climate law.

### Relationship with Adjacent Fields

The field overlaps with several established branches of law, and a doctoral candidate must be able to locate it precisely in relation to each.

**Public international law.** The general rules of public international law — on sources, treaties, state responsibility, jurisdiction, immunity, and dispute settlement — supply the framework within which international energy and resource law operates. The doctrine of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, discussed in Chapter 2, is a principle of general international law. The law of the sea, international watercourse law, and international environmental law are specialised branches of public international law with direct application to resource activities.

**International economic law.** The law of international trade, investment, finance, and taxation governs the cross-border movement of energy commodities, capital, and revenues. The World Trade Organization (WTO) agreements apply to trade in energy goods and, to a more limited extent, services; bilateral and multilateral investment treaties protect foreign investors in the energy and mining sectors; and international tax rules shape the fiscal treatment of resource projects. The relationship between energy law and international economic law is examined in section 1.5 below.

**International environmental and climate law.** Resource activities are among the most significant sources of environmental harm, and international environmental law imposes obligations of prevention, precaution, assessment, and cooperation that constrain the manner in which they may be carried out. Climate change law, in particular, is reshaping energy law by setting long-term objectives for the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions that are incompatible with the indefinite expansion of fossil fuel production.

**Comparative and domestic law.** Most of the rules that govern energy and resource activities on a day-to-day basis are domestic: constitutional provisions on resource ownership, petroleum and mining statutes, electricity regulation, environmental legislation, and tax codes. International energy law cannot be understood without close attention to these domestic regimes and to the patterns of convergence and divergence among them, which are explored in Chapter 24.

**Transnational commercial law.** Energy and resource projects are structured through complex networks of private contracts — joint operating agreements, sales and purchase agreements, engineering and construction contracts, financing agreements — that are frequently governed by English or New York law and subject to international commercial arbitration. The model contracts and standard terms produced by industry associations constitute a form of transnational private ordering that interacts with, and sometimes substitutes for, state regulation.

### The Scope of the Field

The scope of the field, as treated in this book, may be described along three axes.

The first axis is the *resource or energy source* concerned: petroleum (crude oil and natural gas), coal, hard-rock minerals including critical minerals, uranium and nuclear energy, renewable energy sources (solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, biomass, marine), hydrogen and other energy carriers, electricity, water, and marine resources.

The second axis is the *stage of the value chain*: the allocation of rights to explore and develop resources (upstream), the transport and processing of those resources (midstream), and their sale, distribution, and consumption (downstream), together with the decommissioning of installations and the remediation of affected environments at the end of the project life cycle.

The third axis is the *legal function*: the allocation of property and access rights, the regulation of conduct, the protection of investment, the allocation of commercial risk by contract, the raising and distribution of revenue, the protection of the environment and human rights, and the resolution of disputes.

The chapters of this book are organised principally along the third axis, while particular chapters address specific resources (Chapters 5 to 12) and specific legal functions (Chapters 13 to 27).

### Is There a "Lex Petrolea"?

One of the most contested questions in the definition of the field concerns the existence of a distinct transnational legal order governing the international petroleum industry — sometimes described as the *lex petrolea*. The idea has a long pedigree. In the arbitral award in *Kuwait v American Independent Oil Company (Aminoil)*, the tribunal referred to the existence of a body of practice in the oil industry that informed the interpretation of the concession, while stopping short of endorsing an autonomous legal system.[^lp1] Proponents of the *lex petrolea* argue that the repeated use of standard contractual forms, the consistent practice of industry participants, the jurisprudence of arbitral tribunals, and the model contracts of bodies such as the Association of International Energy Negotiators (AIEN) have generated a body of transnational norms that operates partly independently of any national legal system.[^lp2]

Critics respond that the concept is either trivially true — industry practice obviously influences the interpretation of industry contracts — or dangerously overstated, in that it purports to detach contractual relations from the legal systems that ultimately give them force, and that it tends to privilege the interests of international oil companies over those of host states and affected communities. The debate is significant for doctoral researchers because it raises fundamental questions about the nature of law, the legitimacy of private ordering, and the relationship between state and non-state sources of normativity. It is revisited in Chapter 2 in the context of soft law and industry standards and in Chapter 26 in the context of arbitral practice.

[^lp1]: *Government of the State of Kuwait v American Independent Oil Company (Aminoil)*, Award of 24 March 1982, (1982) 21 ILM 976.

[^lp2]: See R. Doak Bishop, "International Arbitration of Petroleum Disputes: The Development of a *Lex Petrolea*" (1998) 23 *Yearbook of Commercial Arbitration* 1131; and Thomas C.C. Childs, "Update on *Lex Petrolea*: The Continuing Development of Customary Law Relating to International Oil and Gas Exploration and Production" (2011) 4 *Journal of World Energy Law and Business* 214.

## 1.2 Historical Development of Resource Governance

A historical perspective is indispensable to an understanding of contemporary energy and resource law. Many of the field's defining doctrines — the stabilisation clause, the doctrine of permanent sovereignty, the standard of compensation for expropriation, the treatment of production sharing contracts — were forged in specific historical controversies, and their meaning cannot be grasped without appreciating the circumstances in which they arose. This section traces five broad phases in the development of resource governance.

### Phase One: The Classical Concession Era (c. 1900–1950)

The modern international petroleum industry was built on the classical concession. In 1901, William Knox D'Arcy obtained from the Shah of Persia a concession conferring the exclusive right to explore for, produce, and export petroleum over approximately three-quarters of the territory of Persia for a period of sixty years, in return for a modest initial payment and a share of annual net profits. The D'Arcy concession became the template for a generation of agreements across the Middle East, Latin America, and Asia.

The classical concession had several characteristic features. It covered vast areas, often a substantial proportion of the host state's territory. It endured for very long periods, frequently between sixty and ninety-nine years. It conferred upon the concessionaire not merely the right to extract petroleum but ownership of the petroleum once produced, together with broad managerial control over all aspects of operations. The host state's financial return was limited, typically to a fixed royalty per ton of production, and the state had little or no involvement in decisions concerning the rate of production, the pricing of crude oil, or the allocation of markets. The concessions were frequently negotiated between unequal parties, in circumstances in which the host state lacked the geological information, technical expertise, and bargaining power necessary to secure favourable terms.

The legal character of these instruments was ambiguous. From the perspective of the international oil companies and their home states, a concession was a contract that created vested rights protected by international law, and any unilateral modification or termination by the host state constituted a wrong for which compensation was owed. From the perspective of host states, the concession was an act of sovereign authority — an administrative grant — that the state could revise in the public interest. This tension between contract and sovereignty is one of the enduring themes of the field.

During this period the industry was dominated by a small number of vertically integrated companies — later known as the "Seven Sisters" — which controlled exploration, production, refining, transportation, and marketing. Arrangements such as the Red Line Agreement of 1928, under which the partners in the Iraq Petroleum Company agreed not to compete with one another for concessions within a defined area of the former Ottoman Empire, and the Achnacarry or "As-Is" Agreement of the same year, which sought to stabilise market shares, illustrate the degree to which the international oil market was organised through private arrangements rather than public regulation.

### Phase Two: Nationalisation and the Assertion of Sovereignty (1938–1960s)

The first major challenge to the concession system came from Latin America. In 1938, Mexico nationalised the holdings of foreign oil companies following a prolonged labour dispute and a confrontation with the companies over compliance with a decision of the Mexican Supreme Court. The Mexican nationalisation prompted a celebrated exchange of diplomatic notes in which the United States Secretary of State, Cordell Hull, asserted that international law required "prompt, adequate, and effective" compensation for the expropriation of foreign property — the formula that became known as the Hull standard. Mexico rejected the claim that international law imposed any such requirement, maintaining that compensation was a matter for domestic law. The dispute was eventually settled by agreement, but the doctrinal disagreement it exposed persisted for decades.

The second major challenge came in Iran. In 1951, the Iranian parliament enacted legislation nationalising the assets of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. The United Kingdom instituted proceedings before the International Court of Justice, but the Court held that it lacked jurisdiction because the relevant Iranian declaration accepting the Court's compulsory jurisdiction was limited to disputes concerning treaties, and the concession of 1933 was not a treaty between Iran and the United Kingdom.[^aioc] The *Anglo-Iranian Oil Co* case is significant for its holding that a concession agreement between a state and a foreign company is not an international treaty, notwithstanding the involvement of the company's home state in its negotiation.

[^aioc]: *Anglo-Iranian Oil Co (United Kingdom v Iran)*, Preliminary Objection, Judgment, ICJ Reports 1952, p. 93.

The same period witnessed the gradual replacement of fixed royalty arrangements with profit-sharing formulas. Beginning with Venezuela in 1943 and spreading to Saudi Arabia in 1950, the so-called "fifty-fifty" principle — under which the host government received approximately half of the profits from petroleum operations through a combination of royalties and income taxes — became the industry norm. The fifty-fifty principle represented a significant shift in the distribution of resource rents, but it left the structure of the concession — and the managerial control of the companies — largely intact.

### Phase Three: Decolonisation and the New International Economic Order (1960–1980)

The third phase was shaped by decolonisation and the emergence of a large number of newly independent states determined to assert control over their natural resources. Three developments were decisive.

First, in September 1960, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela founded the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). OPEC's initial objective was to resist reductions in posted prices unilaterally imposed by the companies, but it rapidly became a forum through which producing states coordinated their policies on pricing, taxation, and participation.

Second, the General Assembly of the United Nations adopted a series of resolutions articulating the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources. The most important of these, Resolution 1803 (XVII) of 14 December 1962, declared that "the right of peoples and nations to permanent sovereignty over their natural wealth and resources must be exercised in the interest of their national development and of the well-being of the people of the State concerned", while also providing that in cases of nationalisation "the owner shall be paid appropriate compensation, in accordance with the rules in force in the State taking such measures in the exercise of its sovereignty and in accordance with international law", and that foreign investment agreements "freely entered into by or between sovereign States shall be observed in good faith".[^r1803] Resolution 1803 represented a carefully negotiated compromise between capital-exporting and capital-importing states, and it is widely regarded as reflecting customary international law in significant respects.

[^r1803]: UN General Assembly Resolution 1803 (XVII), "Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources", 14 December 1962, paras 1, 4, and 8.

Third, in the early 1970s, producing states moved from profit-sharing to participation and outright nationalisation. Libya nationalised the interests of several foreign companies between 1971 and 1974; Algeria took a majority interest in French oil companies in 1971; Iraq nationalised the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1972; and the states of the Persian Gulf negotiated progressive participation agreements that culminated in full state ownership of their producing industries. The oil price increases of 1973–1974, associated with the Arab oil embargo and OPEC's assertion of control over pricing, transformed the economics of the industry and the balance of power between producing states and consuming states.

In 1974, the General Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order and the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States.[^cerds] Article 2 of the Charter asserted the right of every state "to nationalize, expropriate or transfer ownership of foreign property, in which case appropriate compensation should be paid by the State adopting such measures, taking into account its relevant laws and regulations and all circumstances that the State considers pertinent", and provided that any controversy over compensation "shall be settled under the domestic law of the nationalizing State and by its tribunals", unless otherwise agreed. Unlike Resolution 1803, the Charter omitted any reference to international law as the governing standard for compensation and was adopted over the opposition or abstention of most capital-exporting states. Its status as a statement of customary international law has accordingly been doubted.

[^cerds]: UN General Assembly Resolution 3201 (S-VI), "Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order", 1 May 1974; UN General Assembly Resolution 3281 (XXIX), "Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States", 12 December 1974.

The legal consequences of the nationalisations of this period were addressed in a series of landmark arbitral awards, often referred to collectively as the "Libyan oil arbitrations" — *BP v Libya* (1973), *Texaco v Libya* (1977), and *LIAMCO v Libya* (1977) — together with the *Aminoil* award of 1982. These awards, which are examined in detail in Chapters 3 and 14, grappled with the legal status of concession contracts, the effect of stabilisation clauses, the applicable law, and the appropriate standard of compensation. Their reasoning remains foundational to the modern law of foreign investment.
