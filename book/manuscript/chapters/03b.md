## 3.3 Public Trust Doctrine and State Stewardship

### Origins of the Public Trust Doctrine

The public trust doctrine holds that certain natural resources are held by the state in trust for the public and may not be alienated or managed in a manner inconsistent with the public interest. The doctrine traces its origins to Roman law, which recognised that certain things — the air, running water, the sea, and the shores of the sea — were common to all (*res communes*) under the Institutes of Justinian. It was received into English common law in relation to tidal waters and the foreshore, which the Crown held subject to the public rights of navigation and fishing, and it was developed most fully in the United States.

The leading American authority is *Illinois Central Railroad Co v Illinois* (1892), in which the United States Supreme Court held that the State of Illinois could not irrevocably convey to a private railroad company title to the submerged lands of Chicago's harbour on Lake Michigan, because those lands were held in trust for the people for purposes of navigation, commerce, and fishing.[^illinois] The Court reasoned that a state could no more abdicate its trust over property in which the whole people are interested than it could abdicate its police powers. The doctrine was revitalised by Joseph Sax's influential 1970 article, which proposed it as a tool for environmental protection, and was extended by the Supreme Court of California in *National Audubon Society v Superior Court* (1983) to require the state to consider the public trust in allocating water rights affecting Mono Lake.[^audubon]

[^illinois]: *Illinois Central Railroad Co v Illinois*, 146 US 387 (1892).

[^audubon]: *National Audubon Society v Superior Court of Alpine County*, 33 Cal 3d 419 (1983); see Joseph L. Sax, "The Public Trust Doctrine in Natural Resource Law: Effective Judicial Intervention" (1970) 68 *Michigan Law Review* 471.

### The Doctrine Beyond the United States

The public trust doctrine has been received and adapted in several other jurisdictions. The Supreme Court of India, in *M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath* (1997), held that the public trust doctrine was part of Indian law and that the state, as trustee of natural resources, was under a legal duty to protect them; it quashed a lease of riparian forest land granted to a motel company.[^mehta] Courts in Pakistan, the Philippines, Kenya, and South Africa have invoked the doctrine or analogous concepts. Section 3 of South Africa's Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act 28 of 2002 provides that "mineral and petroleum resources are the common heritage of all the people of South Africa and the State is the custodian thereof for the benefit of all South Africans", a formulation that replaced private mineral ownership with state custodianship. The Constitutional Court of South Africa, in *Agri South Africa v Minister for Minerals and Energy* (2013), held that this transition did not amount to an expropriation of private mineral rights requiring compensation, because the state had not acquired the rights for itself but had become their custodian.[^agrisa]

[^mehta]: *M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath* (1997) 1 SCC 388 (Supreme Court of India).

[^agrisa]: *Agri South Africa v Minister for Minerals and Energy* [2013] ZACC 9; 2013 (4) SA 1 (CC).

### Stewardship and Intergenerational Equity

The public trust doctrine is closely related to the concept of intergenerational equity, which holds that each generation holds the natural and cultural resources of the planet in trust for future generations. Edith Brown Weiss's formulation of the principle of intergenerational equity — comprising conservation of options, conservation of quality, and conservation of access — has been influential in international environmental law.[^weiss] In the context of non-renewable resources, intergenerational equity raises questions about the rate of extraction, the investment of resource revenues, and the obligation to leave future generations with comparable opportunities. Sovereign wealth funds, such as Norway's Government Pension Fund Global and Nigeria's Sovereign Investment Authority, established by the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority (Establishment, etc.) Act 2011 with a Future Generations Fund among its constituent funds, represent institutional responses to this concern.

[^weiss]: Edith Brown Weiss, *In Fairness to Future Generations: International Law, Common Patrimony, and Intergenerational Equity* (Transnational/United Nations University 1989).

Climate litigants have invoked the public trust doctrine to argue that governments hold the atmosphere in trust and are obliged to protect it. In *Juliana v United States*, young plaintiffs relied in part on the public trust doctrine to claim that the federal government's promotion of fossil fuels violated their constitutional rights; the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held in 2020 that the plaintiffs lacked standing because the relief sought was beyond the power of an Article III court to provide, and the litigation was ultimately terminated after the Supreme Court declined review in 2025.[^juliana] Courts in other jurisdictions have been more receptive to stewardship-based reasoning, as discussed in Chapter 18.

[^juliana]: *Juliana v United States*, 947 F 3d 1159 (9th Cir 2020).

### Legal Consequences of Stewardship

Where resources are held in trust, several legal consequences may follow. The state may be prohibited from alienating trust resources entirely or from granting rights that substantially impair the public interest. Grants of rights may be subject to implied conditions requiring their exercise consistently with the trust. Courts may review state decisions affecting trust resources with heightened scrutiny. And citizens may have standing to enforce the trust. These consequences can create tension with the security of tenure sought by investors, particularly where a court later concludes that a grant was inconsistent with the trust. Investors and their advisers should therefore assess, as part of legal due diligence, whether the host jurisdiction recognises a public trust doctrine or constitutional stewardship obligations that could affect the validity or scope of resource rights.

## 3.4 Federalism and Subnational Resource Authority

### The Federal Dilemma

In federal states, the allocation of authority over natural resources between the federal government and the constituent units is among the most politically charged constitutional questions. Resources are often geographically concentrated, so that their ownership and revenues may disproportionately benefit particular regions. Federal ownership may be perceived by producing regions as the appropriation of their wealth by the centre; subnational ownership may produce stark inequalities between resource-rich and resource-poor regions and undermine national cohesion. The resolution of this dilemma varies widely.

### Nigeria: Federal Ownership, Derivation, and Resource Control

Nigeria provides a paradigmatic example of the federal dilemma. Before independence and during the First Republic, the regions enjoyed significant fiscal autonomy and the principle of derivation — the allocation of revenue to the region from which it was derived — played a major role in revenue sharing. Following the civil war and the rise of petroleum as the dominant source of public revenue, the federal government progressively centralised control. The Petroleum Act 1969 vested ownership of petroleum in the state (the Federal Military Government), and successive constitutions confirmed federal ownership. The derivation percentage fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s.

The 1999 Constitution restored a minimum derivation component: section 162(2) provides that "the principle of derivation shall be constantly reflected in any approved formula as being not less than thirteen per cent of the revenue accruing to the Federation Account directly from any natural resources". The question of whether offshore revenues should be counted as derived from littoral states gave rise to the resource control litigation. In *Attorney-General of the Federation v Attorney-General of Abia State & 35 Others* (2002), the Supreme Court held that the seaward boundary of a littoral state for the purposes of the derivation principle was the low-water mark of its land surface (or the seaward limit of inland waters), so that revenues from offshore production on the continental shelf and in the EEZ accrued to the Federation rather than to the states. The decision was politically controversial and was effectively reversed in part by the Allocation of Revenue (Abolition of Dichotomy in the Application of the Principle of Derivation) Act 2004, which provided that the 200-metre water depth isobath would be the limit for the purposes of derivation, thereby treating shallow-water offshore production as derived from the adjoining littoral states.

The demand for "resource control" — greater control by producing states and communities over the resources found in their territories and the revenues derived from them — remains a central theme of Nigerian politics. It has been associated with the Ogoni struggle of the 1990s, the Kaiama Declaration of 1998, militancy in the Niger Delta in the 2000s, and the amnesty programme of 2009. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 responded in part by establishing host community development trusts funded by an annual contribution of three per cent of the actual annual operating expenditure of settlors (holders of interests in petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases) in the preceding financial year (section 240(2)), although critics argue that this falls far short of meaningful resource control.

### Electricity Federalism in Nigeria

In contrast to petroleum, electricity in Nigeria has undergone significant decentralisation. The Constitution (Fifth Alteration) (No. 33) Act 2023 amended the Exclusive and Concurrent Legislative Lists to permit states to legislate on electricity generation, transmission, and distribution within their territories. The Electricity Act 2023, which repealed the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005, provides a framework under which states may establish their own electricity markets and regulators, with the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission transferring regulatory oversight of intrastate markets to state regulators once established. A number of states, including Enugu, Ekiti, Ondo, Imo, Oyo, Edo, Kogi, and Lagos, have enacted electricity laws and established regulatory commissions, and regulatory oversight has been transferred to several of them. This process of electricity federalism is examined in Chapter 9.

### Canada: Provincial Ownership

In Canada, the provinces own the natural resources within their boundaries by virtue of section 109 of the Constitution Act 1867 (extended to the prairie provinces by the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930). Section 92A, added by the Constitution Act 1982 following prolonged federal–provincial conflict over energy pricing and taxation (notably the National Energy Program of 1980), confirms exclusive provincial legislative authority over exploration, development, conservation, and management of non-renewable natural resources, forestry resources, and electrical energy, and permits provinces to legislate on the export of such resources to other parts of Canada, subject to federal paramountcy. The federal government retains authority over offshore resources (confirmed by the Supreme Court of Canada in *Reference re Newfoundland Continental Shelf* (1984)), interprovincial and international pipelines, nuclear energy, and matters of national concern. Offshore resources off Newfoundland and Labrador and Nova Scotia are managed jointly through accords implemented by federal and provincial legislation.

The Supreme Court of Canada in *References re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act* (2021) upheld federal carbon pricing legislation under the "peace, order and good government" power as a matter of national concern,[^ggppa] while in *Reference re Impact Assessment Act* (2023) it held that significant parts of the federal impact assessment regime were unconstitutional because they reached beyond federal jurisdiction into matters of provincial competence.[^iaa]

[^ggppa]: *References re Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act*, 2021 SCC 11.

[^iaa]: *Reference re Impact Assessment Act*, 2023 SCC 23.

### Australia: State Ownership Onshore and Offshore Arrangements

In Australia, the states own onshore minerals and petroleum and regulate their development. Offshore, following the High Court's decision in *New South Wales v Commonwealth* (the *Seas and Submerged Lands Case*) (1975), which upheld Commonwealth sovereignty over the territorial sea and continental shelf, the Offshore Constitutional Settlement of 1979 allocated jurisdiction over the first three nautical miles of the territorial sea to the states, with the Commonwealth retaining authority beyond. Petroleum activities in Commonwealth waters are governed by the Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006, administered by the National Offshore Petroleum Titles Administrator and regulated for safety and environmental matters by the National Offshore Petroleum Safety and Environmental Management Authority (NOPSEMA).

### The United States: Layered Ownership

In the United States, mineral ownership is layered among private parties, states, the federal government, and Native American tribes. The federal government owns the mineral estate under approximately 700 million acres of land and administers leasing under the Mineral Leasing Act 1920 and, for the outer continental shelf beyond state waters (generally three nautical miles), under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act 1953. The Submerged Lands Act 1953 confirmed state title to submerged lands within three nautical miles of the coast (three marine leagues for Texas and the Gulf coast of Florida). Regulation of oil and gas production on private and state lands is primarily a matter of state law, administered by bodies such as the Railroad Commission of Texas.

## 3.5 Indigenous Peoples and Resource Governance

### The Encounter between Resource Development and Indigenous Rights

A large proportion of the world's remaining mineral, petroleum, and renewable energy resources lies on or beneath lands traditionally owned, occupied, or used by indigenous peoples. The encounter between resource development and indigenous rights has been one of the most significant sources of legal conflict in the field, and the recognition of indigenous rights has fundamentally altered the legal landscape for resource projects in many jurisdictions. Chapter 21 provides a detailed treatment of the international legal framework; this section focuses on the constitutional and property law dimensions.

### Recognition of Indigenous Title and Rights

The recognition of indigenous land rights has proceeded through constitutional provisions, legislation, judicial decisions, and treaty arrangements. In Australia, the High Court in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992) rejected the doctrine of *terra nullius* and recognised native title at common law,[^mabo] a recognition subsequently regulated by the Native Title Act 1993, which confers upon native title holders a "right to negotiate" with respect to certain future acts, including the grant of mining tenements. In *Wik Peoples v Queensland* (1996), the High Court held that pastoral leases did not necessarily extinguish native title.

[^mabo]: *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992) 175 CLR 1 (High Court of Australia).

In Canada, section 35 of the Constitution Act 1982 recognises and affirms existing aboriginal and treaty rights. The Supreme Court of Canada recognised aboriginal title in *Delgamuukw v British Columbia* (1997) and, for the first time, made a declaration of aboriginal title over a specific territory in *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia* (2014), holding that aboriginal title confers the right to decide how the land will be used and to enjoy its economic benefits, and that governments seeking to use titled land without consent must justify the infringement.[^tsilhqotin] The Court has also developed a duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate aboriginal peoples when the Crown contemplates conduct that might adversely affect asserted or established rights (*Haida Nation v British Columbia (Minister of Forests)*, 2004). These duties have major implications for pipeline and mining projects; in *Clyde River (Hamlet) v Petroleum Geo-Services Inc* (2017), the Supreme Court quashed an authorisation for offshore seismic testing because the consultation with the Inuit had been inadequate.

[^tsilhqotin]: *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia*, 2014 SCC 44, [2014] 2 SCR 257.

In Latin America, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights has developed a substantial jurisprudence on indigenous and tribal peoples' property rights under Article 21 of the American Convention on Human Rights. In *Saramaka People v Suriname* (2007), the Court held that the state must ensure the effective participation of the Saramaka people in development or investment plans within their territory, guarantee that they receive a reasonable benefit, and ensure that no concession is issued without a prior environmental and social impact assessment; for large-scale projects with a major impact, the state has a duty to obtain their free, prior, and informed consent.[^saramaka] In *Kichwa Indigenous People of Sarayaku v Ecuador* (2012), the Court found Ecuador responsible for permitting a petroleum company to carry out seismic exploration on Sarayaku territory without prior consultation.

[^saramaka]: *Saramaka People v Suriname*, Inter-American Court of Human Rights, Judgment of 28 November 2007, Series C No 172, paras 129–137.

In Africa, the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights in *Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council v Kenya* (2010) found that Kenya had violated the rights of the Endorois community by evicting them from their ancestral land around Lake Bogoria without adequate consultation or compensation, and the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights in *African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights v Republic of Kenya* (the *Ogiek* case, 2017) found violations of the Ogiek people's rights to their ancestral land in the Mau Forest.

### Indigenous Ownership of Subsurface Resources

A distinct question is whether indigenous peoples own the subsurface resources beneath their lands. In most regalian systems, the answer is no: the state's ownership of minerals extends beneath indigenous lands, and indigenous rights are limited to the surface and to rights of consultation, compensation, and benefit-sharing. There are exceptions. In the United States, tribes own the mineral estate beneath many reservation lands, and mineral development is governed by the Indian Mineral Leasing Act 1938 and the Indian Mineral Development Act 1982. In Australia's Northern Territory, the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976 confers a veto over exploration on Aboriginal land, subject to a national interest override. In Canada, the content of aboriginal title, as described in *Delgamuukw*, includes subsurface mineral rights.

The question has particular resonance in Africa, where the distinction between indigenous peoples and other local communities is often contested and where customary communities assert claims to resources beneath their lands. Under Nigerian law, such claims are foreclosed by section 44(3) of the Constitution, but communities may assert rights to compensation, environmental protection, and participation in benefit-sharing arrangements.
