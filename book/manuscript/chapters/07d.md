## 7.6 Mine Closure and Rehabilitation

### The Closure Challenge

Every mine eventually closes, whether because the ore body is exhausted, because commodity prices fall below the cost of production, or because of accidents, disputes, or regulatory action. Closure is not the end of a mine's legal life. Open pits, underground workings, waste rock dumps, and tailings facilities remain, and may require monitoring, maintenance, and treatment of water for decades or even in perpetuity. Historically, many mines were simply abandoned at the end of their lives, leaving a legacy of contaminated land and water, unsafe structures, and stranded communities. Thousands of abandoned mines in the United States, Canada, Australia, South Africa, and Europe, and many more in developing countries, impose ongoing costs on governments and communities. In Nigeria, the legacy of colonial and post-colonial tin and columbite mining on the Jos Plateau has left extensive areas of mine ponds and spoil heaps that have affected agriculture and public health. The modern law of mine closure seeks to ensure that closure is planned from the outset, that the costs are borne by the operator rather than the public, and that land is rehabilitated to a safe, stable, and productive condition.

### Closure Planning

Modern mining regimes require mining operators to prepare closure plans as part of their applications for mining licences, and to update them periodically during the life of the mine. A closure plan typically identifies the post-mining land use objectives, agreed where possible with affected communities and authorities; describes the measures for decommissioning infrastructure, stabilising landforms, revegetating disturbed land, managing water, and securing tailings facilities; estimates the cost of closure; and establishes criteria by which successful completion of closure will be assessed. Progressive rehabilitation — the rehabilitation of disturbed areas during the life of the mine, rather than at the end — is increasingly required, since it reduces the eventual closure liability and demonstrates the operator's commitment.

Closure plans must also address the social dimensions of closure: the loss of employment, the loss of revenue for local governments and suppliers, and the loss of services, such as health clinics, schools, and water supply, that the mine may have provided to the community. Social closure planning seeks to reduce the dependence of communities on the mine during its operating life and to support economic diversification. The ICMM's *Integrated Mine Closure: Good Practice Guide* (2nd edn, 2019) addresses both environmental and social dimensions.

### Financial Assurance

The central legal problem of mine closure is ensuring that funds are available to carry out closure when it is required. Operators may become insolvent, particularly at the end of a mine's life when revenues decline; parent companies may dispose of subsidiaries holding closure liabilities; and assets may be sold to under-capitalised purchasers. Mining laws therefore require operators to provide financial assurance — security for the estimated cost of closure — which the state may call upon if the operator fails to carry out its obligations. Forms of financial assurance include cash deposits, trust funds, bank guarantees and letters of credit, insurance bonds, and, in some jurisdictions, corporate guarantees or self-bonding (reliance on the financial strength of the operator itself). Self-bonding has been criticised as inadequate, following the bankruptcies of several major United States coal companies in 2015 and 2016, which left states facing large unfunded reclamation liabilities.

In Nigeria, the Minerals and Mining Act 2007 requires the holder of a mineral title to contribute to an Environmental Protection and Rehabilitation Fund, established to guarantee the environmental obligations of holders, in amounts determined by reference to the approved EPRP. South Africa's National Environmental Management Act 1998, as amended, and the Financial Provisioning Regulations made under it require holders of mining rights to make financial provision for the rehabilitation and remediation of environmental impacts, including latent and residual impacts, and to assess and adjust the provision annually. Western Australia's Mining Rehabilitation Fund Act 2012 established a pooled fund to which most tenement holders contribute an annual levy, used to rehabilitate abandoned mine sites, replacing the previous system of unconditional performance bonds for most tenements.

### Relinquishment and Completion Criteria

A critical question in mine closure law is when the operator's obligations end. Most regimes require the operator to demonstrate that closure objectives have been met and to obtain a certificate of closure or relinquishment from the regulator, after which the financial assurance is released and responsibility for the site passes to the state. Regulators are often reluctant to accept relinquishment, because of the risk of latent impacts emerging later, while operators seek certainty. Some regimes provide for residual liability funds or for long-term stewardship arrangements to manage sites that require perpetual care, such as those with acid mine drainage requiring permanent water treatment. The development of clear, measurable, and agreed completion criteria is essential to the effective operation of the closure regime.

### Closure and the Energy Transition

The energy transition creates both new closure challenges and new opportunities. The phase-out of coal mining in many countries — in Germany, the Coal Phase-out Act (Kohleausstiegsgesetz) of 2020 provides for the end of coal-fired power generation by 2038 at the latest, and in the United Kingdom the last coal-fired power station closed in 2024 — requires the closure and rehabilitation of large mining areas and support for affected communities, issues addressed by just transition policies examined in Chapters 18 and 23. Former mine sites may be repurposed for renewable energy generation, pumped hydro storage, or other uses, and mine waste may contain recoverable critical minerals. The reprocessing of tailings to recover minerals and the regulation of "mining waste as a resource" are emerging areas of law, examined in Chapter 8.

## 7.7 Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining Regulation

### The Scale and Significance of ASM

Artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) — mining by individuals, groups, families, or cooperatives with minimal mechanisation, often in the informal sector — is a major economic activity in many developing countries. The World Bank has estimated that around forty-five million people are directly engaged in ASM worldwide, and many more depend on it indirectly. ASM produces a significant share of the world's gold, tin, tantalum, tungsten, coloured gemstones, and diamonds, and a substantial share of the world's cobalt is produced by artisanal miners in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. For many rural households, ASM is a vital source of income and a buffer against poverty and agricultural failure.

At the same time, ASM is associated with serious problems: dangerous working conditions and frequent fatalities; child labour; environmental degradation, including deforestation, river siltation, and mercury pollution; conflict and the financing of armed groups; tax evasion and smuggling; and conflict with large-scale mining companies over overlapping rights. The use of mercury to amalgamate gold in artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) is the largest source of anthropogenic mercury emissions globally. In Nigeria, the lead poisoning epidemic in Zamfara State in 2010, caused by the artisanal processing of lead-rich gold ore in villages, killed several hundred children and was one of the worst lead poisoning outbreaks in modern history, prompting a major remediation effort.

### Legal Approaches to ASM

Historically, many mining laws ignored ASM or treated it as illegal, focusing exclusively on large-scale industrial mining. Criminalisation has generally failed: it drives ASM underground, exposes miners to extortion and violence, deprives the state of revenue, and makes it impossible to address environmental and safety problems. The prevailing approach in contemporary mining law is formalisation — bringing ASM within the legal framework through simplified licensing, technical and financial support, and integration into formal supply chains.

Formalisation strategies typically include: the creation of a distinct category of ASM licences or permits, with simplified application procedures, lower fees, and reduced technical requirements; the designation of areas reserved for ASM; the encouragement of cooperatives or associations to facilitate licensing, training, and access to finance; the provision of geological information, extension services, and equipment; the establishment of official buying centres to purchase ASM production, particularly of gold, at fair prices; and the integration of environmental, health, and safety requirements adapted to the scale of operations.

Nigeria's Minerals and Mining Act 2007 provides for the grant of small-scale mining leases to individuals, cooperatives, and companies, and requires artisanal miners to form cooperatives in order to obtain titles. The Act established the Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining Department within the Ministry to provide extension services, training, and support. The Presidential Artisanal Gold Development Initiative (PAGDI), launched in 2019, sought to formalise artisanal gold mining by establishing a framework under which the Central Bank of Nigeria would purchase gold from artisanal miners through licensed buying centres for inclusion in the country's external reserves. In 2024 the federal government established a Mining Marshals unit within the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps to combat illegal mining, reflecting concern over the links between illegal mining, insecurity, and the financing of armed groups in the north-west.

In Ghana, illegal artisanal mining, known as *galamsey*, has become a major political issue because of its devastating impact on rivers and forests. The Minerals and Mining (Amendment) Act 2019 (Act 995) increased penalties for illegal mining, and the government has periodically imposed bans on small-scale mining and deployed military task forces, with limited long-term success. The experience demonstrates the difficulty of reconciling the livelihood needs of artisanal miners with environmental protection where governance is weak.

### The Minamata Convention

The **Minamata Convention on Mercury**, adopted in 2013 and in force since 2017, addresses mercury use in ASGM. Article 7 requires each party with ASGM within its territory that is more than insignificant to take steps to reduce, and where feasible eliminate, the use of mercury and mercury compounds in, and the emissions and releases to the environment of mercury from, such mining and processing. Such parties must develop and implement a national action plan in accordance with Annex C, which must include national objectives and reduction targets, actions to eliminate the worst practices (whole ore amalgamation, open burning of amalgam, burning of amalgam in residential areas, and cyanide leaching in sediment, ore, or tailings to which mercury has been added without first removing the mercury), steps to facilitate formalisation, and strategies for promoting the reduction of mercury use. Nigeria is a party to the Convention and has developed a national action plan for ASGM.

### ASM and Large-Scale Mining

Conflicts between ASM and large-scale mining (LSM) are common, particularly where large-scale companies obtain titles over areas historically worked by artisanal miners. Such conflicts have led to violence, including forced evictions and abuses by security forces, examined in Chapter 21. Approaches to managing ASM–LSM coexistence include designating portions of large-scale concessions for ASM, establishing partnerships in which large-scale companies purchase ASM production or provide technical support, and resolving disputes through dialogue mechanisms. The Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights, examined in section 7.8, address the conduct of security forces in such situations.
