::: casebox {title="Case Study: The ICJ Advisory Opinion on Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change (2025) and its Implications for Resource Law"}
**Background.** In March 2023, the UN General Assembly, by consensus, adopted Resolution 77/276, initiated by Vanuatu and supported by a large group of states, requesting an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice on the obligations of states under international law to ensure the protection of the climate system and other parts of the environment from anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, and the legal consequences for states that, by their acts and omissions, have caused significant harm to the climate system. The proceedings attracted an unprecedented number of written and oral submissions from states and international organisations.

**The opinion.** In its unanimous opinion delivered on **23 July 2025**, the Court held, among other things, that states have obligations under the climate change treaties, including to prepare and pursue nationally determined contributions that collectively are capable of achieving the 1.5°C temperature goal, which the Court identified as the primary goal under the Paris Agreement; that states have obligations under customary international law, including the duty to prevent significant harm to the environment, which requires due diligence of a stringent standard in the context of climate change, and the duty to cooperate; that these obligations are informed by human rights law, the protection of the climate system being a precondition for the enjoyment of human rights; and that a breach of these obligations constitutes an internationally wrongful act entailing the full range of legal consequences, including cessation, guarantees of non-repetition, and full reparation, subject to the establishment of a sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus. The Court stated that the failure of a state to take appropriate action to protect the climate system, including through fossil fuel production, fossil fuel consumption, the granting of fossil fuel exploration licences, or the provision of fossil fuel subsidies, may constitute an internationally wrongful act attributable to that state.

**Implications for resource law.** Although advisory opinions are not binding, the opinion carries great authority and is likely to influence national courts, investment tribunals, and law-making. For energy and natural resources law, it raises fundamental questions: how the permanent sovereignty of states over their resources is constrained by climate obligations; whether and how states must assess the climate effects of licensing decisions, a question already addressed in national courts in *Finch* and related cases examined in Chapter 27; how the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities applies to developing country producers; whether fossil fuel subsidies are compatible with international law; how investment tribunals should treat climate measures; and what reparation may be owed by major emitters. The opinion thus marks a potential turning point in the relationship between climate law and resource law, whose consequences will be worked out over the coming decades.
:::

## Conclusion to the Book

International energy and natural resources law stands at a turning point. The legal order that governed the extraction and trade of fossil fuels for a century — built on concessions and contracts, permanent sovereignty and national oil companies, investment treaties and arbitration, and liberalised markets — is being transformed by the imperatives of decarbonisation, the rise of critical minerals, the digitalisation and decentralisation of energy systems, the recognition of climate risk, the claims of justice and human rights, and the fragmentation of the international order. The law that emerges will shape whether the world can meet its climate goals, whether resource-rich developing countries — including Nigeria and its African neighbours — can convert their resources into broad-based prosperity, whether the hundreds of millions of people without modern energy gain access to it, and whether the communities on the frontlines of resource development share in its benefits rather than bearing its burdens.

This book has sought to equip its readers with the knowledge, analytical skills, and methodological tools to engage with these challenges at the highest level: an understanding of the jurisprudential and institutional foundations of the field; command of the law governing petroleum, gas, mining, electricity, renewable energy, and emerging technologies; mastery of the international trade, investment, and contractual frameworks; a grounding in environmental, climate, and human rights law; comparative and geopolitical perspective; familiarity with dispute resolution; and the methods of doctoral research and law reform. Throughout, it has emphasised the importance of rigour, integrity, and attention to context — and, in particular, to the perspectives and experiences of Africa, whose resources and people will be central to the energy future.

The questions that remain unresolved are many and profound. Answering them will be the work of a generation. It is the hope of this book that its readers will be among those who answer them — as scholars, practitioners, regulators, judges, legislators, and leaders — and that they will do so with the knowledge, wisdom, and integrity the task demands.

::: summary
- Global energy governance is a fragmented regime complex being reshaped by the energy transition, the shift of demand to emerging economies, geopolitical fragmentation, the climate regime's engagement with energy, and the rise of clubs and minilateralism; Africa's role is pivotal but under-represented.
- Decarbonisation is shifting resource law from a paradigm of scarcity to one of stranding and managed decline, raising questions of licensing restrictions, phase-outs, compensation, decommissioning, and just transition, while creating demand for transition minerals, contests over deep seabed mining, and new resources such as storage space and natural hydrogen.
- AI transforms energy systems and drives demand for electricity, raising questions of liability, market conduct, safety, transparency, and fairness, addressed by instruments such as the EU AI Act; AI in regulation raises questions of automated decision-making.
- Digitalisation enables prosumers, aggregators, energy communities, and new market models, raising questions of market access, network tariffs, consumer protection, data ownership and access, data sovereignty, cyber security, and smart contracts.
- Climate change is a source of physical, transition, and liability risks, addressed through disclosure (TCFD, ISSB, CSRD), prudential regulation (NGFS, stress tests), taxonomies, and directors' and fiduciary duties.
- Emerging disputes include transition-related investment claims, trade disputes over green industrial policy and carbon border measures, critical minerals disputes, climate liability claims, disputes over digital energy and AI, and regulatory conflicts in a fragmented order.
- Unresolved questions concern sovereignty and the climate, the common heritage, equity in the transition, community rights, a right to energy, investment protection and the right to regulate, stability and change, the architecture of governance, corporate accountability, and the governance of technology.
- A doctoral research agenda for the next generation encompasses managed decline and just transition, transition minerals, investment law and climate, energy access and justice in Africa, Nigerian energy reforms, digital energy law, climate liability, resources beyond national jurisdiction, regional integration, and theory and method.
:::
