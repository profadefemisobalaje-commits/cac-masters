### Gas Supply Security Regulation Beyond Europe

The European experience is the most fully developed, but security of gas supply regulation exists in many jurisdictions. In Japan and Korea, which depend almost entirely on imported LNG, governments have encouraged buyers to maintain diversified portfolios of long-term contracts and have established strategic buffer LNG arrangements; Japan introduced a "strategic buffer LNG" mechanism in 2023 under which the state-backed Japan Organization for Metals and Energy Security (JOGMEC) may procure LNG for use in emergencies. China has imposed gas storage obligations on suppliers and local governments following winter shortages in 2017 and 2018, when an aggressive coal-to-gas switching policy outpaced supply. In the United States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the North American Electric Reliability Corporation have examined the interdependence of gas and electricity systems following Winter Storm Uri in Texas in February 2021, during which freezing of gas production and processing facilities contributed to the failure of the Texas electricity system and the loss of life. These experiences demonstrate that gas security is a function not only of supply sources but also of the physical resilience of infrastructure and the coordination of gas and power markets.

In Nigeria, the principal security of supply challenges are domestic: inadequate gas supply to power stations, attributable to pipeline vandalism, insufficient processing and transportation capacity, payment arrears in the electricity value chain, and the historic preference of producers for export markets that pay in foreign currency. The PIA's domestic gas delivery obligation, the Gas Transportation Network Code, and the initiatives of the Decade of Gas are intended to address these challenges, but their success depends on resolving the liquidity crisis in the electricity sector examined in Chapter 9, on the security of pipeline infrastructure, and on the gradual movement of domestic gas prices towards cost-reflective levels.

::: casebox
**The Gazprom–Naftogaz Arbitrations (SCC, 2017–2018)**

*Background.* Following the January 2009 gas crisis, Gazprom and Naftogaz of Ukraine concluded two ten-year contracts: a supply contract, under which Gazprom sold gas to Naftogaz for consumption in Ukraine, and a transit contract, under which Naftogaz transported Russian gas across Ukraine to European buyers. The supply contract contained a take-or-pay obligation for large annual quantities at an oil-indexed price that, following the fall in oil prices and the deterioration of relations after 2014, Naftogaz regarded as far above the market value of gas. In 2014 the parties commenced arbitration under the Rules of the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce, with claims by Gazprom for unpaid gas and take-or-pay amounts exceeding USD 30 billion, and claims by Naftogaz for a revision of the supply contract price, the removal of the take-or-pay obligation and the prohibition on re-export, and compensation for Gazprom's failure to deliver contracted transit volumes.

*Awards.* In the supply arbitration, the tribunal's final award of December 2017 substantially reduced Gazprom's take-or-pay claim, revised the contract price to reflect the market in continental Europe, and invalidated the prohibition on re-export, while ordering Naftogaz to pay for gas delivered but unpaid. In the transit arbitration, the tribunal's final award of February 2018 held that Gazprom had failed to deliver the agreed transit volumes and awarded Naftogaz damages of approximately USD 4.6 billion. After netting off the amounts awarded in the two proceedings, Gazprom owed Naftogaz approximately USD 2.56 billion. Gazprom's challenges before the Swedish courts were unsuccessful, and Naftogaz commenced enforcement proceedings against Gazprom's assets in several European jurisdictions. In December 2019, alongside the conclusion of a new five-year transit arrangement, Gazprom paid Naftogaz approximately USD 2.9 billion in settlement of the awards, and the parties withdrew pending claims.

*Significance.* The arbitrations are among the largest commercial arbitrations in history. They demonstrate the willingness of arbitral tribunals to revise price and volume provisions in long-term gas contracts where market circumstances have fundamentally changed and the contract so permits, and to sever export restrictions incompatible with competition law. They also illustrate the interaction between commercial arbitration and geopolitics: the enforcement of the awards and their eventual settlement were intertwined with intergovernmental negotiations over the future of gas transit through Ukraine, mediated by the European Commission.
:::

::: practice
**Drafting checklist: key protections for a buyer in a long-term LNG SPA**

1. Downward quantity tolerance sufficient to absorb foreseeable demand fluctuations, including those resulting from decarbonisation policies.
2. Destination flexibility (FOB delivery or DES with diversion rights) and the absence of profit-sharing on resale, subject to applicable competition law.
3. A price review clause with a clear trigger, a defined reference market, express authority for the tribunal to change the indexation, and a time limit for determination.
4. Shortfall remedies that are not exclusive in cases of wilful or deliberate non-delivery, and a cap on the seller's ability to invoke upstream supply failures as force majeure.
5. Quality specifications compatible with the buyer's terminal and downstream network, with clear allocation of the costs of treating off-specification LNG.
6. Methane intensity reporting and reduction commitments across the supply chain, aligned with applicable import regulations such as the EU Methane Regulation.
7. Termination rights for prolonged force majeure and for sanctions that render performance unlawful, and a neutral seat of arbitration in a New York Convention state.
:::

**Simulated price review arbitration (hypothetical).** *All parties, facts, and figures in this exercise are fictitious and created solely for teaching purposes.* Coastal Gas Exporters Ltd, a fictional liquefaction company in the fictional Republic of Ardenia, supplies 2 million tonnes per annum of LNG to Northern Utilities AG, a fictional utility in the fictional state of Valdora, under a twenty-year DES SPA concluded in 2015. The price is 14 per cent of the Brent crude oil price (three-month average) plus USD 0.50 per MMBtu. Clause 12 permits either party, every four years, to request a price review if "there has been a significant change in circumstances in the Buyer's Market, beyond the control of the Parties, as a result of which the Contract Price no longer reflects the value of LNG in the Buyer's Market", and provides that in default of agreement the tribunal "shall determine a revised Contract Price that reflects the value of LNG in the Buyer's Market". In 2025, Northern Utilities requests a price review, arguing that the Valdoran market is now priced predominantly by reference to TTF and that hub prices have been consistently below the contract price. Coastal Gas Exporters argues that the shift to hub pricing predated the 2021 review and that the 2021–2023 crisis showed that oil-indexed prices provide valuable security.

Divide into teams representing the claimant, the respondent, and the tribunal. (a) Prepare written submissions of not more than 2,000 words on the trigger question and the revision question, identifying the evidence each party would need (for example, comparable contract data and expert evidence on market value). (b) Conduct a thirty-minute oral hearing. (c) As the tribunal, draft the dispositive section of a reasoned award, addressing whether the tribunal has authority to introduce hub indexation into the price formula.

::: summary
- The physical properties of natural gas — low energy density, the need for fixed transport infrastructure, and variable composition — explain its distinctive legal regime: long-term contracts, regulation of network monopolies, and detailed provisions on quality, measurement, and balancing.
- Gas markets have developed as regional markets with distinct pricing systems — gas-on-gas competition at hubs such as Henry Hub, NBP, and TTF; oil indexation; regulated pricing; and spot LNG — although LNG trade has promoted convergence.
- Gas-specific upstream regulation addresses rights to non-associated gas, domestic supply obligations, fiscal incentives, and the restriction of flaring and venting, increasingly through binding methane regulation.
- Liberalisation has required third-party access to pipelines and LNG terminals, unbundling of network and supply activities, and regulation of tariffs, models that Nigeria's PIA has adopted for the midstream gas sector.
- LNG projects are structured as integrated, merchant, tolling, or hybrid projects, within host-state frameworks that frequently include project-specific statutes or agreements.
- Long-term gas sales agreements allocate volume risk through take-or-pay clauses and price risk through indexation and price review clauses; competition law has curtailed destination restrictions.
- Cross-border pipelines are governed by intergovernmental and host government agreements and by transit regimes in GATT Article V and ECT Article 7; African initiatives such as the WAGP illustrate the promise and difficulties of regional integration.
- Gas supply disputes — from the Egyptian supply failures to the European crisis of 2021–2023 — reveal the interaction between commercial contracts, investment protection, emergency regulation, and geopolitics.
:::

::: research
1. Has the take-or-pay clause outlived its economic rationale in liberalised gas markets, or does the experience of the 2021–2023 crisis demonstrate its continuing value for security of supply?
2. To what extent do price review tribunals act as market regulators, and what are the implications of the confidentiality of their awards for the development of a coherent body of gas law?
3. Evaluate the adequacy of GATT Article V and ECT Article 7 as frameworks for energy transit in the light of the Russia–Ukraine transit experience and the failure of the Energy Charter Transit Protocol.
4. How should the law reconcile domestic gas supply obligations in producing developing countries with export commitments and investment protection, in the light of the Egyptian experience?
5. Does the principle of energy solidarity in Article 194 TFEU, as interpreted in *Germany v Poland*, offer a model for regional energy integration in West Africa?
6. Can the extraterritorial reach of import-side methane regulation, such as the EU Methane Regulation, be reconciled with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and with WTO law?
