## 16.8 Debt Restructuring and Project Financial Distress

### Sources of Financial Distress

Energy projects and companies experience financial distress for many reasons: construction cost overruns and delays; operational underperformance; collapses in commodity prices, as in the oil price falls of 2014–2016 and 2020; the failure of offtakers to pay; currency devaluation that makes foreign currency debt unaffordable; changes in law or regulation that reduce revenues; and, increasingly, the stranding of fossil fuel assets as a result of climate policy and technological change. Distress may affect individual projects, entire sectors — such as state-owned utilities whose tariffs do not cover costs — and sovereign states whose public finances depend on resource revenues or which have borrowed against future resource production. The legal tools for managing distress differ at each level.

### Project-Level Distress: Waivers, Standstills, and Restructuring

When a project-financed energy project encounters difficulties, the finance documents examined in section 16.2 give lenders extensive rights: to block distributions, to declare events of default, to accelerate the debt, to enforce their security, and to step in under the direct agreements. In practice, however, enforcement is rarely the preferred option. The value of a project lies in its continued operation under its contracts and permits, which may be lost or impaired on enforcement; the market for distressed energy assets in emerging markets may be thin; and enforcement may require the consent of the host government to the transfer of licences or shares. Lenders therefore usually prefer consensual solutions: **waivers** of defaults; **standstill agreements**, under which lenders agree not to enforce for a period while the parties negotiate; **amendments** to the repayment profile, covenants, and financial ratios; injections of new equity or subordinated debt by sponsors; and, in more serious cases, comprehensive **restructurings**, which may involve the conversion of debt into equity, the transfer of ownership of the project to the lenders, or the sale of the project to a new sponsor.

Restructuring requires the cooperation of multiple classes of creditors with different interests — commercial banks, ECAs, DFIs, bondholders, and hedge counterparties — whose relationships are governed by the intercreditor agreement. Unanimity requirements for fundamental changes may allow individual creditors to hold out, and bonds are often more difficult to restructure than loans because of the dispersed ownership of bondholders. Lenders' **step-in rights** under direct agreements allow them to assume control of the project's contractual relationships, but lenders are generally reluctant to exercise them because doing so may expose them to the project company's liabilities.

### Restructuring Procedures and Insolvency

Where consensual restructuring fails, formal procedures may be used. In the United States, **Chapter 11** of the Bankruptcy Code allows a debtor to reorganise under court supervision, with an automatic stay on enforcement, the ability to obtain new financing with priority, and a plan of reorganisation that may be imposed on dissenting classes of creditors ("cram-down"), subject to statutory protections. Chapter 11 was extensively used by United States shale producers during the oil price collapses of 2015–2016 and 2020, when dozens of exploration and production companies filed for bankruptcy. A significant legal issue in these cases was whether debtors could reject long-term gathering and transportation agreements with midstream companies under the Bankruptcy Code's power to reject executory contracts, which depended on whether the dedication of production under those agreements constituted a covenant running with the land under state property law. Courts reached differing conclusions on the facts of different agreements, and the issue illustrates the interaction between insolvency law, property law, and long-term energy contracts.

In the United Kingdom, the **restructuring plan** introduced by the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020, as Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006, allows a company in financial difficulty to propose a compromise with its creditors which the court may sanction even if one or more classes of creditors vote against it, subject to conditions — a form of cross-class cram-down. Restructuring plans and the older schemes of arrangement have been used by companies in the energy and offshore services sectors, including foreign companies with English law-governed debt. Many other jurisdictions, including those of the European Union under the Directive on preventive restructuring frameworks (Directive (EU) 2019/1023), have adopted similar procedures. The **UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency** (1997), adopted by numerous states, facilitates the recognition of foreign insolvency proceedings and cooperation among courts, which is often essential in the restructuring of multinational energy groups. Nigeria's Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020 introduced company voluntary arrangements and administration procedures intended to promote corporate rescue.

### Utility Insolvency and Sector-Wide Distress

The financial distress of state-owned and privatised utilities poses distinctive problems because of their essential public service role. Where tariffs set by governments or regulators do not cover costs, and where losses through theft, non-payment, and technical inefficiency are high, utilities accumulate debts to generators, fuel suppliers, and lenders, which may cascade through the energy supply chain.

**South Africa**'s state utility **Eskom** illustrates the scale of the problem. Operational failures, cost overruns at new coal plants, and accumulated debt led to prolonged load-shedding and a debt burden that threatened the solvency of the utility and the fiscal position of the state. In 2023, the government enacted legislation providing debt relief to Eskom of approximately ZAR 254 billion, subject to conditions, and the utility is being restructured into separate generation, transmission, and distribution entities. In **Nigeria**, the electricity distribution companies created by the 2013 privatisation accumulated large shortfalls in remittances to the market, owing to tariffs below cost, high losses, and weak metering, leading to repeated government interventions, the takeover of several distribution companies by lenders, and the reforms of the Electricity Act 2023, examined in Chapters 9 and 24. In **Ghana**, energy sector arrears, including arrears to IPPs and gas suppliers, became a significant component of the public debt addressed in the country's IMF-supported programme and debt restructuring from 2022.

Utility distress raises legal questions concerning the enforceability of PPAs and guarantees, the priority of payments within the supply chain, the regulatory obligation to set cost-reflective tariffs, the protection of vulnerable consumers, and the potential application of insolvency law to state-owned enterprises, which are frequently excluded from ordinary insolvency procedures.

### Sovereign Debt and Natural Resources

Resource-rich states are particularly exposed to sovereign debt distress because of the volatility of resource revenues, and natural resources are directly linked to sovereign debt through **resource-backed loans** — loans repaid in, or secured on, future revenues from resource production. Such loans, provided by Chinese policy banks, commodity traders, and other lenders, have been used by Angola, the Republic of Congo, Chad, Venezuela, Ecuador, and others. They may enable states with limited market access to finance infrastructure, but they are often opaque, may carry high effective costs, and can complicate debt restructuring because the lenders have a privileged claim on resource revenues. Studies by the Natural Resource Governance Institute have documented the risks associated with resource-backed lending and called for greater transparency.

The restructuring of sovereign debt lacks a statutory framework comparable to corporate insolvency. Sovereign bonds increasingly contain **collective action clauses** allowing a qualified majority of bondholders to bind dissenting holders, and the **G20 Common Framework for Debt Treatments beyond the DSSI**, agreed in November 2020, provides a framework for coordinated restructuring of the official bilateral debt of low-income countries, including debt owed to China and other non-Paris Club creditors, with comparable treatment required from private creditors. Chad, Zambia, Ethiopia, and Ghana requested treatment under the Common Framework. Chad's restructuring, completed in 2022–2023, was notable for involving oil-backed debt owed to the commodity trader Glencore, and for incorporating a mechanism under which creditors would share in the upside of high oil prices. The slowness of Common Framework restructurings, particularly that of Zambia, which defaulted in 2020 and reached agreements with official and bondholder creditors only in 2023–2024, has led to calls for reform.

The relationship between sovereign debt and investment arbitration is complex. Bondholders have brought investment treaty claims arising from sovereign debt restructurings, as in *Abaclat v Argentina*, in which an ICSID tribunal accepted jurisdiction over a mass claim by Italian bondholders in 2011. Holders of arbitral awards against states — including energy investors — may compete with other creditors for the state's assets, as in the protracted enforcement proceedings against Venezuela and its state oil company PDVSA, which culminated in the court-supervised sale of shares in the parent company of CITGO Petroleum in the United States, examined in Chapter 26. Debt-for-nature and debt-for-climate swaps, under which debt is reduced in return for commitments to environmental expenditure, as in transactions involving Ecuador, Belize, and Gabon, illustrate the growing linkage between sovereign debt and environmental objectives, and the broader agenda of reform of the international financial architecture to address climate vulnerability, examined in Chapter 18.

### Stranded Assets and Transition Risk

The energy transition creates a distinctive form of financial distress: the **stranding** of fossil fuel assets whose economic life is cut short by climate policy, technological change, or shifts in demand. Coal-fired power plants financed on the assumption of thirty or forty years of operation may be forced to close early, and upstream petroleum reserves may become uneconomic to develop. Stranding raises questions of who should bear the losses — investors, lenders, consumers, or taxpayers — and whether compensation is payable, examined in Chapter 14 in the context of coal phase-out claims under the Energy Charter Treaty. Innovative financial mechanisms have been developed to accelerate the retirement of coal plants while managing financial losses, such as the Asian Development Bank's **Energy Transition Mechanism**, which proposes to use concessional finance to refinance or acquire coal plants and retire them earlier than planned, and transactions under the JETPs examined in section 16.1. Financial regulators, through the Network for Greening the Financial System and supervisory expectations on climate-related financial risk, increasingly require banks and insurers to assess their exposure to transition risk, an issue examined in Chapter 30.

**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. All parties, places, contracts, and events are fictitious and do not refer to any real persons, companies, or states.**

The Republic of Sabelia, a lower-middle-income West African state with a nascent gas industry, has selected a consortium led by Coastline Power Holdings to build a 450 MW gas-fired power plant. The total project cost is USD 600 million. The single buyer, Sabelia Bulk Power Company (SBPC), a state-owned entity with a history of payment arrears, will purchase the plant's output under a twenty-year take-or-pay PPA with tariffs denominated in US dollars but payable in the local currency, the Sabelian dinar. Gas will be supplied by the national oil company under a fuel supply agreement. Proposed lenders include a commercial bank syndicate, an export credit agency, and a multilateral DFI.

Sabelia's petroleum law imposes a royalty of ten per cent, corporate income tax of thirty per cent, and a production levy of two per cent of operating expenditure for a host communities fund. The Ministry of Finance has proposed a ten-year tax holiday for the project. Coastline proposes to finance part of its equity through a shareholder loan from a Mauritius-incorporated affiliate at an interest rate of twelve per cent.

Five years after commercial operation, the Sabelian dinar has lost sixty per cent of its value against the US dollar; SBPC has accumulated eighteen months of arrears; the project company is in breach of its DSCR covenants; and the government, facing a sovereign debt crisis, has requested treatment under an international debt restructuring framework. Meanwhile, a newspaper has alleged that a consultant engaged by Coastline during the procurement process made payments to an official of the Ministry of Energy.

**Tasks.**

(a) Prepare a term sheet outlining the credit support package that the lenders are likely to require from the government and multilateral institutions, and assess the contingent liabilities it would create for Sabelia.

(b) Advise the Ministry of Finance on whether the proposed tax holiday is advisable, taking into account the global minimum tax and the interests of the state.

(c) Advise the Sabelian Revenue Authority on how it should approach the deductibility of interest on the shareholder loan.

(d) Advise the lenders on the options available to them following the currency devaluation and arrears, and on the risks of each option.

(e) Advise Coastline on the potential consequences of the corruption allegation for the project contracts, any future arbitration, and the company's exposure under anti-bribery laws, and recommend immediate steps.

::: summary
- Project finance enables the financing of large energy projects on a limited-recourse basis through special purpose vehicles, high leverage, contractual risk allocation, comprehensive security, and cash-flow controls.
- The capital structure and finance documents allocate risks among sponsors and diverse lenders — commercial banks, ECAs, DFIs, capital markets, and Islamic financiers — and impose environmental and social standards such as the IFC Performance Standards and the Equator Principles.
- Government support agreements, sovereign guarantees, put and call options, and multilateral instruments such as MIGA cover and World Bank guarantees mitigate political and credit risks, but create contingent liabilities that must be managed transparently.
- Petroleum and mining fiscal regimes combine bonuses, royalties, income taxes, resource rent taxes, production sharing, and state participation, and are evaluated by reference to revenue, progressivity, neutrality, risk-sharing, stability, and administrability.
- Royalties provide early and secure revenue but are regressive; resource rent taxes are neutral and progressive but depend on administrative capacity; windfall taxes respond to price shocks but undermine stability.
- Transfer pricing, intra-group financing, indirect transfers, BEPS, and the global minimum tax are central to the taxation of multinational energy enterprises and to the revenue of resource-rich states.
- Revenue transparency through the EITI, mandatory payment disclosure, contract transparency, and beneficial ownership registers, together with anti-bribery laws, address the high corruption risk of the sector, and corruption may render contracts and awards unenforceable.
- Financial distress at the levels of projects, utilities, and sovereigns is managed through consensual restructuring, insolvency procedures, sector reform, and sovereign debt frameworks, with the energy transition adding the challenge of stranded assets.
:::
