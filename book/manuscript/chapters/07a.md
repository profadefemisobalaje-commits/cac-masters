## Introduction

Mining is one of humanity's oldest economic activities and one of its most legally complex. Minerals extracted from the earth supply the metals of modern infrastructure and technology, the fertilisers of agriculture, the materials of construction, and, increasingly, the components of the energy transition: copper for electrical networks, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and graphite for batteries, rare earth elements for permanent magnets in wind turbines and electric motors, and silver and silicon for solar panels. At the same time, mining transforms landscapes, consumes vast quantities of water and energy, generates enormous volumes of waste rock and tailings, displaces communities, and has been associated in many countries with conflict, corruption, and human rights abuses. The law of mining must reconcile the interests of states seeking revenue and development, investors seeking secure and profitable titles, communities seeking protection and a fair share of benefits, and the broader public interest in environmental protection.

Mining law shares many features with petroleum law, examined in Chapter 5: both regulate the extraction of non-renewable subsurface resources, both are founded on the state's ownership or control of those resources (examined in Chapter 3), and both rely on systems of titles, fiscal regimes, and contracts to allocate rights and revenues between states and investors. But mining differs from petroleum in important respects. Mineral deposits are fixed in place and do not migrate across boundaries in the manner of petroleum reservoirs, so the rule of capture and unitisation are rarely relevant. The diversity of minerals — from bulk commodities such as iron ore and coal, to base metals such as copper and zinc, to precious metals and gemstones, to industrial minerals and construction materials — and the diversity of operators — from multinational corporations operating giant open-pit mines to millions of individual artisanal miners using hand tools — produce a far more heterogeneous regulatory landscape. Mining also has a much larger physical footprint than most petroleum operations and a longer environmental legacy, since tailings facilities and waste dumps must be managed in perpetuity after mining ends.

The modern history of mining law in developing countries has been characterised by successive waves of reform. In the 1960s and 1970s, many newly independent states nationalised their mining industries, as Zambia did with its copper mines in 1969–1970 and Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo) did with the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga in 1967. The poor performance of many state mining enterprises, combined with the debt crises of the 1980s, led in the 1990s to a wave of liberalising reforms, often supported by the World Bank, which privatised state enterprises and adopted investor-friendly mining codes offering secure titles, low royalties, and generous fiscal incentives. The commodity price boom of the 2000s, driven by Chinese demand, led many states to conclude that these codes had transferred too great a share of mineral rents to investors, prompting a third wave of reforms — revised mining codes in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2018), Tanzania (2017), Zambia, Guinea, and elsewhere — that increased royalties and state participation, curtailed stability guarantees, and strengthened local content and community development requirements. The Africa Mining Vision, adopted by African Union heads of state in 2009, articulates the aspiration for "transparent, equitable and optimal exploitation of mineral resources to underpin broad-based sustainable growth and socio-economic development".

This chapter examines the core institutions of mining law. It begins with mineral rights and mining titles (section 7.1), exploration permits and mining licences (section 7.2), and mining concessions and investment agreements (section 7.3). It then examines environmental and social obligations (section 7.4), community development and benefit sharing (section 7.5), and mine closure and rehabilitation (section 7.6). It concludes with the regulation of artisanal and small-scale mining (section 7.7) and international standards for responsible mining (section 7.8). Chapter 8 addresses the distinctive legal issues raised by critical minerals, and Chapter 20 considers deep-seabed mining under the law of the sea.

## 7.1 Mineral Rights and Mining Titles

### Ownership of Minerals

The starting point of any mining regime is the ownership of minerals in their natural state. As explained in Chapter 3, legal systems allocate subsurface mineral ownership according to two broad models: the *accession* model, under which minerals belong to the owner of the surface land (the position at common law, retained for most minerals in the United States in respect of private land); and the *domanial* or *regalian* model, under which minerals are owned or controlled by the state irrespective of surface ownership (the position in most civil law jurisdictions and in almost all developing countries). Even where the accession principle applies, certain minerals are often reserved to the Crown or state: in the United Kingdom, gold and silver in their natural state belong to the Crown under the *Case of Mines* (1568), and coal was nationalised under the Coal Industry Nationalisation Act 1946 and is now vested in the Coal Authority.

In Nigeria, section 44(3) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 vests "the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils and natural gas in, under or upon any land in Nigeria or in, under or upon the territorial waters and the Exclusive Economic Zone of Nigeria" in the Government of the Federation, and "mines and minerals, including oil fields, oil mining, geological surveys and natural gas" appear in the Exclusive Legislative List. Section 1 of the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007 reiterates that the property in and control of all mineral resources in, under, or upon any land in Nigeria is vested in the Government of the Federation for and on behalf of the people of Nigeria. The exclusive federal competence over minerals has been controversial in a federation where states and communities bear the environmental and social costs of mining, and it has complicated the regulation of artisanal mining, which is often more effectively supervised at local level. Proposals to devolve control of solid minerals to the states have featured in constitutional reform debates.

In South Africa, the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act 2002 (MPRDA) made a fundamental change. Before the Act, mineral rights in South Africa could be privately owned and severed from surface ownership, and were concentrated in the hands of a small number of predominantly white-owned mining houses. Section 3(1) of the MPRDA declares that mineral and petroleum resources are "the common heritage of all the people of South Africa" and that the state is "the custodian thereof for the benefit of all South Africans". The Act abolished private ownership of unexploited mineral rights and required holders of "old order rights" to convert them into new order rights granted by the state within transitional periods. In *Agri South Africa v Minister for Minerals and Energy* (2013), the Constitutional Court held by a majority that the MPRDA did not effect an expropriation of the old order rights requiring compensation, because the state had not acquired the rights for itself but had become their custodian.[^agrisa] The decision is significant for the constitutional theory of resource ownership and for the distinction between deprivation and expropriation, examined in Chapter 3.

[^agrisa]: *Agri South Africa v Minister for Minerals and Energy* [2013] ZACC 9, 2013 (4) SA 1 (CC).

### The Nature of Mining Titles

Where minerals belong to the state, a person may lawfully explore for or extract them only by virtue of a title granted by the state. As with petroleum titles, the legal nature of mining titles varies among legal systems. In civil law systems influenced by French law, mining titles have traditionally been characterised as concessions — administrative acts conferring real rights in immovable property that may be mortgaged, transferred with consent, and protected against arbitrary withdrawal. In common law systems, mining leases and licences are statutory rights, the nature of which depends on the construction of the statute, but which are commonly treated as property for the purposes of constitutional protection and as capable of being mortgaged and transferred. In many African mining codes, mining titles are expressly declared to be real rights or immovable property, distinct from the ownership of the surface land, in order to facilitate their use as security for project finance.

Mining titles usually confer the exclusive right to carry out the specified activity within the title area for the specified minerals, together with ancillary rights to use land, water, and timber, to construct infrastructure, and to dispose of the minerals produced. They are subject to obligations: to carry out work programmes, to pay fees, royalties, and taxes, to comply with environmental, health, safety, and labour requirements, to report to the regulator, and to rehabilitate the land at the end of operations.

### Systems for Allocating Mining Titles

Mining regimes employ three principal methods for allocating titles: first-come, first-served; competitive tender; and administrative discretion.

Under the **first-come, first-served** system, the first qualified applicant to apply for an available area is entitled to the title, provided that it meets the statutory requirements. The system has its origins in the "free mining" tradition of the American West, under which prospectors could stake claims on public land by physically marking the boundaries and recording the claim; the United States General Mining Law of 1872 still permits citizens to locate claims to "valuable mineral deposits" on open federal lands. In modern regimes, the first-come, first-served system is typically administered through an electronic mining cadastre that records applications in order of receipt and grants titles to the first applicant that satisfies objective criteria. Its advantages are simplicity, transparency, and the reduction of administrative discretion, which limits opportunities for corruption. It is well suited to exploration licences over areas where geological information is limited and the value of the area is uncertain.

Under the **competitive tender** system, titles over areas of known prospectivity — for example, areas where the state has carried out geological surveys or where previous exploration has identified a deposit — are allocated through a competitive bidding process, with bids evaluated on the basis of work programmes, financial terms, technical capacity, or a combination of these. Competitive tender allows the state to capture a greater share of the value of known deposits, but requires greater administrative capacity and robust procedures to prevent manipulation.

Under **administrative discretion**, the minister or another authority grants titles on the basis of a discretionary assessment of the merits of applications. Discretionary systems have been associated with corruption and the allocation of rights to politically connected persons, and the trend in mining law reform has been to reduce discretion and to rely on objective criteria administered by an independent cadastre.

Many regimes combine these methods: first-come, first-served for exploration licences in open areas, competitive tender for areas of known prospectivity, and some residual discretion for strategic deposits.

### The Mining Cadastre

The mining cadastre — the official register of mining titles and applications — is the central administrative institution of a modern mining regime. A well-functioning cadastre records the location, holder, type, status, and obligations of every title; prevents overlapping grants; processes applications according to transparent rules; monitors compliance with title obligations, such as payment of annual fees and fulfilment of work programmes; and makes information publicly available. Electronic cadastres, often implemented with support from the World Bank and other development partners, have been introduced in many African states, including Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Zambia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Mozambique, many offering online portals that enable investors and the public to view title information.

In Nigeria, the Minerals and Mining Act 2007 established the Mining Cadastre Office (MCO) as the agency responsible for the administration of mineral titles and the maintenance of the cadastral register. The Act provides for applications to be processed on a first-come, first-served basis, and the MCO operates an electronic mining cadastre system (eMC+), which enables online applications and public access to the cadastral map. The Act provides for the revocation of titles for failure to pay annual service fees or to comply with work obligations, and the MCO has periodically revoked large numbers of dormant titles held for speculative purposes.

### Surface Rights and Land Access

Because mineral ownership is usually separated from surface ownership, mining law must regulate the relationship between title holders and surface owners or occupiers. Mining codes typically confer on title holders a right of access to the land, subject to obligations to obtain the consent of, or give notice to, surface owners and occupiers; to pay compensation for disturbance of surface rights, damage to crops, and loss of use of land; and in some cases to acquire the land through compulsory acquisition by the state. The adequacy of compensation, the treatment of customary land rights, and the relationship between mining law and land law are recurrent sources of conflict, particularly in Africa, where much land is held under customary tenure without formal registration.

In Nigeria, the Land Use Act 1978 vests all land in each state in the governor, to be held in trust for the use and common benefit of all Nigerians, and the Minerals and Mining Act 2007 provides that land required for mining purposes shall have priority over other uses, subject to the payment of compensation to the owners or occupiers for disturbance of surface rights, damage to crops, and other loss. In South Africa, the Constitutional Court held in *Maledu v Itereleng Bakgatla Mineral Resources (Pty) Ltd* (2018) that the holder of a mining right could not evict a community holding informal land rights protected by the Interim Protection of Informal Land Rights Act 1996 without complying with that Act, which requires the consent of the community or the expropriation of the rights with compensation.[^maledu] In *Baleni v Minister of Mineral Resources* (2018), concerning the proposed Xolobeni titanium mine on the Wild Coast, the High Court held that the Minister could not grant a mining right over land occupied by the Umgungundlovu community under informal rights without obtaining the community's full and informed consent.[^baleni] These decisions are significant for the emergence of community consent requirements in domestic mining law, examined further in Chapter 21.

[^baleni]: *Baleni v Minister of Mineral Resources* [2018] ZAGPPHC 829, 2019 (2) SA 453 (GP).
