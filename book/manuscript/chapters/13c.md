## 13.4 Export Restrictions and Resource Security

### The Asymmetry of Trade Disciplines on Exports

The multilateral trading system has historically focused on import barriers, and its disciplines on export measures are weaker and less developed. Article XI:1 of the GATT prohibits export prohibitions and restrictions, other than duties, taxes, or other charges, in the same terms as import restrictions. But **export duties** are not prohibited by the GATT, which permits members to impose them subject only to the MFN obligation, and most members have not bound their export duties in their schedules of concessions. Export duties may be disciplined only through specific commitments, such as those undertaken by China, Russia, Viet Nam, and other acceding members in their accession protocols, or through regional trade agreements. This asymmetry reflects the historical bargaining dynamics of the GATT, in which the dominant importing states did not seek, and resource-exporting states resisted, disciplines on export measures.

The weakness of export disciplines has become increasingly significant as states have used export restrictions to promote domestic processing of raw materials (resource-based industrialisation), to stabilise domestic prices, to conserve resources, and to exert geopolitical leverage. The use of export restrictions on critical minerals — including by China, Indonesia, and others — has been examined in Chapter 8. This section examines the broader framework of export restrictions on energy commodities.

### The Exceptions to Article XI

Article XI:2(a) permits "export prohibitions or restrictions temporarily applied to prevent or relieve critical shortages of foodstuffs or other products essential to the exporting contracting party". Article XX(g) permits measures relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources made effective in conjunction with restrictions on domestic production or consumption; Article XX(i) permits restrictions on exports of domestic materials necessary to ensure essential quantities to a domestic processing industry during periods when the domestic price is held below the world price as part of a governmental stabilisation plan, provided that such restrictions do not operate to increase the exports of or the protection afforded to the domestic industry; and Article XX(j) permits measures essential to the acquisition or distribution of products in general or local short supply.

The interpretation of these exceptions was developed in two disputes concerning Chinese export restrictions. In *China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Various Raw Materials* (2012), the Appellate Body upheld the panel's findings that China's export duties and quotas on bauxite, coke, fluorspar, magnesium, manganese, silicon carbide, silicon metal, yellow phosphorus, and zinc violated its accession commitments and Article XI:1, and held that China could not invoke Article XX to justify export duties that violated its accession protocol, which contained no express reference to Article XX. It interpreted Article XI:2(a) to permit restrictions only of limited duration to address situations of decisive importance, rather than long-term measures to conserve resources. In *China — Measures Related to the Exportation of Rare Earths, Tungsten, and Molybdenum* (2014), the Appellate Body again rejected China's Article XX(g) defence, holding that a measure must not only relate to conservation but must also be "made effective in conjunction with" restrictions on domestic production or consumption — that is, the burdens of conservation must be shared even-handedly between foreign and domestic consumers — and that China's export quotas, which were not matched by comparable restrictions on domestic consumption, were designed to secure supplies for domestic industry rather than to conserve resources. These decisions, together with the panel report in *Indonesia — Raw Materials* (2022), examined in Chapter 8, establish that export restrictions designed to promote domestic processing are difficult to justify under the GATT's exceptions.

### OPEC and Production Quotas

The most significant export-related measures in the energy sector are the production quotas agreed by the members of OPEC and, since 2016, the wider "OPEC+" group, which includes Russia and other non-OPEC producers under the Declaration of Cooperation. Production quotas restrict the supply of crude oil and thereby raise world prices, and their compatibility with WTO law has been debated.

The arguments that OPEC production quotas fall outside, or are justified under, the GATT include: first, that production quotas restrict production, not exports, and Article XI applies only to measures applied at the border; second, that the decisions of sovereign states on the exploitation of their natural resources fall within the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources; third, that production quotas may be justified under Article XX(g) as measures for the conservation of exhaustible natural resources, given that they restrict domestic production; and fourth, that OPEC decisions are not themselves measures of WTO members, but intergovernmental decisions implemented by members. The arguments to the contrary include that production quotas have the effect of restricting exports in a manner equivalent to an export quota, particularly for states that export most of their production, and that the term "other measures" in Article XI:1 is broad enough to cover them. No WTO member has challenged OPEC production quotas, reflecting the political sensitivity of the issue and the fact that many members, including major importers, also have interests in oil production. The application of competition law to OPEC has been attempted in the United States, but courts have dismissed antitrust claims against OPEC and its members on the basis of the act of state doctrine and sovereign immunity, as in *International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers v OPEC* 649 F.2d 1354 (9th Cir 1981). Legislative proposals to remove these obstacles (the "NOPEC" bill) have been introduced repeatedly in the United States Congress without enactment.

### Dual Pricing

**Dual pricing** refers to the practice of selling energy domestically at prices lower than export prices, typically through export restrictions, export taxes, or price controls. Dual pricing benefits domestic consumers and energy-intensive industries, such as petrochemicals, fertilisers, and steel, which obtain feedstock at below world market prices. Importing states have argued that dual pricing constitutes a subsidy to downstream industries. Under the SCM Agreement, the provision of goods by a government at less than adequate remuneration may constitute a financial contribution and a benefit, and the question whether low domestic energy prices resulting from export restrictions constitute a subsidy has arisen in countervailing duty investigations. In *US — Softwood Lumber IV* (2004), the Appellate Body held that an investigating authority may use a benchmark other than private prices in the country of provision when those prices are distorted by the government's predominant role in the market, and in *US — Countervailing Measures on Certain Hot-Rolled Carbon Steel Flat Products from India* (2014), it addressed the provision of iron ore by a state-owned enterprise. However, the panel in *US — Measures Treating Export Restraints as Subsidies* (2001) held that an export restraint does not in itself constitute a "financial contribution" in the form of government-entrusted or directed provision of goods, and the Appellate Body in *US — Countervailing Duty Investigation on DRAMS* (2005) emphasised that entrustment or direction requires more than mere encouragement or policy pronouncements. As noted in section 13.3, in *EU — Biodiesel (Argentina)* (2016), the Appellate Body rejected the EU's adjustment of costs reflecting distortions caused by Argentine export taxes in anti-dumping investigations. The accession commitments of Saudi Arabia and Russia on the pricing of natural gas, noted in section 13.1, reflect the concerns of other members about dual pricing.

### Export Licensing of LNG and Energy Security

Energy-importing states have also restricted exports of energy for reasons of energy security and domestic price stability. In the United States, the Natural Gas Act requires authorisation by the Department of Energy for exports of natural gas, including LNG. Exports to countries with which the United States has a free trade agreement requiring national treatment for trade in natural gas are deemed to be in the public interest and must be authorised without modification or delay, while exports to other countries must be authorised unless the Department finds that they are not consistent with the public interest. In January 2024, the Department paused the consideration of new authorisations of LNG exports to non-FTA countries while it updated its analysis of the economic and environmental effects of exports; the pause was challenged by several states and was lifted in January 2025. Some states have restricted exports of energy during crises: Australia introduced the Australian Domestic Gas Security Mechanism in 2017, empowering the government to restrict LNG exports where there is a domestic shortfall, and China and India have periodically restricted exports of refined products and coal. Such measures may raise questions under Article XI of the GATT, which may be answered by Article XI:2(a) or Article XX(j) in cases of genuine critical shortage, or by Article XXI in emergencies in international relations.

### Export Restrictions and Development

The debate over export restrictions has a significant development dimension. Many resource-rich developing states, including in Africa, regard export restrictions and export taxes on unprocessed raw materials as legitimate instruments for promoting domestic value addition, industrialisation, and economic diversification, and point to the historical use of such measures by now-developed states. The **Africa Mining Vision** adopted by the African Union in 2009 calls for the development of resource-based industrialisation through linkages, including the processing of minerals within Africa. Nigeria's policy of encouraging domestic refining of crude oil, including through the "domestic crude supply obligation" under the PIA, which requires producers to supply crude oil to domestic refineries on a willing-buyer, willing-seller basis, raises comparable questions about the relationship between resource-based industrialisation policies and trade disciplines. Whether WTO law should accommodate export restrictions for development purposes, through special and differential treatment or reinterpretation of the exceptions, is a significant question for doctoral research.

## 13.5 Technical Standards and Market Access

### The TBT Agreement

The **Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade** (TBT Agreement) governs technical regulations (mandatory requirements laying down product characteristics or their related processes and production methods), standards (voluntary requirements approved by a recognised body), and conformity assessment procedures (procedures to determine whether technical regulations or standards are fulfilled). Energy-related technical regulations and standards are pervasive: fuel quality specifications, energy efficiency standards for appliances and vehicles, emissions standards, safety standards for energy equipment, labelling requirements, and sustainability criteria for biofuels and hydrogen. Such measures pursue legitimate objectives but may create barriers to trade, especially where they differ between markets or are designed to favour domestic producers.

The TBT Agreement imposes several obligations on technical regulations. Article 2.1 requires members to accord to imported products treatment no less favourable than that accorded to like products of national origin and to like products originating in any other country. As noted in section 13.2, the Appellate Body held in *US — Clove Cigarettes* (2012) that Article 2.1 does not prohibit detrimental impact on imports that stems exclusively from a legitimate regulatory distinction. In *US — COOL* (2012) and *US — Tuna II (Mexico)* (2012), it elaborated the test, examining whether the regulatory distinction was designed and applied in an even-handed manner. Article 2.2 requires that technical regulations not be prepared, adopted, or applied with a view to or with the effect of creating unnecessary obstacles to international trade, and not be more trade-restrictive than necessary to fulfil a legitimate objective, taking account of the risks non-fulfilment would create; legitimate objectives include national security requirements, the prevention of deceptive practices, and the protection of human health or safety, animal or plant life or health, or the environment. Article 2.4 requires members to use relevant international standards as a basis for their technical regulations, except when such standards would be ineffective or inappropriate for the fulfilment of the legitimate objectives pursued. Article 2.7 encourages members to accept as equivalent the technical regulations of other members if satisfied that they adequately fulfil the objectives of their own regulations, and Article 6 encourages the mutual recognition of conformity assessment.

The TBT Agreement's application to measures based on NPR-PPMs is contested, because the definition of a technical regulation refers to "product characteristics or their related processes and production methods", which has been interpreted by some as limited to processes and production methods that affect the characteristics of the final product. The question has significant implications for energy and climate measures based on the carbon intensity of production, such as low-carbon hydrogen standards, biofuel sustainability criteria, and low-carbon product standards for steel and cement. In *EC — Seal Products* (2014), the Appellate Body held that the EU's measure prohibiting the sale of seal products, subject to exceptions, was not a technical regulation because it did not lay down product characteristics, but did not resolve the broader question of NPR-PPMs.

### Fuel Standards and Energy Efficiency Regulations

Fuel standards are among the most important energy-related technical regulations. Low carbon fuel standards, which require reductions in the average carbon intensity of transport fuels over their life cycle, have been adopted by California, Oregon, Washington, and British Columbia, and the EU's Fuel Quality Directive (Directive 98/70/EC, as amended) required fuel suppliers to reduce the life-cycle greenhouse gas intensity of fuels by six per cent by 2020. Such standards assign different carbon intensities to fuels according to their feedstock and production pathway, including higher values for fuels derived from oil sands and other high-intensity crude oils, and have raised trade concerns from producing states. Canada objected to proposals under the Fuel Quality Directive to assign a specific default value to fuels derived from oil sands, which was not adopted in the final implementing measure. Within the United States, California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard was challenged under the dormant Commerce Clause, which prohibits state measures that discriminate against interstate commerce; in *Rocky Mountain Farmers Union v Corey* 730 F.3d 1070 (9th Cir 2013), the Court of Appeals held that the Standard's life-cycle analysis, which took account of emissions from transportation and electricity used in production, did not facially discriminate against out-of-state fuels.

Energy efficiency standards and labelling requirements for appliances, equipment, buildings, and vehicles are widespread and generally uncontroversial in trade terms, provided that they are applied without discrimination. The EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781) extends ecodesign requirements beyond energy-related products to most physical goods placed on the EU market, including requirements relating to durability, repairability, recycled content, and carbon footprint, and requires the introduction of digital product passports. Such measures have extraterritorial effects on producers in exporting states, which must comply with them to access the EU market — a phenomenon sometimes described as the "Brussels effect".

### Market Access for Energy Services

Market access for energy services under the GATS depends on members' specific commitments in their schedules, which specify the sectors in which a member undertakes market access and national treatment obligations and any limitations. Commitments in energy-related services have been limited, reflecting the sensitivity of the energy sector and the prevalence of state ownership and exclusive rights. Many members have made commitments in services incidental to mining and in some engineering and construction services, but fewer in pipeline transportation, electricity transmission and distribution, and energy trading. The GATS permits members to maintain monopolies and exclusive service suppliers, subject to the obligation in Article VIII to ensure that such suppliers do not act inconsistently with the member's MFN obligation and specific commitments, and do not abuse their monopoly position when competing in the supply of services outside the scope of their monopoly rights.

In *EU and its Member States — Certain Measures Relating to the Energy Sector* (DS476, panel report 2018), Russia challenged several elements of the EU's Third Energy Package, including the ownership unbundling requirements for transmission system operators, the "third-country certification" requirement applicable to operators controlled by persons from third countries (the so-called "Gazprom clause"), and the treatment of infrastructure exemptions and the OPAL pipeline. The panel found that some aspects of the measures, including certain aspects of the implementation of the infrastructure exemptions and the conditions imposed on the OPAL pipeline, were inconsistent with the GATT and GATS, but rejected most of Russia's claims, including those against ownership unbundling, which it held did not violate the market access and national treatment obligations of the GATS in the circumstances, and, in the case of the third-country certification requirement, found a violation of the GATS national treatment obligation which was not justified under the public security exception in Article XIV. Both parties appealed, and the appeal has not been heard because of the Appellate Body crisis. The dispute illustrated the potential application of trade law to the structural regulation of energy markets.

### Government Procurement

Public procurement in the energy sector — including the procurement of generation capacity, equipment, and construction services by state-owned utilities — is significant in value. Article III:8(a) of the GATT excludes from the national treatment obligation laws, regulations, or requirements governing the procurement by governmental agencies of products purchased for governmental purposes and not with a view to commercial resale. As the *Canada — Renewable Energy* and *India — Solar Cells* disputes established, this derogation is narrowly interpreted and does not cover local content requirements imposed on private generators selling electricity to state utilities. The plurilateral **Agreement on Government Procurement**, as revised in 2012, applies to procurement by covered entities of its parties above specified thresholds, and some parties have listed energy utilities as covered entities, although many have excluded them. Nigeria, like most African states, is not a party to the GPA.
