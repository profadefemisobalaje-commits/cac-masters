::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 19: Simulated Arbitration over a Dam on a Shared River (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The states, river, institutions, projects, and evidence described are fictitious and do not refer to any real countries or disputes.**

The Sarana River rises in the highlands of the Republic of Kesh, flows through the Republic of Valoria, and enters the sea in the Federal Republic of Ondo-Mare. All three states are parties to the UN Watercourses Convention. Kesh and Ondo-Mare are also parties to a 1962 bilateral agreement, concluded before Valoria's independence, which allocates seventy per cent of the river's average annual flow to Ondo-Mare and thirty per cent to Kesh, without any allocation to the territory that became Valoria. Valoria has never acceded to the 1962 agreement. The three states are members of the Sarana Basin Commission, established in 2005, whose charter requires prior notification of planned measures and the joint review of major projects, but provides no binding dispute settlement procedure.

Valoria, where only thirty-five per cent of the population has access to electricity, has begun constructing the Valoria Unity Dam, a 3,200 MW hydropower project with a reservoir that will take between four and nine years to fill, depending on the filling strategy and rainfall. Valoria notified the Commission of the project after construction had begun, provided a summary environmental assessment, and has declined to share its detailed reservoir operation plans, citing national security. Ondo-Mare, which depends on the Sarana for ninety per cent of its freshwater and irrigates large areas of farmland in the river's delta, claims that rapid filling would reduce its water supply by up to twenty-five per cent in drought years and threaten the delta's ecosystem. Kesh is planning its own irrigation scheme upstream. Valoria has offered to sell electricity from the dam to Ondo-Mare at concessional prices.

The three states have agreed, by special agreement, to submit to an arbitral tribunal the questions whether Valoria has breached its obligations under international law in respect of the dam and, if so, what consequences follow; and to request the tribunal to indicate principles for the filling and operation of the reservoir.

**Tasks.** Divide into teams representing Valoria, Ondo-Mare, and Kesh, and a tribunal of three members.

(a) Each party team should prepare a written memorial (of no more than 3,000 words) addressing: the legal effect of the 1962 agreement on Valoria; the application of equitable and reasonable utilisation and the no-harm rule; Valoria's compliance with procedural obligations; and the remedies sought.

(b) Each party should identify the expert evidence it would adduce (hydrological, ecological, economic) and prepare questions for the cross-examination of the opposing parties' experts.

(c) The tribunal should conduct a two-hour oral hearing and deliver a reasoned award, addressing in particular whether a failure of prior notification renders the project unlawful, and indicating principles for the filling and annual operation of the reservoir, including in drought years.

(d) All participants should reflect, in a short note, on whether a benefit-sharing arrangement — involving electricity trade, joint operation of the dam, or compensation — would provide a better solution than adjudication, and how it might be negotiated.

::: summary
- Freshwater is both an economic resource and a human right; modern international law has rejected absolute territorial sovereignty and absolute territorial integrity in favour of limited territorial sovereignty and a community of interest in shared waters.
- National legal systems govern water through state ownership or public trusteeship, riparian and prior appropriation doctrines, administrative permits, and the public trust doctrine; Nigeria's Water Resources Act 1993 vests control of water in the Federal Government.
- The UN Watercourses Convention (1997, in force 2014), to which Nigeria is a party, codifies equitable and reasonable utilisation, the obligation not to cause significant harm, cooperation, and the procedural obligations relating to planned measures, much of which reflects customary law; the UNECE Water Convention is now open globally.
- Equitable and reasonable utilisation is determined by weighing all relevant factors, with special regard for vital human needs; *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* remains the leading authority.
- River basin organisations range from advisory bodies to institutions owning joint infrastructure, such as the OMVS; the Niger Basin Authority and the Lake Chad Basin Commission are central to West African water governance, and the Lagdo dam floods illustrate the importance of notification and data sharing.
- Hydropower disputes in the Nile, Indus, and Mekong basins illustrate the tensions between historic uses and new development, the significance of procedural obligations and environmental flows, and the vulnerability of cooperation to political conflict.
- The ILC Draft Articles on Transboundary Aquifers and a small number of aquifer agreements address groundwater, which is critical in arid regions and affected by energy and extractive activities.
- The human right to water, recognised in 2010, requires priority for personal and domestic uses and constrains allocations to energy and extractive uses; indigenous relationships with water and public participation are increasingly recognised.
- Water disputes are addressed through treaty mechanisms, international courts and tribunals, facilitated diplomacy, and investment arbitration, while international humanitarian law protects water and energy infrastructure in armed conflict.
:::

::: research
1. Has the principle of equitable and reasonable utilisation provided adequate guidance for the resolution of disputes over hydropower on shared rivers? Evaluate with reference to the Nile and the Indus.
2. Assess the legal effect of colonial-era water agreements on post-colonial upstream states, with reference to the 1929 and 1959 Nile agreements and the law of state succession.
3. To what extent can the benefit-sharing model of the Senegal River Basin Development Organisation be replicated in the Niger basin to optimise hydropower development and protect downstream users, including Nigeria?
4. Evaluate the adequacy of the legal and institutional framework for managing the transboundary flood risks associated with the Lagdo dam on the Benue River.
5. How should the human right to water be balanced against the water requirements of mining, petroleum, and green hydrogen projects in water-scarce regions?
6. Should the ILC Draft Articles on the Law of Transboundary Aquifers be converted into a binding convention, and how should they treat non-recharging aquifers?
:::
