## Introduction

Energy is the most traded commodity group in the world. Crude oil and refined products, natural gas (by pipeline and as LNG), coal, and, increasingly, electricity, hydrogen derivatives, and the equipment and minerals required for renewable energy cross national borders in enormous volumes. The rules governing this trade — on tariffs, quantitative restrictions, subsidies, transit, standards, and the treatment of foreign goods and services — shape the economics of energy production and consumption and the distribution of the gains from trade between producing and consuming states. As the energy transition proceeds, trade law has become a central arena in which states contest the legality of industrial policies supporting clean energy manufacturing, carbon border measures, and export restrictions on critical minerals.

For much of the history of the multilateral trading system, energy occupied an anomalous position. Although the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947 (GATT) made no exception for energy goods, energy trade was, in practice, largely outside the system's effective disciplines. Many major oil-exporting states were not parties to the GATT; international oil markets were dominated first by the major international oil companies and later by the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC); energy was widely regarded as a strategic sector subject to national security considerations; and the principal concerns of trade negotiators were import tariffs and restrictions in manufacturing and agriculture rather than export restrictions on natural resources. The creation of the World Trade Organization in 1995, the accession of major energy producers — including Saudi Arabia in 2005 and Russia in 2012 — and the increasing salience of energy and climate policy have brought energy firmly within the scope of WTO law.

This chapter examines energy commodities and international trade rules (section 13.1), the WTO disciplines relevant to energy (section 13.2), tariffs, subsidies, and trade remedies (section 13.3), export restrictions and resource security (section 13.4), technical standards and market access (section 13.5), regional trade agreements and energy integration (section 13.6), carbon border measures (section 13.7), and trade disputes involving energy and natural resources (section 13.8). The protection of foreign investment in the energy sector, which is governed by a distinct body of international investment law, is examined in Chapter 14; the trade law of critical minerals has been introduced in Chapter 8; and the trade law dimensions of national security are examined further in Chapter 25.

## 13.1 Energy Commodities and International Trade Rules

### The Characteristics of Energy Trade

Trade in energy differs from trade in most other goods in several respects that affect the operation of trade rules.

**Geographical concentration and the "resource curse".** Energy resources are unevenly distributed. A relatively small number of states hold most of the world's proven reserves of oil and gas, and many of these states are heavily dependent on energy exports for government revenue and foreign exchange. Consuming states are correspondingly dependent on imports. This asymmetry gives energy trade a geopolitical dimension and creates incentives for both exporting and importing states to intervene in markets.

**Export-side concerns.** Whereas the traditional concern of trade law has been import barriers, the central concerns in energy trade frequently relate to exports — export restrictions, export taxes, and production quotas that restrict supply and raise world prices, and dual pricing policies under which energy is sold domestically at prices below export prices. As examined in section 13.4, the GATT's disciplines on export measures are weaker than those on import measures.

**Network dependence and transit.** Pipeline gas and electricity are traded through fixed networks that often cross the territories of third states. Trade therefore depends on access to networks and on freedom of transit, and the owners of networks have significant market power. Transit disputes, such as those between Russia and Ukraine examined in Chapter 6, illustrate the vulnerability of networked energy trade.

**State ownership and state trading.** National oil companies and state-owned utilities play a dominant role in the energy sector in many states, raising questions about the application of trade disciplines to state-owned enterprises and state trading enterprises.

**Environmental externalities.** The production and consumption of fossil fuels generate greenhouse gas emissions and other environmental harms that are not reflected in market prices. Measures to address these externalities — carbon taxes, emissions standards, renewable energy support, and carbon border adjustments — have significant trade effects and raise questions of compatibility with trade rules.

### Energy as Goods and Services

A preliminary question is whether energy is a good, a service, or both, because different WTO agreements govern trade in goods (the GATT and associated agreements) and trade in services (the General Agreement on Trade in Services, or GATS). Crude oil, petroleum products, natural gas, coal, and uranium are clearly goods, classified in Chapter 27 (mineral fuels and oils) and other chapters of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS), the international nomenclature for the classification of goods administered by the World Customs Organization. **Electricity** is classified in the HS under heading 27.16, and is generally treated as a good for the purposes of the GATT, although its intangible character and the inseparability of its supply from network services have led some to question the classification. The Court of Justice of the European Union treated electricity as a good for the purposes of the free movement of goods in *Almelo* (Case C-393/92, 1994).

Many activities associated with energy are services: exploration and drilling services, pipeline transportation, the transmission and distribution of electricity, energy trading, engineering and construction, and consulting. These are governed by the GATS, under which members' obligations depend substantially on their specific commitments. There is no specific sectoral classification for "energy services" in the services classification list used in the Uruguay Round, and energy-related services are scattered across several categories — services incidental to mining, pipeline transportation of fuels, services incidental to energy distribution, and wholesale and retail trade in fuels — with the result that commitments in energy services have been limited and uneven. Proposals to improve the classification of energy services were made in the Doha Round negotiations, without result.

### The Historical Position of Energy in the GATT

The GATT of 1947 applied in principle to all goods, including energy. However, energy was largely absent from the agenda of the successive rounds of GATT negotiations, for several reasons. The Havana Charter of 1948, which would have established an International Trade Organization, contained a chapter on intergovernmental commodity agreements that would have permitted arrangements regulating the production and trade of primary commodities, but the Charter never entered into force. Major oil-exporting states, many of which achieved independence after 1947, did not join the GATT for many years. The formation of OPEC in 1960, and its assertion of control over production and pricing in the 1970s, took place outside the GATT framework. The industrialised states that dominated the GATT were importers of energy and did not seek disciplines on export restrictions, preferring to address energy security through the International Energy Agency, established in 1974, examined in Chapter 4. A persistent view held that the GATT did not apply to natural resources in the ground, or to the production decisions of sovereign states over their natural resources, reflecting the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources examined in Chapter 2.

### Energy in the WTO

The Uruguay Round agreements, which established the WTO in 1995, apply to energy goods and services on the same terms as to other goods and services, and there is no specific WTO agreement on energy. The accession of major energy exporters has brought energy trade within the WTO in practice, and accession protocols have included energy-specific commitments. The accession of **Saudi Arabia** in 2005 involved negotiations on the dual pricing of natural gas liquids used as feedstock for the petrochemical industry, with Saudi Arabia undertaking that producers and distributors of natural gas liquids would operate on the basis of normal commercial considerations and recover costs and a reasonable profit. The accession of **Russia** in 2012 included commitments on export duties for a range of products, and commitments on the pricing of natural gas, under which producers and distributors of natural gas would operate on the basis of normal commercial considerations, based on recovery of costs and profit.

The WTO has not, however, adopted rules addressing some of the most significant features of energy trade, such as OPEC's production coordination, investment in energy infrastructure, competition in network industries, and the relationship between trade and energy security. The Energy Charter Treaty, examined in Chapter 14, was designed in part to fill these gaps, applying GATT rules to energy trade among its parties (including those not members of the WTO, under its trade provisions as amended in 1998) and establishing specific obligations on transit and investment.

## 13.2 World Trade Organization Disciplines

### Most-Favoured-Nation Treatment

The cornerstone of the multilateral trading system is the obligation of **most-favoured-nation** (MFN) treatment in Article I:1 of the GATT, which requires that any advantage, favour, privilege, or immunity granted by a member to any product originating in or destined for any other country be accorded immediately and unconditionally to the like product originating in or destined for the territories of all other members. The MFN obligation applies to customs duties and charges on imports and exports, the method of levying them, rules and formalities in connection with importation and exportation, and internal taxes and regulations. In the energy sector, the MFN obligation prohibits members from discriminating between imported energy products according to their country of origin — for example, by imposing higher tariffs on oil from one member than from another — or between exports according to destination. Measures that differentiate between energy products on the basis of their production methods, such as the carbon intensity of production, may raise MFN issues if they result in less favourable treatment of products from particular members, as examined in section 13.7.

### National Treatment

Article III of the GATT requires members to accord imported products treatment no less favourable than that accorded to like domestic products in respect of internal taxes and other internal charges (Article III:2) and laws, regulations, and requirements affecting their internal sale, offering for sale, purchase, transportation, distribution, or use (Article III:4). The national treatment obligation is central to the legality of energy and environmental measures, including fuel taxes, energy efficiency standards, renewable energy support schemes, and local content requirements.

The application of Article III depends on the concept of **"like products"**. In *EC — Asbestos* (2001), the Appellate Body held that the determination of likeness under Article III:4 is fundamentally a determination about the nature and extent of a competitive relationship between products, considering the products' physical properties, end uses, consumers' tastes and habits, and tariff classification, and that health risks associated with a product may be relevant to likeness in so far as they affect the competitive relationship, for example through consumers' perceptions. The question whether products that are physically identical but produced by methods with different environmental impacts — for example, electricity generated from coal and from solar energy, or steel produced with high and low emissions — can be regarded as "unlike" is one of the most contested issues in trade and environment law, known as the issue of **non-product-related processes and production methods** (NPR-PPMs).

Even where products are like, a measure violates Article III:4 only if it accords "less favourable treatment" to imported products. The Appellate Body held in *Korea — Various Measures on Beef* (2001) that less favourable treatment requires a modification of the conditions of competition to the detriment of imported products. In *US — Clove Cigarettes* (2012), interpreting the parallel national treatment obligation of the TBT Agreement, it held that a detrimental impact on imports does not establish less favourable treatment if it stems exclusively from a legitimate regulatory distinction — a reasoning not extended to Article III:4 of the GATT, under which regulatory justifications are to be considered under Article XX.
