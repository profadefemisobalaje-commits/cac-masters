## Introduction

Energy law is constantly being reformed. Over the past two decades, states across the world have liberalised electricity markets, restructured their petroleum sectors, introduced renewable energy support schemes, enacted climate legislation, revised mining codes, and created new regulatory institutions. Nigeria alone has, since 2005, enacted the Electric Power Sector Reform Act, the Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act, the Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative Act, the Petroleum Industry Act, the Climate Change Act, the Electricity Act, and a major package of tax reforms. The energy transition will demand further and deeper reforms: to accommodate new technologies such as storage, hydrogen, and carbon capture; to manage the decline of fossil fuel industries; to mobilise investment in clean energy; to protect consumers and communities; and to meet international climate commitments.

Reform is difficult. Energy reforms affect powerful interests and large revenues; they are technically complex; they often have long gestation periods and uncertain effects; and they require the cooperation of multiple institutions. Many reforms fail — they are never enacted, as Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Bill was not for nearly two decades; or they are enacted but not implemented; or they are implemented but fail to achieve their objectives, or produce unintended consequences. The quality of reform depends on the quality of the processes by which problems are diagnosed, options are assessed, laws are drafted, stakeholders are consulted, institutions are designed, and outcomes are evaluated.

This chapter examines these processes from the perspective of the scholar and practitioner who seeks not only to analyse energy law but to improve it. It considers theories of legal and regulatory reform (section 29.1), regulatory impact assessment (section 29.2), legislative drafting for energy governance (section 29.3), institutional capacity and regulatory independence (section 29.4), public consultation and stakeholder participation (section 29.5), policy evaluation and implementation (section 29.6), governance reform in resource-rich economies (section 29.7), and the design of future-ready energy legal systems (section 29.8). Figure 29.1 presents the reform cycle that structures the chapter. The chapter builds on the theories of regulation examined in Chapter 3 and the comparative analysis in Chapter 24, and is intended to equip doctoral graduates for roles of leadership in law reform.

## 29.1 Theories of Legal and Regulatory Reform

### Why Reform? Rationales for Regulatory Intervention

Theories of regulation, examined in Chapter 3, provide the starting point for understanding why reforms are undertaken. **Public interest** theories hold that regulation corrects **market failures** — natural monopoly in networks, externalities such as pollution and climate change, information asymmetries, public goods such as energy security, and the coordination problems of complex systems — and pursues social objectives such as universal access and distributive justice. On this view, reform is warranted where existing regulation fails to correct market failures efficiently, where circumstances or technologies change, or where social objectives evolve. **Public choice** and **interest group** theories, associated with George Stigler's theory of economic regulation (1971) and the work of Mancur Olson, hold that regulation is supplied in response to the demands of organised interests, and that concentrated interests — such as incumbent energy companies — are better able to organise and influence regulation than diffuse interests such as consumers, leading to **regulatory capture**. On this view, reform may be driven by shifts in the balance of interests, and successful reform requires strategies to overcome the resistance of concentrated interests. **Institutionalist** theories emphasise the role of institutions — formal rules, organisations, and informal norms — in shaping behaviour and outcomes, and the **path dependence** of institutional arrangements, which makes reform difficult once institutions are established.

### Models of the Policy Process

Several models of the **policy process** help explain how and when reforms occur. The **policy cycle** or "stages" model divides the process into agenda setting, policy formulation, decision-making, implementation, and evaluation, as in Figure 29.1; though criticised as an idealisation of a messier reality, it provides a useful framework for analysis and practice. John Kingdon's **multiple streams** framework holds that policy change occurs when three independent streams — **problems** (conditions recognised as requiring action), **policies** (solutions developed by experts and advocates), and **politics** (public mood, elections, and interest group pressure) — converge in a **policy window**, often opened by a crisis or a change of government, which **policy entrepreneurs** exploit to couple solutions to problems. The convergence of fiscal crisis, the availability of a long-debated policy proposal, and a new administration's political commitment helps explain the removal of Nigeria's petrol subsidy in May 2023, examined in Chapter 24, and the enactment of the PIA in 2021 after nearly two decades of debate.

Frank Baumgartner and Bryan Jones's **punctuated equilibrium** theory holds that policy is characterised by long periods of stability, maintained by policy monopolies and incremental change, punctuated by short bursts of radical change when issues are redefined and new actors mobilise — a pattern evident in energy policy following crises such as the oil shocks of the 1970s, the Fukushima accident, and the 2022 energy crisis. Paul Sabatier's **advocacy coalition framework** holds that policy change results from competition among coalitions of actors sharing core beliefs, and from policy-oriented learning within and between coalitions over periods of a decade or more, as in the contest between coalitions favouring and opposing fossil fuel development. **Policy transfer** and **diffusion** theories, examined in Chapter 24 in relation to legal transplants, explain how policies spread among jurisdictions through learning, emulation, competition, and coercion — as in the diffusion of electricity market liberalisation through the conditions of international financial institutions in the 1990s, and the diffusion of feed-in tariffs and renewable auctions.

### Theories of Legal Change

Legal scholarship offers further perspectives on reform. **Responsive law**, as conceptualised by Philippe Nonet and Philip Selznick, describes a legal order that responds to social needs and purposes rather than adhering rigidly to formal rules. **Reflexive law**, developed by Gunther Teubner, holds that law in complex societies cannot directly control social subsystems such as the economy, but can structure their self-regulation by establishing procedures and incentives — an insight reflected in disclosure requirements, environmental management systems, and corporate due diligence obligations, examined in Chapter 22. **Responsive regulation**, associated with Ian Ayres and John Braithwaite, examined in Chapter 3, proposes that regulators respond to the conduct of regulated entities, escalating from persuasion to sanctions through an enforcement pyramid. **Smart regulation** (Gunningham and Grabosky) advocates the use of a mix of instruments and actors, including market-based instruments, self-regulation, and third parties. **Experimentalist governance**, associated with Charles Sabel and Jonathan Zeitlin, describes a recursive process of setting provisional framework goals, allowing local units discretion to pursue them, reporting and peer review of performance, and revision of goals and procedures in the light of experience — a model reflected in the Paris Agreement's cycle of nationally determined contributions and in the EU's governance of the energy union.

### The Political Economy of Reform

Reform is ultimately a **political** process. Its success depends on the alignment of interests, the distribution of gains and losses, the timing and sequencing of measures, and the capacity of reformers to build coalitions and manage opposition. Reforms with **concentrated costs and diffuse benefits** — such as the removal of fuel subsidies, which imposes immediate costs on consumers and transporters while the benefits of fiscal savings are diffuse and delayed — are particularly difficult. Strategies for overcoming resistance include **compensation** of losers (such as cash transfers to poor households accompanying subsidy reform, as Nigeria attempted after 2023), **sequencing** reforms to build support, **communication** to build public understanding, **bundling** reforms so that gains in one area offset losses in another, and **credible commitment** through legislation or international agreements. The International Monetary Fund's studies of fuel subsidy reform across many countries identify the importance of comprehensive reform plans, communication, phasing, compensation, improving the efficiency of state-owned enterprises, and depoliticising pricing through automatic mechanisms.

### Reform in Resource-Rich States

Reform in resource-rich states faces distinctive obstacles, examined in section 29.7: the concentration of rents creates incentives for elites to resist reforms that would reduce their control over resources; patronage networks depend on the discretionary allocation of licences, contracts, and subsidies; and the resource revenues that accrue directly to the state reduce governments' dependence on taxation and thus their accountability to citizens. These dynamics help explain why legal reforms in resource-rich states often take the form of impressive legislation weakly implemented — what has been called **"isomorphic mimicry"** (Pritchett, Woolcock, and Andrews), in which states adopt the forms of successful institutions without their functions — and why successful reform often requires sustained domestic coalitions supported by transparency and international engagement.
