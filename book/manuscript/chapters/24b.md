### The Fiscal Framework of the PIA

Chapter 4 of the PIA established a new fiscal regime for petroleum operations, replacing the Petroleum Profits Tax for converted and new acreage. Its principal elements are:

- **Royalties**, payable at rates that vary according to the terrain and the level of production, with lower rates for deep offshore and frontier acreage and for smaller volumes of production, and price-based royalties at higher oil prices; royalties on gas are generally lower than on oil, to promote gas development.
- **Hydrocarbon tax**, payable on profits from crude oil (but not from associated or non-associated gas) produced from onshore and shallow water acreage, at rates of thirty per cent for petroleum mining leases and fifteen per cent for petroleum prospecting licences, with deep offshore acreage exempt.
- **Companies income tax**, payable at the general rate on the profits of upstream companies.
- **Cost price ratio limits** on the deductibility of costs, and production allowances for new acreage and converted acreage.
- **Rents** on licences and leases.

The PIA also requires the payment of a contribution to the **Frontier Exploration Fund**, equal to thirty per cent of NNPC Limited's share of profit oil and profit gas under production sharing contracts, to finance exploration in frontier basins — a provision criticised by governors of northern and southern states alike for diverting revenues that would otherwise accrue to the Federation Account, and by commentators as a potential source of inefficient spending on exploration in basins of uncertain prospectivity. The fiscal regime was designed to make Nigeria more competitive in attracting investment in the face of declining investment in its petroleum sector, while increasing government take on onshore and shallow water production. The comparative design of petroleum fiscal regimes has been examined in Chapter 16. Subsequently, the federal government introduced fiscal incentives for non-associated gas and deepwater projects by executive orders in 2024 and pursued broader tax reform through legislation enacted in 2025, which consolidated aspects of the tax framework, including for the petroleum industry.

### Host Communities Development

Chapter 3 of the PIA established a regime for **host communities development**, intended to address the grievances of communities in oil-producing areas and to secure their cooperation in the protection of petroleum facilities. Every holder of a petroleum prospecting licence or petroleum mining lease (a "settlor") must incorporate a **host communities development trust** for the benefit of the host communities in which its operations take place. The settlor must make an annual contribution to the trust equal to **three per cent of its actual annual operating expenditure** in the preceding financial year in upstream petroleum operations affecting the host communities. The trust's funds are allocated to a capital fund (for capital projects), a reserve fund (for future use when contributions cease), and, where determined, an endowment fund. The trust is administered by a **board of trustees**, a **management committee**, which includes representatives of the host communities, and an **advisory committee**, and must prepare community needs assessments and development plans.

The regime has been both praised and criticised. Supporters regard it as an advance on the previous arrangements, which relied on voluntary memoranda of understanding between companies and communities, and as providing a predictable source of funding for community development. Critics argue that three per cent of operating expenditure is inadequate, compared with the five per cent or more demanded by community representatives during the legislative process; that the trusts are controlled by settlors, who appoint board members; that the definition of host communities may exclude communities affected by pollution but not hosting facilities; and that the provision under which communities forfeit their entitlement to the extent of the cost of repairing damage caused by vandalism, sabotage, or civil unrest imposes collective punishment for acts that communities may not be able to control, as examined in Chapter 21. The implementation of the regime — including the incorporation of trusts, the selection of community representatives, and the execution of projects — has been uneven, and its success will depend on its governance and transparency.

### Environmental Provisions

The PIA contains environmental provisions, including requirements for environmental management plans approved by the relevant regulator; the establishment by licensees of an **environmental remediation fund** for the rehabilitation or management of negative environmental impacts; the establishment of **decommissioning and abandonment funds**, to ensure that the costs of decommissioning are provided for; and provisions on the elimination of **gas flaring**, under which flaring or venting is prohibited except in limited circumstances, such as emergencies or with an exemption, and flare penalties are payable, the proceeds of which are credited to the Midstream and Downstream Gas Infrastructure Fund. Nigeria has been among the world's largest gas flarers, and its gas flaring regime is examined in Chapter 17. The **Nigerian Gas Flare Commercialisation Programme** sought to auction flare sites to third parties for the commercial use of flared gas.

### Gas Policy and the Midstream and Downstream Sectors

Nigeria has the largest proven gas reserves in Africa, more than two hundred trillion cubic feet, and the federal government declared 2021–2030 the **"Decade of Gas"**, seeking to use gas as a transition fuel for power generation, industry, and clean cooking. The PIA provides for the regulation of gas transportation through **network codes** and third-party access, the establishment of a **domestic gas delivery obligation** requiring producers to supply a specified volume of gas to the domestic market, and a **domestic base price** for gas supplied to the power sector and strategic industries, with a transition to willing-buyer, willing-seller pricing. Major gas infrastructure projects include the **Ajaokuta–Kaduna–Kano (AKK) pipeline**, intended to transport gas from the south to northern Nigeria, and the expansion of **Nigeria LNG**, whose Train 7 project was under construction. Nigeria also participates in the West African Gas Pipeline and has promoted the proposed Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline, examined in Chapter 20.

In the downstream sector, the PIA provided for the deregulation of the petroleum products market, with prices to be determined by market forces. The most significant reform was the **removal of the petrol subsidy**, announced by the incoming President on 29 May 2023. The subsidy, under which the government had fixed the retail price of petrol below the import parity price and compensated NNPC (and, earlier, marketers) for the difference, had cost trillions of naira annually, constituted a growing share of federal revenue, and was associated with smuggling to neighbouring countries and large-scale fraud. Its removal led to a sharp increase in petrol prices, contributing, together with the unification and devaluation of the exchange rate in June 2023, to high inflation and a cost-of-living crisis. The government introduced palliative measures, including cash transfers to vulnerable households, the provision of compressed natural gas buses and conversion kits under the **Presidential Compressed Natural Gas Initiative**, and wage increases, but the social impacts have been severe, illustrating the energy justice challenges of subsidy reform examined in Chapter 23.

The commissioning of the **Dangote Petroleum Refinery** in the Lekki Free Zone near Lagos, with a nameplate capacity of 650,000 barrels per day — the largest single-train refinery in the world — which began producing petroleum products in 2024, has the potential to transform the downstream sector by reducing Nigeria's dependence on imported products. Its start-up gave rise to disputes with the NMDPRA, NNPC, and marketers over crude oil supply, product quality, import licences, and pricing, including litigation, illustrating the regulatory challenges of transition from an import-dependent, state-dominated market to a deregulated market with a dominant private refiner. The federal government introduced a "naira-for-crude" arrangement under which crude oil would be sold to domestic refiners in naira. NNPC's own refineries at Port Harcourt, Warri, and Kaduna underwent rehabilitation, with mixed results.

### The Electricity Sector

Nigeria's electricity sector was reformed under the **Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005** (EPSRA), which unbundled the vertically integrated National Electric Power Authority into eighteen successor companies — six generation companies, eleven distribution companies, and the Transmission Company of Nigeria — and established the **Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission** (NERC). In November 2013, the generation and distribution companies were privatised, while transmission remained in public ownership. The privatisation has been widely regarded as having failed to deliver its objectives: the sector has suffered from a lack of cost-reflective tariffs, high aggregate technical, commercial, and collection losses, inadequate transmission capacity, gas supply constraints, liquidity shortfalls requiring repeated government interventions, and frequent collapses of the national grid. Installed generation capacity exceeds 13,000 MW, but available capacity has generally been around 4,000 to 5,000 MW for a population of more than 200 million — among the lowest per capita electricity supplies in the world.

The **Electricity Act 2023**, signed in June 2023, repealed EPSRA and established a new legal framework. Its key features include:

- **Decentralisation**: implementing the constitutional amendment of 2023, the Act empowers states to make laws regulating electricity generation, transmission, and distribution within the state, and to establish state electricity regulatory commissions. NERC transfers regulatory oversight of the intrastate electricity market to a state once it has established its own market and regulator. Several states, including Enugu, Ekiti, Ondo, Imo, Oyo, Edo, Kogi, Lagos, and Niger, enacted electricity laws and established regulators, and NERC began transferring regulatory oversight to them from 2024, while the Act continues to govern the national grid and interstate and international electricity.
- **Renewable energy**: the Act requires the promotion of renewable energy and contains provisions on renewable energy obligations, licensing of renewable generation, and incentives, and requires the government to adopt an integrated resource plan.
- **Market development**: the Act provides for the evolution of the electricity market, including the establishment of an independent system operator separate from the Transmission Company of Nigeria, and the development of a competitive market.
- **Consumer protection and enforcement**: the Act strengthens consumer protection, criminalises electricity theft and vandalism of electricity infrastructure, and requires licensees to meet performance standards.
- **Rural electrification**: the Act continues the Rural Electrification Agency and the Rural Electrification Fund, examined in Chapter 23.

The Act represents a significant shift towards a multilevel, decentralised electricity governance model, raising questions of coordination between federal and state regulators, the design of state markets, the financial viability of distribution companies operating across state boundaries, and the capacity of state institutions. Tariff reform, including the service-based tariff system and the April 2024 increase in Band A tariffs, has been examined in Chapter 23. Electricity sector law is examined from a comparative perspective in Chapter 9.

### The Mining Sector

The solid minerals sector is governed by the **Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007**, which vests minerals in the federal government and establishes the **Mining Cadastre Office**, which administers mineral titles on a first-come, first-served basis — reconnaissance permits, exploration licences, small-scale mining leases, mining leases, quarry leases, and water use permits. The Act requires mining lease holders to conclude community development agreements with host communities, as examined in Chapter 21, and provides for royalties, environmental obligations, and the rehabilitation of mined land. The sector contributes a small share of GDP, but the federal government has sought to develop it as a source of diversification, including through the promotion of **critical minerals** such as lithium, examined in Chapter 8. In 2022, Nigeria restricted the export of unprocessed solid minerals, and the Ministry of Solid Minerals Development has promoted local processing, including through agreements with investors to build lithium processing plants in Kaduna and Nasarawa States. Illegal mining, the involvement of armed groups, and the environmental and health impacts of artisanal mining remain significant challenges.

### Climate Law and the Tension with Petroleum Development

Nigeria's **Climate Change Act 2021**, examined in Chapter 18, provides a framework for achieving net zero emissions between 2050 and 2070 and establishes the National Council on Climate Change. The **Energy Transition Plan** targets net zero in the energy sector by 2060. These commitments coexist with the PIA, whose central objective is to attract investment in petroleum production, including frontier exploration, and with the Decade of Gas. The tension between climate commitments and the development of hydrocarbon resources — a dilemma faced by many producing states in the Global South, examined in Chapters 18 and 23 — is managed in Nigerian policy through the positioning of gas as a transition fuel, the reduction of gas flaring and methane emissions, the use of carbon markets, and the argument that Nigeria, with low per capita emissions and widespread energy poverty, is entitled to develop its resources. Critics argue that investments in new oil and gas production risk creating stranded assets and locking in emissions, and that Nigeria should prioritise renewable energy and economic diversification. The legal and policy framework does not yet fully reconcile these objectives.

### Assessment

The Nigerian legal framework for energy and resources has undergone its most significant transformation in decades, with the PIA, the Electricity Act 2023, the Climate Change Act 2021, and the removal of the petrol subsidy. These reforms address long-standing weaknesses: the conflation of policy, regulatory, and commercial functions; the lack of a framework for host community benefits; the centralisation of electricity governance; and the fiscal burden of subsidies. Their success depends, however, on implementation: the independence and capacity of the regulators; the commercial discipline of NNPC Limited; the governance of host community trusts; the security of oil infrastructure against theft and vandalism, which has severely curtailed production; the financial viability of the electricity sector; and the coordination of federal and state institutions. Comparative experience, examined in the remainder of this chapter, suggests that legal reform is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the effective governance of energy and resources.
