## 27.5 Contractual and Commercial Litigation

### Courts and Energy Contracts

Despite the prevalence of arbitration clauses in international energy contracts, a large volume of energy contract disputes is litigated in national courts. Domestic energy contracts — between distribution companies and customers, between suppliers and marketers, between operators and local contractors — frequently provide for litigation rather than arbitration. Many international contracts, particularly in finance, trading, and shipping, choose the courts of England or New York. Courts also hear disputes arising from contracts with arbitration clauses, when the validity or scope of the clause is contested, when interim relief is sought, or when the parties waive arbitration. And courts develop the principles of contract law applied in arbitration, as the English courts have done through a substantial body of case law on energy contracts.

### The English Commercial Court and Energy Contracts

The **Commercial Court** in London is among the leading fora for international energy contract litigation, owing to the popularity of English law in energy and commodities contracts, the expertise of its judges, and the enforceability of its judgments. Its decisions on energy contracts have shaped the law in areas examined in Chapter 15, including the interpretation of force majeure clauses (as in ***Classic Maritime v Limbungan Makmur***, Court of Appeal 2019, on the "but for" causation requirement, and ***RTI Ltd v MUR Shipping BV***, UK Supreme Court 2024, which held that a "reasonable endeavours" proviso in a force majeure clause did not require the affected party to accept non-contractual performance, such as payment in a different currency, to overcome a sanctions-related obstacle); the interpretation of take-or-pay clauses (*M&J Polymers v Imerys*, 2008, holding that take-or-pay clauses are not penalties, a conclusion strengthened by the reformulation of the penalty doctrine in *Cavendish Square Holding v Makdessi*, UK Supreme Court 2015); the implication of terms and the duty of good faith in long-term relational contracts (*Bates v Post Office*, 2019, and its application in energy contracts); and the operation of pre-emption, default, and forfeiture provisions in joint operating agreements. The Commercial Court has also heard major disputes arising from Russian sanctions, including claims by aircraft lessors and energy traders, and disputes arising from the collapse of energy trading companies.

### Commercial Litigation in Nigeria

In **Nigeria**, the exclusive jurisdiction of the **Federal High Court** over matters relating to mines and minerals, including oil fields and natural gas, under section 251(1)(n) of the Constitution, examined in section 27.1, channels much energy contract litigation to that court. Recurring categories of commercial litigation in the Nigerian energy sector include disputes between **joint venture partners**, including claims concerning cash calls, the allocation of production, and the exercise of pre-emption rights on the sale of interests; disputes over the **assignment and transfer** of interests in oil mining leases, which require ministerial consent, as illustrated by the litigation surrounding several divestments by international companies examined in Chapter 24; disputes between operators and **marginal field** owners; disputes between **service contractors** and operators over payment, termination, and local content obligations; disputes arising from the **privatised electricity sector**, including between generation companies, distribution companies, and the Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Plc over payment shortfalls and the enforcement of power purchase agreements; and disputes between **banks and borrowers** in oil and gas financing, including the enforcement of security over oil assets. The delays in Nigerian courts, the possibility of interlocutory appeals, and the difficulty of enforcing judgments against government entities have encouraged parties to prefer arbitration, while also leading to the use of the courts to obstruct arbitration through anti-arbitration injunctions — a practice that Nigerian appellate courts have increasingly discouraged, emphasising the policy of the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 to limit court intervention.

### Commercial Litigation Involving States and State Entities

Commercial litigation against states and state-owned enterprises raises questions of **state immunity**, examined in Chapter 26 in the context of the enforcement of awards. Under the restrictive theory, states are not immune from jurisdiction in respect of **commercial transactions**, including contracts for the supply of goods or services and loans. The characterisation of energy transactions as commercial or sovereign acts can be contested: the sale of oil by a national oil company is generally commercial, but the grant of a petroleum licence or the imposition of a tax is sovereign. In ***Republic of Argentina v Weltover*** (1992), the US Supreme Court held that the issuance of government bonds was a commercial activity because it was the type of activity in which private parties engage, regardless of the governmental purpose, a test applied to energy transactions in subsequent cases. Litigation against states has also involved disputes over **sovereign debt** backed by oil revenues, such as the litigation concerning bonds issued by PDVSA and the Republic of Congo's oil-backed loans, and disputes concerning the **Act of State doctrine**, under which US courts decline to adjudicate the validity of the public acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory, as applied to nationalisations of oil interests in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v Sabbatino* (1964).

### Commodity Trading and Price Disputes

Energy commodity trading generates litigation over the performance of trading contracts, the interpretation of standard terms (such as the **ISDA Master Agreement** for derivatives, and the BP, Shell, and other standard terms for crude oil and products trading), the validity of termination and close-out under derivatives contracts, and market manipulation. The **"negative oil prices"** of April 2020, when the price of the West Texas Intermediate futures contract fell below zero, led to litigation over losses incurred by investors and the conduct of trading platforms. The collapse of commodity traders and fraud in commodity trade finance, as in the collapse of **Hin Leong Trading** in Singapore in 2020 and the nickel trading crisis on the **London Metal Exchange** in March 2022 — when the LME suspended nickel trading and cancelled trades executed on the morning of 8 March after prices doubled in a day, a decision upheld by the English High Court against judicial review claims by hedge funds in ***R (Elliott Associates) v London Metal Exchange*** (2023) — illustrate the interaction of commercial and regulatory law in energy and metals markets.

## 27.6 Class Actions and Collective Redress

### The Problem of Mass Harm

Energy and resource activities can cause **mass harm** affecting large numbers of people — oil spills affecting entire communities, as in the Niger Delta and the *Deepwater Horizon* disaster; dam failures, as in the collapse of the Fundão tailings dam at Mariana in Brazil in 2015 and the Brumadinho dam in 2019; mine-related diseases, such as silicosis among gold miners in South Africa; air pollution from power plants; and consumer harms from energy pricing and mis-selling. Individual claims in such cases may be too small to justify the cost of litigation, and the duplication of individual proceedings would be inefficient and inconsistent. **Collective redress** mechanisms — class actions, representative actions, group litigation, and mass claims settlement mechanisms — allow claims to be aggregated, increasing access to justice and the efficiency of proceedings, while raising concerns about abusive litigation, the role of litigation funders, and the protection of absent class members.

### Class Actions in the United States

The **US class action** under **Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure** is the most developed model. A class action may be certified if the class is so numerous that joinder is impracticable, there are questions of law or fact common to the class, the claims of the representative parties are typical of those of the class, and the representatives will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the class; and, for damages class actions under Rule 23(b)(3), if common questions predominate over individual questions and a class action is superior to other methods. US class actions operate on an **opt-out** basis, binding all class members who do not exclude themselves, and are supported by contingency fees and the absence of the "loser pays" rule. The *Deepwater Horizon* litigation, consolidated in multidistrict litigation in New Orleans, resulted in a court-approved economic and property damages settlement and a medical benefits settlement with BP, under which billions of dollars were paid to claimants, as part of total costs to BP of more than USD 65 billion, including the settlement with the federal and Gulf states governments of around USD 20.8 billion, examined in Chapter 17. The US Supreme Court's decision in *Wal-Mart Stores v Dukes* (2011), requiring that common questions generate common answers apt to drive the resolution of the litigation, has made certification more demanding.

### Group Litigation in England and Wales

In **England and Wales**, collective redress is available principally through **Group Litigation Orders** (GLOs) under the Civil Procedure Rules, which allow the court to manage claims giving rise to common or related issues of fact or law, on an **opt-in** basis, and through **representative actions** under CPR 19.8, which allow one or more persons with the "same interest" to represent others, on an opt-out basis. In ***Lloyd v Google*** (2021), the Supreme Court held that a representative action for damages could not proceed where damages required individualised assessment, limiting the use of representative actions for damages, though it suggested that a bifurcated approach, with common issues determined in a representative action and individual damages in subsequent proceedings, could be appropriate. Opt-out collective proceedings are available for competition law claims before the **Competition Appeal Tribunal** under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, and have been brought against energy and other companies.

English group litigation has become a major forum for claims arising from the overseas activities of UK-domiciled energy and mining companies. The ***Bodo Community v Shell Petroleum Development Co*** litigation, concerning two oil spills in 2008 in the Bodo community in Ogoniland, was settled in 2015 for £55 million, paid to more than 15,000 individual claimants and the community, as noted in Chapter 17; a further claim concerning the remediation of the spills proceeded to a trial in 2025. In ***Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell*** (2021), examined in section 27.8, the Supreme Court allowed claims by more than 40,000 Nigerians from the Ogale and Bille communities to proceed against Shell's parent company. In the ***Município de Mariana v BHP Group*** litigation, more than 600,000 Brazilian claimants, including municipalities, businesses, and indigenous communities, brought claims against BHP in respect of the Fundão dam collapse; after the Court of Appeal allowed the claim to proceed in 2022, reversing a decision to strike it out as an abuse of process, the High Court held in **November 2025**, following a trial of liability, that BHP was liable under Brazilian law as a polluter. The case is reportedly the largest group action in English legal history and illustrates the potential and the challenges of group litigation for mass harms abroad, including its interaction with settlements in the home state — in October 2024, BHP, Vale, and their joint venture Samarco agreed a settlement with Brazilian authorities of around BRL 170 billion.

### Collective Redress in the European Union and Other Jurisdictions

In the **European Union**, the **Representative Actions Directive** (Directive (EU) 2020/1828), applicable since June 2023, requires member states to provide for representative actions by qualified entities for the protection of the collective interests of consumers, including for redress measures such as compensation, in areas including energy. The **Netherlands** has become a leading European forum for collective actions under the **Act on Redress of Mass Damages in a Collective Action** (WAMCA) of 2020, which allows opt-out collective damages actions by representative foundations, and under which claims have been brought against energy companies, including in relation to the earthquakes caused by gas production in the **Groningen** field, which led to extensive litigation and compensation schemes administered under public law. **Canada**'s provincial class action regimes, **Australia**'s Part IVA representative proceedings under the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976, which have been used in claims over the Montara oil spill in the Timor Sea (*Sanda v PTTEP Australasia*, 2021, in which the Federal Court found the operator liable in negligence to Indonesian seaweed farmers), and **South Africa**'s judicially developed class action procedure, used in the silicosis litigation against gold mining companies, which resulted in a settlement of around ZAR 5 billion in 2018, provide other models.

### Collective Redress in Nigeria

In **Nigeria**, collective claims are brought principally through **representative actions** under the rules of the Federal High Court and State High Courts, which allow one or more persons to sue on behalf of persons having the same interest, and through the joinder of numerous claimants. Oil pollution claims are frequently brought by community leaders on behalf of their communities, and the courts have addressed questions about the authority of representatives, the identification of the persons represented, and the distribution of compensation. The absence of a developed class action regime, combined with the cost and delay of litigation and the difficulty of enforcing judgments, has led many Niger Delta communities to pursue claims in the courts of England and the Netherlands, as examined in section 27.8. The Petroleum Industry Act's provisions on **host community development trusts**, examined in Chapter 24, and on compensation for environmental damage, may influence the future of community claims, as may the development of **non-judicial grievance mechanisms** and mass claims settlement procedures such as the claims process established in the Bodo settlement.

### Litigation Funding and Costs

Collective redress depends on **funding**. In the United States, contingency fees and the absence of costs-shifting make class actions viable. In England, the combination of conditional fee agreements, damages-based agreements, after-the-event insurance, and **third-party litigation funding** supports group litigation, though the costs-shifting rule exposes unsuccessful claimants to adverse costs. The UK Supreme Court's decision in ***R (PACCAR Inc) v Competition Appeal Tribunal*** (2023), which held that litigation funding agreements under which the funder's return is calculated as a percentage of damages are damages-based agreements subject to statutory regulation, disrupted the funding market, prompting proposals for legislation to reverse it, which the government announced in 2025 it would introduce following a review by the Civil Justice Council. The availability and regulation of litigation funding significantly affect access to justice for claimants harmed by energy activities, particularly in transnational cases.
