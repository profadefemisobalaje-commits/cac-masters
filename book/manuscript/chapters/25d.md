## 25.7 Energy Supply Disruptions and Emergency Powers

### Emergency Powers in Energy Law

Energy supply disruptions — whether caused by geopolitical events, natural disasters, technical failures, or market dysfunction — require rapid governmental responses that may exceed the ordinary powers of regulators. Most legal systems therefore confer **emergency powers** in the energy sector, enabling governments to direct the allocation of supplies, ration consumption, control prices, requisition facilities, suspend market rules, and override contractual arrangements. Such powers raise important questions of constitutional and administrative law: the conditions for their activation, their scope and duration, the safeguards against abuse, the compensation of affected parties, and their compatibility with international obligations under trade and investment law.

### National Emergency Legislation

In the **United Kingdom**, the **Energy Act 1976** empowers the Secretary of State, by order, to regulate or prohibit the production, supply, acquisition, or use of fuels and electricity, and to give directions to energy undertakings, when it appears that an actual or threatened emergency affecting fuel or electricity supplies makes it necessary. In the electricity sector, the system operator's emergency procedures, including **demand control** through rota disconnections, are governed by the Grid Code and the **Electricity Supply Emergency Code**. In the **United States**, the **Federal Power Act** (section 202(c)) authorises the Secretary of Energy, in an emergency, to order the temporary generation, delivery, interchange, or transmission of electricity — a power used in 2025 to require the continued operation of certain fossil fuel power plants scheduled for retirement, a controversial use that was challenged by states and environmental groups. The **Defense Production Act 1950** has been invoked to support domestic production of critical minerals and clean energy technologies. The **Energy Policy and Conservation Act 1975** governs drawdowns of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, as noted in section 25.3.

In **South Africa**, the government declared a **national state of disaster** under the **Disaster Management Act 2002** in February 2023 in response to the electricity crisis and severe load-shedding, enabling exemptions from procurement and other rules to accelerate new generation capacity, and appointed a Minister of Electricity in the Presidency. The declaration was challenged in court and was terminated in April 2023, illustrating the controversy surrounding the use of general emergency legislation to address structural energy problems. In **Nigeria**, the Electricity Act 2023 and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 confer powers on the regulators to issue orders and directives in the interest of security of supply, and the President's powers under the Constitution and emergency legislation may be invoked in extreme situations; episodes of fuel scarcity and grid collapse — the national grid suffered repeated collapses in 2024 — have prompted calls for clearer emergency response frameworks, including for the restoration of the grid and the protection of critical infrastructure.

### European Union Emergency Measures in 2022

The energy crisis of 2022 led the European Union to adopt unprecedented emergency measures on the basis of **Article 122(1) TFEU**, which permits the Council, on a proposal from the Commission, to decide, in a spirit of solidarity between member states, upon measures appropriate to the economic situation, in particular if severe difficulties arise in the supply of certain products, notably in the area of energy. Because Article 122 does not require the participation of the European Parliament, its use was criticised as bypassing democratic procedures. The measures included:

1. **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1369** on coordinated demand-reduction measures for gas, which established a voluntary target for member states to reduce their gas consumption by fifteen per cent between August 2022 and March 2023 compared with the average of the previous five years, with the possibility of a mandatory target in a "Union alert", and which was extended and later converted into a recommendation;
2. **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854** on an emergency intervention to address high energy prices, which required a mandatory reduction of electricity consumption at peak hours, imposed a cap on the market revenues of "inframarginal" generators (such as renewables and nuclear) at EUR 180 per megawatt-hour, with the excess revenues to be redistributed to consumers, and introduced a temporary **solidarity contribution** on the surplus profits of fossil fuel companies;
3. **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2576** on enhancing solidarity through better coordination of gas purchases, reliable price benchmarks, and exchanges of gas across borders; and
4. **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2578** establishing a **market correction mechanism** to limit excessive gas prices on the Dutch TTF trading hub.

The solidarity contribution was challenged before the EU General Court by energy companies, including ExxonMobil, which argued that Article 122 could not serve as a legal basis for a tax measure, which would require unanimity under Article 113 or 115 TFEU; the litigation raised fundamental questions about the scope of emergency powers in the EU legal order. Member states also adopted national measures, including windfall taxes, price caps, the nationalisation or bailout of energy companies — notably Germany's nationalisation of **Uniper**, the largest importer of Russian gas, in 2022 — and the use of state aid under the Commission's **Temporary Crisis Framework**. The reform of the electricity market design in 2024, examined in Chapter 24, includes a mechanism for the Council to declare a regional or Union-wide **electricity price crisis**, enabling member states to set regulated prices below cost for households and small businesses.

### Electricity System Emergencies

Electricity systems face distinctive emergencies because supply and demand must be balanced instantaneously. Major blackouts — such as the **North-East blackout** in the United States and Canada in August 2003, the **Indian blackout** of July 2012, which affected more than 600 million people, the **Texas** crisis during Winter Storm Uri in February 2021, examined in Chapter 24, and the **Iberian blackout** of 28 April 2025, which left Spain and Portugal without power for most of a day — have led to reviews of reliability standards, system operation, and emergency procedures. In the European Union, **Regulation (EU) 2019/941** on risk-preparedness in the electricity sector requires member states to identify regional and national electricity crisis scenarios, prepare **risk-preparedness plans**, and cooperate in a spirit of solidarity, including through regional and bilateral measures to assist each other in crises. In the United States, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation develops mandatory reliability standards enforced by FERC, including standards on cold-weather preparedness adopted after Winter Storm Uri. In Africa, the frequent collapse of national grids, as in Nigeria, and the prolonged load-shedding in South Africa between 2007 and 2024 illustrate the consequences of underinvestment and weak maintenance.

### Force Majeure and Contractual Responses

Energy supply disruptions have significant contractual consequences, examined in Chapters 6 and 16. Suppliers may invoke **force majeure** clauses to suspend performance, and buyers may invoke them to excuse non-acceptance; the interpretation of such clauses in the context of sanctions, wars, and pandemics has generated substantial litigation and arbitration. Following its reduction of supplies to European customers in 2022, Gazprom invoked force majeure, a claim rejected in several arbitrations. In **June 2024**, an arbitral tribunal awarded **Uniper** damages of more than EUR 13 billion against Gazprom Export for non-delivery of gas, and other European buyers, including RWE, OMV, and Ørsted, brought claims or obtained awards. Gazprom obtained **anti-suit and anti-arbitration injunctions** from Russian courts under Article 248.1 of the Russian Arbitrazh Procedure Code, introduced in 2020, which confers exclusive jurisdiction on Russian courts over disputes involving sanctioned Russian persons, and obtained judgments against European companies for continuing arbitrations. The conflict between Russian courts and international arbitration, examined in Chapter 26, illustrates the fragmentation of the legal order governing energy trade in times of geopolitical confrontation.

### Price Controls and Export Restrictions

Energy emergencies also prompt **price controls** and **export restrictions**. Price controls on fuel and electricity, while politically attractive, may cause shortages and deter investment, as Nigeria's long experience with petrol subsidies, examined in Chapter 24, illustrates. Export restrictions, such as **Indonesia's ban on coal exports in January 2022**, imposed to secure supplies for domestic power plants, and China's restrictions on exports of refined products and critical minerals, may conflict with obligations under Article XI of the GATT, subject to the exception in Article XI:2(a) for temporary restrictions applied to prevent or relieve critical shortages of foodstuffs or other products essential to the exporting party, and the general exceptions in Article XX, as examined in Chapter 13. The interpretation of these exceptions in the context of energy emergencies remains largely untested.

## 25.8 Geopolitical Risk and International Legal Responses

### The Geopolitics of the Energy Transition

The energy transition is reshaping energy geopolitics. As the role of oil and gas declines, the geopolitical significance of petrostates may diminish, raising risks of economic and political instability in states dependent on hydrocarbon revenues, including in Africa, as examined in Chapter 23. At the same time, new dependencies are emerging. The supply chains of clean energy technologies are highly concentrated: China accounts for the majority of global refining of lithium, cobalt, and graphite, almost all rare earth processing, and the large majority of solar panel, battery, and electrolyser manufacturing. The **weaponisation** of these dependencies has become a concern: China imposed export controls on gallium and germanium in 2023, graphite in late 2023, antimony in 2024, and certain rare earth elements and related technologies in 2025, in the context of trade and technological rivalry with the United States, as examined in Chapter 22. Clean energy also creates new forms of interdependence through **electricity interconnections** and **hydrogen trade**, which may raise security issues similar to those of gas pipelines.

### Critical Minerals Diplomacy

States have responded to the concentration of critical mineral supply chains with a range of legal and policy instruments, examined in Chapter 8. These include industrial policies, such as the US **Inflation Reduction Act**, whose clean vehicle tax credit included critical mineral sourcing requirements and excluded "foreign entities of concern", and the EU **Critical Raw Materials Act**, which sets benchmarks for domestic extraction, processing, and recycling and limits dependence on any single third country to sixty-five per cent of annual consumption of each strategic raw material; international partnerships, such as the **Minerals Security Partnership**, launched by the United States and partners in 2022, the **G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan** adopted in 2025, and bilateral critical minerals agreements, including the agreements of the United States with Japan (2023), Ukraine (April 2025, establishing a joint reconstruction investment fund financed in part by revenues from new natural resource projects), and Australia (October 2025); and strategic partnerships between the European Union and resource-rich states, including memoranda of understanding with the DRC, Zambia, Namibia, and Rwanda. Resource-rich developing states, including in Africa, are seeking to use their mineral endowments to capture more value through local processing and manufacturing, as in the export bans of Indonesia and Zimbabwe, examined in Chapters 8 and 24, and the **Green Minerals Strategy** developed under the auspices of the African Union. The tension between the supply security of importing states and the developmental aspirations of producing states is a defining feature of critical minerals geopolitics.

### Cyber Security of Energy Infrastructure

Energy infrastructure is increasingly exposed to **cyber attacks**. The attacks on **Ukraine's electricity distribution** in December 2015, which caused outages affecting around 225,000 customers, and in December 2016, using the "Industroyer" malware, were the first confirmed cyber attacks causing power outages, and were attributed to Russian military intelligence. The **ransomware attack on Colonial Pipeline** in May 2021, which led the operator of the largest refined products pipeline in the United States to shut down its operations for several days, causing fuel shortages along the East Coast, demonstrated the vulnerability of energy systems to criminal as well as state actors. The **Stuxnet** worm, discovered in 2010, which damaged centrifuges at Iran's Natanz uranium enrichment facility, illustrated the potential for state-sponsored cyber operations to cause physical damage to energy-related infrastructure.

International law applies to cyber operations, as affirmed by the UN Group of Governmental Experts in 2013 and 2015 and the Open-Ended Working Group. The **2015 report of the UN Group of Governmental Experts**, endorsed by the General Assembly, articulated voluntary norms of responsible state behaviour, including that states should not conduct or knowingly support ICT activity contrary to their obligations that intentionally damages critical infrastructure or otherwise impairs its use and operation to provide services to the public, and should take appropriate measures to protect their critical infrastructure. The **Tallinn Manual 2.0 on the International Law Applicable to Cyber Operations** (2017), prepared by an international group of experts, applies the rules on sovereignty, non-intervention, the use of force, and international humanitarian law to cyber operations, concluding, for example, that a cyber operation causing physical damage comparable to a kinetic attack may constitute a use of force. At the regional level, the European Union's **NIS2 Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) imposes cyber security risk management and incident reporting obligations on entities in critical sectors, including energy, and the **Critical Entities Resilience Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2557) requires member states to identify critical entities and ensure their resilience against physical and other threats. In the United States, the Transportation Security Administration issued mandatory **security directives** for pipeline operators after the Colonial Pipeline attack, and NERC's **Critical Infrastructure Protection** standards apply to the bulk electric system. In Nigeria, the **Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) Act 2015**, amended in 2024, provides for the designation and protection of critical national information infrastructure, which may include energy systems.

### Investment Screening

Concern about foreign control of critical energy infrastructure and resources has led to the expansion of **foreign direct investment screening** regimes. The **Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States** (CFIUS), whose powers were expanded by the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act of 2018, reviews transactions that could result in foreign control of US businesses or certain non-controlling investments in critical technology, critical infrastructure, and sensitive personal data, including energy infrastructure. The **EU Foreign Direct Investment Screening Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2019/452), which has been the subject of a revision intended to require all member states to maintain screening mechanisms covering minimum sectors, establishes a cooperation mechanism among member states and the Commission. The **United Kingdom's National Security and Investment Act 2021** requires mandatory notification of acquisitions in seventeen sensitive sectors, including energy and civil nuclear, and has been used to block or condition transactions. Similar regimes exist in Australia, Canada, Germany, and other states. These regimes reflect the securitisation of energy investment and may conflict with investment treaty obligations, although screening measures are generally accompanied by security exceptions. Producing states have also imposed restrictions: Canada announced in 2022 that it would require the divestment by Chinese companies of interests in Canadian critical minerals companies.

### International Energy Institutions

The institutional architecture of international energy governance, examined in Chapter 2, plays a role in managing geopolitical risk. The **International Energy Agency** has evolved from an oil-consumers' club into a broader forum for energy security and the energy transition; **OPEC** and **OPEC+** manage oil supply; the **Gas Exporting Countries Forum** brings together major gas producers; the **International Energy Forum** promotes dialogue between producers and consumers; the **International Renewable Energy Agency** promotes renewable energy; the **International Atomic Energy Agency** addresses nuclear safety and security; and the **Energy Charter Treaty**, despite the withdrawals of the European Union and several member states, continues to provide a framework for investment protection and transit among its remaining parties, following the adoption of modernised treaty provisions by the Energy Charter Conference in December 2024. Africa's institutional architecture includes the **African Energy Commission** (AFREC), the African Petroleum Producers' Organization (APPO), the regional power pools, and ECOWAS's Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency.

### The Fragmentation of the International Energy Order

The geopolitical turbulence of the 2020s has accentuated the **fragmentation** of the international energy order. The sanctions against Russia have created parallel markets, with Russian oil redirected to China, India, and Turkey, often through a "shadow fleet" and payment systems outside Western financial institutions, examined in Chapter 22. The rivalry between the United States and China is leading to the creation of competing supply chains for clean energy technologies and critical minerals. The withdrawal of the United States from the Paris Agreement, effective in January 2026, as examined in Chapter 18, and the use of tariffs and export controls as instruments of economic statecraft have weakened multilateral cooperation. The paralysis of the WTO's dispute settlement mechanism has limited the enforceability of trade rules. Against this background, international law faces the challenge of maintaining a rules-based framework for energy trade, investment, and security that accommodates legitimate security concerns without becoming a cover for protectionism and coercion.

For African states, including Nigeria, the fragmentation of the international order presents both risks and opportunities: risks of being caught in great-power rivalries, of exposure to the extraterritorial effects of sanctions, and of volatility in commodity markets; and opportunities to diversify partnerships, attract investment in critical minerals and gas from competing partners, and leverage their resource endowments for development. The legal frameworks examined in this book — investment law, contract law, trade law, regional integration through the AfCFTA, and the protection of human rights and the environment — provide tools for navigating these choices.
