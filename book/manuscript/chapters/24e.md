## 24.7 Latin American Resource Sovereignty and Regulation

### The Tradition of Resource Sovereignty

Latin America has been a crucible of resource sovereignty. The **Calvo doctrine**, articulated by the Argentine jurist Carlos Calvo in the nineteenth century, held that foreign investors should be subject to the jurisdiction of host state courts and should not seek the diplomatic protection of their home states — a principle reflected in "Calvo clauses" in contracts and constitutions and, for much of the twentieth century, in the reluctance of Latin American states to accept international arbitration. The **Mexican expropriation** of foreign oil companies in March 1938, under President Lázaro Cárdenas, and the creation of **Pemex**, was the first large-scale nationalisation of a petroleum industry and an enduring symbol of national sovereignty, commemorated as a national holiday. The **Hull formula** of "prompt, adequate and effective" compensation was articulated in the correspondence between US Secretary of State Cordell Hull and Mexico concerning agrarian expropriations, as examined in Chapter 14. Venezuela's nationalisation of its oil industry in 1976 and the creation of **Petróleos de Venezuela** (PDVSA), and Bolivia's repeated nationalisations of its hydrocarbons, most recently in 2006, continued this tradition.

Latin American resource governance has oscillated between cycles of **liberalisation**, in which states opened their resource sectors to foreign investment and signed bilateral investment treaties, often in the 1990s, and cycles of **resource nationalism**, in which states reasserted control, increased their fiscal take, renegotiated contracts, and in some cases withdrew from investment arbitration, notably in the 2000s during the commodity boom. These cycles reflect commodity prices, political ideologies, and the bargaining power of states and investors — illustrating the "obsolescing bargain" described by Raymond Vernon, under which the bargaining power of the host state increases once the investor has sunk its capital, as examined in Chapter 15.

### Mexico

**Mexico**'s Constitution of 1917 vested ownership of subsoil resources in the nation (Article 27), and constitutional amendments after 1938 reserved the petroleum industry exclusively to the state through Pemex and the electricity industry to the state through the **Comisión Federal de Electricidad** (CFE). In 2013, a landmark **energy reform** amended Articles 25, 27, and 28 of the Constitution to open the upstream petroleum sector to private investment through licences, production sharing contracts, and profit-sharing contracts, awarded through competitive rounds by the National Hydrocarbons Commission, and to open electricity generation and supply to competition, with an independent system operator. The reform attracted substantial investment commitments. From 2018, the administration of President López Obrador sought to reverse aspects of the reform, favouring Pemex and the CFE through regulatory and legislative measures, including an amendment to the Electricity Industry Law in 2021 to prioritise CFE dispatch, which was challenged before the Supreme Court and by private generators. In 2024, constitutional amendments redefined Pemex and the CFE as "public state companies" and gave the CFE priority in electricity, and in 2025 a new package of secondary energy laws replaced the legislation of the 2014 reform, dissolving the independent energy regulators and reorganising the sector around the state companies while preserving a role for private investment under state planning. The Mexican experience illustrates the volatility of resource governance and its implications for investors, including under the USMCA and investment treaties.

### Venezuela

**Venezuela**, which holds the world's largest proven oil reserves, consisting largely of extra-heavy crude in the Orinoco Belt, opened its sector to foreign investment in the 1990s through the *apertura petrolera*, including strategic associations for the Orinoco projects. The **Hydrocarbons Law of 2001**, enacted under President Chávez, required that primary activities be conducted by the state or by **mixed companies** (*empresas mixtas*) in which the state holds more than fifty per cent, and raised royalties. In 2007, Venezuela required the migration of the Orinoco associations and operating agreements to mixed companies, leading to the exit of ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips and to investment arbitrations, in which ICSID tribunals found that Venezuela had unlawfully expropriated ConocoPhillips's investments, awarding around USD 8.7 billion in 2019, a decision upheld in annulment proceedings. Venezuela's production collapsed amid mismanagement, the deterioration of PDVSA, and US sanctions imposed from 2017 and 2019. The enforcement of awards against Venezuelan assets, including the court-supervised sale of shares in CITGO's parent company in the United States, examined in Chapter 26, illustrates the long tail of resource nationalism.

### Brazil

**Brazil** broke the state monopoly of Petrobras with a constitutional amendment of 1995 and the **Petroleum Law** (Law No. 9,478 of 1997), which established the **National Agency of Petroleum, Natural Gas and Biofuels** (ANP) and introduced a concession regime under which blocks are awarded in competitive bidding rounds. Following the discovery of the giant **pre-salt** fields offshore in 2006–2007, Brazil adopted a **production sharing regime** for the pre-salt area under Law No. 12,351 of 2010, initially requiring Petrobras to be the operator with a minimum thirty per cent interest in all pre-salt blocks; Law No. 13,365 of 2016 removed the mandatory operatorship, giving Petrobras a right of preference. Brazil thus operates a mixed regime, with concessions outside the pre-salt and strategic areas and production sharing within them, and has become one of the world's largest oil producers, with Petrobras a leader in deepwater technology. The *Lava Jato* corruption scandal at Petrobras has been examined in Chapter 22. Brazil's leadership in biofuels, its large hydropower sector, and its expanding wind and solar capacity, together with its hosting of COP30 in Belém in 2025, illustrate its significance in the energy transition, while its plans to expand oil production, including in the Equatorial Margin near the mouth of the Amazon, illustrate the tensions examined in Chapter 18.

### Argentina

**Argentina**'s Constitution, as amended in 1994, provides that the provinces have original ownership of the natural resources in their territories (Article 124), so that provinces grant hydrocarbon permits and concessions under the federal Hydrocarbons Law (Law No. 17,319 of 1967, as amended, including in 2014 to accommodate unconventional resources). In 2012, Argentina expropriated fifty-one per cent of the shares of **YPF**, the formerly state-owned oil company privatised in the 1990s, from Repsol, which received compensation of around USD 5 billion under a settlement in 2014. Minority shareholders, whose rights under YPF's by-laws to a tender offer had not been honoured, sued in the United States, and in September 2023 the US District Court for the Southern District of New York entered judgment against Argentina for around USD 16.1 billion in ***Petersen Energía Inversora v Argentine Republic***; in 2025 the court ordered Argentina to turn over its YPF shares in partial satisfaction of the judgment, an order that was stayed pending appeal. The development of the **Vaca Muerta** shale formation, one of the largest unconventional resources outside North America, has transformed Argentina into a growing oil and gas exporter. The **Incentive Regime for Large Investments** (RIGI), enacted in 2024, offers tax, customs, and foreign exchange incentives and stability guarantees, with access to international arbitration, for large investments in sectors including energy and mining.

### The Lithium Triangle and Resource Sovereignty in the Transition

The **"lithium triangle"** of Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile holds a majority of the world's identified lithium resources, in brine deposits in high-altitude salt flats. The three states have adopted contrasting legal models. **Chile**, where lithium has been designated a strategic mineral not subject to ordinary mining concessions since 1979, has granted rights through contracts with the state development agency CORFO; its **National Lithium Strategy** (2023) provides for state participation in lithium projects, including through a partnership between the state copper company Codelco and SQM in the Salar de Atacama, under which Codelco acquired a majority interest from 2031. **Bolivia** reserves lithium development to the state through **Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos** (YLB), which has concluded agreements with Chinese and Russian partners for direct lithium extraction projects, subject to legislative approval. **Argentina**'s provinces grant mining concessions to private companies under the federal Mining Code, which has attracted significant investment, while some provinces have sought greater participation through provincial companies. These models illustrate different approaches to capturing value from transition minerals, examined in Chapter 8, and their interaction with the rights of indigenous communities, examined in Chapter 21.

### Rights of Nature and Environmental Constitutionalism

Latin America has also pioneered **environmental constitutionalism**. **Ecuador**'s Constitution of 2008 recognises the **rights of nature** (*Pacha Mama*), including the right to the integral respect for its existence and the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles (Articles 71–74). In the ***Los Cedros*** case (2021), the Constitutional Court of Ecuador held that mining concessions in the Los Cedros protected forest violated the rights of nature and the right to environmental consultation, revoking the concessions. Colombia's courts have recognised rivers and the Amazon as subjects of rights. The **Escazú Agreement**, examined in Chapters 21 and 23, establishes procedural environmental rights across the region. These developments have influenced debates about the legal status of nature in other regions, including Africa.

### Guyana

**Guyana** has become one of the world's newest oil producers since ExxonMobil's discovery of the Liza field in the Stabroek Block in 2015, with production beginning in 2019 and rising rapidly. The **Stabroek production sharing agreement** of 2016, criticised for its fiscal terms, including a two per cent royalty, a fifty-fifty profit oil split, and the payment of the contractor's taxes from the government's share, became the subject of public controversy and litigation. Guyana enacted the **Natural Resource Fund Act 2021**, establishing a sovereign wealth fund, and the **Petroleum Activities Act 2023**, which modernised the legal framework, including by providing for a new model PSA with higher royalties for future contracts. The maritime and territorial dispute with Venezuela over the Essequibo region, examined in Chapter 20, overshadows Guyana's development. Guyana's experience offers lessons for new producers, including in Africa, on the importance of negotiating capacity, transparency, and revenue management.

## 24.8 Comparative Lessons from African Resource-Rich States

### Diversity of Experience

Africa's resource-rich states display a wide diversity of legal frameworks and outcomes. This section surveys selected experiences, emphasising features of comparative significance for the design of resource legal frameworks, including in Nigeria.

### Botswana: Institutions and Partnership

**Botswana** is frequently cited as Africa's foremost example of successful resource governance. Since the discovery of diamonds shortly after independence, Botswana has used diamond revenues to finance infrastructure, education, and health, and has maintained macroeconomic stability and democratic governance. Its legal framework vests minerals in the state under the Mines and Minerals Act, and its diamond production is conducted principally through **Debswana**, a fifty-fifty joint venture between the government and De Beers, under agreements that have evolved to increase the government's share of revenues and its role in sorting, valuation, and sales. In 2023 and 2025, the government and De Beers agreed on new sales and licence arrangements, extending Debswana's mining licences and increasing the share of rough diamonds sold through the state-owned Okavango Diamond Company. Botswana's fiscal rules, including the **Sustainable Budget Index**, under which mineral revenues are to be used only for investment spending, and the **Pula Fund**, examined in Chapter 23, have contributed to prudent revenue management. Botswana's success has been attributed to the quality of its institutions, an inclusive political settlement, a long-term partnership with a single major investor, and conservative fiscal management — factors that may be difficult to replicate, and which have been tested by the decline in diamond demand in the 2020s.

### Ghana: Revenue Management and Transparency

**Ghana**, which became an oil producer with the Jubilee field in 2010, adopted the **Petroleum Revenue Management Act 2011**, examined in Chapter 23, and the **Petroleum (Exploration and Production) Act 2016** (Act 919), which modernised its upstream legal framework, introducing competitive licensing, a public register of petroleum agreements and licences, beneficial ownership disclosure, and strengthened regulation by the Petroleum Commission. Ghana's local content regulations of 2013 set ambitious targets for Ghanaian participation. Ghana's experience demonstrates the value of early adoption of transparency and revenue management frameworks by a new producer, although the country's debt crisis and restructuring in 2022–2024 showed that sound petroleum laws cannot alone ensure macroeconomic stability.

### Tanzania: Resource Nationalism and Renegotiation

**Tanzania** enacted in 2017 a package of laws asserting sovereignty over natural resources: the **Natural Wealth and Resources (Permanent Sovereignty) Act 2017**, which declared permanent sovereignty over natural resources, required that resource agreements be approved by Parliament, and prohibited the settlement of disputes concerning natural resources by international arbitration, requiring adjudication in Tanzanian courts; the **Natural Wealth and Resources Contracts (Review and Re-Negotiation of Unconscionable Terms) Act 2017**, which empowered Parliament to review and require renegotiation of agreements containing "unconscionable" terms; and amendments to the Mining Act increasing royalties and requiring a free carried interest of at least sixteen per cent for the government. The government's dispute with Acacia Mining (a subsidiary of Barrick Gold) over alleged under-declaration of export revenues, which led to an assessment of more than USD 190 billion in unpaid taxes and penalties, was resolved in 2019–2020 through a framework agreement under which Barrick paid USD 300 million and established a joint venture, Twiga Minerals, with the government holding a sixteen per cent interest and sharing economic benefits equally. Tanzania's approach illustrates the tools of resource nationalism and their costs: while the government obtained improved terms, investment in the sector declined, and later administrations sought to restore investor confidence, including by amending the restrictions on arbitration.

### The Democratic Republic of the Congo: Strategic Minerals

The **Democratic Republic of the Congo**, the world's leading producer of cobalt and a major producer of copper, revised its **Mining Code** in 2018, increasing royalties, introducing a fifty per cent tax on "super-profits", reducing the stability period for existing mining rights from ten to five years, and authorising the designation of **strategic substances**, subject to a royalty of ten per cent; cobalt and germanium were so designated. The revision, adopted despite the opposition of major mining companies, which argued that it violated the stability provisions of the 2002 Code, illustrates the use of fiscal reform to increase government take during a commodity boom. In 2025, faced with low cobalt prices due to oversupply, the DRC suspended cobalt exports for several months and then introduced an export quota system, using its market position to influence prices. The DRC's renegotiation of the *Sicomines* infrastructure-for-minerals agreement with Chinese partners in 2024, which increased the infrastructure commitment, illustrates the review of resource-for-infrastructure deals. The DRC's mineral wealth is also intertwined with conflict in the east of the country, examined in Chapter 25, and with concerns about artisanal mining and child labour, examined in Chapter 21.

### Senegal, Mozambique, and Uganda: New Producers

New producers have sought to learn from earlier experience. **Senegal**, which began oil production from the offshore Sangomar field in June 2024 and gas production from the Greater Tortue Ahmeyim LNG project, shared with Mauritania, adopted a new **Petroleum Code** in 2019, a **local content law** in 2019, and a law on the distribution and management of petroleum revenues in 2022, and its constitution, as amended in 2016, provides that natural resources belong to the people. The government elected in 2024 launched an audit and renegotiation of oil, gas, and mining contracts. **Mozambique**, which holds vast gas reserves in the Rovuma Basin, adopted a Petroleum Law in 2014 and special decree-laws for the LNG projects, but its development has been disrupted by the insurgency in Cabo Delgado, examined in Chapter 21. **Uganda**, which discovered oil in the Albertine Graben in 2006, adopted the **Petroleum (Exploration, Development and Production) Act 2013** and the **Public Finance Management Act 2015**, establishing a Petroleum Fund, and is developing its oil fields with TotalEnergies and CNOOC, together with the East African Crude Oil Pipeline, examined in Chapters 6 and 21.

### South Africa: Transformation and Electricity Reform

**South Africa**'s **Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act 2002** (MPRDA) vested mineral and petroleum resources in the custodianship of the state for the benefit of all South Africans, abolishing private mineral rights and requiring holders to convert old-order rights into new-order rights. The **Mining Charter**, issued under the MPRDA and revised several times, set targets for black economic empowerment in ownership, management, procurement, and community development, and its legal status was addressed by the High Court in 2021, which held that the Charter is a policy instrument rather than binding law. In the electricity sector, the crisis of **Eskom**, examined in Chapter 16, led to the **Electricity Regulation Amendment Act 2024**, which provides for the establishment of an independent transmission system operator and a competitive wholesale electricity market, and to the liberalisation of private generation, including the removal of licensing requirements for embedded generation in 2022. The REIPPPP, examined in Chapters 10 and 23, has been an influential model of renewable energy procurement.

### Comparative Lessons

Several lessons emerge from this comparative survey for the design of resource legal frameworks in Africa, including in Nigeria:

1. **Institutions matter more than models.** Similar legal models — production sharing contracts, NOCs, sovereign wealth funds, independent regulators — have produced very different outcomes depending on the quality of institutions, the rule of law, and political commitment. Legal reform must therefore be accompanied by investment in institutional capacity and accountability.
2. **Transparency and accountability are foundational.** The disclosure of contracts, licences, beneficial owners, and payments, and independent oversight, as in Ghana's PIAC and Nigeria's NEITI, constrain corruption and build public trust.
3. **Stability and flexibility must be balanced.** Resource nationalism, as in Tanzania and the DRC, may increase government take in the short term but may deter investment; excessive stability guarantees may prevent states from capturing a fair share of windfalls or responding to changing circumstances. Fiscal regimes with progressive features that respond automatically to prices and profitability may reduce the pressure for renegotiation, as examined in Chapter 16.
4. **Revenue management requires rules and political consensus.** Fiscal rules and savings funds work only when supported by political consensus and legal safeguards, as the contrast between Botswana and Ghana on the one hand and Chad on the other illustrates.
5. **Communities must benefit.** Sustainable resource development requires that host communities share in the benefits and that their rights be respected, through mechanisms such as community development agreements and host community trusts, whose governance must be credible.
6. **The energy transition is reshaping the agenda.** Africa's resource-rich states must manage the risks of stranded fossil fuel assets while capturing value from transition minerals, including through local processing, and must expand energy access for their own populations — challenges addressed by the **Africa Mining Vision** (2009), adopted by the African Union to promote transparent, equitable, and optimal exploitation of mineral resources for broad-based sustainable growth, and by subsequent continental strategies on green minerals.

## Conclusion

Comparative energy and natural resources law reveals both the diversity of legal approaches to common problems and the recurring patterns that shape the sector: the tension between state control and private investment, between stability and adaptability, between national revenues and local benefits, and, increasingly, between the development of fossil fuel resources and the energy transition. Nigeria's recent reforms — the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, the Electricity Act 2023, the Climate Change Act 2021, and the removal of the petrol subsidy — draw on international models while responding to the country's distinctive federal structure, history, and challenges. The experience of the United States, the United Kingdom, and the European Union illustrates the evolution of liberalised energy markets and the legal frameworks of the energy transition; the Middle East, Asia, and Latin America illustrate the range of models of petroleum and mineral governance and the cycles of liberalisation and resource nationalism; and Africa's resource-rich states offer lessons about the primacy of institutions, transparency, and inclusive benefit-sharing. For the doctoral researcher, comparative law offers a powerful method for evaluating and improving legal frameworks, provided that it is conducted with methodological rigour and sensitivity to context — themes to which Chapter 28 returns.
