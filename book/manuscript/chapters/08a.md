## Introduction

The energy transition is, among other things, a transition from a fuel-intensive energy system to a material-intensive one. A conventional car requires little more than steel, aluminium, and copper; an electric car requires, in addition, around two hundred kilograms of minerals for its battery and motor, including lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, graphite, and rare earth elements. An onshore wind plant requires around nine times more mineral resources than a gas-fired power plant of the same capacity. Solar photovoltaic panels require silicon, silver, copper, and, in some technologies, tellurium, indium, and gallium. Electricity networks, which must expand dramatically to accommodate renewable generation and electrification, require vast quantities of copper and aluminium. The International Energy Agency has projected that, in scenarios consistent with the goals of the Paris Agreement, demand for minerals for clean energy technologies could increase several-fold by 2040, with demand for lithium potentially growing by a factor of more than ten.

The minerals on which the transition depends are characterised by a combination of features that generate distinctive legal and political challenges. Their production is geographically concentrated: the Democratic Republic of the Congo produces around seventy per cent of the world's cobalt; Australia, Chile, and China dominate lithium production; Indonesia has become the world's largest producer of nickel; and China accounts for around sixty to seventy per cent of rare earth mining. Processing is even more concentrated: China refines the majority of the world's lithium, cobalt, graphite, and rare earths, and controls most of the world's capacity to manufacture battery cells and permanent magnets. Supply is relatively inelastic, because new mines typically take ten to fifteen years or more from discovery to production. Many deposits are located in countries with weak governance, high levels of poverty, and histories of conflict, and in or near territories of indigenous peoples. Production is associated with serious environmental impacts and, in some contexts, with child labour, forced labour, and other human rights abuses.

These features have transformed critical minerals from a niche concern of defence planners and materials scientists into a central preoccupation of energy, trade, industrial, and foreign policy. Governments in importing states have adopted critical mineral strategies, legislation, and international partnerships aimed at diversifying supply and reducing dependence on China. Governments in producing states, particularly in Africa and Latin America, have sought to capture a greater share of the value of their mineral resources by requiring domestic processing, restricting exports of unprocessed ores, increasing state participation, and renegotiating agreements. China, for its part, has used export controls on minerals and processing technologies as instruments of economic statecraft. The legal regime of critical minerals sits at the intersection of mining law (examined in Chapter 7), trade law (Chapter 13), investment law (Chapter 14), human rights law (Chapter 21), and the law of geopolitics and national security (Chapter 25), and it is evolving with extraordinary speed.

This chapter examines the legal classification of critical and strategic minerals (section 8.1); the distinctive issues associated with lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and rare earth elements (section 8.2); strategic mineral ownership and export controls (section 8.3); resource security and industrial policy (section 8.4); supply chain due diligence (section 8.5); human rights and labour risks in mineral production (section 8.6); processing, recycling, and circular economy regulation (section 8.7); and geopolitical competition and critical mineral agreements (section 8.8). The institutional dimension of critical minerals governance, including the role of the International Energy Agency, was introduced in Chapter 4.

## 8.1 Legal Classification of Critical and Strategic Minerals

### The Concept of Criticality

There is no universally accepted definition of a "critical mineral". The concept of criticality, as developed in the policy literature, generally combines two dimensions: the *economic importance* of a mineral — the extent to which it is essential to economic activity, technologies, or national security, and the difficulty of substituting it — and its *supply risk* — the likelihood of disruption to its supply, arising from geological scarcity, geographic concentration of production and processing, political instability or poor governance in producing countries, export restrictions, low rates of recycling, and similar factors. A mineral is "critical" if it scores highly on both dimensions. The related concept of a "strategic" mineral, which has its origins in military planning during and after the Second World War, emphasises importance for national defence or for strategic sectors of the economy, such as the energy transition, digital technologies, and aerospace.

Criticality assessments are contingent and dynamic. They depend on the perspective of the assessing state (a mineral critical to an importing state may be of little concern to a producing state), on technological developments (new battery chemistries may reduce or increase demand for particular minerals), and on market and geopolitical conditions. Most jurisdictions therefore review their lists periodically.

### Classification in Major Jurisdictions

**The European Union.** The European Commission first published a list of critical raw materials in 2011 and has updated it every three years since. The Critical Raw Materials Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, which entered into force in May 2024 — placed the classification on a legislative footing. Annex II lists thirty-four critical raw materials, assessed on the basis of economic importance and supply risk according to a methodology set out in the Regulation, and Annex I lists seventeen strategic raw materials, selected from among the critical raw materials on the basis of their importance for technologies supporting the green and digital transitions and defence and aerospace applications, the projected growth in demand, and the difficulty of increasing production. The strategic list includes lithium, nickel (battery grade), cobalt, natural graphite, manganese (battery grade), copper, rare earth elements for magnets, gallium, germanium, silicon metal, tungsten, and platinum group metals. The classification has legal consequences: strategic raw materials are the subject of the Act's benchmarks for domestic capacity and diversification and are eligible for strategic project status, examined in section 8.4.

**The United States.** In the United States, the Energy Act of 2020 defines a "critical mineral" as a non-fuel mineral or mineral material essential to the economic or national security of the United States, the supply chain of which is vulnerable to disruption, and that serves an essential function in the manufacturing of a product, the absence of which would have significant consequences for the economic or national security of the United States. The Act requires the Secretary of the Interior, acting through the United States Geological Survey (USGS), to publish and periodically review a list of critical minerals. The 2022 list identified fifty critical minerals, and the USGS's subsequent review in 2025 expanded the list, adding minerals including copper. Separately, the Department of Energy maintains a list of "critical materials" for energy, and the Department of Defense manages the National Defense Stockpile of strategic and critical materials under the Strategic and Critical Materials Stock Piling Act. The classification has legal consequences in, among other things, the eligibility requirements for the clean vehicle tax credit under section 30D of the Internal Revenue Code (as amended by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022), streamlined permitting, and federal financing.

**Other jurisdictions.** Australia, Canada, Japan, India, the United Kingdom, South Korea, and China have also adopted lists of critical or strategic minerals. China's National Mineral Resources Plan (2016–2020) identified twenty-four strategic minerals, including oil, gas, coal, rare earths, lithium, cobalt, and graphite. Producing states have increasingly adopted their own classifications, often for different purposes: whereas importing states classify minerals as critical to identify supply risks, producing states classify them as strategic to justify enhanced state control, higher royalties, and local processing requirements. The Democratic Republic of the Congo's Mining Code of 2018 permits the government to declare minerals "strategic substances" subject to a ten per cent royalty, and in 2018 the government so classified cobalt, germanium, and columbite-tantalite (coltan).

### The African Perspective

African institutions have increasingly articulated their own understanding of critical minerals, emphasising their potential to drive industrialisation rather than merely their role in supplying importing economies. The African Union's Africa Mining Vision (2009), discussed in Chapter 7, already emphasised beneficiation and linkages, and the African Union has since developed an African Green Minerals Strategy aimed at leveraging the continent's mineral endowment for green industrialisation, regional value chains, and economic transformation. The Africa Finance Corporation, the African Development Bank, and the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa have supported initiatives to develop regional battery and electric vehicle value chains, such as the cooperation agreement between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia, signed in 2022, to develop a battery precursor value chain linking Congolese cobalt and Zambian copper and manganese.

In Nigeria, the Ministry of Solid Minerals Development has identified a number of minerals, including lithium, as priority minerals for development, and the federal government has adopted a policy of promoting local processing of lithium and other minerals rather than the export of raw ore. The first lithium processing plants in Nigeria, owned by Chinese investors and located in Kaduna and Nasarawa States, began operations in 2024.

### Legal Consequences of Classification

Classification as critical or strategic may have a range of legal consequences, depending on the jurisdiction:

- **Permitting**: projects involving critical minerals may benefit from expedited permitting procedures, as under the EU Critical Raw Materials Act's strategic project regime and various United States initiatives.
- **Financing**: projects may be eligible for public financing, loan guarantees, or grants, as under Title III of the United States Defense Production Act and the programmes of export credit agencies.
- **Trade**: classification may support export restrictions in producing states or tariff and procurement preferences in importing states.
- **Investment screening**: foreign investment in critical mineral assets may be subject to enhanced scrutiny on national security grounds, as under the Canadian government's November 2022 policy on investments by state-owned enterprises in critical minerals, which led to orders requiring three Chinese companies to divest their interests in Canadian lithium companies.
- **Fiscal**: producing states may impose higher royalties on strategic minerals, as in the DRC.
- **Stockpiling**: governments may maintain strategic stockpiles of critical minerals, analogous to strategic petroleum reserves (examined in Chapter 25).

Because the label "critical" or "strategic" may trigger significant legal consequences, the methodology and transparency of classification are themselves legally significant, and the classification decisions of administrative agencies may be subject to judicial review.
