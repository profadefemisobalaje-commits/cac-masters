## 18.8 Climate Governance and International Legal Reform

### The Limits of the Current Architecture

The international climate regime has achieved universal participation, a shared long-term goal, and an architecture for transparency and progressive ambition. Yet global emissions have continued to rise for most of the period since the UNFCCC was adopted, the world remains on a path towards warming well above the Paris goals, and the adverse effects of climate change are intensifying. The gap between commitments and outcomes has generated extensive debate about the reform of climate governance. The principal proposals concern the decision-making processes of the UNFCCC, the integrity of carbon markets, climate finance and the international financial architecture, the role of other international regimes, the governance of fossil fuel production, and the accountability of non-state actors.

### Reforming the UNFCCC Process

The consensus rule, examined in section 18.1, allows a small number of states to block or dilute decisions, and has been blamed for weak outcomes on fossil fuels. Proposals for reform include the adoption of rules of procedure permitting majority voting on certain matters, as a last resort; the strengthening of the role of science in negotiations; criteria for the selection of COP hosts and presidencies; rules on conflicts of interest and the participation of fossil fuel industry representatives; and a greater focus on implementation, accountability, and the review of whether states are delivering their commitments. A group of eminent persons convened by the Club of Rome argued in 2024 that the COP process was "no longer fit for purpose" and called for smaller, more frequent, solution-oriented meetings. Others have defended the UNFCCC as the only forum in which all states, including the most vulnerable, have a voice, and argue that its weaknesses reflect the political realities of climate action rather than institutional design.

### Minilateralism and Clubs

In parallel with the universal regime, smaller groups of states have formed "clubs" and partnerships to accelerate action in particular sectors. Examples include the **Climate Club** launched by the G7 in 2022 to promote the decarbonisation of industry; the Powering Past Coal Alliance and the Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance, examined in section 18.5; the Global Methane Pledge, examined in Chapter 17; the Breakthrough Agenda, launched at COP26, which promotes cooperation to make clean technologies affordable and accessible; and the JETPs, examined in Chapter 16. Such initiatives can move faster than the UNFCCC and can align trade, finance, and technology policies among like-minded states, but they risk fragmenting the regime, excluding developing countries from decisions that affect them, and creating trade frictions, as illustrated by the controversy over the EU's CBAM, examined in Chapter 13.

### Climate Finance and the International Financial Architecture

The scale of investment required for the energy transition in developing countries, examined in Chapter 16, has led to calls for the reform of the international financial architecture. The **Bridgetown Initiative**, launched by the Prime Minister of Barbados in 2022, proposes measures including the expansion of lending by multilateral development banks, the use of special drawing rights for climate finance, the inclusion of natural disaster clauses in debt instruments, and new international taxes or levies on sectors such as fossil fuels, shipping, and aviation to fund climate action. The World Bank and other multilateral development banks have undertaken reforms to expand their lending capacity and integrate climate considerations, and the IMF established the **Resilience and Sustainability Trust** in 2022 to provide long-term financing for climate resilience and pandemic preparedness. A Global Solidarity Levies Task Force, launched in 2023, has explored options for levies on fossil fuels, aviation, shipping, and financial transactions. The high cost of capital in developing countries — often several times higher than in developed countries, reflecting perceptions of currency, political, and regulatory risk — is a major barrier to renewable energy investment, and reducing it through guarantees, currency risk instruments, and regulatory reform is a central policy objective.

### Other International Regimes

Climate change cuts across many areas of international law, and the reform of climate governance involves the integration of climate objectives into other regimes. **International shipping and aviation**, whose emissions are not allocated to national NDCs, are addressed by the International Maritime Organization and the International Civil Aviation Organization. The IMO adopted a revised strategy in 2023 aiming for net zero emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, and in April 2025 its Marine Environment Protection Committee approved a draft "Net-Zero Framework" combining a global fuel standard with greenhouse gas pricing, the formal adoption of which was postponed in October 2025 after opposition from a number of states. ICAO's Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) requires airlines to offset emissions growth above a baseline. The **trade regime** must accommodate climate measures such as carbon border adjustments and green subsidies, as examined in Chapter 13. The **investment regime**, as examined in Chapter 14, is being reformed to protect the right to regulate for climate purposes, including through the modernisation of and withdrawals from the Energy Charter Treaty. **Human rights** law, through the decisions examined in section 18.6 and the work of the UN Special Rapporteur on climate change and human rights, increasingly frames climate obligations in terms of rights.

### Governing Fossil Fuel Production

The most significant gap in the international climate regime has been its silence, until recently, on the production of fossil fuels. The UNEP **Production Gap Report**, published periodically since 2019 by research institutes and UNEP, has found that governments plan to produce more than double the amount of fossil fuels in 2030 than would be consistent with limiting warming to 1.5°C. The Dubai language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, the ICJ's recognition that fossil fuel licensing and subsidies may engage state responsibility, and the proposal for a Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty all point towards greater international attention to supply. Any international framework for the managed decline of fossil fuel production would have to address the equitable allocation of the remaining production among states, taking into account their dependence on fossil fuel revenues, their capacity to diversify, and their historical responsibility — questions that connect climate law with the law of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, examined in Chapter 2, and with the fiscal and economic diversification challenges of producer states examined in Chapter 16.

### Non-State Actors and Accountability

Cities, regions, companies, and financial institutions have made a large number of net zero pledges. The UN Secretary-General's High-Level Expert Group on the Net Zero Emissions Commitments of Non-State Entities reported in 2022, recommending that net zero pledges should include interim targets, cover all emissions, avoid reliance on low-quality offsets, and exclude new investment in fossil fuel supply, and that pledges should be subject to transparency and accountability. The **Recognition and Accountability Framework** established by the UN to track non-state commitments, mandatory climate disclosure requirements, examined in Chapter 22, and greenwashing litigation, examined in section 18.6, have increased the accountability of non-state actors, while the withdrawal of a number of financial institutions from voluntary net zero alliances in 2024–2025 illustrated the fragility of voluntary commitments in the face of political pressure.

### Geoengineering

As the prospect of exceeding 1.5°C has grown, attention has turned to **geoengineering** — deliberate large-scale intervention in the climate system — including carbon dioxide removal, examined in Chapter 12, and solar radiation modification, which would reduce warming by reflecting sunlight. Solar radiation modification raises profound governance questions, including the risks of unintended effects, the distribution of benefits and harms, the possibility of unilateral deployment, and the moral hazard of reducing incentives for mitigation. The Conference of the Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity adopted a decision in 2010 calling for no climate-related geoengineering activities that may affect biodiversity until there is an adequate scientific basis, and the parties to the London Protocol adopted an amendment in 2013 regulating marine geoengineering, including ocean fertilisation. An international framework for the governance of solar radiation modification does not exist, and proposals range from a non-use agreement to frameworks for research governance.

### Conclusion

Climate change law has evolved from a framework convention with weak commitments, through the binding but narrow Kyoto Protocol, to the universal but largely procedural Paris Agreement, and is now being reshaped by the advisory opinions of international courts, national framework laws, carbon markets, and litigation. For energy law, the consequences are profound: the licensing of fossil fuel production, the design of electricity markets, the allocation of investment risk, the content of contracts, and the duties of company directors are all being reconfigured by the imperative of decarbonisation. At the same time, the transition raises fundamental questions of justice — between developed and developing countries, between producer and consumer states, and between present and future generations — that the law has only begun to address.

**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The state, institutions, and facts described are fictitious and do not refer to any real country.**

The Republic of Tamara is a lower-middle-income country with a population of forty million. It produces 250,000 barrels of oil per day, mostly for export, and petroleum revenues account for half of government revenue. It has substantial undeveloped gas reserves. Sixty per cent of the population has access to electricity, which is generated principally from diesel and heavy fuel oil, and gas flaring is widespread. Tamara is highly vulnerable to coastal flooding and drought. Its updated NDC commits to an unconditional reduction of fifteen per cent below business-as-usual emissions by 2030, and a conditional reduction of forty per cent.

The government has decided to enact a Climate Change and Energy Transition Act and has asked you to prepare the first draft. The Minister of Petroleum insists that the Act must not prevent the development of the country's gas reserves or the award of new oil licences. The Minister of Finance is concerned about the fiscal effects of a carbon tax and of the removal of fuel subsidies. Civil society organisations demand a ban on routine gas flaring within three years, a just transition for oil workers, and the right of citizens to challenge government decisions that are inconsistent with the country's climate commitments.

**Tasks.**

(a) Prepare a structure for the Act, setting out its parts and principal provisions, including the long-term target, carbon budgets, institutional arrangements, and accountability mechanisms.

(b) Draft the provisions of the Act dealing with (i) the assessment of the climate effects of new petroleum licences and projects, including downstream emissions, and (ii) the elimination of routine gas flaring.

(c) Draft a provision on just transition, addressing workers, communities, and low-income consumers.

(d) Advise the government on how the Act should be designed to be consistent with the obligations identified in the ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion while preserving its policy space to develop its gas reserves, and on the risks of investment treaty claims by existing licence holders.

(e) Prepare a short memorandum on the options for using Article 6 of the Paris Agreement to finance the conditional element of Tamara's NDC, identifying the legal safeguards required.

::: summary
- The UNFCCC (1992) establishes the objective of preventing dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system and the principles of equity, CBDR-RC, and precaution, with institutions operating by consensus.
- The Kyoto Protocol imposed binding targets on developed countries only; the Paris Agreement combines universal participation, nationally determined contributions, binding procedural obligations, and a five-yearly ambition cycle, with a temperature goal of well below 2°C and efforts towards 1.5°C.
- NDCs are in substance energy policy documents, but in aggregate fall far short of the Paris goals; national framework laws, including Nigeria's Climate Change Act 2021, establish targets, carbon budgets, and institutions.
- The advisory opinions of ITLOS (2024), the ICJ (2025), and the Inter-American Court (2025) confirm that states have stringent due diligence obligations to prevent climate harm, that the climate treaties do not displace general international law, and that fossil fuel production, licensing, and subsidies may engage state responsibility.
- Transition policies include carbon pricing, Article 6 carbon markets, coal phase-out, supply-side restrictions, subsidy reform, and industrial policy, each raising legal issues under domestic, trade, and investment law.
- Climate litigation against states (*Urgenda*, *Neubauer*, *KlimaSeniorinnen*) and companies (*Milieudefensie v Shell*, *Lliuya v RWE*) has established duties to mitigate, although courts are cautious about prescribing company-specific reductions or awarding damages.
- Just transition addresses the fair distribution of the costs and benefits of decarbonisation among workers, communities, regions, and countries, including the energy access needs of Africa.
- Reform proposals address UNFCCC decision-making, climate finance and the international financial architecture, the integration of climate objectives into other regimes, the governance of fossil fuel production, and non-state accountability.
:::
