## 1.6 Legal Pluralism and Competing Regulatory Authorities

### The Concept of Legal Pluralism

Legal pluralism describes situations in which two or more legal orders coexist in the same social field. Originally developed by legal anthropologists to describe the coexistence of state law and customary law in colonial and post-colonial societies, the concept has been extended to describe the coexistence of national, international, regional, and private normative orders in a globalised world. Energy and resource governance is a paradigmatic site of legal pluralism.

At least five layers of normative authority may be identified in a typical resource project:

1. **Customary and community norms.** In many resource-rich regions, land and resources are held under customary tenure systems that predate, and coexist with, state law. In Nigeria, for example, customary land tenure was overlaid by the Land Use Act 1978, which vested land in each state in the Governor, while section 44(3) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 vests the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils, and natural gas in the Government of the Federation. Communities in the Niger Delta nevertheless continue to assert claims to land and resources grounded in custom, and these claims shape the conduct of operations and the content of community development agreements.

2. **Subnational law.** In federal states, constitutional authority over resources may be divided between the federal government and the constituent units. In Canada, the provinces own and regulate most natural resources under section 92A of the Constitution Act 1867 (inserted in 1982); in Australia, the states own onshore minerals and petroleum; in the United States, mineral rights may be held privately, by the states, or by the federal government. In Nigeria, the Electricity Act 2023, enacted following a constitutional amendment, empowers states to regulate electricity markets within their territories.

3. **National law.** Constitutional provisions, petroleum and mining statutes, environmental legislation, tax law, and administrative law form the core of the regulatory framework.

4. **International and regional law.** Treaties, customary international law, and the law of regional organisations (such as the European Union, the Economic Community of West African States, and the African Union) impose obligations on states and, in some cases, confer rights on private parties.

5. **Transnational private regulation.** Industry standards, model contracts, voluntary codes of conduct, certification schemes, and the environmental and social performance standards of lenders — notably the International Finance Corporation (IFC) *Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability* and the Equator Principles adopted by commercial banks — impose requirements on companies that may exceed those of national law.

### Sources of Conflict

The coexistence of multiple normative orders generates conflicts of several kinds. *Vertical* conflicts arise between different levels of authority — for example, between federal and state governments over the ownership of offshore resources, as in Nigeria's resource-control litigation, in which the Supreme Court held in *Attorney-General of the Federation v Attorney-General of Abia State* that for the purposes of revenue allocation the seaward boundary of a littoral state was the low-water mark of its land surface.[^abia] *Horizontal* conflicts arise between different regimes at the same level — for example, between investment treaty obligations and environmental obligations. *Public–private* conflicts arise between state regulation and private standards or contracts. *Jurisdictional* conflicts arise between courts and tribunals asserting authority over the same dispute, as when an investor pursues parallel proceedings before domestic courts, commercial arbitration, and investment treaty arbitration.

[^abia]: *Attorney-General of the Federation v Attorney-General of Abia State & 35 Others* (2002) 6 NWLR (Pt 764) 542 (Supreme Court of Nigeria).

### Managing Pluralism

Legal systems have developed a range of techniques for managing these conflicts: hierarchical rules of priority (constitutional supremacy, the primacy of European Union law), principles of interpretation (systemic integration under Article 31(3)(c) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, the presumption against conflict), doctrines of comity and deference (the margin of appreciation, the police powers doctrine in investment law), procedural mechanisms (fork-in-the-road and waiver clauses, *lis pendens*, *res judicata*), and institutional coordination (memoranda of understanding between regulators, joint commissions).

The International Law Commission's study of the fragmentation of international law, completed in 2006 under the chairmanship of Martti Koskenniemi, remains the most authoritative analysis of these techniques at the international level.[^ilcfrag] For energy and resource law, the key insight of the fragmentation debate is that specialised regimes — investment law, trade law, environmental law, human rights law — do not operate in isolation; they remain embedded in general international law, and their interpretation must take account of other relevant rules applicable between the parties.

[^ilcfrag]: International Law Commission, *Fragmentation of International Law: Difficulties Arising from the Diversification and Expansion of International Law*, Report of the Study Group finalised by Martti Koskenniemi, UN Doc A/CN.4/L.682 (13 April 2006).

::: comparative
**Federalism and resource authority in three jurisdictions.** In *Canada*, section 92A of the Constitution Act 1867 confirms provincial legislative authority over the exploration, development, conservation, and management of non-renewable natural resources, subject to federal paramountcy for interprovincial export. In *Nigeria*, section 44(3) of the 1999 Constitution vests ownership and control of minerals and petroleum in the Federal Government, and item 39 of the Exclusive Legislative List reserves "mines and minerals, including oil fields, oil mining, geological surveys and natural gas" to the National Assembly; the derivation principle in section 162(2) allocates not less than thirteen per cent of revenue accruing from natural resources to the states of origin. In the *United States*, subsurface minerals may be privately owned and severed from the surface estate, with federal and state governments owning substantial mineral estates in their own right and regulating activities on federal and state lands respectively. These differences shape the bargaining power of communities, the structure of fiscal regimes, and the incidence of resource conflicts.

## 1.7 Contemporary Challenges in Global Resource Governance

The field confronts a set of challenges that are without precedent in their scale and complexity. Six are of particular importance.

### The Energy Transition and Stranded Assets

The first challenge is the transition from an energy system dominated by fossil fuels to one based predominantly on low-carbon sources. The legal implications are profound. Petroleum and coal licences granted for decades may become economically or legally unviable before their expiry, raising questions about compensation, decommissioning liability, and the protection of legitimate expectations. Investors in fossil fuel infrastructure may seek to rely on investment treaties to challenge phase-out measures. Producer states whose economies depend on hydrocarbon revenues face fiscal crises and must manage the social consequences of transition. The concept of "stranded assets" — assets that suffer from premature write-downs or devaluation as a result of the transition — has migrated from financial analysis into law, influencing disclosure obligations, directors' duties, and the interpretation of investment protections.

### Critical Minerals and the New Extractivism

The second challenge arises from the paradox that decarbonisation requires more extraction, not less. The IEA has repeatedly projected that demand for minerals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, graphite, and rare earth elements will grow severalfold under scenarios consistent with the Paris Agreement. These minerals are geographically concentrated, and their processing is concentrated still further. States have responded with industrial policies, export restrictions, strategic stockpiles, and bilateral critical mineral agreements. Communities in mining regions confront the risk that the green transition will reproduce the environmental degradation and human rights abuses associated with earlier extractive booms. Chapter 8 addresses these issues in detail.

### Energy Access and Energy Poverty

The third challenge is that a substantial proportion of humanity still lacks access to modern energy services. Hundreds of millions of people, the majority in sub-Saharan Africa, lack access to electricity, and far more lack access to clean cooking fuels. Sustainable Development Goal 7 commits states to ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all by 2030. Achieving universal access while simultaneously decarbonising energy systems requires legal and regulatory frameworks that mobilise investment in decentralised and grid-based solutions, protect consumers, and allocate the costs of transition fairly. The concept of energy justice, explored in Chapter 23, provides a framework for analysing these distributional questions.

### Geopolitics and Energy Security

The fourth challenge is the re-emergence of energy as an instrument of geopolitical competition. The disruption of gas supplies to Europe following the invasion of Ukraine, the use of sanctions against major energy exporters, attacks on energy infrastructure, and competition over critical mineral supply chains have demonstrated that energy security remains a central concern of state policy. The law governing sanctions, export controls, emergency powers, strategic reserves, and the protection of critical infrastructure — examined in Chapter 25 — has accordingly grown in importance.

### Legitimacy of Investor–State Dispute Settlement

The fifth challenge concerns the legitimacy of the system of investor–state dispute settlement (ISDS). Critics argue that ISDS privileges foreign investors over domestic stakeholders, constrains the regulatory autonomy of states, produces inconsistent decisions, and lacks transparency and accountability. Reform efforts proceed in several forums: the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) Working Group III, the European Union's proposal for a multilateral investment court, the modernisation of the ECT, and the renegotiation or termination of BITs by individual states. The outcome of these reforms will shape the legal environment for energy investment for decades.

### Technological Change and Digitalisation

The sixth challenge arises from technological change. Hydrogen, advanced nuclear reactors, carbon capture, battery storage, offshore floating wind, and deep-sea mining all raise novel regulatory questions. Digitalisation — smart meters, distributed energy resources, automated trading, artificial intelligence in grid management — raises questions of data ownership, cybersecurity, and liability. Regulatory frameworks designed for centralised, fossil-fuel-based systems must be adapted to decentralised, digital, and decarbonised systems. Chapters 12 and 30 address these developments.

## 1.8 Doctoral Research Perspectives and Foundational Debates

### The Nature of Doctoral Contribution

Doctoral research is distinguished from other forms of legal writing by the requirement of an original contribution to knowledge. In international energy and natural resources law, such a contribution may take several forms: the identification and analysis of a new legal problem; a new interpretation of existing rules or cases; the application of a theoretical framework to a body of law; a comparative study revealing previously unrecognised patterns; an empirical investigation of how the law operates in practice; or a reasoned proposal for reform. Chapter 28 discusses research methodology in detail. Here, the purpose is to identify the foundational debates within which most doctoral research in the field is situated.

### Debate One: Sovereignty versus Stability

The most enduring debate in the field concerns the tension between the sovereign right of states to regulate their natural resources in the public interest and the need of investors for legal and fiscal stability. The debate manifests itself in disputes over stabilisation clauses, the standard of compensation for expropriation, the scope of the fair and equitable treatment standard, and the legitimacy of contract renegotiation. Is the doctrine of permanent sovereignty compatible with binding long-term commitments to foreign investors? Can a state contract away its legislative power? How should the law balance the legitimate expectations of investors against the evolving needs of the host society?

### Debate Two: Contract versus Regulation

A second debate concerns the relative roles of contract and regulation in governing resource activities. In some jurisdictions, the terms of resource development are set primarily by individually negotiated contracts; in others, primarily by general legislation and regulation applicable to all licensees. Each approach has advantages and drawbacks. Contracts offer flexibility and can be tailored to the circumstances of particular projects, but they are vulnerable to corruption, asymmetries of information, and lack of transparency. Regulation offers predictability, equality of treatment, and democratic accountability, but may be less responsive to project-specific risks. The trend in many jurisdictions, including Nigeria under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, has been towards greater reliance on legislative frameworks, but contracts remain central.

### Debate Three: Efficiency versus Equity

A third debate concerns the objectives of resource law. Economic approaches emphasise efficiency — the maximisation of resource rents, the minimisation of transaction costs, and the allocation of risk to the party best able to bear it. Equity-oriented approaches emphasise the fair distribution of benefits and burdens among states, investors, communities, and future generations, and the protection of the rights of affected persons. The "resource curse" literature, which examines why resource-rich states often experience slower growth, weaker institutions, and greater conflict than resource-poor states, has highlighted the importance of institutional quality and has prompted reforms focused on transparency, revenue management, and accountability.

### Debate Four: Development versus Sustainability

A fourth debate concerns the relationship between resource development and environmental sustainability. Developing states argue that they have a right to exploit their resources to finance development, as industrialised states did before them, and that the burden of climate mitigation should fall primarily on those states responsible for historical emissions. This argument is reflected in the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, articulated in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. At the same time, the physical limits of the carbon budget mean that a substantial proportion of known fossil fuel reserves cannot be burned if the temperature goals of the Paris Agreement are to be met. How should the right to development be reconciled with the imperative of decarbonisation? Who should bear the costs of leaving resources in the ground?

### Debate Five: State-Centrism versus Transnational Governance

A fifth debate concerns the appropriate unit of analysis. Traditional approaches treat the state as the principal actor and source of law. Transnational approaches emphasise the role of corporations, international organisations, civil society, communities, and private standard-setters in creating and enforcing norms. The rise of corporate climate litigation, supply chain due diligence obligations, and private sustainability standards suggests that governance is increasingly polycentric. Doctoral research must decide how to account for this complexity without losing analytical clarity.

::: casebox
**Texaco Overseas Petroleum Co v Libyan Arab Republic (1977)**

*Facts.* Libya had granted deeds of concession to two American companies under its Petroleum Law of 1955. The concessions contained a stabilisation clause providing that the contractual rights expressly created by the concession should not be altered except by mutual consent, and an arbitration clause. In 1973 and 1974 Libya nationalised the companies' interests. The companies invoked arbitration; Libya refused to participate, and a sole arbitrator was appointed by the President of the International Court of Justice.

*Holding.* Sole Arbitrator Dupuy held that the concessions were internationalised contracts; that they were governed by principles of Libyan law common to international law and, in the absence of such common principles, by general principles of law; that the stabilisation clause was valid and binding; and that Libya had breached its obligations. Notably, he ordered *restitutio in integrum* — specific performance — as the primary remedy, although the parties subsequently settled.

*Significance.* The award is the high-water mark of the internationalisation theory. Its analysis of Resolution 1803 as reflecting customary law, and its treatment of the later Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States as lacking that status, remain influential. Its remedy of restitution has rarely been followed; later tribunals, beginning with *LIAMCO* and *Aminoil*, preferred damages.
:::

::: exercise
**The Kestrel Basin concession (hypothetical).** The Republic of Aranda, a fictional coastal state, granted a thirty-year petroleum concession in 1998 to Northwind Energy plc, a company incorporated in a fictional state with which Aranda has a bilateral investment treaty. The concession contains (i) a choice of Arandan law "supplemented by such principles of international law as may be applicable"; (ii) a "freezing" stabilisation clause; and (iii) ICC arbitration seated in Geneva. In 2025, following discoveries of significant gas reserves and a change of government, Aranda enacts a Petroleum Revenue Act that raises the royalty rate from 8 to 15 per cent, imposes a windfall profits tax, and requires all existing concessionaires to convert to production sharing contracts within two years.

Prepare a memorandum of not more than 2,500 words that: (a) identifies each legal order potentially applicable to the dispute; (b) explains the competing characterisations of the measures as sovereign regulation or contractual breach; (c) assesses the legal effect of the stabilisation clause in the light of the doctrine of permanent sovereignty; and (d) recommends a dispute resolution strategy for Northwind, identifying the risks of parallel proceedings. *All parties, places, and instruments in this exercise are fictitious.*

::: summary
- International energy and natural resources law is a functional, pluralist, distributive, and transnational field concerned with the allocation of rights over energy sources and natural resources, the regulation of activities involving them, and the distribution of the resulting benefits and burdens.
- The field integrates energy law and natural resources law, a combination justified by shared history, doctrine, institutions, and — increasingly — the material interdependence created by the energy transition.
- Its history may be organised into five overlapping phases: the classical concession era, nationalisation, decolonisation and the New International Economic Order, liberalisation and investment protection, and the current era of resource nationalism, sustainability, and transition.
- Energy markets are highly internationalised, but energy regulation remains predominantly national; internationalisation proceeds through treaties, institutions, regulatory convergence, private ordering, and extraterritorial regulation.
- The dual character of the state as sovereign and contracting party is the source of many of the field's enduring problems, notably the status of state contracts and the distinction between contract and treaty claims.
- Legal pluralism is a defining feature of resource governance, requiring techniques of conflict management drawn from constitutional, international, and private law.
- Doctoral research is situated within five foundational debates: sovereignty versus stability, contract versus regulation, efficiency versus equity, development versus sustainability, and state-centrism versus transnational governance.
:::

2. Has the energy transition rendered the integrated study of energy and natural resources law analytically indispensable, or does it obscure important differences between extractive and non-extractive energy sources?

4. Can the techniques developed to manage the fragmentation of international law adequately address conflicts between investment protection and climate obligations, or is structural reform required?
5. What role should customary land tenure and community norms play in the allocation of resource rights in states, such as Nigeria, where the Constitution vests mineral ownership in the federal government?
