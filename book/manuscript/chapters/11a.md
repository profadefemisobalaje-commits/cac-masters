## Introduction

Nuclear energy occupies a unique position in energy law. It is a low-carbon source of firm electricity that provides around a tenth of the world's electricity and a much larger share of its low-carbon electricity. It is also the only energy technology whose materials and know-how can be diverted to the production of weapons of mass destruction, and whose accidents can cause radioactive contamination across national borders and over generations. These characteristics have produced a body of law that is more internationalised, more technically detailed, and more closely bound up with questions of international peace and security than any other branch of energy law.

The legal framework of nuclear energy is commonly described by reference to "three Ss" — safety, security, and safeguards — to which a fourth pillar, liability, is usually added, together with the management of radioactive waste and decommissioning. **Safety** concerns the protection of workers, the public, and the environment from the harmful effects of ionising radiation, including the prevention and mitigation of accidents. **Security** concerns the prevention and detection of, and response to, theft, sabotage, unauthorised access, and other malicious acts involving nuclear material and facilities. **Safeguards** concern the verification that nuclear material is not diverted from peaceful uses to nuclear weapons. **Liability** concerns compensation for damage caused by nuclear incidents. Each pillar has its own international instruments, institutions, and domestic legal requirements.

After decades of stagnation following the accidents at Three Mile Island (1979) and Chernobyl (1986), and renewed doubt after the Fukushima Daiichi accident (2011), nuclear energy has experienced a revival of interest driven by climate commitments, energy security concerns following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and the electricity demand of data centres. At COP28 in 2023, a group of states launched a declaration aiming to triple global nuclear energy capacity by 2050; the European Union included nuclear energy, subject to conditions, in its taxonomy of sustainable activities; the World Bank announced in 2025 that it would resume financing nuclear energy projects; and technology companies have concluded long-term contracts to purchase nuclear electricity, including from the restart of a closed reactor. At the same time, several countries, including Germany, have phased out nuclear power, and the costs and delays of large nuclear construction projects in Europe and the United States have been severe. Several African states, including Egypt, Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, and Uganda, have pursued nuclear power programmes, and Egypt is constructing the continent's second nuclear power plant, after South Africa's Koeberg.

This chapter examines the peaceful uses of nuclear energy (section 11.1), the international nuclear legal framework (section 11.2), nuclear safety and regulatory independence (section 11.3), safeguards and non-proliferation (section 11.4), nuclear security (section 11.5), nuclear liability (section 11.6), radioactive waste and decommissioning (section 11.7), and small modular reactors and emerging legal issues (section 11.8).

## 11.1 Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy

### From Atoms for Peace to the Right to Peaceful Uses

The international law of nuclear energy originated in the effort, after the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, to reconcile the promise of civil nuclear technology with the danger of nuclear weapons proliferation. The first resolution of the United Nations General Assembly, adopted in January 1946, established the United Nations Atomic Energy Commission to make proposals for the control of atomic energy "to the extent necessary to ensure its use only for peaceful purposes". The proposals for international ownership and control of nuclear activities presented by the United States (the Baruch Plan) failed amid the onset of the Cold War. In his "Atoms for Peace" address to the General Assembly in December 1953, President Eisenhower proposed an international agency to which states would contribute fissile material for peaceful uses, leading to the adoption of the Statute of the International Atomic Energy Agency in 1956 and its entry into force in 1957.

The bargain at the heart of the international nuclear order was codified in the **Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons** (NPT), opened for signature in 1968 and in force since 1970. The NPT distinguishes between "nuclear-weapon States" — defined in Article IX(3) as states that manufactured and exploded a nuclear weapon or other nuclear explosive device before 1 January 1967 (the United States, the Soviet Union (now Russia), the United Kingdom, France, and China) — and "non-nuclear-weapon States". Under Articles I and II, nuclear-weapon states undertake not to transfer nuclear weapons or assist non-nuclear-weapon states to acquire them, and non-nuclear-weapon states undertake not to receive, manufacture, or otherwise acquire nuclear weapons. Under Article III, non-nuclear-weapon states undertake to accept IAEA safeguards on all source and special fissionable material in all their peaceful nuclear activities. Under Article VI, all parties undertake to pursue negotiations in good faith on effective measures relating to cessation of the nuclear arms race and to nuclear disarmament.

### Article IV of the NPT

Article IV(1) of the NPT provides that nothing in the Treaty shall be interpreted as affecting "the inalienable right of all the Parties to the Treaty to develop research, production and use of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes without discrimination and in conformity with articles I and II of this Treaty". Article IV(2) commits parties to facilitate, and grants them the right to participate in, the fullest possible exchange of equipment, materials, and scientific and technological information for the peaceful uses of nuclear energy, and requires parties in a position to do so to cooperate in contributing to the further development of applications of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes, "especially in the territories of non-nuclear-weapon States Party to the Treaty, with due consideration for the needs of the developing areas of the world".

The interpretation of Article IV has been contested. Many developing states regard the "inalienable right" as encompassing the entire nuclear fuel cycle, including uranium enrichment and spent fuel reprocessing, which are "sensitive" technologies because they can produce weapons-usable material. Supplier states have sought to restrict the spread of enrichment and reprocessing through export controls, coordinated through the **Nuclear Suppliers Group** (NSG), an informal arrangement established in 1974–1975 after India's first nuclear test, whose guidelines restrict the transfer of sensitive technologies and, since 1992, require full-scope safeguards as a condition of the supply of nuclear materials and equipment to non-nuclear-weapon states. The controversy over Iran's enrichment programme, examined in section 11.4, was in significant part a dispute over the scope of Article IV. The qualification that the right must be exercised "in conformity with articles I and II", and, by implication, with the safeguards obligations of Article III, means that the right is not unconditional.

### Peaceful Applications Beyond Electricity

Although this chapter focuses on nuclear power, the peaceful uses of nuclear technology extend far beyond electricity generation. Nuclear and radiation technologies are used in medicine (diagnostic imaging and cancer radiotherapy), agriculture (the sterile insect technique and plant mutation breeding), industry (non-destructive testing and sterilisation), water resource management (isotope hydrology), and scientific research. The IAEA's technical cooperation programme supports these applications in developing states, and they constitute a substantial part of the nuclear activities regulated in most countries, including Nigeria. The legal frameworks for the safety and security of radioactive sources used in these applications, including the IAEA's Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources (2003), are an important component of nuclear law.

### The Decision to Pursue Nuclear Power

A state's decision to introduce nuclear power is a long-term commitment requiring the development of a comprehensive legal and institutional infrastructure. The IAEA's **Milestones Approach**, set out in its publication *Milestones in the Development of a National Infrastructure for Nuclear Power*, identifies nineteen infrastructure issues — including national position, nuclear safety, management, funding and financing, legal framework, safeguards, regulatory framework, radiation protection, electrical grid, human resource development, stakeholder involvement, site and supporting facilities, environmental protection, emergency planning, nuclear security, nuclear fuel cycle, radioactive waste management, industrial involvement, and procurement — to be addressed in three phases, from the consideration of the nuclear option through the preparation for construction to the activities to implement the first plant. The IAEA conducts Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review (INIR) missions to assess newcomer states' progress, and several African states, including Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Egypt, Uganda, and Morocco, have hosted such missions.

The decision to pursue nuclear power raises legal questions beyond technical regulation: democratic legitimacy and public participation (the Aarhus Convention's Compliance Committee and the parties' Meeting have addressed the application of public participation requirements to decisions on nuclear power, including decisions to extend the lifetime of reactors); transboundary consultation under the Espoo Convention on Environmental Impact Assessment in a Transboundary Context (1991), whose Implementation Committee and parties have considered the application of the Convention to the lifetime extension of nuclear power plants; and the compatibility of nuclear power with constitutional rights and environmental principles. The Court of Justice's judgment in *Inter-Environnement Wallonie and Bond Beter Leefmilieu Vlaanderen* (Case C-411/17, 2019) held that the extension of the operating life of Belgian nuclear reactors required an environmental impact assessment under the EIA Directive and an appropriate assessment under the Habitats Directive, illustrating the application of general environmental law to nuclear decisions.

## 11.2 International Nuclear Legal Frameworks

### The International Atomic Energy Agency

The IAEA, an autonomous international organisation within the United Nations family with its headquarters in Vienna, is the central institution of international nuclear law. Its objectives, set out in Article II of its Statute, are to "seek to accelerate and enlarge the contribution of atomic energy to peace, health and prosperity throughout the world" and to "ensure, so far as it is able, that assistance provided by it or at its request or under its supervision or control is not used in such a way as to further any military purpose". The dual mandate of promotion and control has been a source of tension, and critics have questioned whether an organisation charged with promoting nuclear energy can effectively regulate it.

The IAEA's statutory functions under Article III include encouraging research and development, facilitating the exchange of information, establishing and administering safeguards, and establishing or adopting "standards of safety for protection of health and minimization of danger to life and property", and providing for the application of these standards. The IAEA's governing organs are the General Conference of all member states and the Board of Governors of thirty-five member states, which has the power under Article XII(C) of the Statute to report a member state's non-compliance with its safeguards obligations to the Security Council and the General Assembly. The institutional structure of the IAEA is examined further in Chapter 4.

### The Treaty Framework

International nuclear law consists of a network of treaties, most adopted in response to specific events, together with a large body of non-binding standards and guidance. The principal treaties may be grouped by pillar.

**Safety.** The *Convention on Nuclear Safety* (CNS, 1994; in force 1996) applies to land-based civil nuclear power plants and requires parties to establish a legislative and regulatory framework, an independent regulatory body, and safety requirements for siting, design, construction, and operation. The *Joint Convention on the Safety of Spent Fuel Management and on the Safety of Radioactive Waste Management* (1997; in force 2001) applies analogous obligations to spent fuel and radioactive waste. Both are "incentive" conventions, relying on national reporting and peer review at review meetings of the parties rather than on enforcement.

**Emergency preparedness.** The *Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident* (1986) and the *Convention on Assistance in the Case of a Nuclear Accident or Radiological Emergency* (1986), both adopted within months of the Chernobyl accident, require states to notify potentially affected states and the IAEA of nuclear accidents with transboundary effects and establish a framework for international assistance.

**Security.** The *Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material* (CPPNM, 1980; in force 1987), as amended in 2005 (the amendment in force since 2016), and the *International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism* (ICSANT, 2005; in force 2007), examined in section 11.5.

**Safeguards and non-proliferation.** The NPT, safeguards agreements concluded between states and the IAEA, regional treaties establishing nuclear-weapon-free zones (including the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty, or Treaty of Pelindaba, signed in 1996 and in force since 2009), and the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017; in force 2021), examined in section 11.4.

**Liability.** The *Paris Convention on Third Party Liability in the Field of Nuclear Energy* (1960), the *Brussels Supplementary Convention* (1963), the *Vienna Convention on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage* (1963), the *Joint Protocol* (1988), the protocols amending the Vienna (1997) and Paris and Brussels (2004) Conventions, and the *Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage* (1997; in force 2015), examined in section 11.6.

### IAEA Safety Standards as Soft Law

The most detailed and practically important rules of international nuclear safety law are found not in treaties but in the **IAEA Safety Standards Series**, adopted under Article III(A)(6) of the IAEA Statute. The standards are arranged in a hierarchy: the *Fundamental Safety Principles* (Safety Fundamentals, SF-1, 2006), which set out the fundamental safety objective — "to protect people and the environment from harmful effects of ionizing radiation" — and ten safety principles; *Safety Requirements*, which establish the requirements that must be met to ensure safety, expressed as "shall" statements; and *Safety Guides*, which provide recommendations on how to comply with the requirements. The ten principles of SF-1 address responsibility for safety (which rests with the licensee), the role of government (including an independent regulatory body), leadership and management for safety, justification of facilities and activities, optimisation of protection, limitation of risks to individuals, protection of present and future generations, prevention of accidents, emergency preparedness and response, and protective actions to reduce existing or unregulated radiation risks.

The Safety Standards are not binding on states as such. However, they are binding on the IAEA in relation to its own operations, and on states in relation to operations assisted by the IAEA under project and supply agreements. They acquire legal effect through incorporation into national law, which is widespread, and through reference in treaties: the CNS and the Joint Convention refer to internationally endorsed safety principles, and the review process under those conventions uses the standards as benchmarks. In the European Union, the Euratom Nuclear Safety Directive (Council Directive 2009/71/Euratom, as amended by Council Directive 2014/87/Euratom) gives binding effect to principles derived from the standards. The IAEA standards are therefore a leading example of the transformation of soft law into hard obligations through domestic and regional incorporation, a theme examined in Chapter 2.

### Euratom

The *Treaty establishing the European Atomic Energy Community* (Euratom Treaty), signed in Rome in 1957 alongside the Treaty establishing the European Economic Community, established a distinct regional legal order for nuclear energy among the member states of the European Union. The Euratom Treaty provides for the promotion of research, the establishment of uniform safety standards for the protection of workers and the public (Articles 30–39), the supply of nuclear materials through the Euratom Supply Agency, which has a right of option on ores, source materials, and special fissile materials produced in member states and an exclusive right to conclude supply contracts (Article 52), a regional safeguards system administered by the Commission (Chapter VII), and the ownership by the Community of special fissile materials (Article 86). On the basis of Articles 31 and 32 of the Treaty, the Council has adopted the Basic Safety Standards Directive (Council Directive 2013/59/Euratom), the Nuclear Safety Directive, and the Radioactive Waste and Spent Fuel Directive (Council Directive 2011/70/Euratom). In *Commission v Council* (Case C-29/99, 2002), the Court of Justice held that the Community had competence in the field of nuclear installation safety under the Euratom Treaty, opening the way for the adoption of the Nuclear Safety Directive.

The Euratom Treaty also requires member states to notify the Commission of investment projects in the nuclear field (Article 41) and to provide general data on plans for the disposal of radioactive waste (Article 37). In *Austria v Commission* (Case C-594/18 P, 2020), the Court of Justice dismissed Austria's appeal against the General Court's judgment upholding the Commission's approval, under the State aid rules, of United Kingdom support for the Hinkley Point C nuclear power plant, holding that the promotion of nuclear energy was a legitimate objective of common interest under the Euratom Treaty and that State aid for nuclear power need not pursue an objective of common interest beyond that. In September 2025, the General Court dismissed Austria's action challenging the inclusion of nuclear energy and gas in the EU taxonomy of sustainable activities by Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/1214.

### The Nigerian Framework

Nigeria's nuclear legal framework is based principally on the **Nigeria Atomic Energy Commission Act 1976**, which established the Nigeria Atomic Energy Commission (NAEC) as the body responsible for the promotion and development of atomic energy, including nuclear power, and the **Nuclear Safety and Radiation Protection Act 1995**, which established the **Nigerian Nuclear Regulatory Authority** (NNRA) as the regulatory body responsible for nuclear safety, radiation protection, and the regulation of nuclear and radiation activities. The NNRA has adopted regulations on matters including radiation safety, the security of radioactive sources, and the licensing of nuclear installations. Nigeria operates a research reactor, the Nigeria Research Reactor-1 (NIRR-1), a miniature neutron source reactor at the Centre for Energy Research and Training of Ahmadu Bello University, Zaria, which was converted from highly enriched to low enriched uranium fuel in 2018 with the assistance of the IAEA, China, and the United States — an achievement in nuclear security that removed weapons-usable material from Nigeria. Nigeria has concluded intergovernmental agreements with the Russian Federation on cooperation in the construction of nuclear power plants, and has hosted IAEA missions to review its nuclear infrastructure. Nigeria is a party to the NPT, the Treaty of Pelindaba, and several of the IAEA conventions. The separation of the promotional functions of NAEC from the regulatory functions of the NNRA reflects the requirement of regulatory independence examined in section 11.3.
