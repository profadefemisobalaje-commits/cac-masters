## 17.8 Environmental Compliance and Enforcement

### The Implementation Gap

The proliferation of environmental treaties and national laws since the 1970s has not been matched by corresponding improvements in environmental outcomes. The first global assessment of the environmental rule of law, published by UNEP in 2019, found that while environmental laws had increased thirty-eight-fold since 1972, their implementation and enforcement were frequently weak, owing to inadequate institutional capacity, insufficient funding, poor coordination among agencies, lack of political will, corruption, and limited public access to information and justice. This "implementation gap" is particularly pronounced in the energy and extractive sectors in many developing countries, where regulators may lack the technical capacity and independence to monitor powerful operators, and where the state's dual role as resource owner and regulator — often as a partner in joint ventures, as examined in Chapter 15 — creates conflicts of interest. This section examines the mechanisms by which compliance with international and national environmental obligations is promoted and enforced.

### Compliance with Multilateral Environmental Agreements

Theories of compliance with international environmental law have emphasised two broad approaches. The **enforcement** approach emphasises the deterrent effect of sanctions for non-compliance. The **managerial** approach, associated with the work of Abram and Antonia Chayes, holds that non-compliance with environmental treaties usually results not from deliberate defiance but from ambiguity in treaty obligations, limited capacity, and the time required for implementation, and that compliance is best promoted through transparency, reporting, review, capacity building, and assistance. Most MEAs combine elements of both approaches, with a strong emphasis on managerial techniques.

The principal compliance mechanisms of MEAs include the following.

**Reporting and review.** Most MEAs require parties to report periodically on their implementation, and many provide for the review of reports by expert bodies or by the conference of the parties. The Paris Agreement's enhanced transparency framework, examined in Chapter 18, is the most elaborate example.

**Non-compliance procedures.** The **Montreal Protocol** on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer pioneered a non-compliance procedure, adopted in 1992, under which an Implementation Committee considers cases of possible non-compliance referred by parties, the secretariat, or the party concerned, and recommends measures to the Meeting of the Parties, which may include assistance, cautions, and the suspension of rights under the Protocol. The procedure is complemented by the Protocol's **Multilateral Fund**, which finances the incremental costs of compliance for developing countries — a combination of facilitation and potential sanction that is widely credited with the Protocol's success in phasing out ozone-depleting substances. The **Kyoto Protocol**'s compliance mechanism comprised a facilitative branch and an enforcement branch, the latter empowered to determine non-compliance with emission targets and to impose consequences; it suspended the eligibility of several parties to participate in the flexibility mechanisms for failure to meet reporting requirements. The compliance committee of the Paris Agreement, established under Article 15, is expressly facilitative, non-adversarial, and non-punitive.

**Public communications.** The **Aarhus Convention Compliance Committee** is distinctive in allowing members of the public to submit communications alleging non-compliance by a party, and has made findings of non-compliance against numerous parties, including the European Union and the United Kingdom, in respect of matters such as public participation in decisions on energy projects and access to justice. The Espoo Convention's Implementation Committee similarly considers information from the public, and has examined many cases concerning nuclear power plants and other energy installations.

**Reactive monitoring.** Under the World Heritage Convention, the World Heritage Committee may examine the state of conservation of sites threatened by extractive or energy projects, request information and missions, and inscribe sites on the List of World Heritage in Danger, as examined in section 17.5.

### International Dispute Settlement

Inter-state litigation and arbitration concerning environmental matters have increased, though they remain relatively rare. Cases before the International Court of Justice have addressed transboundary harm and environmental impact assessment, as examined in sections 17.1 to 17.4, and in *Whaling in the Antarctic* (*Australia v Japan*, 2014), the Court held that Japan's whaling programme was not conducted for purposes of scientific research within the meaning of the International Convention for the Regulation of Whaling. The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea and Annex VII arbitral tribunals under UNCLOS have addressed marine environmental issues, including in the *South China Sea Arbitration* (2016), in which the tribunal found that China had breached its obligations under Part XII of UNCLOS through land reclamation and the harvesting of endangered species, examined in Chapter 20. The advisory opinions on climate change, examined in Chapter 18, have clarified states' obligations in a manner that may be relied upon in future contentious proceedings. Human rights courts and treaty bodies have become important forums for environmental claims, as examined in Part VI.

### National Enforcement

The primary responsibility for enforcing environmental law lies with national authorities. Enforcement tools include the following.

**Administrative enforcement.** Environmental agencies issue permits and licences with conditions, conduct inspections, require monitoring and reporting, issue compliance and stop-work orders, impose administrative penalties, and suspend or revoke permits. In Nigeria, environmental regulation of the petroleum industry is distributed among several bodies: the **Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission** (NUPRC) and the **Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority** (NMDPRA), established under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, which regulate environmental aspects of upstream and midstream and downstream operations; NOSDRA, responsible for oil spill detection and response; the Federal Ministry of Environment, responsible for EIA; and the **National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency** (NESREA), established in 2007, whose enforcement mandate expressly excludes the oil and gas sector. The fragmentation of responsibilities, overlapping mandates, and limited resources of these agencies have been identified as obstacles to effective enforcement.

**Criminal enforcement.** Serious environmental violations are increasingly treated as crimes. The European Union's revised **Environmental Crime Directive** (Directive (EU) 2024/1203) expanded the list of environmental offences, introduced a "qualified offence" for conduct causing catastrophic damage comparable to ecocide, and harmonised minimum penalties for individuals and companies. At the international level, an Independent Expert Panel convened by the Stop Ecocide Foundation proposed in 2021 a definition of **ecocide** as unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge that there is a substantial likelihood of severe and either widespread or long-term damage to the environment, and in 2024 Vanuatu, Fiji, and Samoa formally proposed an amendment to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court to include ecocide as a crime. The Office of the Prosecutor of the International Criminal Court has also indicated that it will give particular consideration to crimes committed by means of, or resulting in, environmental destruction and the illegal exploitation of natural resources.

**Civil enforcement and public interest litigation.** Citizen suit provisions, such as those of the United States Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act, allow private persons to sue violators and regulators. In many common law jurisdictions, courts have relaxed traditional standing requirements to allow public interest litigation on environmental matters. In Nigeria, the Supreme Court held in *Centre for Oil Pollution Watch v Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation* (2018) that a non-governmental organisation committed to environmental protection had standing to bring an action concerning an oil spill that had polluted a community's water sources, without having to show that it had suffered a personal injury, a decision that significantly expanded access to justice in environmental matters. The Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules 2009 also permit public interest litigation for the enforcement of fundamental rights.

**Specialised environmental courts.** Specialised environmental courts and tribunals have been established in many jurisdictions, including India's **National Green Tribunal**, established in 2010, Kenya's Environment and Land Court, and the Land and Environment Court of New South Wales. Such courts bring expertise and efficiency to environmental disputes, and the Indian Supreme Court's development of environmental jurisprudence, including the application of the precautionary and polluter-pays principles and the public trust doctrine, has been influential across the common law world.

### Transparency, Technology, and Private Governance

Compliance is increasingly promoted through transparency and technology. Satellite monitoring enables the detection of gas flaring, methane leaks, oil spills, and deforestation, and the public availability of such data allows civil society, investors, and regulators to identify non-compliance. Corporate sustainability reporting requirements — including the European Union's **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2464), whose scope and timetable were revised by simplification measures adopted in 2025, and the sustainability disclosure standards issued by the International Sustainability Standards Board (IFRS S1 and S2) in 2023 — require companies to disclose environmental risks and impacts. Environmental management systems certified to standards such as ISO 14001, lender covenants enforcing environmental and social action plans, and supply chain due diligence obligations, examined in Chapter 22, extend environmental governance beyond the reach of public authorities.

### Conclusion

International environmental law has developed from a handful of arbitral decisions into a dense body of principles, treaties, and institutions that shape every stage of energy and resource development. Its core principles — prevention, precaution, polluter-pays, and environmental impact assessment — have been progressively hardened into binding obligations, interpreted by courts with increasing rigour, and extended to new challenges such as the assessment of downstream emissions. Yet the gap between the law's aspirations and environmental outcomes remains wide, particularly in resource-rich developing countries, where the effectiveness of environmental law depends on institutional capacity, regulatory independence, transparency, and access to justice. For the energy lawyer, environmental compliance is no longer a peripheral concern but a central determinant of the legality, financing, and social acceptance of projects.

**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. All parties, places, installations, and events are fictitious and do not refer to any real persons, companies, or states.**

The Republic of Coralia and the Federal Republic of Marenia are neighbouring coastal states in a semi-enclosed gulf. Both are parties to UNCLOS, the OPRC Convention, and a regional seas convention modelled on the Abidjan Convention, but neither is party to the Additional Protocol on offshore oil and gas activities.

Deltamar Petroleum, a company incorporated in Coralia and wholly owned by Atlantica Energy plc, a company listed in London, operates the Stella field on Coralia's continental shelf, twenty kilometres from the maritime boundary with Marenia. Coralia's petroleum regulator approved Deltamar's field development plan and environmental impact assessment in 2019. The assessment did not consider transboundary effects, and Coralia did not notify Marenia of the project. Coralia's regulator, which is also responsible for maximising petroleum revenues, conducted no inspections of the installation after 2021, citing budgetary constraints.

In March of the present year, a well control incident at Stella released oil for forty days. Oil reached Marenia's coast, damaging mangroves, fisheries, and a Ramsar-listed wetland, and affecting the livelihoods of an estimated twelve thousand artisanal fishers. Deltamar claims that the incident was caused by unforeseeable geological conditions. Coralia's petroleum law caps the operator's liability for pollution damage at USD 50 million. Deltamar's assets in Coralia are worth approximately USD 80 million, and Atlantica has announced that it intends to sell Deltamar to a small independent company.

**Tasks.**

(a) Advise the government of Marenia on whether Coralia has breached its obligations under international law, identifying the substantive and procedural obligations engaged and the evidence required.

(b) Identify the forums in which Marenia might bring a claim against Coralia and assess the remedies available, including the methodology that a tribunal might use to assess compensation for environmental damage.

(c) Advise a group of Marenian fishers on the options for civil claims against Deltamar and Atlantica, including possible forums, causes of action, and obstacles, drawing on the case law discussed in this chapter.

(d) Prepare a memorandum for Coralia's regulator on the measures it should take in relation to the proposed sale of Deltamar to protect the state's interests in remediation and decommissioning.

::: summary
- International environmental law developed from *Trail Smelter* and *Corfu Channel*, through the Stockholm (1972) and Rio (1992) conferences, into a body of treaties, customary rules, principles, and institutions centred on sustainable development.
- The principle of prevention, an obligation of due diligence, is established customary law; the precautionary principle addresses scientific uncertainty and is widely incorporated in treaties; the polluter-pays principle underlies liability, decommissioning, and environmental taxation.
- Environmental impact assessment is a requirement of general international law where there is a risk of significant transboundary harm, and is required by national law for most energy projects; *Finch* establishes in the United Kingdom that downstream combustion emissions of extracted fossil fuels must be assessed.
- States must prevent significant transboundary harm and notify and consult potentially affected states; major accidents have shaped regimes on nuclear emergencies, industrial accidents, mining waste, and hazardous wastes.
- The Convention on Biological Diversity and the Kunming-Montreal Framework, World Heritage, Ramsar, and lender standards such as IFC Performance Standard 6 constrain extraction in biodiverse areas, generating contests such as Virunga, Yasuní, and EACOP.
- Offshore petroleum pollution lacks a global regime; regional instruments such as OSPAR, the Barcelona Offshore Protocol, and the Abidjan Additional Protocol, and national reforms following Deepwater Horizon and Montara, fill the gap.
- Environmental damage is compensable under international law, as recognised in *Costa Rica v Nicaragua*; civil liability regimes, national statutes, and transnational litigation address oil pollution, including in the Niger Delta.
- Compliance relies on reporting, non-compliance procedures, public communications, litigation, administrative and criminal enforcement, standing reform, specialised courts, and transparency technologies, yet a substantial implementation gap remains.
:::
