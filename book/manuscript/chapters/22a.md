## Introduction

The energy and extractive industries are characterised by features that make governance and compliance especially challenging: very large capital investments and revenue streams; the central role of governments as owners of resources, licensors, regulators, partners, and purchasers; the prominence of state-owned enterprises; operations in jurisdictions with weak institutions and high levels of corruption; complex chains of contractors, agents, and joint venture partners; exposure to geopolitical risks, including sanctions; and growing scrutiny of their environmental and social impacts by investors, regulators, and civil society. The sector has been the setting for some of the largest corruption scandals and enforcement actions in history, from the bribery of Nigerian officials in connection with the Bonny Island LNG project to the *Lava Jato* investigation surrounding Petrobras in Brazil, and for leading cases on the duties of directors in relation to climate change.

This chapter examines the legal frameworks governing the conduct of energy enterprises and the individuals who direct them. It begins with corporate governance in energy enterprises, including national oil companies (section 22.1), and the responsibilities and fiduciary duties of boards (section 22.2). It then examines anti-bribery and anti-corruption frameworks (section 22.3), beneficial ownership and corporate transparency (section 22.4), sanctions compliance and export controls (section 22.5), environmental, social, and governance disclosure (section 22.6), whistleblower protection and corporate investigations (section 22.7), and the design and enforcement of compliance programmes (section 22.8). The chapter builds on the treatment of human rights due diligence in Chapter 21: whereas Chapter 21 examined due diligence from the perspective of affected rights-holders and remedies, this chapter examines the governance structures, legal duties, and compliance systems through which companies organise their response to legal and ethical risks.

## 22.1 Corporate Governance in Energy Enterprises

### The Concept and Purpose of Corporate Governance

**Corporate governance** refers to the system by which companies are directed and controlled — in the formulation of the Cadbury Report of 1992 in the United Kingdom — and involves the relationships among a company's management, its board, its shareholders, and other stakeholders. It addresses the "agency problem" arising from the separation of ownership and control: the risk that managers will act in their own interests rather than those of the shareholders, and, in companies with controlling shareholders, the risk that controlling shareholders will extract private benefits at the expense of minority shareholders. Good governance is associated with better access to capital, lower costs of capital, better performance, and reduced risks of misconduct.

Two broad models have shaped corporate governance. The **shareholder primacy** model, dominant in the United States and the United Kingdom, holds that the purpose of the company is to maximise value for its shareholders, with the interests of other stakeholders protected principally by contract and regulation. The **stakeholder** model, associated with continental Europe and Japan, recognises the interests of employees, creditors, communities, and others in corporate decision-making, reflected for example in employee representation on the supervisory boards of large German companies under co-determination legislation. The distinction has become less sharp: the United Kingdom's "enlightened shareholder value" approach requires directors to have regard to stakeholder interests in promoting the success of the company, examined in section 22.2, and debates about corporate purpose, stakeholder capitalism, and ESG have brought stakeholder considerations into the mainstream, though they have also provoked a political backlash, particularly in the United States.

### Sources of Governance Standards

Corporate governance standards derive from company law, securities regulation and listing rules, codes of corporate governance, and the policies of institutional investors. The **G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance**, revised in 2023 to address sustainability and resilience, provide the leading international benchmark, addressing the rights of shareholders, the role of institutional investors, disclosure and transparency, and the responsibilities of the board. National codes, such as the **UK Corporate Governance Code**, operate principally on a "comply or explain" basis.

In **Nigeria**, the principal company law is the **Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020** (CAMA), which replaced the Act of 1990 and introduced significant reforms, including provisions on the disclosure of persons with significant control, examined in section 22.4, the codification of directors' duties, and enhanced protection of minority shareholders. The **Nigerian Code of Corporate Governance 2018**, issued by the Financial Reporting Council of Nigeria, applies to public companies, private companies that are holding companies of public companies, concessioned or privatised companies, and regulated private companies, on an "apply and explain" basis. Sector regulators, including the Central Bank of Nigeria for banks, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the National Insurance Commission, issue sector-specific governance codes. The Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission and the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission impose governance-related requirements on licensees through regulations and licence conditions.

### National Oil Companies and State-Owned Enterprises

A distinctive feature of the energy sector is the prominence of **state-owned enterprises** (SOEs), including national oil companies (NOCs), which control the majority of the world's proven oil and gas reserves, and state-owned electricity utilities. NOCs vary widely: some, such as Saudi Aramco, Equinor, and Petronas, are commercially sophisticated and internationally active; others serve primarily as instruments of government policy, revenue collection, and patronage. The Natural Resource Governance Institute has estimated that NOCs collectively control assets worth trillions of dollars and that their governance has major consequences for public finances in resource-rich states. NOCs face distinctive governance challenges: multiple and sometimes conflicting objectives (commercial returns, revenue for the treasury, energy security, employment, and social programmes); political interference in appointments and investment decisions; conflicts of interest where the NOC also regulates the sector or manages the state's participation; soft budget constraints; and weak transparency.

The **OECD Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises**, first adopted in 2005 and revised in 2015 and 2024, recommend that the state act as an informed and active owner, ensuring that SOE governance is carried out in a transparent and accountable manner with professionalism and effectiveness; that there be a clear separation between the state's ownership function and its other functions, particularly market regulation; that SOEs compete on a level playing field with private enterprises; that their objectives, including any public policy objectives, be clearly defined and disclosed and their costs funded transparently; that their boards have the authority, competencies, and objectivity to carry out their functions; and that they observe high standards of transparency and disclosure, including in relation to sustainability. For **sovereign wealth funds**, the **Santiago Principles** (Generally Accepted Principles and Practices, 2008), developed by the International Working Group of Sovereign Wealth Funds, address governance, accountability, and investment practices. Nigeria's sovereign wealth fund, the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority, established in 2011, is a member of the International Forum of Sovereign Wealth Funds.

The **Norwegian model**, which separates the commercial NOC (Equinor, formerly Statoil, partially listed since 2001), the regulator (the Norwegian Offshore Directorate, formerly the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate), the policy ministry, and the state's direct financial interest (managed by Petoro), is often cited as a benchmark of NOC governance, although commentators caution that it depends on institutional preconditions not easily replicated. The listing of shares in NOCs — such as the partial listing of Petrobras, Equinor, Ecopetrol, and Saudi Aramco, whose initial public offering in December 2019 was the largest in history — subjects them to securities regulation and market discipline, while also exposing them, as in the case of Petrobras, to securities litigation by investors.

### NNPC Limited and the Petroleum Industry Act

The governance of Nigeria's national oil company was among the central concerns of the **Petroleum Industry Act 2021**. The Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation, established in 1977 as a statutory corporation, had been criticised for decades for opacity, inefficiency, its dual role as commercial operator and quasi-regulator, the remittance of revenues to the Federation Account, and the subsidisation of petroleum products. The PIA required the incorporation of a successor company, **NNPC Limited**, under the Companies and Allied Matters Act, with its shares held by the Government of the Federation through the Ministry of Finance Incorporated and the Ministry of Petroleum Incorporated, and the transfer to it of the Corporation's assets, interests, and liabilities as determined by the Minister. NNPC Limited was incorporated and commenced operations in July 2022. The PIA requires NNPC Limited to operate on a commercial basis, without recourse to government funds, to declare dividends, and to be subject to the same company law and governance requirements as other limited liability companies, and it separated regulatory functions, transferring them to the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission and the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority. The PIA also contemplates the eventual offering of shares in NNPC Limited to the public. The transformation is a significant test of whether legal restructuring can change the governance and performance of an NOC, and early experience has highlighted continuing challenges, including transparency in relation to crude-for-loan arrangements and the cost of fuel subsidies before their removal in 2023, examined in Chapter 24.

### Governance Failures in Energy SOEs

Several high-profile cases illustrate the consequences of governance failures in energy SOEs. The ***Lava Jato*** ("Car Wash") investigation, launched in Brazil in 2014, revealed a scheme in which construction and engineering companies colluded to inflate contracts with **Petrobras** and paid bribes to Petrobras executives and politicians. The investigation led to the convictions of numerous executives and politicians, although some convictions were later annulled on procedural grounds, and to enforcement actions in Brazil, the United States, and elsewhere, including Petrobras's resolution with US authorities in 2018 for USD 1.78 billion and Odebrecht's and Braskem's global resolution in 2016 of at least USD 3.5 billion. In **South Africa**, the Judicial Commission of Inquiry into State Capture (the Zondo Commission), which reported in 2022, documented the "capture" of **Eskom** and other SOEs by networks connected with the Gupta family, including through irregular coal supply contracts and board and management appointments, contributing to the utility's financial and operational crisis examined in Chapter 16. In **Angola**, the "Luanda Leaks" investigation published in 2020 raised allegations concerning the conduct of Isabel dos Santos, daughter of the former president, who had chaired the NOC **Sonangol**; Angolan authorities froze assets and brought charges. These cases demonstrate that governance failures in energy SOEs can have systemic consequences for public finances, energy security, and political stability.

### Governance of Joint Ventures

Much energy investment is undertaken through **joint ventures** — unincorporated joint operating agreements in upstream petroleum, examined in Chapter 7, and incorporated joint ventures in midstream, power, and mining projects. Joint venture governance raises distinctive issues: the allocation of decision-making between the operator and the operating committee; voting thresholds and veto rights; the information and audit rights of non-operators; the management of conflicts of interest when the operator also provides services; and the responsibility of non-operating partners for compliance failures by the operator or the joint venture. Anti-corruption and sanctions risks are acute in joint ventures with state entities and local partners, and companies increasingly insist on compliance covenants, audit rights, and termination rights in joint venture agreements, examined in section 22.8.

## 22.2 Board Responsibilities and Fiduciary Duties

### The Role of the Board

The **board of directors** is responsible for the overall direction and supervision of the company: setting strategy, appointing and overseeing senior management, overseeing risk management and internal control, ensuring the integrity of financial reporting, and accounting to shareholders. In energy enterprises, the board's oversight extends to major operational risks, including safety and environmental risks, whose materialisation can threaten the survival of the company, as the *Deepwater Horizon* disaster demonstrated for BP. Boards typically operate through committees — audit, risk, remuneration, nomination, and, increasingly, safety, sustainability, or ethics committees.

### Directors' Duties in English and Nigerian Law

In English law, the **Companies Act 2006** codifies the general duties of directors, which are owed to the company. Section 171 requires directors to act within their powers; **section 172** requires a director to act in the way he or she considers, in good faith, would be most likely to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in doing so to have regard (among other matters) to the likely consequences of any decision in the long term, the interests of the company's employees, the need to foster business relationships with suppliers, customers, and others, the impact of the company's operations on the community and the environment, the desirability of maintaining a reputation for high standards of business conduct, and the need to act fairly as between members; section 173 requires independent judgment; **section 174** requires a director to exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence, measured by both an objective standard (the knowledge, skill, and experience that may reasonably be expected of a person carrying out the director's functions) and a subjective standard (the director's actual knowledge, skill, and experience); and sections 175 to 177 address conflicts of interest. The **strategic report** required of large companies must include a statement describing how directors have had regard to the matters in section 172.

**Nigerian law** follows a similar structure. CAMA 2020 provides that directors stand in a fiduciary relationship towards the company and must observe the utmost good faith in any transaction with it or on its behalf, must act in what they believe to be the best interests of the company as a whole so as to preserve its assets, further its business, and promote the purposes for which it was formed, and must exercise the powers and discharge the duties of their office honestly, in good faith, and in the best interests of the company, with the degree of care, diligence, and skill that a reasonably prudent director would exercise in comparable circumstances. The Act provides that the matters to which directors must have regard include the interests of the company's employees in general, as well as the interests of its members, and contains provisions on conflicts of interest, secret profits, and the misuse of corporate opportunities. Directors who breach their duties may be liable to the company, and the Act provides for derivative actions by shareholders.

### The Duty of Oversight

The duty of care requires directors to oversee the company's compliance with law and the management of risk. In the United States, the Delaware Court of Chancery in ***In re Caremark International Inc Derivative Litigation*** (1996) held that directors have a duty to attempt in good faith to assure that a corporate information and reporting system, which the board concludes is adequate, exists, and that failure to do so may, in some circumstances, render directors liable for losses caused by non-compliance with applicable legal standards. Liability under *Caremark* requires that directors utterly failed to implement any reporting or information system or controls, or, having implemented such a system, consciously failed to monitor or oversee its operations, as clarified in *Stone v Ritter* (2006). Although historically difficult to establish, *Caremark* claims have been allowed to proceed in cases involving "mission critical" risks, such as food safety in ***Marchand v Barnhill*** (Delaware Supreme Court, 2019), and aircraft safety in the *Boeing* derivative litigation (Court of Chancery, 2021). For energy companies, safety, environmental compliance, and, increasingly, climate risks may be regarded as mission-critical, requiring board-level reporting systems.

### Directors' Duties and Climate Change

Whether, and how, directors' duties require directors to consider climate-related risks has been the subject of extensive analysis. In Australia, legal opinions by Noel Hutley SC and Sebastian Hartford Davis, commissioned by the Centre for Policy Development in 2016 and updated in 2019 and 2021, concluded that directors who fail to consider climate change risks that are foreseeable and material to their company could be found liable for breach of the duty of care and diligence. Similar analyses have been published in many jurisdictions under the auspices of the Commonwealth Climate and Law Initiative. Regulators, including the Australian Securities and Investments Commission and the Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority, have endorsed the view that climate risk is a financial risk that boards must address. In *McVeigh v Retail Employees Superannuation Pty Ltd*, a member of an Australian pension fund claimed that the trustee had failed to manage climate risk; the case settled in 2020 with the fund committing to net zero by 2050 and to enhanced climate risk management and disclosure.

::: casebox {title="ClientEarth v Shell plc: Climate Strategy and the Derivative Claim"}
**ClientEarth v Shell plc [2023] EWHC 1897 (Ch) (High Court of England and Wales, 24 July 2023).**

**Facts.** ClientEarth, an environmental law organisation holding a small number of shares in Shell plc, sought permission under Part 11 of the Companies Act 2006 to bring a **derivative claim** on behalf of the company against its directors, alleging that they had breached their duties under sections 172 and 174 by failing to adopt and implement an energy transition strategy that properly managed the risks to the company from climate change, including by failing to set adequate interim emission reduction targets, and by failing to comply with the order of the District Court of The Hague in *Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell* (2021), which had required Shell to reduce the emissions of the Shell group by forty-five per cent by 2030 relative to 2019 levels (an order later overturned on appeal in November 2024, as examined in Chapter 18). ClientEarth sought declarations and an order requiring the directors to adopt a strategy consistent with the Paris Agreement and to comply with the Dutch order.

**Decision.** Trower J refused permission, first on the papers in May 2023 and then, after an oral hearing, in July 2023. He held that ClientEarth had not established a *prima facie* case. The directors' duties under sections 172 and 174 are owed to the company, and the management of climate risk involves commercial judgments on which directors must balance a range of competing considerations; it is for the directors, not the court, to determine how best to promote the success of the company, and the court will not interfere with the directors' judgment in matters of commercial management absent evidence that they have acted irrationally or in bad faith. The evidence did not establish that the directors' approach to climate risk fell outside the range of reasonable approaches, and the general duties do not impose specific, absolute obligations of the kind alleged. The judge also considered that the small size of ClientEarth's shareholding and its broader purposes suggested that the claim was brought to advance its own policy agenda rather than in the interests of the company.

**Significance.** The decision indicates the difficulty of using directors' duties to compel a particular climate strategy through litigation, in light of the deference accorded to directors' business judgment and the hurdles of the derivative action. It does not, however, preclude liability where directors ignore climate risk altogether or act irrationally, and climate risk remains a matter that directors must consider in discharging their duties of care and to promote the success of the company. Shareholder engagement, disclosure regulation, and litigation against companies themselves have proved more effective avenues for influencing corporate climate strategies.
:::

### Shareholder Engagement and Activism

Shareholders exercise influence over the governance of energy companies through voting, engagement, and resolutions. In **May 2021**, the activist hedge fund **Engine No. 1**, holding a very small stake in ExxonMobil, succeeded with the support of major institutional investors in electing three of its four nominees to ExxonMobil's board, arguing that the company lacked a credible strategy for the energy transition — a landmark in climate-related activism. Shareholder resolutions calling on oil and gas companies to set emission reduction targets, including Scope 3 targets, have been filed regularly by organisations such as Follow This, with varying levels of support. "**Say on climate**" votes, in which companies submit their climate transition plans to an advisory shareholder vote, have been adopted by several energy companies. Since 2022, however, support for climate-related resolutions has declined amid political opposition to ESG in the United States, concerns about energy security, and the reassertion by some companies, including BP and Shell, of strategies emphasising oil and gas returns.

### Parent Company Responsibility

The board of a parent company may also face legal exposure for harms caused by subsidiaries. As examined in Chapters 17 and 21, the UK Supreme Court in *Vedanta* and *Okpabi* recognised that a parent company may owe a duty of care to persons affected by its subsidiary's operations where it has assumed responsibility for relevant aspects of those operations, including through group-wide policies that it implements, supervises, or holds out as applying. This creates a governance dilemma: group-wide policies on safety, environment, and human rights may be necessary to manage risks effectively and to satisfy due diligence obligations, but they may also be cited as evidence of the parent's assumption of responsibility. The emerging mandatory due diligence laws, examined in Chapter 21, resolve this dilemma in favour of active parent company oversight, by requiring it. Boards should therefore ensure that group policies are not merely promulgated but implemented and monitored, since a gap between policy and practice is likely to be the most damaging scenario both legally and reputationally.
