## 26.5 Jurisdiction, Admissibility, and Applicable Law

### Jurisdiction and Admissibility Distinguished

Objections to the hearing of a claim may go to **jurisdiction** — the power of the tribunal to decide the dispute — or to **admissibility** — whether the claim, although within the tribunal's jurisdiction, should be heard. The distinction, developed in inter-state litigation and adopted in investment arbitration, has practical consequences: decisions on jurisdiction are subject to review by courts and annulment committees, whereas decisions on admissibility are generally regarded as matters for the tribunal; jurisdictional objections must generally be raised at an early stage; and a lack of jurisdiction cannot be cured by the parties' conduct in the same way as an admissibility defect. Objections concerning the existence and scope of consent, the nationality of the investor, the existence of a protected investment, and the temporal scope of the treaty are jurisdictional; objections concerning the ripeness of a claim, compliance with cooling-off periods (on some views), the exhaustion of local remedies where required, abuse of process, and the effect of parallel proceedings are commonly treated as going to admissibility.

### Consent and its Limits

Jurisdiction in arbitration rests on **consent**. In contract-based arbitration, consent is expressed in the arbitration agreement; in treaty-based arbitration, the state's standing offer to arbitrate in the treaty is accepted by the investor when it commences arbitration. The scope of consent depends on the terms of the offer, which may be limited to certain disputes (for example, disputes concerning the amount of compensation for expropriation, as in some older Soviet-era and Chinese treaties), to investments made in accordance with host state law, or to disputes arising after the treaty's entry into force. The **Energy Charter Treaty** contains an unconditional consent in Article 26(3), subject to the possibility for states listed in Annex ID to exclude disputes previously submitted to other fora, and for states listed in Annex IA to exclude claims under the last sentence of Article 10(1) (the "umbrella clause"). The ECT's **provisional application** under Article 45, examined in Chapter 14, was the basis for the *Yukos* tribunals' jurisdiction over Russia, which had signed but not ratified the Treaty, and the interpretation of the "limitation clause" in Article 45(1) — under which provisional application applies to the extent that it is not inconsistent with the signatory's constitution, laws, or regulations — was central to the litigation in the Dutch courts.

### Protected Investors: Nationality and Corporate Structuring

Investment treaties protect investors who are nationals of the other contracting state. For natural persons, nationality is determined by the law of the state concerned, and dual nationals of the host state may be excluded, as under Article 25(2)(a) of the ICSID Convention. For companies, most treaties define nationality by reference to the place of incorporation, sometimes combined with a requirement of seat or substantial business activities, or subject to a **denial of benefits** clause allowing the host state to deny treaty protection to companies owned or controlled by nationals of third states or of the host state that have no substantial business activities in the home state. Article 17 of the Energy Charter Treaty contains such a clause, which the *Plama v Bulgaria* tribunal (2005) held operates prospectively from the time it is invoked, rather than retrospectively — a conclusion rejected by later treaties that expressly allow retrospective denial.

The protection of investors who channel their investments through holding companies in states with favourable treaties — **treaty planning** or "nationality planning" — is a recurring issue in energy disputes, where investments are commonly held through intermediate companies in jurisdictions such as the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Cyprus, and Mauritius. Tribunals have generally accepted that investors may structure their investments to obtain treaty protection, as in *Aguas del Tunari v Bolivia* (2005), but have held that a restructuring undertaken when a dispute is foreseeable, for the purpose of gaining access to treaty protection, constitutes an **abuse of process**. In ***Philip Morris Asia v Australia*** (2015), the tribunal declined jurisdiction because the restructuring had occurred when the dispute was reasonably foreseeable; in ***Mobil v Venezuela*** (2010), arising from the nationalisation of oil investments, the tribunal held that it had jurisdiction over disputes arising after the restructuring of the investment through a Dutch holding company, but not over disputes that already existed at the time of the restructuring; and in ***Pac Rim Cayman v El Salvador*** (2012), arising from a gold mining project, the tribunal found that a change of nationality to gain access to CAFTA arbitration was an abuse of process as regards disputes that had already arisen. The **"shell company"** problem has led to the inclusion in new treaties of substantial business activity requirements and denial of benefits clauses.

### Protected Investments: Legality and Origin

Many treaties protect only investments made **"in accordance with the law"** of the host state, and tribunals have held, even in the absence of such a clause, that investments procured through **corruption or fraud** are not protected. In ***World Duty Free v Kenya*** (2006), the tribunal held that a contract procured by a bribe to the President of Kenya was unenforceable as contrary to international public policy. In ***Metal-Tech v Uzbekistan*** (2013), the tribunal declined jurisdiction because the investment had been established through corrupt payments, notwithstanding that the state's officials had been the recipients of the bribes. In the energy sector, allegations of corruption in the procurement of licences and contracts are frequently raised by states as defences, as examined in Chapter 22, raising difficult questions about the standard and burden of proof — tribunals have variously applied a heightened standard of "clear and convincing evidence" or the ordinary balance of probabilities, sometimes drawing inferences from "red flags" — and about the fairness of allowing a state to rely on the corruption of its own officials. The P&ID case, examined in section 26.7, illustrates the consequences of corruption for the enforceability of awards.

### Parallel Proceedings, Contract Claims, and Treaty Claims

Energy investments typically involve both a **contract** with the state or a state entity, often containing its own dispute resolution clause, and **treaty** protection. The relationship between contract and treaty claims was clarified in ***Compañía de Aguas del Aconquija and Vivendi Universal v Argentina*** (*Vivendi I* annulment decision, 2002), in which the committee held that whether there has been a breach of a treaty and whether there has been a breach of contract are different questions, each determined by reference to its own proper law, and that a contractual forum selection clause does not preclude a treaty tribunal from deciding a treaty claim, even if it requires consideration of the contract. **Umbrella clauses**, under which the host state undertakes to observe any obligation it has entered into with regard to investments, may elevate contractual breaches to treaty breaches, though tribunals have differed on their scope, as in the contrasting decisions in *SGS v Pakistan* (2003) and *SGS v Philippines* (2004), examined in Chapter 14.

Parallel proceedings may also arise when an investor, or its affiliates at different levels of the corporate chain, bring claims under different treaties, or when the investor pursues both domestic litigation and international arbitration. The doctrines of ***lis pendens*** and ***res judicata***, the **fork-in-the-road** clause, and the prohibition of **abuse of process** may limit duplicative claims. In *Orascom v Algeria* (2017), the tribunal held that the claimant's pursuit of claims at several levels of its corporate structure, under different treaties, in relation to the same measures and harm, was an abuse of rights, and declined to hear the claim. The **consolidation** of related proceedings, available under some treaties and institutional rules, may reduce the risk of inconsistent decisions.

### Applicable Law

The law applicable to the substance of the dispute depends on the basis of the claim and the forum. In **commercial arbitration**, the tribunal applies the law chosen by the parties or, in the absence of a choice, the law or rules of law it determines to be appropriate, as provided in the applicable arbitration rules and the *lex arbitri*; energy contracts commonly choose English or New York law, the law of the host state, or the law of the host state combined with international law, as examined in Chapter 15. In **ICSID arbitration**, **Article 42(1)** of the Convention provides that the tribunal shall decide the dispute in accordance with the rules of law agreed by the parties, and, in the absence of agreement, the law of the contracting state party to the dispute (including its rules on the conflict of laws) and such rules of international law as may be applicable. Tribunals have interpreted the second sentence as giving international law a **supplemental and corrective** role, so that host state law inconsistent with international law will not be applied, as the annulment committees in *Klöckner* and *Amco* held, and as later tribunals, including the *Wena Hotels v Egypt* annulment committee (2002), affirmed. In **treaty arbitration**, the treaty itself is the primary source of applicable law, interpreted in accordance with the **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties** (Articles 31 to 33), supplemented by general international law; Article 26(6) of the Energy Charter Treaty provides that the tribunal shall decide the issues in accordance with the Treaty and applicable rules and principles of international law. Host state law remains relevant to incidental questions, such as the existence and scope of the investor's property rights, the legality of the investment, and the content of contractual rights.

### The Interaction of International Law and Other Regimes

Energy arbitrations increasingly raise questions about the interaction between investment law and other areas of international law, including human rights, environmental law, and climate change. States have invoked their environmental and human rights obligations as defences or as the basis for **counterclaims**. In ***Burlington v Ecuador*** (2017) and ***Perenco v Ecuador*** (2015 interim decision and 2019 award), the tribunals upheld Ecuador's environmental counterclaims and awarded it compensation for environmental damage caused by the investors' oil operations in the Amazon, applying Ecuadorian environmental law. In ***Urbaser v Argentina*** (2016), a water concession case, the tribunal accepted jurisdiction over a human rights counterclaim, though it rejected it on the merits. The relevance of the **Paris Agreement** and the obligations of states in respect of climate change, clarified by the International Court of Justice's advisory opinion of July 2025, examined in Chapter 18, to the interpretation of investment treaties and the assessment of claims arising from climate measures is likely to be a major issue in the coming decade, as in the claims brought by RWE and Uniper against the Netherlands in respect of its coal phase-out, which were withdrawn in 2022–2023 after the Court of Justice's *Komstroy* judgment, and the claims against Canada and Slovenia concerning restrictions on fossil fuel projects.

## 26.6 Evidence, Expert Testimony, and Damages

### Evidence in International Arbitration

International arbitration combines elements of the **common law** and **civil law** traditions of evidence. Tribunals generally have broad discretion over the admissibility, relevance, materiality, and weight of evidence, under Article 34 of the ICSID Arbitration Rules (2022 Rule 36) and Article 27(4) of the UNCITRAL Rules. The **IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence in International Arbitration** (2010, revised 2020) reflect a compromise between traditions: they provide for the production of documents, but only of narrow and specific categories that are relevant to the case and material to its outcome, rejecting the broad discovery of US litigation; they treat written witness statements as the evidence-in-chief, with oral evidence focused on cross-examination; and they provide for party-appointed and tribunal-appointed experts. The **Prague Rules on the Efficient Conduct of Proceedings in International Arbitration** (2018) offer an alternative, more inquisitorial model, emphasising the active role of the tribunal and limiting document production.

The **burden of proof** generally lies on the party asserting a fact (*actori incumbit probatio*), and the standard of proof is usually the balance of probabilities, although tribunals have applied heightened standards to allegations of fraud and corruption, as noted in section 26.5. Tribunals may draw **adverse inferences** from a party's failure to produce documents or witnesses within its control. In energy disputes, key evidence often includes technical data on reserves and production, contractual and regulatory correspondence, internal valuation documents, board minutes, and, in disputes involving states, government records, which states may withhold on grounds of political or institutional sensitivity or confidentiality, as recognised in Article 9 of the IBA Rules.

### Expert Evidence

Expert evidence is central to energy arbitration. Experts address technical issues — the geology and reserves of a petroleum field, the design and construction of a power plant, the causes of delay or defects, the operation of pricing mechanisms — and, most importantly, the **quantum** of damages. In the common practice of international arbitration, each party appoints its own experts, who submit reports and are cross-examined at the hearing. The tendency of party-appointed experts to reach widely divergent conclusions, which may undermine their credibility and the tribunal's confidence, has led to procedural innovations. **Expert conferencing** or **"witness conferencing"** (sometimes called "hot-tubbing"), in which experts in the same discipline give evidence together and are questioned by the tribunal and counsel, can narrow the issues and expose the reasons for disagreement. **Joint expert reports**, prepared after meetings between the experts, identify the matters on which they agree and disagree. Tribunals may also appoint their own experts, under Article 6 of the IBA Rules, Article 29 of the UNCITRAL Rules, or the ICSID Rules, though this practice is less common in investment arbitration. The **CIArb Protocol for the Use of Party-Appointed Expert Witnesses in International Arbitration** and the IBA Rules emphasise the expert's overriding duty of independence and objectivity.

### Principles of Compensation

The principles governing compensation differ depending on whether the claim is for a lawful expropriation or for an internationally wrongful act. For **lawful expropriation**, investment treaties typically require compensation equivalent to the **fair market value** of the investment immediately before the expropriation or before it became publicly known, often expressed in the **Hull formula** of "prompt, adequate and effective" compensation, as examined in Chapter 14. For **unlawful acts**, including unlawful expropriation and breaches of other treaty standards, the customary standard is **full reparation**, as articulated by the Permanent Court of International Justice in ***Factory at Chorzów*** (1928): reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act and re-establish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed — a principle codified in Articles 31 and 34 to 36 of the ILC Articles on State Responsibility. The practical difference is that, for unlawful expropriation, the investor may be entitled to the value of the investment at the date of the award, if higher than at the date of expropriation, together with consequential losses. In ***ADC v Hungary*** (2006), the tribunal applied this principle to award the value of the investment at the date of the award, which had increased substantially, and in ***ConocoPhillips v Venezuela*** (2019), the tribunal, having found that Venezuela had expropriated unlawfully by failing to negotiate in good faith for compensation, valued the investments at the date of the award, taking into account subsequent oil price movements.

For breaches of standards other than expropriation, such as fair and equitable treatment, tribunals generally award damages measured by the difference between the investor's position with and without the breach, which may require the valuation of the investment in the "but-for" scenario and the "actual" scenario. **Causation** must be established: the investor must show that the loss was caused by the state's breach rather than by other factors, such as market conditions or the investor's own conduct. **Contributory fault** may reduce the award, as in *Occidental v Ecuador* (2012), in which the tribunal reduced the damages by twenty-five per cent on account of the investor's breach of its contract by transferring an interest without authorisation, and in *Yukos*, in which the tribunals reduced the damages by twenty-five per cent on account of the claimants' contributory fault, including abuse of tax regimes.

### Valuation Methods

The principal methods of valuation used in energy arbitrations, summarised in Figure 26.2, are:

1. **Income-based methods**, principally the **discounted cash flow** (DCF) method, which calculates the present value of the expected future net cash flows of the investment, discounted at a rate reflecting the time value of money and the risks of the investment. DCF is the preferred method for valuing going concerns with a record of profitability, such as producing oil fields and operating power plants, and is the standard approach of the energy industry. It is, however, highly sensitive to assumptions about future prices, production volumes, costs, and the discount rate — including the **country risk premium** — and disputes over these assumptions frequently account for most of the difference between the parties' valuations.
2. **Market-based methods**, which value the investment by reference to **comparable transactions**, trading multiples of comparable listed companies, or bona fide offers for the investment, adjusted for differences in timing and characteristics.
3. **Asset-based or cost methods**, which value the investment by reference to the amount invested (**sunk costs**), the book value, or the replacement cost. Tribunals have preferred these methods where the investment had no record of profitability, as in early-stage projects, where future cash flows are too speculative to be estimated with reasonable certainty.
4. **Loss of opportunity**, which values the chance of obtaining a benefit, discounted by the probability of its realisation, and has been applied by some tribunals to exploration-stage investments where the existence of commercially viable reserves was uncertain, although others have rejected it.

The **"reasonable certainty"** standard for future profits has been decisive in mining and exploration disputes. In ***Tethyan Copper v Pakistan*** (2019), arising from the denial of a mining lease for the Reko Diq copper and gold deposit, the tribunal accepted a DCF valuation of the undeveloped project, on the basis that the deposit had been extensively explored and a feasibility study completed, awarding around USD 5.9 billion including interest — an award subsequently resolved by a settlement under which the project was reconstituted with Barrick Gold and Pakistani state entities, as noted in Chapter 7. By contrast, tribunals in other mining cases, such as *Crystallex v Venezuela* (2016) and *Rusoro v Venezuela* (2016), used a range of methods, including market-based approaches, owing to the lack of production history.

### Interest and Costs

**Interest** may account for a substantial part of the total award in energy disputes, which often last for many years. Tribunals award **pre-award interest** from the date of the breach or valuation to the date of the award, and **post-award interest** until payment, and modern practice favours **compound interest**, as reflecting commercial reality, rather than the simple interest traditionally awarded. The choice of rate — a risk-free rate, the investor's cost of borrowing, or a commercial rate — can have dramatic effects on the total award. **Costs**, including the fees of the tribunal and the institution and the parties' legal and expert costs, which may run to tens of millions of dollars in major energy arbitrations, are allocated at the tribunal's discretion; the trend has been towards "costs follow the event", under which the losing party pays a significant proportion of the successful party's costs, adjusted for the parties' conduct and relative success.
