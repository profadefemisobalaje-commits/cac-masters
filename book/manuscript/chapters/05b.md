## 5.3 Petroleum Licensing and Concession Systems

### Typology of Petroleum Arrangements

Petroleum arrangements between host states and investors are conventionally classified into two families: *concessionary* (or licensing) systems and *contractual* systems. Contractual systems are further divided into production sharing contracts and service contracts. The classification turns principally on the ownership of produced petroleum and the form of the state's financial return.

![Principal families of petroleum arrangements, classified by ownership of production and the form of the state's return. *Source:* prepared for this edition.](/figures/fig-05-01.png){width=94%}

Under a **concessionary or licensing system**, the investor acquires the exclusive right to explore for and produce petroleum within a defined area and owns the petroleum produced at the wellhead (or at another defined point), subject to the payment of royalties and taxes to the state. The state's return derives from fiscal instruments — bonuses, rentals, royalties, and taxes — rather than from a share of physical production. Modern licensing systems are used in the United Kingdom, Norway, the United States (for federal and state leases), Australia, Brazil (for its concession regime), and many other jurisdictions. Nigeria's historic oil mining lease system and the PIA's petroleum mining lease are licensing instruments, although in Nigeria they have been combined with joint ventures and production sharing contracts involving the national oil company.

Under a **production sharing contract (PSC)**, the state (usually acting through its national oil company) retains ownership of the petroleum, and the investor acts as a contractor that bears the exploration and development risk and costs. In return, the contractor is entitled to recover its costs from a portion of production ("cost oil") and to receive a share of the remaining production ("profit oil"), with the balance going to the state. Title to the contractor's share passes at a defined delivery point.

Under a **service contract**, the state retains ownership of all production, and the contractor is paid a fee for its services, either in cash or in kind. In a *pure* service contract, the fee is fixed and the state bears the risk; in a *risk* service contract, the contractor bears the exploration risk and is remunerated only if production is achieved. Service contracts are used where constitutional or political considerations preclude any transfer of ownership of petroleum to foreign companies, notably in Iran (whose buyback contracts and the subsequent Iran Petroleum Contract reflect constitutional prohibitions on foreign ownership), Iraq (whose technical service contracts for major fields awarded from 2009 paid a fixed remuneration fee per barrel), and Mexico (prior to the 2013 reforms).

### The Modern Licence

The modern petroleum licence differs fundamentally from the classical concession described in Chapter 1. Where the classical concession covered vast areas for very long periods with minimal state control, the modern licence covers a defined block for a limited exploration period, imposes work obligations and relinquishment requirements, subjects the licensee to comprehensive regulatory control over operations, and secures a substantial share of the resource rent for the state through a progressive fiscal regime. The modern licence is embedded in a statutory framework that regulates health and safety, environmental protection, resource management, decommissioning, and local content.

In the United Kingdom, for example, the North Sea Transition Authority (NSTA, formerly the Oil and Gas Authority) regulates licensing and resource management under the Petroleum Act 1998, the Energy Act 2016, and the model clauses; the Health and Safety Executive regulates safety under the Offshore Installations (Safety Case) Regulations 2015; the Offshore Petroleum Regulator for Environment and Decommissioning (OPRED), within the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, regulates environmental matters and decommissioning; and HM Revenue and Customs administers a fiscal regime comprising ring-fence corporation tax, a supplementary charge, and the Energy Profits Levy introduced in 2022. Since 2021, the NSTA has been required to apply its "Strategy", which includes a central obligation on licensees and others to take the steps necessary to secure that the maximum value of economically recoverable petroleum is recovered from the strata beneath relevant UK waters, while also assisting the Secretary of State in meeting the net zero target.

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Licensing

Licensing systems have several advantages. They are relatively simple to administer, since the state's return derives from fiscal instruments applied through the tax system. They provide investors with clear title to production, facilitating financing. They are compatible with general legislation applicable to all licensees, promoting transparency and equality of treatment. And they keep the state at arm's length from commercial operations, reducing the need for state technical capacity.

Their principal disadvantages, from the host state's perspective, are that the state has limited direct control over operations and limited access to information; that fiscal regimes based on royalties and taxes may fail to capture the full resource rent, especially when prices rise unexpectedly; and that the transfer of ownership of petroleum to foreign companies may be politically sensitive.

## 5.4 Production Sharing Contracts

### Origins

The production sharing contract was pioneered in Indonesia in the mid-1960s by the state oil company Permina (later Pertamina) under the leadership of Ibnu Sutowo, in response to political pressure to end the concession system and assert national control over petroleum. The Indonesian model, which provided for the state to retain ownership and management control while the foreign contractor bore the costs and risks and recovered them from production, was adopted by many other states in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. By the 1990s, the PSC had become the predominant form of petroleum arrangement in the developing world.

In Nigeria, PSCs were introduced in 1973 with Ashland Oil and became the primary vehicle for deep offshore development following the 1993 licensing round. The Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contracts Act 1999 (as amended in 2019) established the fiscal framework for deep offshore PSCs, including royalty rates that decline with water depth. The 2019 amendment, which increased royalty rates and introduced a price-based royalty, was enacted after the federal government had lost significant revenue because the 1999 Act's provision for review of the royalty rates once oil prices exceeded USD 20 per barrel had never been implemented. The PIA now governs new upstream arrangements, while preserving the terms of existing PSCs subject to the conversion provisions discussed below.

### Structure and Key Provisions

A typical PSC contains the following key provisions:

**Parties and contract area.** The PSC is concluded between the state or national oil company and the contractor (often a consortium of companies). It defines the contract area, usually by reference to geographical coordinates.

**Term and phases.** The PSC specifies an exploration period, divided into phases with associated work commitments, and a production period that commences upon a declaration of commerciality and approval of a development plan.

**Work programme and budget.** The contractor must submit annual work programmes and budgets for approval by a management committee on which the national oil company is represented.

**Management committee.** The management committee, composed of representatives of the national oil company and the contractor, supervises operations. The allocation of voting rights between the parties determines the degree of state control.

**Cost recovery.** The contractor is entitled to recover its exploration, development, and operating costs from a defined percentage of production — the "cost oil" or "cost recovery limit" — commonly between 50 and 80 per cent of production in any period. Costs that exceed the limit may usually be carried forward to future periods. Accounting procedures annexed to the PSC define which costs are recoverable and how they are to be allocated.

**Profit oil split.** Production remaining after royalty and cost oil — "profit oil" — is divided between the state and the contractor in accordance with an agreed formula. The split may be fixed or, more commonly, may vary according to a sliding scale based on cumulative production, daily production rates, the contractor's rate of return, or an "R-factor" (the ratio of the contractor's cumulative revenues to its cumulative costs).

**Royalty and taxation.** Many PSCs also require payment of a royalty and subject the contractor's share of profit oil to income tax. In some PSCs, the national oil company pays the contractor's income tax out of the state's share of profit oil (a "pay-on-behalf" arrangement), to enable the contractor to claim foreign tax credits in its home jurisdiction.

**Title and lifting.** Title to the contractor's share of production passes at the delivery point, and lifting arrangements govern the allocation of cargoes.

**Ownership of assets.** Title to equipment and facilities typically passes to the state either upon acquisition or upon full cost recovery, although the contractor retains the right to use them for operations.

**Domestic supply obligations, local content, training, and stabilisation clauses** are also common.

### A Worked Illustration

The mechanics of a PSC may be illustrated with a simplified, hypothetical example. Suppose that in a given year a field produces 10 million barrels of oil, the PSC provides for a royalty of 10 per cent, a cost recovery limit of 60 per cent of production after royalty, and a profit oil split of 60:40 in favour of the state, and that the contractor has recoverable costs of 7 million barrels' worth for the year.

1. Royalty: 10 per cent of 10 million = 1 million barrels to the state, leaving 9 million barrels.
2. Cost oil: the cost recovery limit is 60 per cent of 9 million = 5.4 million barrels. The contractor recovers 5.4 million barrels; the unrecovered balance of 1.6 million barrels' worth is carried forward.
3. Profit oil: 9 million minus 5.4 million = 3.6 million barrels, of which the state receives 60 per cent (2.16 million) and the contractor 40 per cent (1.44 million).
4. Tax: if the contractor's profit oil is subject to income tax at, say, 30 per cent, a further portion of the contractor's share is payable to the state.

In this illustration, before tax the state receives 3.16 million barrels (31.6 per cent of production) and the contractor 6.84 million barrels, of which 5.4 million represent cost recovery. The "government take" — the share of the economic rent (revenues minus costs) accruing to the state — is a more meaningful measure than the share of gross production, and is the standard metric for comparing fiscal regimes. Chapter 16 examines fiscal design in more detail.

### Advantages, Disadvantages, and Disputes

PSCs offer host states several advantages: formal ownership of petroleum is retained, satisfying political and constitutional requirements; the national oil company participates in management and gains access to information and expertise; and the state's share of production rises with profitability under progressive profit-sharing formulas. For investors, PSCs offer the right to recover costs from production and a share of production that can be booked as reserves under some accounting rules.

The disadvantages include administrative complexity, especially in auditing cost recovery; the incentive for contractors to "gold-plate" costs, since higher recoverable costs increase their share of production; and the potential for disputes over cost allocation, the interpretation of profit oil formulas, and the lifting of entitlements. The Nigerian experience includes significant disputes concerning the deep offshore PSCs. In the dispute concerning the Oil Mining Lease 133 (Erha) PSC, an arbitral tribunal in 2011 upheld claims by the contractors, Esso Exploration and Production Nigeria Ltd and Shell Nigeria Exploration and Production Co Ltd, against NNPC concerning the lifting of tax oil and royalty oil. NNPC applied to the Federal High Court in Abuja, which set the award aside on the basis that the dispute concerned tax matters that were not arbitrable under Nigerian law, generating further appeals in Nigeria and parallel proceedings in the United States to enforce the award. The litigation illustrates the jurisdictional complexities that arise when fiscal provisions are embedded in contracts and the tension between the finality of arbitral awards and national courts' views on arbitrability.

### The PIA and the Conversion of Existing Arrangements

The PIA introduced a framework for the voluntary conversion of existing oil prospecting licences and oil mining leases, including those underlying PSCs and joint ventures, to the new petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases. Conversion is incentivised by more favourable fiscal terms under the new regime, while holders that do not convert continue under their existing terms until expiry but cannot benefit from the new fiscal incentives and face restrictions on renewal. The Act also required the conversion of NNPC's joint venture interests into incorporated joint ventures within a specified period after its commencement. These transitional provisions illustrate a characteristic technique of petroleum law reform: the use of incentives rather than compulsion to migrate investors from legacy arrangements, thereby reducing the risk of investment treaty claims.

### Service Contracts in Practice

Service contracts, although less common than licences and PSCs, deserve attention because of their use in some of the world's largest oil provinces. The Iraqi technical service contracts awarded in 2009 and 2010 for super-giant fields such as Rumaila, West Qurna, and Majnoon paid contractors a fixed remuneration fee per barrel of incremental production above an agreed baseline, plus recovery of costs, with fees subject to an R-factor adjustment. Because contractors earned the same fee per barrel regardless of oil prices, the contracts transferred almost the entire price upside to the state, but they also left contractors exposed to production disruptions, export capacity constraints, and delays in cost recovery payments. Several contracts were subsequently renegotiated to lower production targets. The experience illustrates that the allocation of risk and reward in petroleum arrangements is determined not by the label attached to the contract but by the detailed economic and legal terms.
