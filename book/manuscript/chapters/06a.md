## Introduction

Natural gas occupies a distinctive and increasingly contested position in international energy law. For much of the twentieth century it was treated as an unwanted by-product of oil production, flared or reinjected because there was no economical way to bring it to market. Over the past five decades it has become a global commodity in its own right, supplying almost a quarter of world primary energy, fuelling a large share of electricity generation, providing heat for homes and industry, and serving as a feedstock for fertilisers and petrochemicals. Its lower carbon intensity relative to coal and oil has led many governments to describe it as a "bridge" or "transition" fuel, while its methane emissions and long-lived infrastructure have led critics to warn that new gas investment risks locking in emissions incompatible with the goals of the Paris Agreement. The legal regime of natural gas must therefore accommodate both its role in energy security and its contested position in the energy transition.

The legal regime of natural gas differs from that of oil in ways that flow directly from the physical properties of the commodity. Oil is a dense liquid that can be stored easily and transported cheaply by tanker, rail, or pipeline to almost any market; this fungibility gave rise, from the 1980s onwards, to a global oil market with transparent spot and futures prices. Gas, by contrast, has a very low energy density at atmospheric pressure. It must be transported either through high-pressure pipelines, which are fixed, capital-intensive, and tie producer and consumer together for decades, or as liquefied natural gas, which requires cooling the gas to approximately minus 162 degrees Celsius, shipping it in specialised vessels, and regasifying it at the destination. Both modes of transport require very large, specific, and long-lived investments. As a consequence, gas markets historically developed as regional rather than global markets, organised around long-term contracts that allocated the risks of these investments between sellers and buyers, and subject to extensive regulation of the natural monopoly elements of the supply chain.

Three transformations over the past four decades have reshaped the legal regime of gas. The first is liberalisation: the introduction of competition in gas supply in North America from the 1980s, in the United Kingdom from the 1990s, and in the European Union from the late 1990s onwards, requiring the unbundling of transmission from supply and the creation of rights of third-party access to pipelines. The second is the growth of the LNG trade, which has expanded from a handful of point-to-point projects linking specific producers with Japanese and European buyers into a global market involving dozens of exporting and importing countries, with an increasing share of spot and short-term trade and greater price convergence among regions. The third is the politicisation of gas supply, illustrated most dramatically by the Russian Federation's curtailment of gas deliveries to Europe in 2021 and 2022, which precipitated an energy crisis, extraordinary regulatory interventions, and a fundamental restructuring of European gas supply.

This chapter examines the legal regime of natural gas from wellhead to burner tip. It begins with the legal and commercial characteristics of gas (section 6.1) and the regulation of gas exploration and production (section 6.2). It then examines gas transportation and pipeline access (section 6.3), the structures used to develop LNG projects (section 6.4), long-term gas sales and purchase agreements (section 6.5), and the mechanisms of price review, take-or-pay, and contractual flexibility (section 6.6). It concludes with the legal framework for cross-border gas infrastructure and transit (section 6.7) and the law of gas supply disputes and regulatory intervention (section 6.8). The treatment of submarine pipelines under the law of the sea is reserved for Chapter 20, and the geopolitics of pipeline dependence for Chapter 25; this chapter focuses on the commercial and regulatory law of gas.

## 6.1 Legal and Commercial Characteristics of Natural Gas

### Physical Properties and Their Legal Consequences

Natural gas is a mixture of hydrocarbon gases composed predominantly of methane, together with varying proportions of heavier hydrocarbons (ethane, propane, butane, and pentanes), and non-hydrocarbon components such as carbon dioxide, nitrogen, hydrogen sulphide, and water vapour. Before gas can be delivered to a transmission system, it must usually be processed to remove impurities and to extract natural gas liquids, which are valuable products in their own right. The composition of gas varies from field to field, and its energy content — its calorific or heating value — varies accordingly. Gas is therefore sold not by volume alone but by energy content, commonly measured in British thermal units (Btu), therms, gigajoules, or kilowatt hours, and gas contracts and network codes specify detailed quality specifications, including ranges for calorific value, the Wobbe index (a measure of the interchangeability of gases in burners), and maximum levels of contaminants.

These physical characteristics have several legal consequences. First, the specification of quality and the allocation of responsibility for off-specification gas are central issues in gas contracts and network codes. A seller that delivers gas outside the contractual specification may be in breach, and the buyer may be entitled to reject it, while a transmission system operator may refuse to accept gas that would compromise the safety or integrity of the network. Second, the measurement of gas — volume, pressure, temperature, and composition — requires agreed metering standards and procedures for resolving measurement disputes. Third, because gas from different sources is commingled in pipelines and storage, title to gas is usually a matter of contractual allocation rather than physical identification; the gas a buyer receives is not physically the same gas that its seller delivered. Gas law therefore relies heavily on concepts such as nominations, allocations, and balancing, which translate physical flows into contractual entitlements.

### Natural Monopoly and Network Characteristics

High-pressure transmission pipelines and low-pressure distribution networks exhibit strong economies of scale and are commonly regarded as natural monopolies: it is usually more efficient for a single network to serve a given area than for competing networks to be duplicated. Natural monopoly provides the classic justification for economic regulation: in the absence of competition, the owner of a network could charge monopoly prices or refuse access to competitors in upstream production or downstream supply. Gas law therefore distinguishes between the potentially competitive segments of the supply chain — production, wholesale trading, and retail supply — and the monopoly segments — transmission, distribution, and, in some cases, storage and LNG terminals — which are subject to regulation of tariffs, access, and investment.

### Asset Specificity and the Long-Term Contract

The economics of gas infrastructure are characterised by high asset specificity. A pipeline linking a particular field to a particular market, or an LNG liquefaction plant built to process gas from a particular reservoir, has little value in any alternative use. Once the investment is made, its owner is exposed to opportunistic behaviour by its counterparties — the "hold-up" problem analysed in transaction cost economics by Oliver Williamson and others. A producer that has built a pipeline to a single buyer may find the buyer demanding lower prices once the pipeline is complete; a buyer that has built power stations relying on a single supplier may find the supplier demanding higher prices.

The long-term gas contract emerged as the principal legal device for managing this risk. By committing the buyer to purchase, or pay for, specified quantities of gas over periods of fifteen to twenty-five years, and the seller to deliver them, the long-term contract provides the revenue certainty necessary to finance the infrastructure, while price indexation and price review mechanisms allow the price to adjust to changing market conditions without reopening the fundamental bargain. The long-term contract is thus a form of private governance structure for a relationship in which neither party can easily exit. Its central provisions — take-or-pay, destination restrictions, price indexation, and price review — are examined in sections 6.5 and 6.6.

### Regional Markets and Pricing Systems

Partly because of the costs of transport, gas markets developed historically as regional markets with distinct pricing systems. Four broad systems may be identified.

**Gas-on-gas competition** prevails in North America, the United Kingdom, and much of continental Europe, where liberalised markets with liquid trading hubs determine prices through the interaction of supply and demand. The principal hubs are Henry Hub in Louisiana, which serves as the delivery point for the New York Mercantile Exchange natural gas futures contract; the National Balancing Point (NBP) in Great Britain, a virtual trading point within the National Gas transmission system; and the Title Transfer Facility (TTF) in the Netherlands, which has become the benchmark for continental European gas prices.

**Oil indexation** was the traditional pricing method in continental Europe and remains significant in Asia. Under oil-indexed contracts, the gas price is linked by formula to the prices of competing fuels, typically crude oil or oil products. In Asian LNG contracts, the price has commonly been expressed as a percentage ("slope") of the Japan Customs-Cleared Crude price (the "JCC" or "Japanese Crude Cocktail") or, increasingly, Brent crude, sometimes with an "S-curve" that moderates the effect of very high or very low oil prices. The rationale for oil indexation, which originated in the pricing of Groningen gas in the Netherlands in the 1960s, was the "market value" or "replacement value" principle: gas should be priced at a level that allowed it to compete with the alternative fuel the buyer would otherwise use.

**Regulated pricing** prevails in many producing countries and some importing countries, where governments set gas prices administratively, often below the cost of supply, to support domestic industry and power generation or to protect consumers. Regulated domestic gas prices have been a significant obstacle to gas development in many countries, including Nigeria, where the historically low regulated domestic price discouraged investment in gas supply to the domestic market.

**Spot and short-term LNG pricing** has grown with the expansion of the LNG trade, and Asian spot LNG is now commonly priced by reference to the Japan Korea Marker (JKM), an assessed price published by a price reporting agency, while increasing quantities of LNG from the United States are sold on the basis of Henry Hub prices plus a fixed liquefaction fee.

The International Gas Union's periodic wholesale gas price surveys have documented a long-term shift from oil indexation towards gas-on-gas competition, particularly in Europe, although the extreme price volatility of hub prices during the 2021–2023 crisis led some buyers to reconsider the value of oil-linked contracts as a hedge.

### Gas in the Energy Transition

The role of gas in the energy transition is legally significant because it shapes the policy frameworks within which gas investment takes place. The European Union's Taxonomy Regulation, through a Complementary Climate Delegated Act adopted in 2022, classifies certain gas-fired electricity generation and heat activities as transitional activities that may qualify as environmentally sustainable, subject to stringent emissions thresholds and conditions, including a commitment to switch to renewable or low-carbon gases by 2035. The inclusion of gas (and nuclear) in the taxonomy was controversial and was challenged before the General Court of the European Union by Austria and by environmental organisations; in September 2025 the General Court dismissed Austria's action. The EU's Regulation on the reduction of methane emissions in the energy sector, adopted in 2024, imposes measurement, reporting, and verification obligations on oil, gas, and coal operators, restricts routine venting and flaring, and will progressively apply equivalent monitoring requirements to imported fossil fuels — a measure with significant implications for exporting states, including Nigeria. At the international level, the outcome of the first global stocktake under the Paris Agreement, adopted at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, called on parties to contribute to "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner", while recognising that "transitional fuels can play a role in facilitating the energy transition while ensuring energy security".

For gas-producing developing countries, the transition raises profound questions of equity. African governments, including Nigeria, have argued that gas is essential to expanding energy access, industrialisation, and clean cooking, and that it would be unjust for countries that bear little historical responsibility for climate change to be denied financing for gas development. Nigeria declared 2021–2030 the "Decade of Gas", reflecting a strategy of using its large gas reserves — among the largest in Africa — to drive domestic industrialisation and power generation. The tension between this position and the climate policies of international lenders, which have restricted financing for upstream gas, is examined further in Chapters 18 and 23.

## 6.2 Gas Exploration and Production Regulation

### The Historical Neglect of Gas in Petroleum Legislation

Petroleum legislation was designed historically around oil. Early concessions and petroleum statutes often made little distinction between oil and gas, or treated gas as an incidental by-product. As a result, petroleum regimes frequently failed to provide adequate incentives for gas development. Gas discovered in the course of oil exploration might be reinjected, flared, or left undeveloped because the fiscal terms designed for oil made gas development uneconomic, because there was no domestic market or infrastructure, or because the rights to gas were unclear. The development of gas-specific legal provisions — governing rights to non-associated gas, the fiscal treatment of gas, the obligations of producers to supply gas to the domestic market, and the restriction of flaring — has been a significant feature of petroleum law reform over the past three decades.

### Rights to Gas Under Petroleum Titles

Petroleum titles usually confer rights to both oil and gas within the licence area, but they may differ in their treatment of the two. Some regimes provide for separate gas licences or for distinct terms applicable to non-associated gas discoveries, recognising that gas development requires longer periods for market development and a different fiscal structure. A common device is the extended "gas holding" or "gas retention" period, which allows a licensee that has discovered non-associated gas but cannot yet develop it commercially to retain the discovery area for a defined period while it seeks a market, without being required to relinquish it. Without such provisions, licensees may be forced to relinquish gas discoveries before markets have developed.

Under Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021, the general framework for petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases applies to both oil and gas, but the Act contains specific provisions recognising the distinct characteristics of gas. Holders of petroleum prospecting licences that discover non-associated gas may be granted a period to establish its commerciality before conversion to a lease, and the Act's fiscal provisions treat gas production more favourably than oil, notably by subjecting gas to companies income tax rather than the hydrocarbon tax applicable to crude oil, and by providing reduced royalty rates for gas, with lower rates for gas delivered to the domestic market. These provisions reflect the policy of the Act to promote the development of gas for domestic use.

### Domestic Gas Supply Obligations

Many gas-producing states impose domestic supply obligations requiring producers to make a proportion of their gas available for the domestic market, often at regulated prices. Such obligations reflect the policy of ensuring that national resources contribute to domestic energy supply and industrial development, rather than being exported exclusively. They may, however, deter investment if the regulated price is below the cost of supply or the export price.

In Nigeria, the Nigerian Gas Master Plan of 2008 and the National Domestic Gas Supply and Pricing Regulations 2008 introduced a domestic gas delivery obligation requiring producers to supply specified volumes of gas to the domestic market. The PIA continues this approach, empowering the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority (NMDPRA) to determine the domestic gas delivery obligation and to set a domestic base price for gas supplied to the strategic sectors (notably power generation) until a willing-buyer, willing-seller market develops. In Western Australia, the Domestic Gas Reservation Policy, introduced in 2006, requires LNG export projects to make gas equivalent to fifteen per cent of their LNG production available to the domestic market, and the federal Australian Domestic Gas Security Mechanism, introduced in 2017, empowers the government to restrict LNG exports where there is a domestic shortfall. In 2022 and 2023, Australia introduced emergency price caps on wholesale gas and a mandatory code of conduct for the east coast gas market in response to the price surge triggered by the European energy crisis. Similar obligations exist in Indonesia, Egypt, Israel, and other producing countries.

Domestic supply obligations raise legal questions in international trade law, since they may function as export restrictions, and in investment law, since their introduction or tightening after an investment is made may affect the investor's legitimate expectations. They are discussed further in Chapter 13 in the context of export restrictions.

### Gas Flaring Regulation

Gas flaring — the burning of associated gas at the wellsite — wastes a valuable resource, emits carbon dioxide and black carbon, and exposes nearby communities to air pollution, noise, heat, and light. The World Bank's Global Gas Flaring Tracker reports that over 140 billion cubic metres of gas are flared globally each year, with Russia, Iraq, Iran, the United States, Venezuela, Algeria, Libya, and Nigeria among the largest flaring countries. Venting — the direct release of unburned gas, predominantly methane — is even more damaging to the climate, since methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide over short time horizons.

Legal approaches to flaring include outright prohibitions with exceptions for safety and emergencies, flare fees or penalties, requirements for gas utilisation plans as a condition of development approval, and mechanisms to allocate flare gas to third parties that can commercialise it. Nigeria's experience with flaring regulation, examined in Chapter 5, illustrates both the difficulties of enforcement and the evolution of policy from prohibition to commercialisation through the Flare Gas (Prevention of Waste and Pollution) Regulations 2018 and the Nigerian Gas Flare Commercialisation Programme. The *Gbemre* case, also discussed in Chapter 5 and Chapter 21, established the human rights dimension of flaring in Nigerian law.

Internationally, the World Bank's Zero Routine Flaring by 2030 Initiative, launched in 2015, has been endorsed by governments, oil companies, and development institutions, which commit not to engage in routine flaring in new oil field developments and to end legacy routine flaring as soon as possible and no later than 2030. The Global Methane Pledge, launched at COP26 in 2021, commits participating countries to collective action to reduce global anthropogenic methane emissions by at least thirty per cent from 2020 levels by 2030. The Oil and Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 (OGMP 2.0), administered by the United Nations Environment Programme, has established a reporting framework for company methane emissions that has been incorporated by reference into the EU Methane Regulation. These instruments are largely voluntary, but they are increasingly being translated into binding domestic and regional law.

### Gas Processing and Natural Gas Liquids

Gas processing — the separation of natural gas liquids, removal of impurities, and conditioning of gas to pipeline specification — occupies a regulatory borderland between upstream and midstream activity. In some regimes, processing facilities associated with a specific field are treated as part of upstream operations under the petroleum licence; in others, they require separate licences. The PIA treats gas processing as a midstream activity requiring a licence from the NMDPRA, distinct from the upstream licence regulated by the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission (NUPRC). The classification affects the applicable fiscal regime, the identity of the regulator, and the rights of third parties to access the facilities. Access to upstream infrastructure, including processing facilities, by third parties is examined in section 6.3.

### Fiscal Treatment of Gas

Because gas development is typically less profitable than oil development, owing to higher transport costs, lower prices per unit of energy, and the need for market development, many petroleum fiscal regimes provide more favourable terms for gas. Common incentives include lower royalty rates, exemption from or reduced rates of resource rent taxes, accelerated depreciation for gas infrastructure, and tax holidays for gas utilisation projects. Nigeria's Associated Gas Framework Agreement of 1992 and the incentives for gas utilisation in the Companies Income Tax Act, now carried forward in modified form in the PIA, are examples. The design of gas fiscal incentives must balance the need to make gas development commercially viable against the risk of conferring windfall gains on projects that would have proceeded without them, and against the need to avoid distortions between oil and gas production that might encourage artificial classification of production. Fiscal regimes generally are examined in Chapter 16.
