## 8.5 Supply Chain Due Diligence

### The Concept of Supply Chain Due Diligence

Mineral supply chains are long, complex, and opaque. A cobalt atom in a smartphone battery may have been mined by an artisanal miner in the Congolese Copperbelt, sold to a trader at a local depot, processed at a refinery in the DRC or China, converted into cathode material in China, South Korea, or Japan, incorporated into a battery cell, and assembled into a device sold by a brand headquartered in the United States or Europe. At each stage, minerals from different sources are mixed, and the connection between the final product and the conditions of extraction is obscured. Supply chain due diligence is the process by which companies identify, assess, prevent, mitigate, and account for the adverse impacts associated with their supply chains, including impacts caused by their suppliers and their suppliers' suppliers.

The concept derives from the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011), examined in Chapter 21, which provide that business enterprises should carry out human rights due diligence to identify, prevent, mitigate, and account for how they address their adverse human rights impacts, including impacts directly linked to their operations, products, or services by their business relationships. In the mineral sector, due diligence was first developed in response to the financing of armed conflict in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo by the trade in tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold (the "3TG" or "conflict minerals").

### The OECD Due Diligence Guidance

The **OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas**, first adopted in 2010 and approved as an OECD Council Recommendation in 2011 (now in its third edition, 2016), is the leading international standard for mineral supply chain due diligence. It establishes a five-step framework:

1. **Establish strong company management systems**, including a supply chain policy consistent with the model policy in Annex II of the Guidance, internal structures to support due diligence, a system of controls and transparency over the supply chain (including chain of custody or traceability systems), and a grievance mechanism.
2. **Identify and assess risks in the supply chain**, including by mapping the supply chain and assessing the conditions of extraction, trade, handling, and export.
3. **Design and implement a strategy to respond to identified risks**, which may involve continuing trade while pursuing measurable risk mitigation, temporarily suspending trade, or disengaging from a supplier.
4. **Carry out independent third-party audits** of due diligence practices at identified points in the supply chain, notably smelters and refiners.
5. **Report annually** on supply chain due diligence.

The Guidance distinguishes between "upstream" companies (from the mine to smelters and refiners) and "downstream" companies (from smelters and refiners to retailers), recognising that smelters and refiners constitute a "choke point" in the supply chain through which minerals from many sources pass and at which audits can be most effectively focused. Annex II identifies the most serious risks — including torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment, forced or compulsory labour, the worst forms of child labour, gross human rights violations, war crimes, direct or indirect support to non-state armed groups, and bribery and money laundering — and the expected responses to them. Although originally developed for 3TG, the Guidance applies to all minerals, and supplements have addressed specific issues. The OECD has also developed guidance for responsible business conduct in other sectors, including the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct (2018).

### Mandatory Due Diligence: Dodd-Frank Section 1502

The first mandatory mineral due diligence regime was **section 1502 of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010** in the United States, which required companies listed on United States exchanges to determine whether their products contained 3TG originating in the DRC or adjoining countries and, if so, to exercise due diligence on the source and chain of custody of those minerals and to file a report with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The SEC's implementing rule, adopted in 2012, required companies to describe products that had not been found to be "DRC conflict free". In *National Association of Manufacturers v SEC* (2014, reaffirmed 2015), the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held that the requirement to describe products as not "DRC conflict free" violated the First Amendment by compelling speech, while upholding other aspects of the rule.[^nam] Section 1502 was praised for raising awareness and driving industry due diligence, but was also criticised for causing a *de facto* embargo on minerals from the eastern DRC, which harmed the livelihoods of artisanal miners without proportionately reducing conflict financing.

[^nam]: *National Association of Manufacturers v Securities and Exchange Commission*, 748 F.3d 359 (DC Cir 2014), reaffirmed on rehearing, 800 F.3d 518 (DC Cir 2015).

### The EU Conflict Minerals Regulation

The **EU Conflict Minerals Regulation** — Regulation (EU) 2017/821, which became fully applicable on 1 January 2021 — requires Union importers of 3TG ores, concentrates, and metals above specified volume thresholds to comply with supply chain due diligence obligations aligned with the OECD Guidance, including management systems, risk management, third-party audits, and disclosure. Unlike Dodd-Frank, the Regulation applies to minerals from any conflict-affected and high-risk area, not only the DRC region, and focuses on upstream importers rather than downstream manufacturers. Member state authorities are responsible for enforcement, and the Commission has published an indicative list of conflict-affected and high-risk areas.

### The EU Batteries Regulation

The **EU Batteries Regulation** — Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 — extends mandatory due diligence to the principal battery minerals. It requires economic operators placing batteries on the Union market, subject to a turnover threshold, to adopt and implement battery due diligence policies in respect of cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, and nickel, and chemical compounds based on them, covering risks related to human rights, labour rights, the environment, and community life, in line with internationally recognised due diligence instruments including the UN Guiding Principles and the OECD Guidance. Operators must have their due diligence policies verified by a notified body and must make information publicly available. The due diligence obligations were originally due to apply from August 2025, but their application was postponed to 2027 by an amendment adopted in 2025 as part of the Commission's simplification agenda. The Regulation also introduces a "battery passport" — an electronic record containing information on each industrial and electric vehicle battery, including its composition, carbon footprint, and recycled content — and requirements on carbon footprint declarations, recycled content, and recycling efficiency, examined in section 8.7.

### General Mandatory Human Rights Due Diligence

Beyond mineral-specific regimes, general mandatory human rights and environmental due diligence laws apply to companies in mineral supply chains. France's Duty of Vigilance Law (*Loi relative au devoir de vigilance*) of 2017 requires large French companies to establish, publish, and implement a vigilance plan identifying and preventing serious violations of human rights, health and safety, and the environment resulting from their activities and those of their subsidiaries, subcontractors, and suppliers. Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (*Lieferkettensorgfaltspflichtengesetz*), in force since 2023, imposes similar obligations on large companies operating in Germany. The EU **Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive** — Directive (EU) 2024/1760 — requires large companies to identify, prevent, mitigate, and remediate adverse human rights and environmental impacts in their own operations, those of their subsidiaries, and those of their business partners in their "chain of activities", and to adopt climate transition plans. The Directive's transposition and application dates were postponed, and its scope and requirements were reduced, through the "Omnibus" simplification package negotiated in 2025. These general regimes are examined in Chapters 21 and 22.

### Import Bans Based on Forced Labour

A further category of supply chain regulation consists of import bans on goods produced with forced labour. In the United States, section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930 prohibits the importation of goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part by forced labour, and the **Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act** of 2021 establishes a rebuttable presumption that goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region of China, or by entities on a designated list, are made with forced labour and are prohibited from importation. The Act has significant implications for supply chains of polysilicon (used in solar panels), aluminium, and battery materials. The EU **Forced Labour Regulation** — Regulation (EU) 2024/3015 — prohibits products made with forced labour from being placed on the Union market or exported from it, with application from December 2027.

### Industry and Multi-Stakeholder Initiatives

Industry initiatives play a central role in implementing due diligence. The **Responsible Minerals Initiative** (RMI) operates the Responsible Minerals Assurance Process, which audits smelters and refiners against standards aligned with the OECD Guidance, and publishes lists of conformant facilities; it has developed standards for cobalt, mica, and other minerals in addition to 3TG. The **London Bullion Market Association** Responsible Gold Guidance requires refiners accredited to the London Good Delivery list to undergo annual audits. The **International Conference on the Great Lakes Region** (ICGLR), an intergovernmental organisation of twelve African states, operates a Regional Certification Mechanism for 3TG under its 2006 Protocol against the Illegal Exploitation of Natural Resources. Initiatives addressing artisanal cobalt include the Fair Cobalt Alliance and the cobalt standard of the DRC's Entreprise Générale du Cobalt.

### Evaluating Due Diligence

Mineral supply chain due diligence has become a central feature of the legal regime of critical minerals, but its effectiveness is debated. Proponents argue that it has improved transparency, reduced the financing of armed groups in some areas, and established expectations of corporate responsibility. Critics argue that due diligence has too often become a box-ticking exercise focused on audits and certifications rather than on actual improvements in conditions at mine sites; that it may lead companies to disengage from high-risk areas, harming the communities that depend on mining rather than improving conditions; that traceability systems are vulnerable to fraud, as illustrated by allegations concerning the laundering of minerals from conflict areas through neighbouring countries; and that the costs fall disproportionately on artisanal and small-scale producers. The design of due diligence regimes that promote responsible engagement rather than disengagement is a key challenge for regulators.

## 8.6 Human Rights and Labour Risks in Mineral Production

### The Spectrum of Risks

The production of critical minerals is associated with a wide spectrum of human rights and labour risks. These include the worst forms of child labour, particularly in artisanal mining; forced labour, including in state-sponsored labour transfer programmes; unsafe working conditions, resulting in deaths and injuries from collapses, accidents, and exposure to toxic substances; violations of the rights of indigenous peoples, including the right to free, prior, and informed consent; forced evictions and inadequate resettlement; violence by public and private security forces; environmental harms affecting the rights to health, water, and an adequate standard of living; and the financing of armed conflict. These risks are examined generally in Chapter 21; this section highlights their particular manifestations in critical mineral supply chains.

### Child Labour and Artisanal Cobalt

In 2016, Amnesty International and African Resources Watch published *"This is what we die for": Human rights abuses in the Democratic Republic of the Congo power the global trade in cobalt*, documenting the use of child labour and hazardous working conditions in artisanal cobalt mining in the DRC and tracing cobalt from artisanal sources through processing companies to battery manufacturers and consumer brands. The report contributed to the development of industry due diligence for cobalt and to the inclusion of cobalt in the EU Batteries Regulation. In *Doe v Apple Inc*, a group of former child miners and the families of deceased child miners from the DRC sued Apple, Alphabet, Dell, Microsoft, and Tesla in the United States, alleging that the companies had knowingly benefited from forced child labour in their cobalt supply chains in violation of the Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act. The District Court dismissed the claims in 2021, and in March 2024 the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit affirmed, holding that purchasing cobalt in the global market did not amount to participation in a "venture" with the suppliers that used forced labour within the meaning of the statute.[^doeapple] The case illustrates the difficulty of establishing the legal liability of downstream companies for abuses in their supply chains under existing law, and the significance of mandatory due diligence legislation in filling that gap.

[^doeapple]: *Doe v Apple Inc*, 96 F.4th 403 (DC Cir 2024).

### Forced Labour and State-Sponsored Labour Programmes

Reports by researchers, journalists, and civil society organisations have documented the involvement of state-sponsored labour transfer programmes in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in the production of polysilicon, aluminium, and other materials used in energy transition technologies. The United Nations Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, in its 2022 assessment of human rights concerns in Xinjiang, concluded that serious human rights violations had been committed and that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention may constitute international crimes. Such programmes raise the risk that solar panels, batteries, and other clean energy products incorporate materials produced with forced labour. The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act and the EU Forced Labour Regulation, examined in section 8.5, are the principal legal responses.

### Indigenous Peoples and Lithium

A large share of known lithium, nickel, copper, and other transition mineral resources is located on or near the lands of indigenous peoples. A study published in *Nature Sustainability* in 2022 estimated that more than half of energy transition mineral projects are located on or near indigenous peoples' lands. In the lithium triangle, indigenous Atacameño (Lickanantay), Colla, and Kolla communities have raised concerns about the impact of brine extraction on scarce water resources, wetlands, and traditional livelihoods, and have challenged lithium projects before national courts and the Inter-American human rights system. In Argentina, communities in the Salinas Grandes and Laguna de Guayatayoc basin have invoked their right to free, prior, and informed consent under ILO Convention No 169 and the Argentine Constitution. In the United States, the Thacker Pass lithium project in Nevada, located near a site of a historic massacre of Paiute people, was challenged by indigenous groups, ranchers, and environmental organisations; the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit in 2023 rejected the challenges to the project's federal approvals.[^thacker] These disputes illustrate the tension between the urgency of the energy transition and the rights of indigenous peoples, which some commentators have described as a risk of "green colonialism" or "green extractivism".

[^thacker]: *Bartell Ranch LLC v McCullough* (9th Cir, July 2023) (memorandum disposition), affirming the District Court of Nevada.

### Labour Conditions in Processing and Manufacturing

Labour risks are not confined to mining. Nickel smelters in Indonesia's industrial parks have experienced fatal accidents, including an explosion at a smelter in the Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park in December 2023 that killed around twenty workers, and reports have documented long working hours, inadequate safety measures, and restrictions on freedom of association. Battery manufacturing and recycling involve exposure to hazardous substances. The ILO's core conventions on freedom of association, forced labour, child labour, discrimination, and occupational safety and health, and the ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work (as amended in 2022 to include a safe and healthy working environment), provide the international framework.

### The UN Secretary-General's Panel

In recognition of the risks associated with the expansion of critical mineral production, the United Nations Secretary-General established in 2024 the **Panel on Critical Energy Transition Minerals**, comprising governments, intergovernmental organisations, industry, and civil society. The Panel's report, published in September 2024, set out seven guiding principles: that human rights must be at the core of all mineral value chains; that the integrity of the planet, its environment, and biodiversity must be safeguarded; that justice and equity must underpin mineral value chains; that development should be fostered through benefit sharing, value addition, and economic diversification; that investment, finance, and trade should be responsible and fair; that transparency, accountability, and anti-corruption measures are necessary to ensure good governance; and that multilateral and international cooperation should underpin global action. The Panel recommended, among other things, the establishment of a high-level expert group to advance the principles, a global traceability, transparency, and accountability framework, and a global mining legacies fund to address abandoned mines. Although not binding, the principles represent the first United Nations-endorsed framework specifically addressed to energy transition minerals.
