## 20.5 Submarine Pipelines and Cables

### Significance

Submarine pipelines and cables form the hidden infrastructure of the global energy and information economy. Subsea gas pipelines connect Norway to continental Europe and the United Kingdom, North Africa to Southern Europe, Russia to Germany and Turkey, and Azerbaijan to Europe through the Southern Gas Corridor. **High-voltage direct current (HVDC) interconnectors** link national electricity systems across seas — for example, NorNed between Norway and the Netherlands, NordLink between Norway and Germany, North Sea Link between Norway and the United Kingdom, and Viking Link between the United Kingdom and Denmark, which entered into operation in 2023 as one of the world's longest land and subsea interconnectors. Export cables connect offshore wind farms to shore. Telecommunications cables carry the overwhelming majority of intercontinental data traffic, on which the digital management of energy systems and markets depends. Proposals for much longer interconnectors — such as the Xlinks project to bring solar and wind power from Morocco to the United Kingdom, and the Great Sea Interconnector between Greece, Cyprus, and Israel — illustrate the ambitions and difficulties of such projects.

### The UNCLOS Framework

The right to lay submarine cables and pipelines is one of the traditional freedoms of the high seas, recognised in the 1958 Geneva Conventions and in UNCLOS. Under **Article 87**, the freedom of the high seas includes the freedom to lay submarine cables and pipelines, subject to Part VI. Under **Article 58**, all states enjoy this freedom in the EEZ of other states. Under **Article 79**, which applies to the continental shelf:

- all states are entitled to lay submarine cables and pipelines on the continental shelf (Article 79(1));
- subject to its right to take reasonable measures for the exploration of the continental shelf, the exploitation of its natural resources, and the prevention, reduction, and control of pollution from pipelines, the coastal state may not impede the laying or maintenance of such cables or pipelines (Article 79(2));
- the **delineation of the course** for the laying of pipelines on the continental shelf is subject to the consent of the coastal state (Article 79(3)) — a requirement that does not apply to cables;
- nothing in Part VI affects the right of the coastal state to establish conditions for cables or pipelines entering its territory or territorial sea, or its jurisdiction over cables and pipelines constructed or used in connection with the exploration of its continental shelf or exploitation of its resources (Article 79(4)); and
- states laying cables or pipelines must have due regard to cables or pipelines already in position, and in particular must not prejudice the possibilities of repairing existing ones (Article 79(5)).

The distinction between pipelines and cables in Article 79(3) reflects the greater environmental risk posed by pipelines. In practice, coastal states also require environmental impact assessments and permits for pipelines and cables crossing their continental shelves, relying on their jurisdiction over the protection of the marine environment in the EEZ and their rights to take reasonable measures against pollution. The extent to which coastal states may regulate cables that are not connected with the exploitation of their resources — for example, international telecommunications cables or interconnectors that merely cross their shelves — is a matter of debate, and practice varies.

In the territorial sea, the coastal state's sovereignty means that cables and pipelines may be laid only with its consent and subject to its conditions. In archipelagic waters, archipelagic states must respect existing submarine cables laid by other states and permit their maintenance and replacement (Article 51(2)).

### Protection against Damage

Articles 113 to 115 of UNCLOS, building on the **Convention for the Protection of Submarine Telegraph Cables** of 1884, require states to adopt laws and regulations providing that the breaking or injury, by a ship flying their flag or by a person subject to their jurisdiction, of a submarine cable or pipeline beneath the high seas, done wilfully or through culpable negligence, is a punishable offence (Article 113); that owners who cause damage to other cables or pipelines in laying or repairing their own bear the cost of repairs (Article 114); and that ship owners who sacrifice anchors or nets to avoid injuring a cable or pipeline are indemnified by the owner of the cable or pipeline (Article 115). These provisions focus on the obligations of flag states and states of nationality and do not themselves confer enforcement jurisdiction on coastal states over foreign ships that damage cables or pipelines in their EEZs or on their continental shelves beyond the territorial sea — a significant gap in the light of recent incidents.

The overwhelming majority of damage to submarine cables results from accidental causes, principally fishing and anchoring, with the industry's International Cable Protection Committee estimating that between 150 and 200 faults occur each year. However, a series of incidents has raised concern about deliberate attacks on submarine infrastructure:

- In **September 2022**, explosions ruptured three of the four strings of the **Nord Stream** and Nord Stream 2 gas pipelines in the Baltic Sea, in the EEZs of Denmark and Sweden. Danish and Swedish investigations concluded that the pipelines had been deliberately sabotaged but were closed without identifying those responsible, while a German investigation continued and led to the arrest of suspects.
- In **October 2023**, the Balticconnector gas pipeline between Finland and Estonia and telecommunications cables were damaged; Finnish investigators attributed the damage to the dragging of an anchor by a Hong Kong-flagged container ship.
- In **November and December 2024**, further cables in the Baltic Sea and the Estlink 2 power interconnector between Finland and Estonia were damaged, the latter allegedly by the anchor of a tanker associated with the "shadow fleet" carrying Russian oil, which Finnish authorities detained. In January 2025, NATO launched the "Baltic Sentry" activity to enhance the surveillance and protection of critical undersea infrastructure.

These incidents raise difficult legal questions: whether coastal states may board and detain foreign ships suspected of damaging infrastructure in their EEZs; whether the deliberate destruction of infrastructure may constitute a use of force or an armed attack, and how attribution may be established; the adequacy of criminal laws under Article 113; and the responsibility of flag states. States and the European Union have responded with action plans on cable security, enhanced surveillance, and proposals for stronger legal frameworks, and some commentators have proposed a new international instrument on the protection of critical submarine infrastructure. The security of energy infrastructure, including its protection in armed conflict and against hybrid threats, is examined in Chapter 25.

### Pipeline Treaties and Regional Regimes

Transboundary submarine pipelines are often governed by **intergovernmental agreements** between the states concerned, supplemented by host government agreements with project sponsors, as examined in Chapter 6. Examples include the agreements between Norway and the United Kingdom concerning the Frigg and Langeled pipelines, and the intergovernmental agreements for the Trans Adriatic Pipeline among Greece, Albania, and Italy. Such agreements address jurisdiction over the pipeline, the applicable technical and safety standards, taxation, access, and dispute settlement.

The **West African Gas Pipeline**, which transports Nigerian gas to Benin, Togo, and Ghana along a largely offshore route of around 680 kilometres, is governed by a **Treaty** concluded in 2003 among the four states, which established the West African Gas Pipeline Authority, together with an International Project Agreement with the West African Gas Pipeline Company. The pipeline has suffered from interruptions of supply, including damage by a ship's anchor off Lomé in 2012, which illustrated its vulnerability. The proposed **Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline**, intended to run around 5,600 kilometres or more, largely offshore along the West African coast and connecting to Europe, has been the subject of memoranda of understanding between Nigeria, Morocco, ECOWAS, and transit states since 2016, and of front-end engineering studies, but its feasibility and financing remain uncertain. Such a project would require a multilateral legal framework addressing transit through many maritime zones, as examined in Chapter 6.

The **Convention on the Legal Status of the Caspian Sea**, signed in Aktau in 2018 by the five littoral states, provides that states may lay submarine cables and pipelines on the seabed, provided that their projects comply with environmental standards, and that the route is to be determined in agreement with the state through whose seabed sector the pipeline is to be laid — a provision relevant to proposals for a Trans-Caspian gas pipeline from Turkmenistan to Azerbaijan.

### The Nord Stream 2 Saga

The legal history of the **Nord Stream 2** pipeline illustrates the interaction of the law of the sea, EU law, investment law, and geopolitics. Because the pipeline crossed the territorial seas and EEZs of Russia, Finland, Sweden, Denmark, and Germany, it required the consent of each state to its route. Denmark, which had refused a permit for the route through its territorial sea, adopted legislation in 2017 allowing the refusal of permits on foreign policy and security grounds, and ultimately permitted a route through its EEZ in 2019. The European Union amended the Gas Directive in 2019 (Directive (EU) 2019/692) to extend its unbundling and third-party access requirements to gas pipelines between member states and third countries, within the territory and territorial sea of the member state. Nord Stream 2 AG challenged the amendment before the EU courts and brought a claim against the European Union under the Energy Charter Treaty, alleging discrimination. The pipeline was completed in 2021 but never entered into operation: Germany suspended its certification in February 2022 before the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and one of its strings was destroyed in the September 2022 explosions. The project illustrates how the freedom to lay pipelines is conditioned by coastal state consent, regional regulation, and political considerations.

## 20.6 Deep-Seabed Minerals Regulation

### The Area and the Common Heritage of Mankind

The **Area** comprises the seabed and ocean floor and subsoil beyond the limits of national jurisdiction (Article 1(1)(1)), that is, beyond the outer limits of the continental shelves of coastal states. Under Article 136, the Area and its resources are the **common heritage of mankind**. No state may claim or exercise sovereignty or sovereign rights over any part of the Area or its resources, and no state or person may appropriate any part of them (Article 137(1)). All rights in the resources of the Area are vested in mankind as a whole, on whose behalf the **International Seabed Authority** acts (Article 137(2)). Activities in the Area are to be carried out for the benefit of mankind as a whole, taking into particular consideration the interests and needs of developing states, and the ISA must provide for the equitable sharing of financial and other economic benefits derived from activities in the Area (Article 140). The Area is to be used exclusively for peaceful purposes (Article 141).

The resources of the Area include three principal types of mineral deposit, each of interest as a potential source of minerals for the energy transition:

- **polymetallic nodules**, potato-sized concretions lying on the abyssal plains at depths of four to six thousand metres, containing manganese, nickel, copper, and cobalt, most notably in the **Clarion-Clipperton Zone** of the Pacific Ocean;
- **polymetallic sulphides**, formed at hydrothermal vents along mid-ocean ridges, containing copper, zinc, gold, and silver; and
- **cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts**, formed on the flanks of seamounts, containing cobalt, nickel, manganese, and rare earth elements.

### The Original Part XI and the 1994 Agreement

The original Part XI established a parallel system under which mining would be undertaken both by the ISA's operating arm, **the Enterprise**, and by states and private entities sponsored by states, with mandatory transfer of technology to the Enterprise and developing states, production limitations to protect land-based producers, and a review conference. These provisions, reflecting the New International Economic Order, were unacceptable to the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, and other industrialised states, which refused to ratify the Convention.

The **1994 Agreement relating to the Implementation of Part XI**, negotiated under the auspices of the UN Secretary-General, substantially modified the regime, providing that its provisions and Part XI are to be interpreted and applied together as a single instrument and that, in the event of inconsistency, the Agreement prevails. It abolished mandatory technology transfer and production limitations, provided that the Enterprise would conduct its initial operations through joint ventures and operate in accordance with sound commercial principles, established a Finance Committee, gave major contributors a decisive role in decision-making through a chambered voting system in the Council, and provided for the application of market principles. The 1994 Agreement enabled the entry into force of UNCLOS with broad participation.

### The ISA and the Mining Code

The ISA, whose organs include the **Assembly** of all members, the thirty-six-member **Council**, which is its executive organ, the **Legal and Technical Commission**, the **Finance Committee**, and the **Secretariat**, regulates prospecting, exploration, and exploitation in the Area through rules, regulations, and procedures collectively known as the **Mining Code**. The ISA has adopted regulations on prospecting and exploration for polymetallic nodules (2000, revised 2013), polymetallic sulphides (2010), and cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts (2012), and has granted around thirty exploration contracts, each for fifteen years with possible extensions, to state enterprises and private companies sponsored by states including China, Russia, India, the Republic of Korea, Japan, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Belgium, and several Pacific island states — Nauru, Tonga, Kiribati, and the Cook Islands — in partnership with private companies.

Exploitation, however, requires the adoption of **exploitation regulations**, which have been under negotiation in the ISA Council since 2014. The negotiations address environmental protection, including environmental thresholds and regional environmental management plans; the payment mechanism and royalty rates; the equitable sharing of benefits; inspection, compliance, and enforcement; and the role of the Enterprise.

### The Two-Year Rule and the Moratorium Debate

In June 2021, **Nauru**, which sponsors Nauru Ocean Resources Inc., a subsidiary of The Metals Company, invoked **Section 1, paragraph 15 of the Annex to the 1994 Agreement**, which provides that where a member state notifies the Council that an entity it sponsors intends to apply for approval of a plan of work for exploitation, the Council must complete the adoption of the relevant rules, regulations, and procedures within two years, and that if it fails to do so, it must nonetheless consider and provisionally approve a plan of work based on the provisions of the Convention and any rules, regulations, and procedures that the Council may have adopted provisionally. The two-year period expired in July 2023 without the adoption of exploitation regulations, and the Council decided that the commercial exploitation of mineral resources should not be carried out until such regulations had been adopted, while continuing negotiations with a view to their adoption.

Meanwhile, a growing number of states — more than thirty by 2025, including France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Canada, Brazil, Chile, and several Pacific states — called for a **moratorium**, precautionary pause, or ban on deep-seabed mining until its environmental impacts are adequately understood and effective regulation is in place. Scientists have warned that mining would cause the irreversible loss of poorly understood deep-sea biodiversity and ecosystem functions, including through the destruction of habitats, sediment plumes, and noise. Proponents argue that seabed minerals could supply critical minerals with lower social and environmental impacts than land-based mining, as examined in Chapter 8.

### The Responsibilities of Sponsoring States

In its advisory opinion on the ***Responsibilities and Obligations of States Sponsoring Persons and Entities with respect to Activities in the Area*** (2011), requested by the ISA Council following a proposal from Nauru, the ITLOS Seabed Disputes Chamber held that sponsoring states have two kinds of obligation: a "responsibility to ensure" that sponsored contractors comply with the terms of their contracts and the Convention, which is an obligation of **due diligence** — an obligation of conduct requiring the adoption of laws, regulations, and administrative measures that are reasonably appropriate; and **direct obligations**, including to apply a precautionary approach, to apply best environmental practices, to ensure the availability of recourse for compensation, and to conduct environmental impact assessments. The Chamber held that these obligations apply equally to developed and developing sponsoring states, to prevent the emergence of "sponsoring states of convenience". The opinion was significant not only for seabed mining but also for the development of the law on due diligence and the precautionary approach, and it prompted states, including the United Kingdom, Belgium, Germany, Singapore, Nauru, and Tonga, to adopt sponsoring state legislation.

### The United States and Unilateral Licensing

The United States, as a non-party to UNCLOS, has maintained under the **Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act** of 1980 a domestic system for licensing exploration and permitting commercial recovery by US citizens beyond national jurisdiction, administered by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. In April 2025, the President issued an executive order directing the expedited processing of applications under the Act and the development of seabed mineral resources within and beyond US jurisdiction, and The Metals Company, through a US subsidiary, applied for a commercial recovery permit in respect of an area of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. These steps were criticised by the ISA, by many states parties, and by commentators as inconsistent with the common heritage principle, which many regard as reflecting customary international law binding on all states. The ISA Council took steps to examine the position of contractors that might pursue exploitation outside the ISA framework. The controversy raises fundamental questions about the authority of the ISA, the customary status of the common heritage principle, and the relationship between treaty regimes and unilateral action by non-parties.

### Seabed Mining within National Jurisdiction

Seabed mining may also take place within national jurisdiction, under national law. The failure of the **Solwara 1** project in the territorial waters of Papua New Guinea, whose developer, Nautilus Minerals, became insolvent in 2019, illustrated the commercial and regulatory risks. The **Cook Islands** has granted exploration licences for nodules in its EEZ. **Norway** decided in January 2024 to open part of its continental shelf in the Norwegian and Greenland Seas to mineral exploration, but the first licensing round was postponed in December 2024 as part of a political agreement on the budget. Seabed mining within national jurisdiction is subject to the obligations of Part XII of UNCLOS, including Article 208, which requires coastal states to adopt laws to prevent, reduce, and control pollution from seabed activities subject to their jurisdiction that are no less effective than international rules, standards, and recommended practices.
