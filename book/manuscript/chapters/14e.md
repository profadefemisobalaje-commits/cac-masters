## 14.8 Investment Treaty Reform and the Energy Transition

### Investment Law and Climate Policy

The relationship between investment law and climate policy has become one of the most pressing issues in international economic law. The energy transition requires states to phase out fossil fuel infrastructure, to cancel or refuse permits for new fossil fuel projects, to reform energy markets, and to reallocate resources towards clean energy — measures that may adversely affect existing investments and give rise to claims under investment treaties. At the same time, the transition requires enormous private investment in clean energy, which investment protection may encourage by reducing political and regulatory risk. The challenge for reform is to preserve the protective function of investment law for clean energy investment, while ensuring that it does not impede, or impose unaffordable costs on, legitimate climate action.

### Claims Arising from Fossil Fuel Phase-Out

Several investment claims have arisen from measures phasing out fossil fuels or restricting fossil fuel projects.

- In *RWE AG and RWE Eemshaven Holding II BV v Netherlands* and *Uniper SE and others v Netherlands*, brought under the ECT in 2021, German energy companies claimed compensation for the Dutch law prohibiting the use of coal for electricity generation from 2030. The claims were brought after the companies had recently commissioned new coal plants. The Dutch courts rejected the companies' domestic challenges to the law, and both claims were withdrawn in 2022, Uniper's in the context of its rescue by the German government.
- In *Rockhopper Italia SpA and others v Italy*, brought under the ECT, a British company claimed compensation for Italy's refusal to grant a production concession for the Ombrina Mare offshore oil field following the reintroduction of a ban on new oil and gas activities within twelve nautical miles of the coast. In 2022, the tribunal awarded the claimants around EUR 190 million plus interest. An ICSID annulment committee annulled the award in 2025.
- In *Westmoreland Coal Co v Canada*, a US coal company challenged Alberta's phase-out of coal-fired electricity generation, which provided transition payments to the owners of coal plants but not to the claimant, a supplier of coal. The claim was dismissed on jurisdictional grounds in 2022.
- In *TC Energy Corp and TransCanada Pipelines Ltd v United States*, the Canadian sponsor of the Keystone XL pipeline claimed more than USD 15 billion following the revocation of the pipeline's presidential permit in 2021. In 2024, the tribunal dismissed the claim for lack of jurisdiction, holding that the USMCA's legacy provisions did not extend to measures adopted after NAFTA's termination.

These cases illustrate the potential for fossil fuel investors to use investment treaties to challenge climate measures, and the debate about whether such claims are a legitimate protection of property rights against abrupt policy change or an illegitimate constraint on climate action. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change noted in its Sixth Assessment Report that international investment agreements may be used by fossil fuel companies to block the phase-out of fossil fuel infrastructure, and the risk of "regulatory chill" has been widely discussed. On the other hand, tribunals have generally recognised the right of states to regulate in the public interest, and fossil fuel investors have not uniformly prevailed.

### The Modernisation of the Energy Charter Treaty

The ECT, negotiated in the early 1990s with the primary objective of protecting Western investment in the energy sectors of the former Soviet Union, came to be regarded by many as inconsistent with the objectives of the Paris Agreement, because it protects investments in fossil fuels on the same terms as investments in clean energy, and because it has been used extensively to challenge regulatory changes in EU member states. In 2017, the Energy Charter Conference launched a process of modernisation of the Treaty, and negotiations began in 2020. The principal elements of the modernised Treaty, agreed in principle in 2022 and adopted by the Energy Charter Conference in December 2024, include:

- **Flexibility mechanism for fossil fuels.** Contracting parties may exclude from investment protection new investments in fossil fuels, and phase out protection for existing investments in fossil fuels after a transitional period. The European Union and the United Kingdom used this mechanism to exclude fossil fuel investments from protection in their territories.
- **Clarified standards.** The FET standard is defined by an exhaustive list of breaches, including denial of justice, fundamental breach of due process, manifest arbitrariness, targeted discrimination, and abusive treatment. Indirect expropriation is clarified, and the right to regulate in pursuit of legitimate policy objectives, including climate change mitigation and adaptation, is expressly affirmed.
- **Definitions.** The definitions of "investor" and "investment" are narrowed, including through a requirement of substantial business activities to address treaty shopping.
- **Expanded scope.** The Treaty's coverage is extended to new technologies, including hydrogen, ammonia, synthetic fuels, and carbon capture, utilisation, and storage.
- **Procedural reforms.** These include provisions on frivolous claims, security for costs, third-party funding, and transparency.

Amendments to the Treaty were provisionally applied from September 2025 by contracting parties that did not object.

### Withdrawal from the Energy Charter Treaty

Before the modernisation was adopted, several states concluded that it did not go far enough and announced their withdrawal from the ECT. Italy withdrew in 2016, for reasons unrelated to climate policy. From 2022, France, Germany, Poland, Luxembourg, Slovenia, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Denmark, Ireland, and the United Kingdom announced or completed their withdrawal. In 2024, the European Union and Euratom notified their withdrawal, which took effect in 2025, while the Council authorised the remaining EU member states that wished to do so to approve the modernised Treaty.

Withdrawal from the ECT does not immediately terminate its effect. Under Article 47(3), the Treaty's provisions continue to apply to investments made in the territory of a withdrawing party, or by investors of a withdrawing party in the territory of other parties, for a period of **twenty years** from the date when the withdrawal takes effect — the so-called **"sunset clause"**. Withdrawing states have therefore sought to neutralise the sunset clause. In 2024, the European Union and most of its member states signed an inter se agreement on the interpretation of the ECT, confirming their view, consistent with the judgment of the Court of Justice in *Komstroy* (2021), that Article 26 does not apply to intra-EU disputes and that the sunset clause does not apply to intra-EU relations. Whether such an inter se agreement can bind arbitral tribunals, or affect the rights of investors from third states, is contested.

### UNCITRAL Working Group III and the Multilateral Investment Court

Since 2017, **UNCITRAL Working Group III** has considered the reform of investor–state dispute settlement, identifying concerns relating to the consistency, coherence, predictability, and correctness of arbitral decisions; the independence, impartiality, and diversity of arbitrators; and the costs and duration of proceedings. Its work has produced several instruments:

- a **Code of Conduct for Arbitrators** in International Investment Dispute Resolution, adopted in 2023, which addresses independence and impartiality, disclosure, and limits on double-hatting;
- a **Code of Conduct for Judges** in International Investment Dispute Resolution, adopted in 2023;
- a **Statute of the Advisory Centre on International Investment Dispute Resolution**, adopted in 2024, to provide legal assistance and capacity-building to developing states and least developed countries.

Discussions continue on more structural reforms, including a **standing multilateral investment court** with an appellate mechanism, championed by the European Union, and mechanisms for the selection and appointment of tribunal members. The EU has included a two-tier Investment Court System in its agreements with Canada (CETA), Viet Nam, Singapore, Mexico, and Chile.

### New Generation Treaties

States have increasingly adopted "new generation" investment treaties and model treaties that seek to rebalance investor protection and the right to regulate. Common features include:

- preambular references to sustainable development and the right to regulate;
- narrower definitions of investment and investor;
- FET clauses limited to an exhaustive list of breaches or tied to the customary minimum standard;
- annexes clarifying indirect expropriation;
- general exceptions modelled on Article XX of the GATT;
- exclusion of MFN for dispute settlement;
- obligations on investors relating to corporate social responsibility, human rights, environment, and anti-corruption;
- limits on ISDS, including exhaustion of local remedies, short limitation periods, or the replacement of ISDS by state-to-state arbitration or domestic remedies.

Examples include the Netherlands Model BIT (2019), which provides that tribunals may take into account non-compliance by investors with the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights and the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises in determining compensation; the Indian Model BIT (2015); the SADC Model BIT (2012); and the Morocco–Nigeria BIT (2016). Some commentators have proposed "climate-aligned" investment treaties that would deny protection to new fossil fuel investments, provide enhanced protection for clean energy investments, and include obligations on home and host states to promote climate-friendly investment.

### Implications for Africa

African states face distinctive choices in investment treaty reform. On the one hand, they need substantial foreign investment to develop their energy resources, expand electricity access, and capture the opportunities of the energy transition, including in critical minerals and green hydrogen. This creates an incentive to offer credible protection to investors and reduce the cost of capital. On the other hand, African states have been respondents in significant energy and mining arbitrations — including the *Process and Industrial Developments (P&ID) v Nigeria* commercial arbitration, in which an award of around USD 11 billion arising from a gas processing contract was set aside by the English High Court in 2023 on the ground that it had been obtained by fraud, examined in Chapter 26 — and they are concerned about the constraints of investment treaties on their regulatory autonomy and the burden of awards on their public finances. The AfCFTA Protocol on Investment, the ECOWAS Supplementary Act, and new generation treaties such as the Morocco–Nigeria BIT represent an African approach that seeks to balance these concerns, emphasising investor obligations, the right to regulate, and sustainable development. The design of investment protection in Africa's energy transition is a subject of considerable importance for doctoral research.

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise: The Republic of Azuri Coal Phase-Out and Gas Windfall Levy (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
*This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The Republic of Azuri, the Kingdom of Westria, Westria Power AG, Meridian Gas BV, and all other entities named are fictional.*

The Republic of Azuri is a party to the ICSID Convention and to a 2005 BIT with the Kingdom of Westria. The BIT contains an unqualified FET clause, an umbrella clause, a standard expropriation clause, and ISDS under the ICSID Convention or UNCITRAL Rules, with a six-month cooling-off period and no exceptions for taxation or environmental measures. Azuri is also a contracting party to the original (unmodernised) Energy Charter Treaty. It has not ratified the modernised Treaty and has not notified withdrawal.

**The coal plant.** In 2016, Westria Power AG (HYPOTHETICAL) commissioned a 1,200 MW coal-fired power plant in Azuri. It operates under a twenty-five-year PPA with the state utility. The PPA contains an economic equilibrium clause covering "any change in law that materially increases the Seller's costs". In 2025, Azuri enacted the Clean Air and Climate Act, which prohibits the generation of electricity from coal after 2030. The Act offers compensation for unrecovered book value of investments, but not for lost future profits. It also exempts a coal plant owned by a domestic state-owned company until 2035 "for reasons of security of supply".

**The gas levy.** Meridian Gas BV (HYPOTHETICAL) is a company incorporated in Westria. Its ultimate parent is incorporated in a third state with no investment treaty with Azuri. Meridian holds a production sharing contract with a stabilisation clause freezing "the fiscal regime" for fifteen years from 2015. In 2022, Meridian's parent restructured its holding of the PSC through Meridian. In the same year, Azuri imposed a "temporary solidarity levy" of 33 per cent on the profits of gas producers above 120 per cent of their 2018–2021 average profits.

1. Advise Westria Power on its potential claims under the BIT and the ECT, including FET, national treatment (considering the exemption for the state-owned plant), expropriation, and the umbrella clause. What is the significance of the economic equilibrium clause, and of the compensation offered under the Act?
2. Advise Azuri on its defences, including the police powers doctrine, the right to regulate, the relevance of its obligations under the Paris Agreement, and the reasonableness of the investor's expectations in 2016.
3. Advise Meridian on jurisdiction, including whether the 2022 restructuring could be characterised as an abuse of process, and on the merits of a claim relating to the solidarity levy, with reference to the stabilisation clause and the taxation provisions of the ECT.
4. Azuri's government is considering withdrawing from the ECT and terminating the Westria BIT. Advise it on the legal consequences, including the sunset clauses, and on alternative strategies, including renegotiation and an interpretive agreement with Westria.

::: summary
- Energy investment is capital-intensive, immobile, and politically salient, and is subject to the "obsolescing bargain". This explains the predominance of energy disputes in investment arbitration.
- Foreign investors are protected by host state law, by contracts with the state, and by more than 2,000 BITs, the ECT, and regional instruments. Threshold questions of "investment", "investor", and nationality planning determine access to treaty protection, and restructuring when a dispute is foreseeable may be an abuse of process.
- The FET standard protects legitimate expectations, particularly those based on specific commitments, against arbitrariness, lack of transparency and due process, bad faith, and denial of justice. Tribunals balance investor expectations against the state's right to regulate.
- National treatment and MFN protect against nationality-based discrimination in like circumstances. The use of MFN to import procedural provisions from other treaties remains contested.
- Treaties prohibit direct and indirect expropriation without compensation. The boundary between indirect expropriation and non-compensable regulation is drawn using effects-based, police powers, and proportionality approaches, increasingly codified in treaty annexes. *Yukos* and the Venezuelan cases illustrate the scale of modern expropriation disputes.
- Stabilisation clauses interact with treaty standards through umbrella clauses and legitimate expectations. The Argentine gas cases reveal the significance and inconsistency of the necessity defence, and contemporary practice limits stabilisation to protect the right to regulate.
- ISDS rests on "arbitration without privity". Preconditions, the distinction between contract and treaty claims, counterclaims, and transparency reforms shape its operation, and legitimacy concerns have prompted diverse state responses.
- The energy transition has generated claims challenging fossil fuel phase-outs. The ECT has been modernised and abandoned by the EU and many member states, subject to its sunset clause. UNCITRAL Working Group III and new generation treaties seek to rebalance the regime.
:::
