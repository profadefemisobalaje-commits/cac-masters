## 28.4 Socio-Legal and Empirical Research

### Law in Action

**Socio-legal research** studies law as a social phenomenon, investigating how legal rules and institutions operate in practice, how they are experienced by those subject to them, and what effects they have. It responds to the limitation of doctrinal research identified above: the gap between **law in the books** and **law in action**, a distinction drawn by Roscoe Pound in 1910. In the energy and resource sector, this gap is often the most important object of inquiry. Nigeria's prohibition of routine gas flaring dates from 1984, yet flaring continued for decades; environmental impact assessment has been mandatory since 1992, yet its quality and influence vary widely; host community development trusts were established by the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, but their effects on community welfare and conflict depend on their implementation. Understanding why laws succeed or fail requires evidence of how they operate in practice, which doctrinal analysis alone cannot provide.

### Themes of Socio-Legal Research in Energy and Resources

Socio-legal research in energy and natural resources has addressed themes including: **implementation and compliance** — the factors that explain whether companies comply with environmental, local content, and transparency obligations, and whether regulators enforce them; **regulatory capture and governance** — the relationships between regulators, companies, and political actors; **access to justice** — the experience of communities seeking remedies for pollution through courts and grievance mechanisms; **legal pluralism** — the interaction of state law with customary land tenure and community norms in resource areas, as examined in Chapter 21; **the resource curse** — the relationship between resource wealth, institutions, and development outcomes, examined in Chapter 23; **energy justice** — the distribution of the benefits and burdens of energy systems, also examined in Chapter 23; **the political economy of reform** — why some reforms, such as the Petroleum Industry Act, take decades to enact, and how interests shape their content; and **the effects of transparency** — whether initiatives such as the EITI change the behaviour of governments and companies.

### Research Designs

Socio-legal research employs a range of **research designs**:

1. **Case studies** examine a single case or a small number of cases in depth — a particular community, project, regulatory agency, or legal reform — using multiple sources of evidence. Case studies are well suited to understanding complex processes in context and generating theory, and they predominate in socio-legal research on energy and resources. Their generalisability is limited, but can be enhanced by careful case selection (typical, deviant, critical, or most-likely and least-likely cases) and by **analytic generalisation** to theory rather than statistical generalisation to populations, as Robert Yin has emphasised.
2. **Comparative case studies** examine several cases to identify the conditions associated with particular outcomes — for example, comparing host communities where development trusts have and have not reduced conflict.
3. **Process tracing** reconstructs the causal processes linking causes and outcomes within a case, using detailed evidence of sequences of events and decisions — for example, tracing how the political bargaining over the Petroleum Industry Bill shaped the fiscal provisions of the final Act.
4. **Surveys** collect standardised data from samples of respondents — for example, surveys of community members' perceptions of benefit-sharing, or of investors' perceptions of regulatory risk.
5. **Experimental and quasi-experimental designs** estimate causal effects by comparing outcomes between groups exposed and not exposed to an intervention; true experiments are rare in legal research, but **natural experiments** and quasi-experimental methods such as difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity are increasingly used to evaluate the effects of laws and policies, such as the effect of mining royalties or community development agreements on local welfare.
6. **Ethnography** involves the researcher's immersion in a social setting over an extended period, observing and participating in its life, to understand the meanings and practices of its members — as in anthropological studies of oil communities in the Niger Delta and of corporate social responsibility practices in mining companies.

### Data Sources

Socio-legal researchers draw on diverse **data sources**: **documents**, including legislation, regulatory decisions, court records, company reports, EITI reports, parliamentary debates, and media reports; **interviews** with officials, company representatives, community members, lawyers, and NGOs; **observation** of hearings, consultations, and community meetings; **surveys**; and **administrative and statistical data**, such as data on production, revenues, spills, flaring, electricity access, and court caseloads. Energy researchers increasingly use **satellite and remote sensing data** — for example, satellite observations of gas flaring by the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the World Bank's Global Gas Flaring Reduction Partnership, of night-time lights as a proxy for electricity access, and of deforestation and land use change around mines — which provide independent evidence that can be compared with official reports.

### Challenges of Empirical Research in the Energy Sector

Empirical research in the energy and resource sector faces practical challenges. **Access** to companies and government officials may be difficult, as these actors may be reluctant to discuss sensitive matters; researchers may need to build relationships of trust, use intermediaries, and offer confidentiality. **Security** risks may arise in conflict-affected resource areas, such as parts of the Niger Delta, the Sahel, or the eastern DRC, requiring careful risk assessment and planning. **Power imbalances** between researchers and participants, particularly vulnerable communities, raise ethical issues addressed in section 28.6. **Data quality** may be poor, with incomplete or unreliable official statistics, and **positionality** — the researcher's identity and relationship to the research setting — may influence access and interpretation. These challenges do not preclude empirical research, but require careful design, ethical reflection, and transparency about limitations.

## 28.5 Qualitative and Quantitative Research Approaches

### The Distinction

Research methods may be broadly classified as **qualitative** or **quantitative**. **Qualitative** methods collect and analyse non-numerical data — texts, interview transcripts, observations — to understand meanings, processes, and contexts, and are associated with interpretivist paradigms. **Quantitative** methods collect and analyse numerical data to measure variables, describe distributions, and test hypotheses about relationships among variables, and are associated with post-positivist paradigms. **Mixed methods** research combines qualitative and quantitative methods within a single study. The choice of approach should be driven by the research question: questions about meanings, experiences, and processes generally call for qualitative methods; questions about prevalence, magnitude, and statistical relationships call for quantitative methods; and many questions in energy law benefit from both.

### Qualitative Methods

The principal qualitative methods in legal research include:

1. **Semi-structured interviews**, which follow an interview guide but allow flexibility to explore issues raised by the interviewee, and are widely used to obtain the perspectives of officials, practitioners, and community members. Interviewees may be selected by **purposive sampling** (selecting those with relevant knowledge), **snowball sampling** (asking interviewees to suggest others), or **theoretical sampling** (selecting cases to develop emerging theory). The number of interviews is typically determined by the principle of **saturation** — the point at which further interviews yield no new insights.
2. **Focus groups**, which bring together small groups of participants to discuss a topic, useful for exploring collective views, for example within host communities.
3. **Documentary analysis**, the systematic analysis of documents, which may include **qualitative content analysis**, identifying themes and categories in texts, and **discourse analysis**, examining how language constructs meaning and power — for example, how corporate sustainability reports or regulatory documents frame the energy transition.
4. **Thematic analysis**, a widely used method for identifying, analysing, and reporting patterns in qualitative data, through coding of data, development of themes, and their interpretation, as systematised by Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke.
5. **Grounded theory**, associated with Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss, which develops theory inductively from data through iterative coding and comparison.

Qualitative analysis may be supported by **computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software** (CAQDAS), such as NVivo or ATLAS.ti, which assists in organising and coding data but does not replace the researcher's interpretation. The quality of qualitative research is assessed by criteria such as **credibility**, **transferability**, **dependability**, and **confirmability** (Lincoln and Guba), supported by practices such as triangulation of sources, member checking, thick description, and an audit trail of analytical decisions.

### Quantitative Methods

Quantitative methods in legal research include:

1. **Descriptive statistics**, summarising data on legal phenomena — for example, the number and outcomes of investment arbitrations in the energy sector, the duration of oil spill litigation in Nigerian courts, or the frequency of particular clauses in petroleum contracts.
2. **Systematic content analysis** of legal texts, coding features of large numbers of documents — such as the presence of stabilisation clauses in petroleum agreements, the content of investment treaties, or the reasoning of arbitral awards — to identify patterns and trends. Datasets of investment treaties and awards, such as those compiled by UNCTAD and academic projects, have enabled quantitative studies of treaty design and arbitral outcomes.
3. **Inferential statistics and econometrics**, testing hypotheses about relationships among variables using regression analysis and related techniques — for example, whether investment treaties increase foreign investment in the energy sector, whether resource wealth is associated with weaker institutions, or whether arbitral outcomes are associated with the characteristics of arbitrators.
4. **Computational legal analysis**, using techniques of natural language processing and machine learning to analyse large corpora of legal texts — for example, to classify clauses in contracts or to predict outcomes of cases — an area transformed by advances in artificial intelligence, discussed in Chapter 30.

Quantitative research requires attention to **validity** (whether the measures capture the concepts of interest), **reliability** (whether measurements are consistent), the representativeness of samples, and the distinction between **correlation and causation**. Legal phenomena are often difficult to quantify without losing nuance, and quantitative legal research has been criticised for reducing complex norms to crude variables, as in the debates over the legal origins literature and the World Bank's former *Doing Business* indicators, discontinued in 2021 after an investigation into data irregularities.

### Mixed Methods

**Mixed methods** research combines qualitative and quantitative approaches to exploit their complementary strengths. Common designs include the **convergent** design, in which qualitative and quantitative data are collected in parallel and compared; the **explanatory sequential** design, in which quantitative results are followed by qualitative research to explain them; and the **exploratory sequential** design, in which qualitative findings inform the development of quantitative instruments. A doctoral study of local content regulation in Nigeria might, for example, combine a doctrinal analysis of the Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act 2010, a quantitative analysis of data on Nigerian participation in contracts, and qualitative interviews with officials and contractors to explain the patterns observed. The integration of methods must be planned, and the thesis should explain how the different strands contribute to answering the research question.

### Legal Research Combining Doctrinal and Empirical Methods

For many doctoral researchers in law, the most productive design combines **doctrinal analysis** with **targeted empirical inquiry**. The doctrinal component establishes the legal framework and its interpretation; the empirical component investigates its operation and effects; and the evaluative and normative components draw on both to assess the law and propose reforms. This combination, sometimes called **"empirical legal research"** or **"law in context"**, is particularly well suited to energy and resource law, where the effectiveness of legal frameworks is often the central question. It requires the researcher to acquire competence in empirical methods — through training, collaboration, or supervision — and to be realistic about the scale of empirical work feasible within a doctoral programme.
