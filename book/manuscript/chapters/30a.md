## Introduction

This book has traced the development of international energy and natural resources law from the concessions of the early twentieth century, through the assertion of permanent sovereignty and the rise of national oil companies, the liberalisation of energy markets and the proliferation of investment treaties, to the climate regime, the energy transition, and the geopolitical upheavals of the 2020s. This final chapter looks forward. It does not attempt to predict the future — energy forecasts have a notoriously poor record — but to identify the forces that are reshaping the field, the legal questions they raise, and the research agenda they generate for the next generation of scholars and practitioners.

Eight forces, depicted in Figure 30.1, appear likely to define the field in the coming decades: **decarbonisation**, as the world seeks to limit global warming by transforming energy systems built on fossil fuels; **digitalisation and artificial intelligence**, which are transforming the operation of energy systems and the practice of regulation; **decentralisation**, as distributed generation, storage, and prosumers challenge centralised utility models; **geopolitical fragmentation**, as rivalry among major powers reshapes trade, investment, and security; the rise of **critical minerals** as strategic resources; the recognition of **climate risk** as a financial and legal risk; the growing salience of **justice and rights** in energy governance; and **new frontiers** of resource development, from the deep seabed to hydrogen and beyond. These forces interact: decarbonisation drives demand for critical minerals, which intensifies geopolitical competition and raises questions of justice for producing communities; digitalisation enables decentralisation while creating new security risks. The chapter examines the changing architecture of global energy governance (section 30.1), decarbonisation and the transformation of resource law (section 30.2), artificial intelligence and automated energy regulation (section 30.3), digitalisation, data ownership, and energy markets (section 30.4), climate-related financial and legal risks (section 30.5), emerging international disputes and regulatory conflicts (section 30.6), unresolved questions in global resource governance (section 30.7), and a doctoral research agenda for the next generation (section 30.8).

## 30.1 The Changing Architecture of Global Energy Governance

### A Fragmented Architecture

Unlike trade, which has the WTO, or climate change, which has the UNFCCC, energy has never had a single, comprehensive international regime. Global energy governance, examined in Chapter 2, is a **regime complex** — a loosely coupled set of institutions with overlapping mandates and memberships: the **International Energy Agency**, founded as a consumers' organisation and now a leading source of energy data and transition analysis; **OPEC** and **OPEC+**, representing major oil producers; the **Gas Exporting Countries Forum**; the **International Energy Forum**, a producer–consumer dialogue; the **International Renewable Energy Agency**; the **International Atomic Energy Agency**; the **Energy Charter Treaty** and its Conference; the climate regime; the WTO; the multilateral development banks; the G7 and G20; initiatives such as **Sustainable Energy for All**; regional organisations, including the European Union, ECOWAS, and the African Union; and a growing array of plurilateral clubs and partnerships. This fragmentation reflects the strategic importance of energy, the divergent interests of producers and consumers, and the reluctance of states to cede sovereignty over their resources.

### Forces of Change

Several forces are reshaping this architecture. First, the **energy transition** is shifting the centre of gravity from institutions focused on oil and gas towards those concerned with renewables, electricity, efficiency, and critical minerals. The IEA's mandate has broadened accordingly, IRENA's membership has become near-universal, and new initiatives on critical minerals — the Minerals Security Partnership, the G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan, and the UN Secretary-General's **Panel on Critical Energy Transition Minerals**, which in 2024 proposed guiding principles for the responsible development of transition minerals, including respect for human rights, benefit-sharing, and value addition in producing countries — have emerged. Second, the **shift in energy demand** to emerging economies, particularly China, India, and South-East Asia, and the projected growth of demand in Africa, are challenging the dominance of OECD-centred institutions. The IEA's association with major emerging economies and the expansion of the **BRICS** grouping in 2024 to include major energy producers such as Saudi Arabia (whose membership status has remained ambiguous), the UAE, and Iran, as well as Egypt and Ethiopia, and later Indonesia, illustrate the diversification of energy diplomacy. Third, **geopolitical fragmentation**, examined in Chapter 25, is producing competing blocs and supply chains, undermining universal institutions. Fourth, the **climate regime's** increasing engagement with energy — in the COP28 decision of 2023 calling for a transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems and a tripling of renewable capacity and doubling of efficiency by 2030, examined in Chapter 18 — is making the UNFCCC an increasingly important forum for energy governance.

### Clubs, Partnerships, and Minilateralism

In the face of the difficulties of universal agreement, energy governance is increasingly conducted through **clubs** and **minilateral** arrangements among smaller groups of like-minded states. These include **Just Energy Transition Partnerships**, examined in Chapter 23; **climate clubs**, such as the G7-initiated Climate Club launched in 2023 to promote industrial decarbonisation; the **Powering Past Coal Alliance**; the **Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance**, launched in 2021 by Denmark and Costa Rica to promote the managed phase-out of oil and gas production; sectoral initiatives on methane, such as the **Global Methane Pledge** (2021) to reduce methane emissions by at least thirty per cent from 2020 levels by 2030; and bilateral and plurilateral critical minerals agreements. Clubs can achieve deeper cooperation among willing participants and may expand over time, but raise concerns about exclusion, legitimacy, and the fragmentation of rules, particularly where they impose standards or trade measures on non-members, as in the case of carbon border adjustment mechanisms.

### Proposals for Reform

Scholars and policymakers have proposed various reforms of global energy governance: the transformation of the IEA into a genuinely global energy agency including major emerging economies; a **Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty**, promoted by a coalition of states (initially Pacific island states, led by Vanuatu and Tuvalu), cities, and civil society, to end the expansion of fossil fuel production and manage a global transition — a proposal that gained momentum when Colombia and the Netherlands announced at COP30 in 2025 that they would co-host a first international conference on the transition away from fossil fuels in 2026; a multilateral framework for critical minerals; the reform or replacement of the Energy Charter Treaty; and the integration of energy access and transition finance into the reform of the international financial architecture. The **ICJ advisory opinion of July 2025** on the obligations of states in respect of climate change, which affirmed that the failure of a state to take appropriate action to protect the climate system from greenhouse gas emissions — including through fossil fuel production, the granting of exploration licences, or fossil fuel subsidies — may constitute an internationally wrongful act, as examined in Chapter 18, may influence the evolution of energy governance by clarifying the legal obligations that constrain state energy policies.

### Africa in the Emerging Architecture

Africa's place in the emerging architecture of energy governance is pivotal. The continent holds a large share of the world's critical mineral reserves, vast renewable energy potential, and significant oil and gas resources, and is projected to account for a growing share of global energy demand and population. Yet Africa has historically been a rule-taker in global energy governance, under-represented in its institutions. The **African Union's** Agenda 2063, the Africa Mining Vision, the Green Minerals Strategy, the **African Single Electricity Market** initiative, and the **AfCFTA** offer frameworks for a more coherent African voice, and the **Africa Climate Summit** declarations of Nairobi (2023) and Addis Ababa (2025) articulated African positions on climate finance and the transition. The challenge for African states is to translate their resource endowments into bargaining power in global forums and value addition at home, while ensuring universal energy access and a just transition — a challenge in which legal scholarship has an important role.
