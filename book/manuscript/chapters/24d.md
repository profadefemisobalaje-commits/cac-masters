## 24.5 Middle Eastern Petroleum Governance

### From Concessions to National Control

The Middle East holds around half of the world's proven oil reserves and a large share of its gas reserves, and its petroleum governance has shaped the international petroleum industry and its law. The early concessions — the D'Arcy concession granted by Persia in 1901, the concessions granted in Iraq to the Turkish (later Iraq) Petroleum Company, the Saudi concession granted to Standard Oil of California in 1933, and the Kuwaiti concession of 1934 — granted foreign companies exclusive rights over vast areas for very long periods in return for modest royalties, as examined in Chapter 1. Mid-century, producing states renegotiated these terms, introducing fifty-fifty profit sharing from 1950, and then, following the creation of **OPEC** in 1960 and the assertion of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, acquired control of their industries through participation agreements and nationalisation in the 1970s. The arbitral awards arising from these processes — *Aramco* (1958), the Libyan nationalisation cases (*BP*, *Texaco*, and *LIAMCO*, 1973–1977), *Aminoil* (1982) — are foundational texts of international petroleum law, examined in Chapters 1 and 15.

### Saudi Arabia

**Saudi Arabia**'s petroleum sector is dominated by **Saudi Aramco**, the successor to the Arabian American Oil Company, which the state acquired in stages between 1973 and 1980, and which holds exclusive rights over the Kingdom's hydrocarbon resources under its concession, as restated by royal decree. Saudi Arabia has no comprehensive petroleum statute comparable to those of many other producers; the sector is governed by royal decrees, the Basic Law of Governance (1992), which declares natural resources to be the property of the state, the policies of the Ministry of Energy, and Aramco's governance documents. Aramco's partial listing on the Saudi stock exchange in December 2019, followed by a secondary offering in 2024, required the adoption of a new concession agreement in 2019 that clarified the fiscal relationship between the company and the state, including a royalty structure, and subjected the company to securities regulation and enhanced disclosure. Saudi Arabia does not generally permit foreign equity participation in upstream oil, although foreign companies participate in gas, downstream, and petrochemical projects through joint ventures. As the leading member of OPEC and of the **OPEC+** grouping with Russia and other producers, established through the **Declaration of Cooperation** of 2016 and the **Charter of Cooperation** of 2019, Saudi Arabia plays a pivotal role in managing global oil supply. Its **Vision 2030** programme seeks to diversify the economy, including through renewable energy, hydrogen, and mining.

### Iran

**Iran**'s nationalisation of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1951, led by Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh, was a landmark in the history of resource sovereignty, and its aftermath, including the *Anglo-Iranian Oil Co* case before the International Court of Justice (1952), in which the Court held that it lacked jurisdiction, and the coup of 1953, shaped attitudes to foreign investment for decades. The Constitution of the Islamic Republic (1979) prohibits the granting of concessions to foreigners, and foreign participation in the upstream sector has taken the form of **buy-back contracts**, a type of service contract under which the contractor finances development and is repaid from production, without acquiring ownership of reserves. In 2016, Iran introduced the **Iranian Petroleum Contract** (IPC), a longer-term contract model with features of production sharing, intended to attract foreign investment after the lifting of nuclear-related sanctions; the reimposition of US sanctions in 2018 and of UN sanctions in 2025, as examined in Chapter 22, largely frustrated this objective.

### Iraq

**Iraq**'s petroleum governance illustrates the interaction of constitutional federalism, resource ownership, and contract models in a post-conflict state. The **Constitution of 2005** provides that oil and gas are owned by all the people of Iraq in all the regions and governorates (Article 111), and that the federal government, with the producing governorates and regional governments, shall undertake the management of oil and gas extracted from present fields, with revenues distributed fairly in proportion to the population distribution (Article 112). The ambiguity of these provisions — particularly concerning "present" versus future fields and the respective roles of the federal government and the **Kurdistan Regional Government** (KRG) — has been the source of persistent dispute. A federal oil and gas law has been under discussion since 2007 without being enacted. The KRG enacted its own **Oil and Gas Law** in 2007 and concluded production sharing contracts with international companies, which the federal government regarded as unlawful. In **February 2022**, the Federal Supreme Court of Iraq held that the KRG's Oil and Gas Law was unconstitutional and that the KRG must hand over oil production to the federal government. In **March 2023**, an ICC arbitral tribunal, in proceedings brought by Iraq against Turkey under the **Iraq–Turkey Pipeline Agreement** of 1973, held that Turkey had breached the agreement by allowing the export of Kurdish oil without the federal government's authorisation, leading to the suspension of exports through the pipeline; exports resumed in September 2025 under an arrangement among the federal government, the KRG, and international producers.

In federal Iraq, the Ministry of Oil awarded **technical service contracts** to international companies in licensing rounds beginning in 2009, under which contractors receive a fixed remuneration fee per barrel of incremental production, rather than a share of production or profits, reflecting political sensitivities about foreign ownership of resources. The model enabled a substantial increase in production but was criticised by companies for its low returns and rigidity, and Iraq has since moved towards profit-sharing terms in certain contracts, including a major agreement with TotalEnergies in 2023 for an integrated gas, solar, and water project in the south.

### Qatar and the United Arab Emirates

**Qatar** has built one of the world's largest LNG industries on the North Field, the world's largest non-associated gas field, which it shares with Iran's South Pars. Its national company, **QatarEnergy** (formerly Qatar Petroleum), partners with international companies in joint ventures for LNG production, under development and production sharing agreements and long-term LNG sales and purchase agreements, as examined in Chapter 6. The **North Field expansion** projects, with partners selected in 2022 and 2023, will substantially increase Qatar's LNG export capacity by the late 2020s. Qatar's model combines state control through QatarEnergy with long-term partnerships with international companies holding minority interests.

The **United Arab Emirates** — particularly Abu Dhabi — has retained a **concession** model, under which the **Abu Dhabi National Oil Company** (ADNOC) grants concessions to joint ventures in which ADNOC holds a majority and international companies hold minority interests, with concessions renewed through competitive processes since 2015. ADNOC has listed several subsidiaries on the Abu Dhabi Securities Exchange and has expanded internationally, including through its investment arm, XRG. The UAE's **Masdar**, a renewable energy company, has invested in renewable projects worldwide, including in Africa. The UAE hosted COP28 in 2023, as examined in Chapter 18, highlighting the dual role of Gulf states as fossil fuel producers and investors in clean energy.

### Comparative Observations

Middle Eastern petroleum governance displays a spectrum of models, from the exclusive control of the NOC in Saudi Arabia, through the service contracts of Iran and federal Iraq and the PSCs of the KRG, to the concession and joint venture models of the UAE and Qatar. Common features include the dominance of NOCs, the importance of OPEC and OPEC+ in market management, the absence or limited role of comprehensive petroleum legislation in some states, reliance on executive and contractual instruments, and, increasingly, diversification strategies in the face of the energy transition. The region's experience demonstrates that effective resource governance does not depend on a single legal model, but on the coherence of the institutional framework, the commercial capacity of the NOC, and the stability of the relationship between the state and its partners.

## 24.6 Asian Energy and Mineral Resource Frameworks

### China

**China** is the world's largest energy consumer, the largest emitter of greenhouse gases, the largest producer and consumer of coal, the largest importer of oil, and the dominant manufacturer of solar panels, wind turbines, batteries, and electric vehicles, and it controls a large share of the processing of critical minerals. Its energy governance combines state ownership, central planning, and markets. Mineral resources, including oil and gas, are owned by the state under the Constitution and the **Mineral Resources Law** of 1986, which was comprehensively revised in 2024 with effect from July 2025, introducing competitive allocation of mining rights, strengthening the security of supply of strategic minerals, and adding provisions on ecological restoration. The upstream petroleum sector is dominated by three state-owned companies — **China National Petroleum Corporation** (CNPC/PetroChina), **China Petroleum and Chemical Corporation** (Sinopec), and **China National Offshore Oil Corporation** (CNOOC) — which are also major international investors. Policy is set by the **National Development and Reform Commission** and the **National Energy Administration**.

In November 2024, the National People's Congress Standing Committee adopted the **Energy Law of the People's Republic of China**, in force from January 2025, China's first comprehensive framework law for the energy sector, which addresses energy planning, the development of renewable energy and the "new energy system", energy reserves and emergency response, and the obligations of energy enterprises, and confirms the priority of energy security alongside the low-carbon transition. China's **Renewable Energy Law** of 2005, amended in 2009, introduced feed-in tariffs and mandatory purchase obligations that supported the rapid expansion of wind and solar, followed by a transition to market-based pricing. China launched its **national emissions trading system** for the power sector in 2021, expanding it to steel, cement, and aluminium from 2025, and has pledged to peak emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality before 2060. Its updated NDC, announced in 2025, committed to reduce net emissions by seven to ten per cent from peak levels by 2035. China's external energy and mineral investments, including through the **Belt and Road Initiative**, and its export controls on critical minerals, examined in Chapters 8 and 22, give its legal and policy choices global significance.

### India

**India**, the world's most populous country and third-largest energy consumer, governs energy through a federal system in which the Union has legislative competence over the regulation and development of oil fields and mineral oil resources (Union List, Entry 53) and of mines and mineral development to the extent declared by Parliament to be in the public interest (Entry 54), while states have competence over the regulation of mines and mineral development subject to Union law (State List, Entry 23), and electricity is on the Concurrent List. In the petroleum sector, the **Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy** (HELP) of 2016 introduced a revenue-sharing model, a uniform licence for conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons, and open acreage licensing, replacing the profit-sharing PSCs of the earlier New Exploration Licensing Policy. The Oilfields (Regulation and Development) Amendment Act 2025 sought to modernise the legal framework for petroleum operations, including by introducing a petroleum lease and strengthening investor protections.

In the mining sector, the **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act 1957** (MMDR Act) was amended in 2015 to require the allocation of mineral concessions through **auctions**, following the Supreme Court's decision in ***Manohar Lal Sharma v Principal Secretary*** (2014), which held that the allocation of coal blocks by the government without competitive bidding since 1993 was arbitrary and illegal and cancelled the allocations of 214 blocks. The **Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act 2015** provided for the re-allocation of these blocks by auction. A 2023 amendment of the MMDR Act empowered the Union government to auction concessions for specified **critical minerals**, including lithium, and introduced exploration licences for deep-seated and critical minerals. The **Electricity Act 2003** unbundled state electricity boards and established central and state electricity regulatory commissions, while the **Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022** provided for a domestic carbon credit trading scheme. India's **National Green Hydrogen Mission** (2023) and its target of 500 GW of non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030 reflect its ambitions in the energy transition, while coal remains dominant in power generation.

### Indonesia

**Indonesia**, the birthplace of the production sharing contract, is a major producer of coal, nickel, and gas. Article 33 of its **1945 Constitution** provides that the land, waters, and natural resources within them are controlled by the state and shall be used for the greatest prosperity of the people — a provision that the Constitutional Court has applied to strike down legislation, including, in 2012, the provisions of the Oil and Gas Law of 2001 establishing the upstream regulator BPMIGAS, on the ground that the arrangement undermined the state's control over oil and gas resources; BPMIGAS was replaced by SKK Migas. In the mining sector, the **Mining Law** of 2009 (Law No. 4/2009), amended in 2020 (Law No. 3/2020), replaced the contract of work system with licences and required the domestic processing of minerals. Indonesia's ban on the export of **nickel ore** from January 2020, designed to promote domestic smelting and the development of a battery industry, attracted massive investment, principally from Chinese companies, and made Indonesia the world's leading producer of nickel. The European Union challenged the ban at the WTO, and in 2022 a panel found it inconsistent with Article XI of the GATT and not justified under the exceptions, but Indonesia appealed into the void created by the paralysis of the Appellate Body, as examined in Chapter 13. The nickel boom has raised serious environmental and social concerns, including deforestation, pollution, coal-fired captive power, and labour conditions. Indonesia's **Just Energy Transition Partnership**, examined in Chapter 23, aims to accelerate the retirement of coal power.

### Japan

**Japan**, which has very limited domestic energy resources, relies heavily on imports and has pursued energy security through diversification, long-term LNG contracts, strategic reserves, and nuclear power. The **Fukushima Daiichi** nuclear accident of 2011, examined in Chapter 11, led to the shutdown of all nuclear reactors, the establishment of the independent **Nuclear Regulation Authority** in 2012, and stricter safety standards, under which reactors have been restarted gradually. Japan liberalised its retail electricity market in 2016 and required the legal unbundling of transmission and distribution from 2020. In 2023, Japan enacted the **GX (Green Transformation) Promotion Act**, providing for the issuance of "climate transition bonds" to finance investment in decarbonisation and the introduction of carbon pricing, including an emissions trading system to become mandatory from 2026 and a carbon levy on fossil fuel importers, and legislation allowing the operation of nuclear reactors beyond sixty years by excluding periods of shutdown. Japan's support for hydrogen and ammonia, including co-firing in coal plants, has been controversial internationally.

### Australia

**Australia**, a federation in which the states own most onshore mineral and petroleum resources and the Commonwealth regulates offshore resources beyond state coastal waters under the **Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006**, is one of the world's largest exporters of coal, LNG, iron ore, and lithium. The **National Electricity Market**, covering the eastern and southern states, is governed by the National Electricity Law, a model law applied by participating jurisdictions, with rules made by the Australian Energy Market Commission, regulation by the Australian Energy Regulator, and operation by the Australian Energy Market Operator. Australia reformed its **Safeguard Mechanism** in 2023 to require large industrial emitters to reduce their emissions baselines by around five per cent per year, and legislated targets of a forty-three per cent emissions reduction by 2030 and net zero by 2050 in the Climate Change Act 2022. The Federal Court's decision in ***Santos NA Barossa Pty Ltd v Tipakalippa*** (Full Federal Court, 2022), which upheld the setting aside of the approval of an environment plan for offshore drilling because the operator had failed to consult traditional owners of the Tiwi Islands, illustrates the significance of consultation obligations in Australian offshore regulation. Australia's native title regime and the Juukan Gorge controversy have been examined in Chapter 21.
