## 15.2 Concession and Production Sharing Agreements

### Petroleum Agreements as Instruments of Risk Allocation

The principal forms of host government petroleum arrangement — concessions or licences, production sharing contracts (PSCs), service contracts, and joint ventures with state companies — and their place in national licensing regimes have been examined in Chapter 5. This section examines these arrangements from a contractual perspective, focusing on how their terms allocate risk and reward between the host state and the investor, and on the clauses that most frequently generate disputes.

Every petroleum agreement must allocate several fundamental risks. **Geological risk** — the risk that exploration will not discover commercial quantities of petroleum — is almost always borne by the investor, which funds exploration at its own risk and recovers nothing if no commercial discovery is made. **Price risk** — the risk that petroleum prices will fall or rise — is shared through the fiscal terms, which determine how the proceeds of production are divided at different price levels. **Cost risk** — the risk that development and operating costs will exceed expectations — is allocated through cost recovery provisions. **Political and regulatory risk** — the risk that the host state will change the terms unilaterally — is addressed through stabilisation provisions, examined in section 15.6, and treaty protections, examined in Chapter 14. The design of these provisions determines the "government take" — the share of the economic rent from petroleum production captured by the state — and the investor's incentives to explore, develop, and produce efficiently.

### The Concession or Licence

Under a **concession** or **licence**, the host state grants the investor the exclusive right to explore for and produce petroleum in a defined area, and the investor acquires title to the petroleum produced at the wellhead or another delivery point, in exchange for the payment of royalties, taxes, and other fees. The concession is the oldest form of petroleum arrangement and remains the dominant model in many jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom, Norway, the United States, Australia, and Brazil (for most areas). In Nigeria, the licence model was historically used through oil prospecting licences and oil mining leases, often operated through joint ventures with the state oil company, and continues under the PIA through petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases.

The principal contractual or regulatory terms of a concession include the duration of the exploration and production phases; the minimum work programme and expenditure commitments; the relinquishment of portions of the area at specified intervals; the procedure for declaring a commercial discovery and approving a field development plan; the fiscal terms, including royalties and taxes; the state's right to participate; obligations relating to local content, training, and community development; environmental and decommissioning obligations; and provisions on assignment, change of control, termination, and dispute settlement.

### The Production Sharing Contract

Under a **production sharing contract**, first used by Indonesia in the 1960s, the host state (usually through its national oil company) retains ownership of the petroleum, and the investor (the contractor) is engaged to conduct petroleum operations at its own risk and expense. If production is achieved, the contractor is entitled to recover its costs from a portion of production (**cost oil**), subject to a limit, and the remaining production (**profit oil**) is shared between the state and the contractor according to an agreed formula. The contractor may also be liable to pay income tax, which in some PSCs is paid on its behalf by the national oil company out of the state's share of profit oil.

The key risk-allocation provisions of a PSC include the following.

**Cost recovery limit.** The **cost recovery limit** (or "cost stop") caps the proportion of production in any period that can be used for cost recovery, typically between fifty and eighty per cent, ensuring that the state receives a share of production from the outset. Unrecovered costs may be carried forward to subsequent periods. Disputes frequently arise over whether particular expenditures are recoverable, the allocation of overhead costs, and the treatment of costs incurred outside the contract area. Detailed **accounting procedures**, annexed to the PSC, define recoverable costs, and the state typically has audit rights.

**Profit oil split.** Profit oil may be split in fixed proportions or according to a sliding scale linked to production rates, cumulative production, the **R-factor** (the ratio of the contractor's cumulative revenues to cumulative costs), or the contractor's internal rate of return. Progressive splits, under which the state's share increases as profitability increases, allocate a greater share of windfall gains to the state and reduce the pressure for renegotiation when prices rise.

**Ring-fencing.** Ring-fencing provisions require costs to be recovered only from production in the contract area in which they were incurred, preventing the contractor from recovering exploration costs in one area from production in another.

**Domestic market obligation.** Some PSCs require the contractor to supply a proportion of its share of production to the domestic market, often at a discounted price. This obligation reflects the policy considerations examined in Chapter 13 in the context of dual pricing.

**Title and lifting.** PSCs specify the point at which title to the contractor's share of production passes, and contain lifting arrangements governing how each party takes its entitlement.

### PSCs in Nigeria

Nigeria introduced PSCs in 1993 for deepwater and inland basin acreage, and the fiscal terms of PSCs for deep offshore and inland basin areas were set by the **Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contracts Act 1999**. The Act provided for royalty rates that varied with water depth, falling to zero for production in water depths exceeding one thousand metres, and for a review of the terms when the price of oil exceeded twenty US dollars per barrel. The failure to implement the price review mechanism became a source of controversy, and in 2019 the National Assembly amended the Act to introduce a royalty based on both water depth and price, with an additional royalty when oil prices exceed specified levels. The PIA has since introduced a new fiscal framework for new licences, while preserving existing PSCs subject to conversion options, as examined in Chapter 24.

Disputes under Nigerian PSCs have raised important questions of arbitrability and the relationship between contract and public law. In a dispute between Esso Exploration and Production Nigeria Ltd and the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation concerning the allocation of crude oil under a PSC to satisfy royalty and tax obligations ("tax oil" and "royalty oil"), an arbitral tribunal rendered an award in favour of Esso in 2011. The Nigerian courts subsequently set aside the award, holding, among other things, that disputes concerning the assessment and collection of petroleum profits tax were not arbitrable because the relevant tax legislation vested jurisdiction in the Federal High Court and the tax authorities. The case illustrates the risk that disputes under host government contracts touching on taxation and other public law matters may be held non-arbitrable by the courts of the host state.

### Service Contracts

Under a **service contract**, the contractor performs exploration and production services for the state or its national oil company in exchange for a fee, without acquiring title to petroleum or a share of production. Under a **risk service contract**, the contractor bears exploration risk and is remunerated, if production is achieved, by a fee per barrel or a share of revenues; under a **pure service contract**, the contractor is paid for its services regardless of the outcome. Service contracts are used in jurisdictions where constitutional or political constraints prohibit the grant of rights to petroleum to foreign companies, such as Iran (through its "buy-back" contracts and the subsequent Iran Petroleum Contract), Mexico (before its 2013 reform), and Iraq, where the technical service contracts awarded in 2009 for the redevelopment of major producing fields provided for a remuneration fee per barrel of incremental production, subject to production targets and cost recovery. Service contracts allocate most of the price risk and upside to the state, and have been criticised by investors for offering inadequate returns.

### Clauses Frequently in Dispute

Beyond the fiscal terms, several clauses of petroleum agreements frequently give rise to disputes.

**Assignment and change of control.** Petroleum agreements typically prohibit the assignment of the contractor's interest without the consent of the state or the regulator, and many extend the requirement of consent to indirect transfers through a change of control of the contractor. Unauthorised transfers may lead to termination, as in *Occidental v Ecuador*, examined in Chapter 14. Host states have increasingly sought to tax indirect transfers of interests, as in the disputes over capital gains tax on the sale of interests in Ugandan oil licences, examined in Chapter 16.

**Commerciality and development obligations.** Disputes may arise over whether a discovery is commercial, whether a development plan must be approved, and whether the contractor has diligently pursued development. Host states may seek to terminate agreements for failure to develop discoveries, while contractors may argue that development was not commercially viable or was delayed by the state.

**Relinquishment and expiry.** Questions arise as to the extent of the area to be relinquished, the treatment of discoveries in areas due for relinquishment, and the rights of the parties on expiry, including in relation to the renewal of licences. The renewal of Nigerian oil mining leases has generated disputes between licensees and the government.

**Decommissioning.** As examined in Chapter 5, the allocation of decommissioning liabilities between current and former licensees, and between the contractor and the state on the expiry of the agreement, is an increasingly significant source of disputes, particularly in mature basins and in onshore divestments in Nigeria.

## 15.3 Engineering, Procurement, and Construction Contracts

### The EPC Model

Energy facilities — power plants, refineries, LNG trains, offshore platforms, pipelines, and renewable energy installations — are constructed under construction contracts that allocate the risks of design, procurement, construction, completion, and performance. The **engineering, procurement, and construction** (EPC) contract, also known as a **turnkey** contract, is the predominant model for project-financed energy facilities. Under an EPC contract, a single contractor undertakes responsibility for the design, procurement of equipment and materials, construction, commissioning, and testing of the facility, delivering a completed facility that meets specified performance requirements, for a fixed **lump-sum price** and by a fixed **completion date**. The EPC model offers the project company and its lenders a single point of responsibility, price certainty, and time certainty, which are essential to the financing of projects whose revenues will service debt only once the facility is operational. In exchange, the contractor assumes substantial risks, for which it charges a risk premium.

The **FIDIC Conditions of Contract for EPC/Turnkey Projects** (the "Silver Book", first published in 1999 and revised in 2017) is the most widely used international standard form for EPC contracts. It allocates most risks to the contractor, including the risk of unforeseeable physical conditions, which is allocated to the employer under FIDIC's other standard forms. The FIDIC "Yellow Book" (Plant and Design-Build) and "Red Book" (Construction, for works designed by the employer) adopt more balanced risk allocations. Large energy projects frequently use bespoke contracts, adapted from standard forms, reflecting the parties' bargaining power and the requirements of lenders.

### Allocation of Construction Risks

The key risk-allocation provisions of an EPC contract include the following.

**Scope and design responsibility.** The contractor is typically responsible for the design of the facility, based on the employer's requirements, and for any errors in the employer's requirements that an experienced contractor should have discovered. Disputes frequently arise over the scope of the works and whether particular work constitutes a variation entitling the contractor to additional payment and time.

**Ground conditions.** The risk of unforeseen ground or subsurface conditions is particularly significant for energy projects, including offshore wind foundations, tunnels, pipelines, and hydropower dams. Under the Silver Book, the contractor bears this risk, while other forms allocate it to the employer where conditions could not reasonably have been foreseen by an experienced contractor.

**Time and delay.** The contractor must complete the works by the completion date, subject to extensions of time for delays caused by the employer, variations, force majeure, and other specified events. If the contractor fails to complete on time, it must pay **delay liquidated damages** at a specified daily or weekly rate, usually subject to a cap, which are intended to compensate the employer for lost revenues and additional financing costs. Under English law, the **prevention principle** provides that an employer cannot recover liquidated damages for delay that it has caused, and an extension of time mechanism is necessary to preserve the employer's right to liquidated damages where it has caused delay. The treatment of **concurrent delay** — where delay is caused by both an employer risk event and a contractor risk event — is a frequent source of dispute; in *North Midland Building Ltd v Cyden Homes Ltd* [2018] EWCA Civ 1744, the Court of Appeal upheld a clause providing that concurrent delay would not entitle the contractor to an extension of time, holding that parties are free to allocate the risk of concurrent delay by clear words. **Time bar** provisions, which require the contractor to give notice of claims within a specified period as a condition of entitlement, are generally enforced by English courts; in *Obrascon Huarte Lain SA v Her Majesty's Attorney General for Gibraltar* [2014] EWHC 1028 (TCC), the court held that the twenty-eight-day notice requirement in the FIDIC Yellow Book was a condition precedent to the contractor's entitlement to an extension of time.

**Performance.** The contractor guarantees that the completed facility will meet specified performance criteria — such as output capacity, efficiency (heat rate), availability, and emissions — demonstrated through performance tests. If the facility fails to meet the guaranteed levels, the contractor must pay **performance liquidated damages**, typically calculated by reference to the net present value of the lost revenues or increased costs over the life of the facility, or, if performance falls below a minimum level, the employer may reject the facility.

**Liquidated damages and penalties.** Under English law, a liquidated damages clause is enforceable unless it is a **penalty**. In *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi; ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67, the Supreme Court reformulated the penalty doctrine, holding that the test is whether the clause is a secondary obligation which imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in the enforcement of the primary obligation. The decision makes it more difficult to challenge liquidated damages clauses negotiated by commercial parties of comparable bargaining power. In *Triple Point Technology Inc v PTT Public Co Ltd* [2021] UKSC 29, a case arising from a software contract with a Thai state-owned energy company, the Supreme Court held that, where a contract is terminated before completion, liquidated damages for delay accrue up to the date of termination, after which the employer's remedy is general damages for breach — confirming the orthodox position.

**Limitation of liability.** EPC contracts typically cap the contractor's aggregate liability (often at a percentage of, or the whole of, the contract price), with separate sub-caps for delay and performance liquidated damages, and exclude liability for indirect and consequential losses, subject to exceptions for fraud, wilful misconduct, and, in some contracts, gross negligence. Liability allocation is examined in section 15.7.

**Security.** The contractor's obligations are typically secured by performance bonds, advance payment guarantees, retention money, and parent company guarantees, examined in section 15.7.

::: casebox {title="MT Højgaard A/S v E.ON Climate & Renewables UK Robin Rigg East Ltd [2017] UKSC 59"}
**Facts.** MT Højgaard contracted to design, fabricate, and install the foundations for the turbines of the Robin Rigg offshore wind farm in the Solway Firth. The technical requirements of the contract had two relevant elements. First, they required the foundations to be designed in accordance with an international standard for offshore wind turbine design, J101. Second, they provided that the design of the foundations "shall ensure a lifetime of 20 years in every aspect without planned replacement". The J101 standard contained an error, discovered after installation, that substantially overestimated the axial capacity of grouted connections. Shortly after completion, the grouted connections began to fail. E.ON incurred remedial costs of around EUR 26 million and claimed them from MT Højgaard.

**Decision.** The Supreme Court held that MT Højgaard was liable. Its obligation to ensure that the foundations had a design life of twenty years was a free-standing obligation, not inconsistent with the obligation to comply with J101. Compliance with J101 was a minimum requirement and did not preclude a more onerous obligation to achieve a specified result. The Court observed that, where a contract contains two provisions, one requiring a product to be designed in accordance with a specified standard and the other requiring it to achieve a particular performance, the courts will generally treat the latter as an additional, more rigorous requirement. The contractor must comply with both.

**Significance.** The decision confirms that fitness for purpose and performance obligations in energy construction contracts may impose strict liability for achieving a specified result. The contractor may be liable even where it exercised reasonable skill and care and complied with the industry standard. The case is a leading authority on the interpretation of technical requirements in offshore energy contracts, and it emphasises the importance of careful drafting and of the contractor's insurance and liability caps.
:::

### EPC Structures for Energy Projects

Large energy projects often deviate from the single EPC model. **Split EPC contracts** divide the works into an offshore supply contract (for equipment supplied from outside the host country) and an onshore construction contract, to manage tax, customs, and licensing issues, with a **wrap-around guarantee** or coordination agreement ensuring single-point responsibility. **Multi-contract** structures, in which the employer contracts separately with suppliers of major equipment (such as turbines or gas compressors) and with construction contractors, may reduce costs but leave the employer with interface risk, which lenders may be reluctant to accept. LNG projects frequently use consortia of specialist contractors, and offshore wind projects typically use multi-contract structures, with separate contracts for turbines, foundations, cables, and installation vessels. The allocation of interface risk among multiple contractors is a recurrent source of disputes.

Large energy construction projects are prone to cost overruns and delays, often resulting in major disputes. The construction of LNG facilities in Australia and nuclear power plants in Europe and the United States has been marked by significant overruns, and project disputes are frequently resolved by international arbitration involving complex delay and quantum analysis, examined in Chapter 26.
