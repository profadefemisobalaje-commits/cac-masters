## 8.7 Mineral Processing, Recycling, and Circular Economy Regulation

### The Processing Bottleneck

The concentration of mineral processing — the conversion of ores and concentrates into refined metals and chemicals suitable for industrial use — is the most acute vulnerability in critical mineral supply chains. Even where mining is geographically diverse, processing may be concentrated in a single country: lithium mined in Australia is shipped as spodumene concentrate to China for conversion to lithium hydroxide; cobalt mined in the DRC is refined predominantly in China; and almost all rare earth separation and magnet manufacturing takes place in China. Processing is capital-intensive, technically demanding, energy-intensive, and environmentally hazardous, and the economics of new processing capacity outside China are challenged by the scale, experience, and low costs of Chinese producers, as well as by price volatility that Chinese oversupply can intensify.

The law relating to processing has several dimensions. Environmental regulation of processing facilities — governing emissions to air and water, waste management (including radioactive waste from rare earth processing), and energy use — is critical, and the strength of environmental regulation affects both the location of processing and its environmental footprint. The controversy over the Lynas rare earth processing plant in Kuantan, Malaysia, which faced protests and litigation over the management of low-level radioactive waste and was required by the Malaysian government to relocate its cracking and leaching operations to Australia, illustrates the issues. Trade and industrial policy measures — export restrictions on unprocessed ores in producing states and subsidies for processing in importing states — shape the location of processing, as examined in sections 8.3 and 8.4. Investment law protects processing investments, and investment screening may restrict foreign ownership of processing facilities.

### The Circular Economy

The circular economy — an economic model in which materials are kept in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair, remanufacturing, and recycling, and waste is minimised — offers a partial solution to the supply challenges of critical minerals. Recycling of end-of-life batteries, electronics, magnets, and other products can provide a secondary source of critical minerals that is located in consuming countries, reduces the environmental impacts of primary mining, and reduces dependence on concentrated supply chains. However, recycling cannot meet growing demand in the near term, because the stock of end-of-life products is small relative to projected demand, particularly for batteries, most of which are still in use. The IEA has projected that recycling could reduce primary supply requirements for lithium, cobalt, and nickel by a meaningful share by 2040 in scenarios with ambitious recycling policies, but primary mining will remain essential for decades.

### The EU Batteries Regulation: Circularity Requirements

The EU Batteries Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), introduced in section 8.5, is the most comprehensive regulatory framework for battery circularity. Its principal circularity provisions include:

- **Recycled content**: from 2031, industrial batteries with a capacity above 2 kWh, electric vehicle batteries, and certain other batteries must contain minimum shares of recovered cobalt (16 per cent), lead (85 per cent), lithium (6 per cent), and nickel (6 per cent), rising in 2036 to 26 per cent for cobalt, 12 per cent for lithium, and 15 per cent for nickel;
- **Recycling efficiency and material recovery**: recycling processes must achieve minimum recycling efficiencies (for lithium-based batteries, 65 per cent by the end of 2025 and 70 per cent by the end of 2030), and minimum material recovery rates (for lithium, 50 per cent by the end of 2027 and 80 per cent by the end of 2031; for cobalt, copper, lead, and nickel, 90 per cent by the end of 2027 and 95 per cent by the end of 2031);
- **Extended producer responsibility**: producers must organise and finance the collection and treatment of waste batteries, with rising collection targets for portable batteries;
- **Carbon footprint**: electric vehicle batteries and certain other batteries must be accompanied by a carbon footprint declaration and will become subject to maximum carbon footprint thresholds;
- **Battery passport**: from February 2027, electric vehicle batteries, industrial batteries above 2 kWh, and light means of transport batteries must have a digital battery passport.

The Regulation also addresses removability and replaceability of portable batteries and labelling requirements. Its extraterritorial effect is significant: any battery placed on the EU market, wherever manufactured, must comply, and the recycled content and carbon footprint requirements create incentives for battery manufacturers worldwide to adopt EU standards — an example of the "Brussels effect" in energy transition regulation.

### Waste and the Transboundary Movement of E-Waste

The recycling of critical minerals depends on the collection and transport of waste products, which are subject to the law on hazardous waste. The **Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and their Disposal** (1989) requires the prior informed consent of importing and transit states for transboundary movements of hazardous wastes, and prohibits exports to states that have prohibited imports. The Basel Ban Amendment, which entered into force in 2019, prohibits exports of hazardous waste from OECD and EU member states and Liechtenstein to other states for any purpose. Amendments to the Convention's Annexes adopted in 2022, which entered into force on 1 January 2025, extend the prior informed consent procedure to all electronic and electrical waste, whether or not hazardous. The **Bamako Convention** (1991) of the African Union goes further, prohibiting the import of hazardous wastes into Africa from non-parties. These regimes are designed to prevent the dumping of hazardous waste in developing countries, a practice exemplified by the 1988 Koko incident in Nigeria, in which several thousand tonnes of toxic waste from Italy were dumped in the port town of Koko in present-day Delta State, prompting Nigeria's Harmful Waste (Special Criminal Provisions, etc.) Act 1988. The legal challenge is to distinguish legitimate trade in recyclable materials, which can support the circular economy, from the export of waste for disposal or substandard recycling in countries lacking adequate facilities, which harms workers and communities, as in the informal e-waste processing sites of Agbogbloshie in Accra, Ghana, and parts of Lagos.

### Mine Waste as a Resource

Mine wastes and tailings may contain recoverable quantities of critical minerals that were not economically extractable when the waste was produced. The reprocessing of tailings to recover cobalt, copper, rare earths, and other minerals is attracting investment, and the EU Critical Raw Materials Act requires member states to compile databases of closed extractive waste facilities with potential for recovery of critical raw materials. Reprocessing raises legal questions concerning ownership of tailings (whether they belong to the state as unworked minerals, to the operator as property, or to the owner of the land), the allocation of liability for historical contamination, and the applicability of mining, waste, and environmental law to reprocessing operations.

## 8.8 Geopolitical Competition and Critical Mineral Agreements

### The Geopolitics of Critical Minerals

Critical minerals have become an arena of intense geopolitical competition. The principal axis of competition is between China, which has built dominance across critical mineral supply chains through decades of state-supported investment in domestic and overseas mining and processing, and the United States and its allies, which seek to reduce their dependence on Chinese supply. Producing states in Africa, Latin America, and Asia find themselves courted by competing powers, which offers opportunities to negotiate better terms but also risks entanglement in great power rivalry. The geopolitics of critical minerals is examined further in Chapter 25 in the context of energy security; this section focuses on the legal instruments through which states cooperate and compete.

### Types of Critical Mineral Agreements

A diverse and rapidly evolving array of international instruments addresses critical minerals:

**Plurilateral partnerships among consumer states.** The **Minerals Security Partnership** (MSP), launched in June 2022 by the United States and a group of partners including Australia, Canada, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, and the United Kingdom, aims to catalyse public and private investment in responsible critical mineral supply chains, including through a Minerals Security Partnership Finance Network. The **G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan**, adopted at the Kananaskis summit in June 2025, commits G7 members to cooperation on standards-based markets, investment, recycling, and innovation. The IEA's **Critical Minerals Security Programme**, launched in 2024, supports its members in assessing supply risks and coordinating responses.

**Bilateral critical minerals agreements.** The Agreement between the Government of the United States of America and the Government of Japan on Strengthening Critical Minerals Supply Chains (March 2023) commits the parties not to impose export duties on critical minerals traded between them, to promote labour rights and environmental protection in critical mineral supply chains, and to cooperate on investment screening and recycling. Its principal practical function was to enable Japan to be treated as an FTA partner for the purposes of the IRA clean vehicle credit.

**Strategic partnerships between consumers and producers.** The European Union has concluded memoranda of understanding on strategic partnerships on raw materials with numerous producing states, typically covering integration of value chains, cooperation on research and innovation, application of environmental, social, and governance standards, infrastructure development, and capacity building. The United States has supported the **Lobito Corridor**, a rail and port infrastructure project linking the copper and cobalt mining regions of the DRC and Zambia to the Angolan port of Lobito on the Atlantic coast, in partnership with the European Union, the African Development Bank, and the Africa Finance Corporation, as an alternative to routes to the Indian Ocean.

**Minerals-for-security and minerals-for-reconstruction arrangements.** In April 2025, the United States and Ukraine concluded an agreement establishing a United States–Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund, under which Ukraine contributes revenues from new licences for natural resources, including critical minerals, oil, and gas, to a jointly governed fund that invests in Ukrainian projects. In June 2025, the DRC and Rwanda signed a peace agreement facilitated by the United States, accompanied by a regional economic integration framework addressing mineral supply chains in the Great Lakes region. These arrangements, which link access to minerals with security guarantees or peace processes, raise questions about the permanent sovereignty of states over their natural resources, the legitimacy of agreements concluded in conditions of conflict and dependence, and the risk of reproducing historical patterns of extraction.

**Resource-for-infrastructure agreements.** Chinese state-backed companies have concluded numerous agreements under which infrastructure is financed and constructed in exchange for mining rights or mineral offtake, notably the Sicomines agreement in the DRC (examined in section 8.2). Such agreements have been criticised for opacity, undervaluation of mineral rights, and inadequate benefits for host states, and several have been renegotiated.

### The Legal Character of Critical Mineral Agreements

Many critical mineral instruments are memoranda of understanding or political declarations rather than legally binding treaties. Their non-binding character provides flexibility and enables rapid conclusion, but it limits their enforceability and their capacity to provide the legal certainty required for long-term investment. Where they are binding, as in the case of the United States–Japan agreement, they are often narrow in scope. The emerging architecture of critical mineral agreements is thus fragmented and largely soft, contrasting with the comprehensive multilateral frameworks that govern trade generally under the WTO. Some commentators have proposed a multilateral critical minerals agreement, or a critical minerals plurilateral within the WTO, to establish common rules on export restrictions, subsidies, standards, and investment; others have proposed producer-consumer dialogues analogous to the International Energy Forum in the oil sector.

### Producer Coordination

Producing states have explored coordination to strengthen their bargaining position. Proposals have been made for a lithium producers' organisation among Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile, analogous to OPEC, and Indonesia has floated the idea of a nickel producers' organisation. African states have pursued coordination through the African Union and regional economic communities, including the development of regional value chains. Such coordination faces the obstacles that undermined earlier producer associations for copper, bauxite, and other minerals in the 1970s — divergent interests, the availability of substitutes, and the entry of new producers — but it reflects the determination of producing states to avoid a repetition of the historical pattern in which resource wealth flowed outward while host countries remained dependent on raw material exports.

### Assessment

The legal regime of critical minerals is in the midst of rapid and contested formation. It is shaped by the imperatives of the energy transition, the pursuit of national security, the aspirations of producing states for development and industrialisation, and the claims of communities, workers, and indigenous peoples for respect for their rights. The instruments that constitute the regime — national legislation, trade rules, export controls, industrial policy, due diligence obligations, and bilateral and plurilateral agreements — are fragmented and sometimes contradictory. A central question for international energy and natural resources law is whether this regime can be developed in a manner that secures mineral supply for the energy transition while respecting the permanent sovereignty of producing states, ensuring that they and their populations share equitably in the benefits, and protecting human rights and the environment.

::: casebox
**Indonesia — Measures Relating to Raw Materials (WTO, DS592, Panel Report 2022)**

*Measures.* Indonesia banned the export of nickel ore with effect from 1 January 2020, and required domestic processing of nickel ore before export. The European Union, whose stainless steel industry relied on imported nickel ore, challenged the measures.

*Findings.* The panel found that the export ban was a prohibition on exportation within the meaning of Article XI:1 of the GATT 1994. It rejected Indonesia's defence under Article XI:2(a), holding that the ban was not "temporarily applied", since Indonesia intended it to remain in place indefinitely, and that Indonesia had not demonstrated a "critical shortage" of nickel ore, given its very large reserves. It also rejected Indonesia's defence under Article XX(d), holding that the ban was not "necessary" to secure compliance with Indonesia's mining laws, since a requirement in domestic law could not itself justify a measure inconsistent with the GATT simply because the measure implemented it. Having rejected the defences, the panel did not need to address the domestic processing requirement in detail.

*Aftermath.* Indonesia appealed in December 2022 to the Appellate Body, which has been unable to hear appeals since December 2019 because of the blocking of appointments by the United States; the report therefore has not been adopted and is not binding. Indonesia has maintained the ban. The European Union's Enforcement Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 654/2014), as amended in 2021 by Regulation (EU) 2021/167, permits it to adopt countermeasures where a dispute is blocked by an appeal into the void, although it has not done so in this dispute.

*Significance.* The case is the leading recent authority on the application of GATT disciplines to export restrictions designed to promote downstream processing. It confirms the narrow scope of Article XI:2(a) and Article XX(d), and illustrates the practical limits of WTO enforcement in the absence of a functioning Appellate Body. It has been widely discussed in debates about the policy space available to resource-rich developing countries.

::: summary
- "Critical" and "strategic" minerals are classified by reference to economic importance and supply risk; classifications such as those in the EU Critical Raw Materials Act and the United States Energy Act of 2020 carry significant legal consequences.
- Lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and rare earth elements each present distinctive legal issues, from Chile's state-controlled lithium regime to the DRC's cobalt export controls, Indonesia's nickel ore ban, Panama's Cobre Panamá litigation, and China's rare earth dominance.
- Export restrictions on minerals are constrained by GATT Article XI and narrowly interpreted exceptions, as in *China — Raw Materials*, *China — Rare Earths*, and *Indonesia — Raw Materials*; China's export controls on minerals and technologies have become instruments of economic statecraft.
- Importing states pursue industrial policies combining benchmarks, accelerated permitting, public finance, and partnerships, notably under the EU Critical Raw Materials Act and United States legislation.
- Supply chain due diligence, grounded in the OECD Guidance, has become mandatory under Dodd-Frank section 1502, the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation, the EU Batteries Regulation, and general due diligence laws, supplemented by forced labour import bans.
- Critical mineral production is associated with child labour, forced labour, indigenous rights violations, and unsafe conditions; liability of downstream companies remains difficult to establish, as *Doe v Apple* shows.
- Circular economy regulation, especially the EU Batteries Regulation, and the law on transboundary waste shape the recycling of critical minerals.
- Critical mineral agreements form a fragmented, largely soft-law architecture of partnerships, bilateral agreements, and minerals-for-security arrangements, raising questions about permanent sovereignty and equity.
:::
