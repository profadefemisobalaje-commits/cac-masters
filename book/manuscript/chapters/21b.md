## 21.3 Free, Prior, and Informed Consent

### The Elements of FPIC

The principle of **free, prior, and informed consent** (FPIC) expresses the idea that indigenous peoples should be able to give or withhold their consent to projects and measures affecting their lands, territories, and resources, through their own representative institutions. Each element has a recognised content, articulated in UN and regional practice, including the reports of the UN Special Rapporteur on the rights of indigenous peoples and the Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, whose study of 2018 provides a comprehensive analysis, and summarised in Figure 21.1:

- **Free** implies the absence of coercion, intimidation, manipulation, or undue pressure — including from security forces, threats of withdrawal of services, bribery, or the division of communities — and requires that the community itself determines the process, timeline, and decision-making structure.
- **Prior** implies that consent is sought sufficiently in advance of any authorisation or commencement of activities, respecting the time requirements of indigenous consultation and consensus processes, and before key decisions — such as the grant of a concession — have been taken.
- **Informed** implies that the community receives full, objective, and accessible information, in a language and form it understands, on the nature, size, pace, reversibility, and scope of the proposed project; its purpose and duration; the areas affected; a preliminary assessment of its economic, social, cultural, and environmental impacts and risks; the personnel likely to be involved; and the procedures the project may entail — and has access to independent advice.
- **Consent** implies a collective decision reached through the community's customary decision-making processes and representative institutions, which may be given, given subject to conditions, or withheld, and which may need to be revisited as the project evolves.

### Legal Status and Content: Consultation or Consent?

The central controversy surrounding FPIC is whether it confers on indigenous peoples a **right to veto** projects affecting them, or merely a right to be consulted in good faith with the objective of obtaining consent. The instruments differ. ILO Convention No. 169 requires consultation with the objective of achieving agreement or consent (Article 6(2)) and requires consent only for relocation, subject to an exception (Article 16). UNDRIP refers to FPIC in several contexts: it provides that indigenous peoples shall not be forcibly removed from their lands without their FPIC (Article 10); that no storage or disposal of hazardous materials shall take place on their lands without their FPIC (Article 29(2)); and that redress is due for lands taken without their FPIC (Article 28). In the provisions most relevant to resource development, however, UNDRIP requires states to "consult and cooperate in good faith with the indigenous peoples concerned through their own representative institutions **in order to obtain** their free, prior and informed consent" before adopting legislative or administrative measures that may affect them (Article 19) and "prior to the approval of any project affecting their lands or territories and other resources, particularly in connection with the development, utilization or exploitation of mineral, water or other resources" (Article 32(2)).

Former UN Special Rapporteur James Anaya argued that FPIC should not be regarded as a general veto power, but that the strength of the requirement varies with the nature and severity of the impact: as a general rule, extractive activities should not take place within the territories of indigenous peoples without their consent, and consent is required where a project would have a significant, direct impact on their lives or territories, while states may proceed without consent in limited circumstances where the rights limitation is necessary, proportionate, and in pursuit of a valid public purpose within a human rights framework. Many indigenous organisations and scholars reject this approach and maintain that FPIC is an expression of self-determination that necessarily includes the right to say no. States and companies have tended to emphasise the consultative dimension.

::: casebox {title="Saramaka People v Suriname: Consent for Large-Scale Projects"}
**Saramaka People v Suriname (Inter-American Court of Human Rights, Judgment of 28 November 2007; Interpretation Judgment of 12 August 2008).**

**Facts.** The Saramaka, a tribal people descended from Africans who escaped slavery in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and established autonomous communities in the interior of Suriname, claimed that the state had granted logging and mining concessions on their traditional territory without consultation, and that the Afobaka dam, built in the 1960s to supply power to an aluminium smelter, had flooded part of their territory and displaced communities. Suriname's legal system did not recognise collective property rights of indigenous or tribal peoples, and Suriname owned all subsoil resources.

**Judgment.** The Court held that the Saramaka, as a tribal people sharing the distinctive characteristics of indigenous peoples, were entitled to the protection of Article 21 of the American Convention (right to property) in respect of the lands they had traditionally used and occupied, and to the natural resources on and within the land that were necessary for their survival. The state could restrict their property rights to grant concessions only if it complied with three safeguards: it must ensure the **effective participation** of the Saramaka, in conformity with their customs and traditions, in any development, investment, exploration, or extraction plan within their territory; it must guarantee that they receive a **reasonable benefit** from such plans; and it must ensure that no concession is issued until independent and technically capable entities, under the state's supervision, perform a prior **environmental and social impact assessment**. The Court further held that, regarding **large-scale development or investment projects that would have a major impact** within Saramaka territory, the state has a duty not only to consult but also to **obtain their free, prior, and informed consent**, according to their customs and traditions. It ordered Suriname to delimit, demarcate, and grant collective title over Saramaka territory, to review existing concessions, and to adopt legislation recognising collective rights.

**Significance.** *Saramaka* is the leading international judicial authority for the proposition that consent, and not merely consultation, is required for large-scale projects with a major impact on indigenous or tribal territories. Its three safeguards — participation, benefit-sharing, and impact assessment — have been widely adopted, including by the African Commission in *Endorois*. Its implementation by Suriname has been slow, illustrating the gap between judicial pronouncement and practice.

### FPIC in the Standards of Financial Institutions and Industry

FPIC has been incorporated into the standards of international financial institutions and industry bodies, giving it practical effect through financing conditions. The **International Finance Corporation's Performance Standard 7** on Indigenous Peoples, as revised in 2012, requires the client to obtain the FPIC of affected indigenous communities in specified circumstances: impacts on lands and natural resources subject to traditional ownership or under customary use; relocation of indigenous peoples from such lands; and significant impacts on critical cultural heritage, or the use of cultural heritage for commercial purposes. PS7 defines FPIC as established through good faith negotiation and states that it does not necessarily require unanimity and may be achieved even when individuals or groups within the community explicitly disagree. The **Equator Principles**, adopted by financial institutions to manage environmental and social risk in project finance, incorporate the IFC Performance Standards and, in their fourth iteration (2020), require FPIC in the circumstances identified in PS7 regardless of the project's location. The **World Bank's Environmental and Social Standard 7** under its Environmental and Social Framework (2016, in effect 2018) similarly requires FPIC in specified circumstances.

Among industry bodies, the **International Council on Mining and Metals** (ICMM) adopted a position statement on indigenous peoples and mining in 2013, committing its members to work to obtain the consent of indigenous peoples for new projects and changes to existing projects located on their lands and likely to have significant adverse impacts, while acknowledging that it would be for governments to decide whether a project proceeds where consent is withheld. Standards such as the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance and the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil also incorporate FPIC.

### The Practice of FPIC

The practical implementation of FPIC raises numerous issues: identifying the communities entitled to give consent and their legitimate representatives; the risk of manipulation by companies or governments through the selection of representatives or the payment of benefits to sections of a community; the role of women, young people, and other groups within communities; the relationship between FPIC and the state's ownership of subsoil resources; the treatment of projects that predate the recognition of FPIC; and whether consent, once given, may be withdrawn. Many indigenous peoples have developed their own **FPIC protocols**, setting out how they expect to be consulted, which some courts and governments have recognised.

National implementation varies. The **Philippines**' Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act 1997 requires FPIC, certified by the National Commission on Indigenous Peoples, for the grant of concessions on ancestral domains — one of the few statutory consent requirements — although its implementation has been criticised for manipulation of consent processes. In Australia, the destruction by Rio Tinto in May 2020 of the 46,000-year-old rock shelters at **Juukan Gorge** in the Pilbara, which had been lawfully authorised under Western Australian heritage legislation dating from 1972, despite the objections of the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura peoples, led to a parliamentary inquiry whose report *Never Again* (2021) recommended reform of cultural heritage law and the adoption of FPIC-based standards, the resignation of Rio Tinto's chief executive and other senior executives, and a reappraisal of agreement-making practices in the mining industry. Western Australia enacted a new Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act in 2021, but repealed it in 2023 following opposition from landholders, reverting to amended earlier legislation.

### FPIC and the Energy Transition

The energy transition has brought FPIC to the centre of debates about renewable energy and transition minerals. A large proportion of the known reserves of lithium, nickel, cobalt, copper, and other minerals, and of the land suitable for wind and solar projects, lies on or near the lands of indigenous and peasant communities. The Business and Human Rights Resource Centre's Transition Minerals Tracker has recorded hundreds of allegations of human rights abuses linked to the mining of transition minerals. Disputes have arisen over lithium extraction in the salt flats of Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile, affecting indigenous communities' access to water; over the Thacker Pass lithium mine in Nevada, where indigenous groups claimed inadequate consultation; and over wind farms on indigenous lands in Mexico's Isthmus of Tehuantepec, Kenya's Lake Turkana region, and Norway's Fosen peninsula. Advocates argue that a **just transition** must respect FPIC and ensure that indigenous peoples share in the benefits of renewable energy, including through equity participation and community ownership, a theme developed in Chapter 23.

## 21.4 Land Acquisition, Resettlement, and Compensation

### The Need for Land and the Risk of Displacement

Resource projects require land — for mines, wells, pipelines, processing facilities, reservoirs, transmission lines, solar parks, and wind farms — and its acquisition often involves the physical displacement of people from their homes, or the economic displacement of people from their sources of livelihood, such as farmland, fishing grounds, and forests. Large dams alone are estimated to have displaced between forty and eighty million people in the twentieth century, according to the World Commission on Dams. Displacement carries well-documented risks of impoverishment — which the sociologist Michael Cernea identified as landlessness, joblessness, homelessness, marginalisation, food insecurity, increased morbidity, loss of access to common property, and social disarticulation — and has disproportionate effects on women, indigenous peoples, and the poor, particularly those whose rights to land are customary or informal.

### International Standards

International human rights law prohibits **forced evictions**, defined by the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights in **General Comment No. 7** (1997) as the permanent or temporary removal against their will of individuals, families, or communities from the homes or land which they occupy, without the provision of, and access to, appropriate forms of legal or other protection. Evictions may be justified only in exceptional circumstances, in accordance with law and the principles of reasonableness and proportionality, and subject to procedural protections, including genuine consultation, adequate and reasonable notice, information, legal remedies, and legal aid. The **UN Basic Principles and Guidelines on Development-based Evictions and Displacement** (2007) elaborate these requirements, including the obligation to explore all feasible alternatives, to ensure that evictions do not result in homelessness, and to provide just compensation and adequate resettlement.

The standards of international financial institutions are the most detailed and widely applied. The **IFC's Performance Standard 5** on Land Acquisition and Involuntary Resettlement and the **World Bank's Environmental and Social Standard 5** require clients to avoid or minimise displacement by exploring alternative project designs; to avoid forced eviction; to mitigate adverse impacts by providing **compensation for loss of assets at full replacement cost** and other assistance to help displaced persons improve or at least restore their livelihoods and standards of living; to improve the living conditions of physically displaced persons through the provision of adequate housing with security of tenure; to provide compensation and assistance to persons without formal legal rights to the land they occupy; to ensure informed participation and consultation; and to prepare a resettlement action plan or livelihood restoration plan. The **Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests** (2012), endorsed by the Committee on World Food Security, provide guidance on the recognition and protection of legitimate tenure rights, including customary rights.

### Nigerian Law

The acquisition of land for resource development in Nigeria is governed principally by the **Land Use Act 1978**, which vests all land comprised in the territory of each state in the Governor of the state, to be held in trust and administered for the use and common benefit of all Nigerians. The Governor grants statutory rights of occupancy in urban areas, and local governments grant customary rights of occupancy in non-urban areas. Under section 28, the Governor may revoke a right of occupancy for **overriding public interest**, which includes the requirement of the land for mining purposes or oil pipelines or for any purpose connected therewith. Compensation under section 29 is payable for the value of unexhausted improvements — buildings, crops, and other developments — and, in the case of land required for mining or oil pipelines, the holder and occupier are entitled to compensation under the appropriate provisions of the minerals or oil pipelines legislation. The Act does not provide compensation for the value of the land itself, reflecting its vesting in the state, a feature that has been criticised as inadequate in the light of international standards requiring compensation at replacement cost. The Act is entrenched in the Constitution and can be amended only by the procedure for constitutional amendment, which has impeded reform.

Section 44(3) of the Constitution vests the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils, and natural gas in, under, or upon any land in Nigeria, its territorial waters, and its EEZ in the Government of the Federation. Section 44(1) prohibits the compulsory acquisition of property except in the manner and for the purposes prescribed by a law that requires prompt payment of compensation and gives the owner a right of access to a court for the determination of the amount, although section 44(2) excepts certain cases. The **Oil Pipelines Act** provides for the grant of permits and licences for oil pipelines and for compensation to owners and occupiers for disturbance and for damage caused by the construction or operation of pipelines, including by leaks. The **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** addresses access to land for petroleum operations and requires the payment of fair and adequate compensation for the disturbance of surface rights and for damage. The **Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007** provides for compensation to owners and occupiers of land for disturbance of surface rights and damage, and requires mining lease holders to conclude a **Community Development Agreement** with host communities, addressing matters such as employment, education, infrastructure, and the protection of the environment. The PIA's provisions on **host communities development trusts**, which channel three per cent of the actual annual operating expenditure of upstream operators to trusts for host communities, are examined in Chapter 24.

### Comparative Perspectives

Other African jurisdictions have strengthened the protection of customary land rights in the context of resource development. **Kenya**'s Constitution of 2010 recognises community land as a category of land tenure, and the **Community Land Act 2016** provides for its registration and requires that any agreement relating to the investment in community land be made with the free, prior, and informed consent of the community. **Mozambique**'s Land Law of 1997 recognises customary rights acquired through occupation and requires community consultation before the grant of rights to investors. **South Africa**'s Interim Protection of Informal Land Rights Act 1996 protects holders of informal rights, and in *Maledu v Itereleng Bakgatla Mineral Resources* (2018), the Constitutional Court held that a mining right did not entitle the holder to evict a community holding informal land rights without their consent or expropriation under the Act.

### Resettlement in Practice

The resettlement associated with large energy projects in Africa illustrates both the application of international standards and their limitations. The **Mozambique LNG** project in Cabo Delgado required the resettlement of several hundred households from the Afungi peninsula and affected the livelihoods of many more, including fishers who lost access to the sea; the project was suspended in 2021 following an insurgent attack on the town of Palma, and the resumption of construction was announced in 2025. The **East African Crude Oil Pipeline** and associated **Tilenga** and **Kingfisher** projects in Uganda and Tanzania have required the acquisition of land affecting more than a hundred thousand people, with complaints concerning delays in compensation, inadequate valuation, and restrictions on land use during the period between valuation and payment; the projects have been the subject of litigation in France under the Duty of Vigilance Law, examined in Chapter 22, and of campaigns that have led many banks and insurers to decline involvement. The **Lake Turkana Wind Power** project in Kenya, Africa's largest wind farm, faced litigation by communities claiming that the land had been acquired without their consent; in 2021, the Environment and Land Court held that the acquisition of the land had been irregular, while declining to order the removal of the project. These cases illustrate the importance of secure tenure, early and meaningful consultation, fair valuation, prompt payment, livelihood restoration, and independent monitoring.
