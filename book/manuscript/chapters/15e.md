## 15.8 Interpretation, Renegotiation, and Termination of Energy Contracts

### Principles of Contractual Interpretation

The meaning of contractual provisions is frequently the central issue in energy disputes, and the approach to interpretation varies across legal systems. English law, which governs a large proportion of international energy contracts, has developed its principles of interpretation through a series of leading decisions. In *Investors Compensation Scheme Ltd v West Bromwich Building Society* [1998] 1 WLR 896, Lord Hoffmann stated that interpretation is the ascertainment of the meaning which the document would convey to a reasonable person having all the background knowledge which would reasonably have been available to the parties in the situation in which they were at the time of the contract, an approach that emphasised context. In *Arnold v Britton* [2015] UKSC 36, the Supreme Court emphasised the primacy of the natural and ordinary meaning of the words, cautioning that commercial common sense should not be invoked to undervalue the language of the provision or to rescue a party from a bad bargain. In *Wood v Capita Insurance Services Ltd* [2017] UKSC 24, the Court reconciled these approaches, describing interpretation as a unitary exercise in which textual analysis and contextual analysis are tools to be used in different proportions depending on the nature, formality, and quality of drafting of the contract — sophisticated, professionally drafted contracts, such as most energy agreements, being more likely to be interpreted principally by textual analysis.

The implication of terms is approached restrictively. In *Marks and Spencer plc v BNP Paribas Securities Services Trust Co (Jersey) Ltd* [2015] UKSC 72, the Supreme Court held that a term will be implied only if it is necessary for business efficacy or so obvious that it goes without saying, and not merely because it would be reasonable or fair. The restrictive approach to implication reinforces the importance of comprehensive drafting.

Civil law systems give greater weight to the common intention of the parties, which may be ascertained from pre-contractual negotiations and subsequent conduct, both of which English law generally excludes from consideration. Article 4.1 of the UNIDROIT Principles provides that a contract shall be interpreted according to the common intention of the parties, or, if such an intention cannot be established, according to the meaning that reasonable persons of the same kind as the parties would give to it in the same circumstances, and Article 4.3 permits consideration of preliminary negotiations, practices established between the parties, and subsequent conduct. International arbitral tribunals, which frequently include arbitrators from different legal traditions, may be influenced by these differing approaches even where the governing law is English law, a phenomenon that experienced counsel take into account in presenting their cases.

### Renegotiation and Adaptation

Long-term energy contracts frequently require adaptation over their life, as market conditions, technology, regulation, and the parties' circumstances change. Several mechanisms facilitate adaptation.

**Price review clauses.** Long-term gas supply contracts typically contain price review clauses permitting either party to request a review of the contract price at specified intervals or upon a significant change in market circumstances, with recourse to arbitration if the parties fail to agree, as examined in Chapter 6. Price review arbitrations, which require tribunals to determine a revised price in light of changed market conditions, are among the most commercially significant forms of energy dispute.

**Renegotiation clauses.** Many state contracts contain clauses obliging the parties to renegotiate in good faith upon specified events, such as fundamental changes in circumstances. The effect of such clauses is limited where the parties fail to agree, unless the contract provides for a third party to adapt the contract. In *Kuwait v American Independent Oil Co (Aminoil)* (1982), the tribunal recognised that the parties' long-term relationship, and the evolution of their contractual arrangements over time, implied an obligation to negotiate in good faith, while holding that the government's nationalisation of the concession was lawful subject to the payment of appropriate compensation, as examined in Chapter 14.

**Adaptation by tribunals.** Whether an arbitral tribunal may adapt a contract to changed circumstances depends on the governing law and the parties' agreement. Under the UNIDROIT Principles and French law, a tribunal may adapt the contract in cases of hardship, while English law does not empower courts or tribunals to rewrite contracts. Parties may expressly empower tribunals to adapt contracts, as price review clauses do.

### Government-Initiated Contract Review

Host governments have periodically undertaken comprehensive reviews of existing contracts with investors, particularly in the extractive sector, on the grounds that the contracts were negotiated under unfavourable conditions, procured through corruption, or became unbalanced as commodity prices rose. The **Democratic Republic of Congo** launched a review of sixty-one mining contracts in 2007–2008, following concerns that contracts concluded during the conflict period were inequitable; the review recommended that a number of contracts be renegotiated or cancelled. Several other African states, including Guinea, Zambia, and Liberia, have undertaken similar reviews of mining contracts.

The most far-reaching recent example is **Tanzania**, which in 2017 enacted the Natural Wealth and Resources (Permanent Sovereignty) Act and the Natural Wealth and Resources Contracts (Review and Re-Negotiation of Unconscionable Terms) Act. The first statute declared the permanent sovereignty of the people of Tanzania over natural resources, prohibited the export of raw minerals, and provided that disputes over natural resources should be resolved by Tanzanian judicial bodies, purporting to exclude international arbitration. The second empowered Parliament to review contracts and to direct the government to renegotiate terms considered unconscionable, failing which the terms would be treated as ceasing to have effect. The legislation led to extensive renegotiations with mining companies, including the settlement of a major dispute with Acacia Mining (later acquired by Barrick Gold), under which the government obtained a share of the economic benefits of the mines and a minority interest in the operating companies, and to investment arbitration claims by other investors.

Government-initiated contract reviews raise the issues of sanctity of contract and sovereign regulatory authority examined throughout Part IV. Under international law, a state's unilateral modification or termination of a contract with a foreign investor may constitute a breach of fair and equitable treatment or an expropriation if it involves the exercise of sovereign power, and may be a breach of an umbrella clause, as examined in Chapter 14. At the same time, contracts procured by corruption may be unenforceable, as examined in Chapter 16, and the legitimacy of renegotiation is enhanced where it follows transparent processes and offers fair compensation for the investor's legitimate expectations.

### Termination and Termination Payments

Energy contracts contain detailed provisions on termination, specifying the grounds on which each party may terminate — such as material breach, insolvency, prolonged force majeure, and change of control — and the consequences of termination, including **termination payments**. In PPAs and concession agreements, the termination payment typically depends on the cause of termination:

- termination for **government default** or political force majeure typically entitles the private party to a payment covering outstanding debt, equity, and a return on equity, reflecting the principle that the government should bear the consequences of its own default or of events within its sphere of control;
- termination for **natural force majeure** typically entitles the private party to a payment covering outstanding debt, but not necessarily equity returns, reflecting the sharing of the risk of events outside either party's control;
- termination for **private party default** may entitle the private party to a reduced payment, often limited to outstanding senior debt or the market value of the project, so as to protect lenders while penalising the defaulting sponsor.

Upon termination of a concession or PPA, the project assets are commonly transferred to the government or its designee, either under build-operate-transfer arrangements or on payment of the termination amount. The adequacy and enforceability of termination payments are critical to the bankability of energy projects, and lenders scrutinise these provisions closely. Termination payment provisions also affect the calculation of damages in investment arbitration, where tribunals may use the contractual termination payment as a reference point for compensation, as examined in Chapter 26.

### Decommissioning and Post-Termination Obligations

Termination or expiry of an upstream contract or licence triggers obligations to decommission facilities and restore sites, as examined in Chapter 5. Contracts typically address the allocation of decommissioning costs among co-venturers, the establishment of decommissioning funds or security, and the continuing liability of former participants. The obligations of parties that have transferred their interests may continue under the contract, the JOA, or applicable legislation, as illustrated by the United Kingdom's regime under which the regulator may require former licensees to fund decommissioning if current licensees default. Post-termination obligations relating to confidentiality, data, environmental liabilities, and dispute resolution also survive termination.

**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. All parties, places, contracts, and events are fictitious and do not refer to any real persons, companies, or states.**

The Republic of Kelora has awarded a gas-to-power project to a consortium consisting of Northbridge Energy (fifty-one per cent), the Kelora National Petroleum Company (KNPC, thirty per cent), and Tamari Infrastructure Fund (nineteen per cent). The project comprises the development of an offshore gas field under a production sharing contract, a pipeline to shore, and a 600 MW combined-cycle power plant, which will sell electricity to the state-owned Kelora Power Distribution Company (KPDC) under a twenty-year PPA. The plant is to be built by a Korean EPC contractor under a lump-sum turnkey contract.

The following issues have arisen during negotiations and early implementation:

1. KNPC insists that the joint operating agreement for the upstream field require unanimous approval of all work programmes and budgets. Northbridge wishes to retain control as operator. Tamari is concerned about funding defaults by KNPC, which has a history of arrears on cash calls in other ventures.
2. The draft PPA defines "Change in Law" to cover only changes that discriminate specifically against the project. Tamari's lenders insist that the definition extend to general changes in tax law and to any carbon tax. KPDC refuses.
3. Eighteen months into construction, an insurgent attack on a coastal town fifty kilometres from the plant site leads the EPC contractor to withdraw its expatriate staff and declare force majeure. The consortium doubts that the attack prevents construction, since the site itself has not been attacked.
4. The EPC contract contains a mutual exclusion of "consequential loss", defined to include "loss of revenue, loss of profit, and loss of production". The contractor's delay will cause the project company to lose revenue under the PPA and to pay liquidated damages to KPDC for late commissioning.
5. The government of Kelora announces a review of all energy contracts concluded in the past ten years, on the ground that several were "unconscionable".

**Tasks.**

(a) Draft a voting and default regime for the JOA that balances the interests of the three parties.

(b) Prepare a negotiation brief for the consortium on the change-in-law definition, proposing a compromise acceptable to the lenders and KPDC.

(c) Advise the project company on whether the EPC contractor's force majeure declaration is valid, identifying the information needed and the relevant authorities.

(d) Advise whether the project company can recover from the contractor its lost PPA revenue and the liquidated damages payable to KPDC, in light of the consequential loss exclusion and the EPC delay damages regime.

(e) Advise the consortium on its legal position, under the contracts and international law, in the event that the contract review leads to a demand for renegotiation of the PPA tariff.

::: summary
- Energy projects rest on a web of interrelated contracts, whose terms must be coordinated to ensure that risks allocated under one contract are matched by corresponding protections under others.
- The formation and governing law of international energy contracts raise issues of pre-contractual liability, choice of law, the role of the UNIDROIT Principles and lex petrolea, and the internationalisation of state contracts.
- Host government agreements take the form of concessions, production sharing contracts, and service contracts, which allocate risk and reward between the state and investors in distinct ways.
- EPC contracts allocate construction risk to the contractor through fixed prices, completion dates, performance guarantees, and liquidated damages, whose enforceability is governed by the modern penalty doctrine.
- Joint operating agreements and shareholders' agreements govern relations among co-venturers, addressing operatorship, decision-making, sole risk, default, transfers, and deadlock.
- Force majeure clauses, hardship mechanisms, and the doctrine of frustration address supervening events, with English courts interpreting force majeure clauses strictly according to their terms.
- Change-in-law clauses require careful drafting of definitions, thresholds, and remedies, and increasingly address climate policy measures expressly.
- Liability allocation through knock-for-knock indemnities, consequential loss exclusions, liability caps, security instruments, and insurance determines who bears the cost of failures and incidents.
- Interpretation, renegotiation, government-initiated contract review, and termination payments determine how contracts respond to change over their long life.
:::
