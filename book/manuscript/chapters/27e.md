::: casebox {title="Case Study: The Lago Agrio Litigation and Chevron v Ecuador"}
**Background.** From 1964 to 1992, Texaco Petroleum Company (TexPet) operated oil fields in the Oriente region of the Ecuadorian Amazon in a consortium with the state oil company, later Petroecuador. Residents alleged that the operations caused extensive contamination through the discharge of produced water and the use of unlined waste pits. In 1993, residents sued Texaco in New York (*Aguinda v Texaco*), but the claims were dismissed in 2002 on the ground of forum non conveniens, on condition that Texaco submit to the jurisdiction of the Ecuadorian courts. Meanwhile, in 1995 and 1998, TexPet concluded settlement and release agreements with the government of Ecuador under which it carried out a remediation programme in exchange for a release from environmental claims by the state. Chevron acquired Texaco in 2001.

**The Lago Agrio judgment.** In 2003, residents sued Chevron in the Provincial Court of Sucumbíos in Lago Agrio, Ecuador. In **February 2011**, the court entered judgment against Chevron for around USD 18 billion, including punitive damages that would be removed if Chevron apologised; the National Court of Justice reduced the judgment to around **USD 9.5 billion** in 2013, and the Constitutional Court upheld it in 2018. Chevron, which had no significant assets in Ecuador, refused to pay.

**The RICO judgment.** Chevron sued the plaintiffs' lead US lawyer, Steven Donziger, and others in New York under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act. In **March 2014**, the District Court for the Southern District of New York held that the Lago Agrio judgment had been procured by fraud, including the bribery of the judge and the ghostwriting of the judgment by the plaintiffs' team, and imposed an injunction preventing Donziger and others from profiting from the judgment in the United States; the Second Circuit affirmed in 2016.

**The investment arbitration.** Chevron and TexPet commenced UNCITRAL arbitration under the US–Ecuador BIT, administered by the PCA. In **August 2018**, the tribunal held that Ecuador had committed a **denial of justice** under customary international law and breached the fair and equitable treatment and other obligations of the BIT, because the Lago Agrio judgment was procured through fraud and corruption, and that the 1995 settlement had released TexPet and its affiliates from diffuse environmental claims of the kind pursued in Lago Agrio. It ordered Ecuador to take steps to prevent the enforcement of the judgment. Ecuador's challenge to the award in the Dutch courts was rejected.

**Enforcement attempts.** The plaintiffs sought to enforce the judgment against Chevron's subsidiaries in **Canada**, **Brazil**, and **Argentina**. In *Chevron Corp v Yaiguaje* (2015), the Supreme Court of Canada held that the Ontario courts had jurisdiction to hear the enforcement action, as no real and substantial connection between the defendant and the forum was required in an action to enforce a foreign judgment; but the Ontario courts subsequently held that Chevron's Canadian subsidiary was a separate legal entity whose assets could not be seized to satisfy a judgment against its indirect parent, and the enforcement action failed. Enforcement actions in Brazil and Argentina also failed.

**Significance.** The saga illustrates the full complexity of transnational energy litigation: the consequences of forum non conveniens dismissals, the conflict between national courts and investment arbitration, the role of fraud and corruption, the limits of the enforcement of foreign judgments against corporate groups, and the question whether the communities affected by the contamination have obtained any remedy at all. It remains a central reference point in debates about corporate accountability, access to justice, and the integrity of litigation.
:::

## Conclusion

Courts are indispensable actors in energy law. They review the legality of regulatory and licensing decisions, with recent decisions such as *Finch* reshaping the assessment of fossil fuel projects; they adjudicate commercial disputes and develop the principles of energy contract law; they provide remedies for communities harmed by pollution and, increasingly, for the harms of climate change; they supervise regulatory enforcement; and they determine the reach of national jurisdiction over transnational corporate activity. Energy litigation is shaped by procedural questions — jurisdiction, justiciability, standing, evidence, causation, collective redress, funding, remedies, and enforcement — as much as by substantive law. Recent developments have expanded access to courts in some respects, as in the liberalisation of standing in Nigeria after *Centre for Oil Pollution Watch*, the recognition of parent company duties in *Vedanta*, *Okpabi*, and the Dutch Shell litigation, the BHP judgment, and the climate jurisprudence of the European Court of Human Rights, while narrowing it in others, as in the US Supreme Court's restrictions on the Alien Tort Statute and on agency deference. The Lago Agrio saga demonstrates that litigation can also become a theatre of fraud and conflict, and that remedies may remain elusive even after decades of proceedings. For the doctoral researcher and practitioner, the challenge is to understand litigation not only as a means of resolving disputes but as an instrument of governance and accountability, whose effectiveness depends on institutional design, access to justice, and the capacity to enforce outcomes. Part VIII turns to the role of scholarship, law reform, and professional leadership in shaping the future of energy and natural resources law.

::: summary
- Domestic courts resolve disputes, review governmental action, interpret constitutional resource provisions, enforce regulation, and develop the law; Nigeria's Federal High Court has exclusive jurisdiction over matters relating to mines, minerals, oil fields, and natural gas; specialised environmental courts exist in many jurisdictions; and justiciability doctrines vary, with US courts more restrictive than European courts in climate cases.
- Regulatory decisions may be challenged through merits appeals and judicial review, balancing accountability and regulatory certainty; the overruling of *Chevron* deference in *Loper Bright* has intensified judicial scrutiny of US energy regulation.
- Licensing decisions are reviewed for illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety, legitimate expectation, and proportionality; *Finch* (2024) and subsequent decisions require assessment of downstream emissions, and consultation failures have invalidated approvals in Australia, South Africa, and Canada.
- Environmental and climate litigation turns on standing, liberalised in Nigeria by *Centre for Oil Pollution Watch v NNPC* (2018), evidence and causation, including the burden of proving sabotage in oil spill cases, attribution science, recognised in principle in *Lliuya v RWE* (2025), and strategic choices of claimants, defendants, legal bases, fora, and remedies.
- Commercial litigation in energy contracts is significant in the English Commercial Court and the Nigerian Federal High Court, raising issues of contract interpretation, joint venture disputes, state immunity, and commodity trading.
- Collective redress mechanisms — US class actions, English group litigation and representative actions, EU representative actions, and the Dutch WAMCA — enable mass claims, as in the *Deepwater Horizon*, *Bodo*, and BHP Mariana litigation; Nigerian collective claims rely on representative actions.
- Remedies include damages, interim and final injunctions, declarations, structural orders, and remediation; regulators enforce through penalties, licence sanctions, and prosecution; enforcement of judgments, especially against public bodies and in environmental cases, remains difficult.
- Transnational litigation against parent companies depends on jurisdictional rules (Brussels I *bis*, forum non conveniens, substantial justice), as in *Lubbe*, *Vedanta*, *Okpabi*, and the Dutch Shell case; the ATS has been curtailed; the enforcement of foreign judgments faces corporate separateness, as in the Lago Agrio saga; and inter-state adjudication addresses transboundary resource disputes.
:::
