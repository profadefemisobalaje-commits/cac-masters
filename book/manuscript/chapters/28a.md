## Introduction

The preceding twenty-seven chapters have surveyed the substantive law of international energy and natural resources. This final Part turns from what the law *is* to how it is studied, reformed, and led. Doctoral research differs from professional legal work and from taught study not merely in its length but in its purpose: the doctoral researcher must make an **original contribution to knowledge** — establishing something that was not known, explaining something that was not understood, or proposing something that had not been conceived — and must do so through a method that others can scrutinise and evaluate. The quality of a doctoral thesis therefore depends as much on the rigour of its methodology as on the significance of its conclusions.

Energy and natural resources law presents distinctive methodological challenges. It is **multi-level**, spanning international treaties, regional instruments, national legislation, subnational regulation, and private contracts. It is **interdisciplinary**, intersecting with economics, engineering, geology, environmental science, political science, and international relations. It is **dynamic**, as the energy transition, technological change, and geopolitical upheaval transform the legal landscape faster than the literature can follow. Much of its most important law is **private or confidential** — petroleum agreements, arbitral awards, and regulatory correspondence — or exists in the practice of institutions rather than in published texts. And it is **normatively charged**, raising questions of justice, sovereignty, and sustainability on which researchers may hold strong views. These features require the researcher to make deliberate methodological choices and to justify them.

This chapter examines the epistemological foundations of legal scholarship (section 28.1), doctrinal legal research (section 28.2), comparative and transnational legal research (section 28.3), socio-legal and empirical research (section 28.4), qualitative and quantitative research approaches (section 28.5), research ethics and academic integrity (section 28.6), legal databases, primary authorities, and citation methods (section 28.7), and the development of original doctoral contributions (section 28.8). Figure 28.1 presents the research process as a sequence of stages, each requiring specific methodological decisions. Appendices N, O, and P provide a doctoral research proposal template, a model dissertation structure and evaluation criteria, and a guide to legal research, citation, and case analysis.

## 28.1 Epistemological Foundations of Legal Scholarship

### What Kind of Knowledge is Legal Knowledge?

**Epistemology** is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, sources, and limits of knowledge. Every research project rests, explicitly or implicitly, on epistemological assumptions: about what counts as knowledge, how it can be obtained, and how claims to knowledge can be justified. In legal scholarship, these questions are particularly contested, because law is simultaneously a body of authoritative norms, a social practice, a system of institutions, and an object of moral evaluation. The researcher who asks "what is the law governing the decommissioning of offshore installations?" is asking a different kind of question from one who asks "why do states fail to enforce decommissioning obligations?" or "what should the law on decommissioning be?", and each question calls for a different kind of knowledge and a different method of inquiry.

### The Internal Perspective: Law as a Normative System

The dominant tradition of legal scholarship adopts what H.L.A. Hart called the **internal point of view** — the perspective of participants in a legal system who accept its rules as standards for guiding and evaluating conduct. From this perspective, legal knowledge consists of knowledge of valid legal norms and their correct interpretation, and legal scholarship is a normative and interpretive discipline, closely allied with the practice of judges and lawyers. **Legal positivism**, associated with Hart, Hans Kelsen, and Joseph Raz, holds that the existence and content of law depend on social facts — such as enactment by a legislature or recognition by courts according to a rule of recognition — rather than on its moral merits. Positivism supports the conception of doctrinal scholarship as the systematic exposition of valid law, which can be undertaken without endorsing the law's moral value. **Natural law** theories, by contrast, hold that the validity or authority of law depends at least in part on its conformity with moral principles, and **interpretivism**, associated with Ronald Dworkin, holds that legal propositions are true if they figure in or follow from the principles of justice, fairness, and procedural due process that provide the best constructive interpretation of the community's legal practice. On interpretivist views, legal scholarship is inescapably evaluative, because determining what the law requires involves judgments about which interpretation shows the legal practice in its best light. These theories, examined in relation to energy law in Chapter 1, have practical consequences for research: a positivist researcher examining the legality of a stabilisation clause will focus on the sources and their authoritative interpretation; an interpretivist will ask how the clause fits within the principles that best justify the legal order, including principles of sovereignty and the right to regulate.

### The External Perspective: Law as a Social Phenomenon

An alternative tradition adopts the **external point of view**, studying law as a social phenomenon whose causes, functions, and effects can be investigated by the methods of the social sciences. **Legal realism**, in its American form associated with Oliver Wendell Holmes, Karl Llewellyn, and Jerome Frank, emphasised that law is what courts and officials actually do, rather than what rules say, and directed attention to the social and psychological factors that shape judicial decisions. Scandinavian realism, associated with Alf Ross, sought to reduce legal concepts to empirically observable phenomena. The **law and society** movement and **socio-legal studies** investigate the operation of law in society — how legal rules are implemented, used, avoided, and experienced — drawing on sociology, anthropology, and political science. **Law and economics** applies the tools of economic analysis to explain and evaluate legal rules, asking whether they promote efficiency, as in the analysis of regulatory regimes, property rights over resources, and liability rules for environmental harm examined in Chapters 3 and 17. From the external perspective, legal knowledge is a species of social scientific knowledge, to be established through empirical evidence and theory testing.

### Critical Perspectives

**Critical** approaches challenge the claims of both internal and external perspectives to neutrality and objectivity. **Critical legal studies** argued that legal doctrine is indeterminate and that legal reasoning conceals political choices that serve dominant interests. **Feminist legal theory** examines how law reflects and reinforces gender hierarchies, including in the distribution of the benefits and burdens of resource development — as in the gendered effects of displacement, compensation, and employment in extractive projects. **Critical race theory** and **Third World Approaches to International Law** (TWAIL), associated with scholars such as Antony Anghie, B.S. Chimni, and Makau Mutua, examine how international law, including the law of resources and investment, was shaped by colonialism and continues to reproduce inequalities between the Global North and South — a perspective of obvious relevance to the history of concessions, the doctrine of permanent sovereignty, and the contemporary governance of critical minerals examined throughout this book. **Environmental and ecological** approaches, including "Earth jurisprudence" and the rights of nature examined in Chapter 24, question the anthropocentrism of legal thought. Critical perspectives emphasise **reflexivity**: the researcher's awareness of his or her own position, assumptions, and interests and their influence on the research.

### Paradigms and Methodological Pluralism

Social science research methodology distinguishes **research paradigms** — sets of assumptions about the nature of reality (**ontology**) and knowledge (**epistemology**). **Positivist** (in the social scientific rather than legal sense) or **post-positivist** paradigms assume that there is an objective reality that can be known, at least approximately, through observation and measurement, and favour quantitative methods and hypothesis testing. **Interpretivist** or **constructivist** paradigms hold that social reality is constructed through human meaning and interaction, and favour qualitative methods that seek to understand the meanings actors attach to their actions. **Critical** paradigms seek not only to understand but to transform social reality, exposing power relations. **Pragmatist** paradigms, associated with philosophers such as John Dewey, judge methods by their usefulness in answering the research question, and support **mixed methods** research.

For the doctoral researcher in energy law, the lesson is not that one epistemology is correct, but that the choice of research question entails epistemological commitments that must be recognised and made consistent with the chosen methods. A thesis that asks a doctrinal question but answers it with anecdotal empirical claims, or that asks an empirical question but answers it through doctrinal analysis alone, suffers from a mismatch between question and method. **Methodological pluralism** — the combination of different approaches within a single project — can enrich legal research, provided that the researcher is explicit about how the approaches relate to each other and what each contributes.

### Types of Research Questions

Research questions in law may be classified by the kind of knowledge they seek:

1. **Descriptive** or **expository** questions ask what the law is: "What obligations does the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 impose on settlors of host community development trusts?"
2. **Explanatory** questions ask why the law is as it is, or why it has particular effects: "Why have host community development trusts failed to reduce conflict in some Niger Delta communities?"
3. **Evaluative** questions ask how well the law achieves its objectives or conforms to a standard: "Do host community development trusts satisfy the international standards for benefit-sharing with affected communities?"
4. **Normative** or **prescriptive** questions ask what the law should be: "How should Nigerian law be reformed to ensure equitable benefit-sharing with host communities?"
5. **Comparative** questions ask how legal systems differ and what can be learned from the differences: "What can Nigeria learn from the benefit-sharing regimes of Ghana, Canada, and Australia?"
6. **Historical** questions ask how the law developed and what explains its trajectory: "How did the concept of host community benefits evolve in Nigerian petroleum legislation from 1969 to 2021?"

Most doctoral theses combine several types — typically moving from description and explanation through evaluation to normative proposals — and the research design should make clear how each type of question will be answered.
