## 6.5 Long-Term Gas Sales and Purchase Agreements

### Function and Forms

The gas sales and purchase agreement (GSPA, or SPA in the LNG context) is the central commercial instrument of the gas industry. It allocates between seller and buyer the volume risk (the risk that the buyer will not need, or the seller will not be able to supply, the contracted quantities), the price risk (the risk that market prices will move against one party), and the operational risks of delivery. Long-term GSPAs of fifteen to twenty-five years' duration underpin the financing of most major pipeline and LNG export projects. Medium-term contracts (two to ten years), short-term contracts (up to two years), and spot transactions have grown in importance with the development of liquid hubs and the LNG spot market, and are commonly concluded under master agreements that set out general terms, with individual transactions confirmed by short confirmation notices.

Several standard forms are used in the industry. For trading on European hubs, the European Federation of Energy Traders (EFET) General Agreement Concerning the Delivery and Acceptance of Natural Gas is widely used; in North America, the North American Energy Standards Board (NAESB) Base Contract for Sale and Purchase of Natural Gas performs a similar function. For LNG, the Association of International Energy Negotiators (AIEN) has published a Model Master LNG Sale and Purchase Agreement, and the International Group of Liquefied Natural Gas Importers (GIIGNL) has published a Master LNG Sale and Purchase Agreement for spot and short-term trades. Long-term SPAs, by contrast, are typically bespoke, although they follow well-established structures. Appendix D contains a model international gas sales agreement with annotations.

### Contract Quantity Provisions

The quantity provisions of a long-term GSPA typically define:

- the **Annual Contract Quantity (ACQ)**, the quantity of gas the seller undertakes to make available and the buyer undertakes to take or pay for in each contract year;
- the **Daily Contract Quantity (DCQ)**, usually the ACQ divided by the number of days in the year, and the **Maximum Daily Quantity**, often 110 or 120 per cent of the DCQ, which defines the buyer's daily flexibility;
- **downward quantity tolerance** or **downward flexibility**, permitting the buyer to reduce its annual nominations by a specified percentage without incurring take-or-pay liability;
- adjustments to the ACQ for gas not taken because of the seller's failure to deliver, force majeure, or the buyer's rejection of off-specification gas; and
- the **build-up period** at the start of the contract and the **plateau period** during which the ACQ remains constant.

LNG SPAs specify quantities in terms of the Annual Contract Quantity expressed in tonnes or million British thermal units, and contain an **Annual Delivery Programme** procedure under which the parties agree before each contract year on the schedule of cargoes, subject to adjustments for operational reasons. The seller may have rights to reduce quantities for scheduled maintenance of the liquefaction facilities, and the buyer may have rights to divert or cancel cargoes, depending on the terms of the contract.

### Delivery Point and Transfer of Title and Risk

The GSPA defines the delivery point at which title and risk pass from seller to buyer. In pipeline contracts, the delivery point may be at the outlet of the seller's processing facilities, at a border crossing, at a trading hub, or at the buyer's facilities. In LNG contracts, the delivery point depends on whether the contract is FOB (title and risk pass at the loading port, usually as the LNG passes the flange connecting the loading arms to the vessel's manifold) or DES (title and risk pass at the discharge port). Under a CIF contract, risk passes at the loading port but the seller arranges and pays for shipping and insurance. The choice affects which party controls the vessel and, consequently, the ability to divert cargoes.

### Quality and Measurement

GSPAs specify the quality of gas to be delivered and the consequences of off-specification deliveries. The buyer may be entitled to reject off-specification gas, or to accept it subject to a price reduction or compensation for costs incurred in treating it. The allocation of risk for off-specification gas is particularly important where the gas is to be used in sensitive equipment, such as gas turbines, or injected into networks with strict specifications. Measurement provisions define the methods, standards, and equipment to be used for measuring quantity and quality, the procedures for calibration and testing, and the resolution of measurement disputes, often by reference to an independent surveyor in the LNG context.

### Destination Clauses and Diversion Rights

Traditional long-term gas contracts frequently contained **destination clauses** (or territorial restriction clauses) prohibiting the buyer from reselling gas outside a defined territory, and **profit-sharing mechanisms** requiring the buyer to share with the seller any profit from resale to a different destination. Sellers defended these provisions as necessary to prevent arbitrage between markets in which they sold at different prices, and to protect their pricing strategies. Buyers and competition authorities criticised them as restrictions on competition and on the development of integrated markets.

In the European Union, destination clauses in contracts with EU buyers have been treated as restrictions of competition contrary to Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). From the early 2000s, the European Commission pursued investigations concerning the contracts of suppliers including Gazprom, Sonatrach, and Nigeria LNG, resulting in the removal of destination clauses from their contracts with European buyers. In its 2018 decision accepting commitments from Gazprom in Case AT.39816 (*Upstream Gas Supplies in Central and Eastern Europe*), the Commission addressed concerns that Gazprom had pursued an overall strategy of partitioning Central and Eastern European gas markets, including through territorial restrictions, and Gazprom committed to remove contractual barriers to the cross-border resale of gas and to facilitate gas flows to isolated markets. In 2018 the Commission also opened an investigation into whether Qatar Petroleum's long-term LNG supply agreements with European importers contained territorial restrictions.

In Asia, the Japan Fair Trade Commission published a report in 2017 concluding that destination restrictions in FOB LNG contracts were likely to violate Japan's Antimonopoly Act, and that profit-sharing mechanisms and certain restrictions in DES contracts were also problematic. The report has encouraged Japanese buyers to negotiate greater flexibility in new contracts, contributing, together with the growth of United States LNG supplied on destination-free FOB terms, to the increasing liquidity and flexibility of the global LNG market.

### Force Majeure and Seller's Failure to Deliver

GSPAs contain detailed force majeure provisions excusing performance where a party is prevented from performing by events beyond its reasonable control. In the gas context, force majeure clauses often enumerate specific events, such as failures of facilities, governmental action, and natural disasters, and may treat certain events — such as failures of the seller's upstream supply or of the buyer's downstream market — as either within or outside the clause depending on the parties' negotiated allocation of risk. Force majeure in energy contracts is examined in Chapter 15; here it is sufficient to note that the treatment of upstream and transportation failures has been a recurring source of disputes in gas contracts, and that the 2022 crisis generated significant litigation concerning Gazprom's declaration of force majeure in relation to deliveries to European buyers.

GSPAs also provide remedies for the seller's failure to deliver contracted quantities otherwise than as a result of force majeure. These commonly include a price discount on shortfall quantities, sometimes expressed as a percentage of the contract price, and may be the buyer's exclusive remedy for shortfalls up to a specified level. The adequacy of such remedies was tested during the 2022 crisis, when replacement gas cost many times the contract price; buyers argued that the contractual shortfall remedies did not apply to deliberate non-performance, and sought full damages in arbitration.

## 6.6 Price Review, Take-or-Pay, and Contractual Flexibility

### Take-or-Pay

The **take-or-pay** clause is the defining feature of the long-term gas contract. It obliges the buyer to pay for a minimum quantity of gas in each contract year (the "take-or-pay quantity", often 80 to 90 per cent of the ACQ, after allowing for downward flexibility) whether or not it actually takes delivery of that quantity. If the buyer takes less than the minimum, it must pay for the shortfall, but it typically acquires a right to take the paid-for but untaken gas ("make-up gas") in subsequent years, usually after it has taken the ACQ for that year, and sometimes at the price applicable at the time of payment. Conversely, if the buyer takes more than the ACQ in a year, it may accumulate "carry-forward" credits that reduce its minimum take obligation in future years.

The economic function of take-or-pay is to allocate volume risk to the buyer, providing the seller with predictable revenues sufficient to service the debt raised to finance the production and transportation facilities. In return, the buyer obtains security of supply over the long term. Lenders to gas projects typically require a substantial proportion of the project's output to be committed under take-or-pay contracts with creditworthy buyers before they will provide financing.

The enforceability of take-or-pay clauses has been challenged on the ground that they are penalties. Under English law, the rule against penalties applies only to clauses that operate upon a breach of contract; a clause that provides for payment as a primary obligation in the event of a contractual contingency, rather than a breach, is not subject to the rule. In *M&J Polymers Ltd v Imerys Minerals Ltd*, the High Court considered a take-or-pay clause in a supply contract and held that, although the rule against penalties was potentially applicable on the particular construction of that clause, the clause was commercially justifiable and not a penalty.[^mj] Following the restatement of the penalty rule in *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi* (discussed in Chapter 5), the predominant view is that a properly drafted take-or-pay clause, which creates a primary obligation to pay for gas made available, does not engage the penalty rule at all, and that even if it did, it would usually be justified by the seller's legitimate interest in recovering its investment. In the United States, courts have generally upheld take-or-pay clauses, treating them as alternative performance obligations, although extensive litigation followed the collapse of gas prices in the 1980s, when pipeline companies that had contracted to take gas at high prices sought to escape their take-or-pay obligations.

[^mj]: *M&J Polymers Ltd v Imerys Minerals Ltd* [2008] EWHC 344 (Comm), [2008] 1 Lloyd's Rep 541.

### Price Indexation

The price provisions of a long-term GSPA typically combine a base price with an indexation formula that adjusts the price periodically to reflect changes in the value of gas. In oil-indexed contracts, the formula links the gas price to the prices of crude oil, gas oil, or fuel oil, often averaged over a preceding period (for example, six or nine months) and applied with a time lag, smoothing the effect of short-term volatility. In hub-indexed contracts, the price is linked to a hub price, such as TTF or NBP, perhaps with a fixed discount or premium. Hybrid formulas combine oil and hub indexation.

### Price Review

Because long-term contracts run for decades, and because the indexation formula may cease to reflect the market value of gas as markets evolve, most long-term GSPAs contain a **price review** (or price reopener) clause permitting either party to request a revision of the price formula at specified intervals (commonly every three or five years) and, in some cases, at any time upon the occurrence of exceptional changes in market circumstances ("wildcard" reviews). Price review clauses typically provide that if, since the last price review, there has been a significant change in the economic circumstances in the buyer's market beyond the control of the parties, and the contract price no longer reflects the value of gas in that market, the parties shall negotiate in good faith a revision of the price formula. If they fail to agree within a specified period, either party may refer the matter to arbitration or to an expert.

Price review clauses raise distinctive legal issues. The *trigger* question is whether the conditions for a revision are satisfied — whether there has been a sufficiently significant change in circumstances of the specified kind. The *revision* question is what the revised price should be — what price reflects the value of gas in the buyer's market, often measured by reference to the prices paid by comparable buyers under comparable contracts or by reference to the "netback" value of gas from the price of competing fuels. The *scope* question is what the tribunal is empowered to change — whether it may alter only the base price, or also the indexation, the weighting of indices, or the introduction of hub indexation.

The wave of price review arbitrations that followed the decoupling of European hub prices from oil-indexed contract prices after 2009 — when the development of hub trading and the arrival of LNG intended for the United States market led to hub prices falling well below oil-indexed prices — transformed the European gas market. European buyers, committed to purchasing large quantities at oil-indexed prices while their customers could buy cheaper gas at the hubs, invoked price review clauses against their suppliers, including Gazprom, Statoil (now Equinor), and Sonatrach. Many disputes were settled through renegotiation, often introducing a significant element of hub indexation; others were decided by arbitral tribunals, which in a number of reported cases accepted that the emergence of hub prices was a relevant change in circumstances and introduced hub indexation into the contract formula. Because most price review awards are confidential, the jurisprudence is known primarily through practitioners' commentaries and the limited number of awards that have entered the public domain through court proceedings.

One such case is *Atlantic LNG Co of Trinidad and Tobago v Gas Natural Aprovisionamientos SDG SA*, in which a price review award in favour of Gas Natural concerning LNG supplied from Trinidad was challenged before the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. The court confirmed the award in 2008, rejecting the argument that the tribunal had exceeded its powers in determining the revised price.[^alng] The case illustrates the deference shown by courts in leading arbitral seats to tribunals' interpretation of price review clauses.

[^alng]: *Atlantic LNG Co of Trinidad and Tobago v Gas Natural Aprovisionamientos SDG SA*, (SDNY 2008).

### Hardship and Price Review Compared

Price review clauses perform a function similar to hardship clauses in general commercial contracts and to doctrines such as *imprévision* in French administrative law, *Wegfall der Geschäftsgrundlage* (now codified in § 313 of the German Civil Code), and Article 6.2.2 of the UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts. They differ, however, in that they are designed to operate routinely and periodically, to keep the price aligned with the market, rather than to address exceptional events that fundamentally alter the equilibrium of the contract. A price review tribunal is not concerned with whether performance has become excessively onerous for one party, but with whether the contract price reflects the market value of gas. Chapter 15 examines hardship clauses in energy contracts generally.

### Contractual Flexibility in a Changing Market

The trend in gas contracting over the past two decades has been towards greater flexibility: shorter contract durations, smaller volumes, greater downward quantity tolerance, removal of destination restrictions, hub indexation, and rights to divert and cancel cargoes. In the LNG market, portfolio players — international oil companies and trading houses that aggregate supply from multiple projects and sell to multiple buyers — have intermediated between sellers seeking long-term commitments and buyers seeking flexibility.

The 2021–2023 crisis complicated this trend. The extreme volatility of hub prices and the scarcity of uncommitted LNG led some buyers, particularly in Asia and in Europe, to sign long-term contracts again, including twenty-year contracts with Qatari and United States suppliers, while European buyers sought to balance supply security against the risk that gas demand would fall over the life of the contract as a result of decarbonisation policies. The resulting contracts frequently include provisions addressing the energy transition, such as rights to reduce volumes in response to changes in climate policy, commitments to reduce methane emissions across the supply chain, and provisions for the future supply of low-carbon gases. The capacity of contract law to accommodate the energy transition is examined further in Chapter 15.
