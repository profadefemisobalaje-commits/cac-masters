## 20.3 Continental Shelf Resource Rights

### The Legal Concept of the Continental Shelf

The continental shelf has both a geological and a legal meaning. Geologically, the continental margin — the submerged prolongation of the land mass — comprises the shelf, a gently sloping platform; the slope, where the seabed descends more steeply; and the rise, which merges with the deep ocean floor. Legally, under Article 76(1) of UNCLOS, the continental shelf of a coastal state comprises the seabed and subsoil of the submarine areas that extend beyond its territorial sea throughout the **natural prolongation** of its land territory to the outer edge of the continental margin, or to a distance of **two hundred nautical miles** from the baselines where the outer edge of the margin does not extend up to that distance.

The definition therefore combines two criteria. Every coastal state has a continental shelf of at least two hundred nautical miles, regardless of the geology of the seabed — a distance criterion that coincides with the EEZ. Where the continental margin extends further, the coastal state's shelf extends to the outer edge of the margin, determined by one of two formulae in Article 76(4): a line connecting points at which the thickness of sedimentary rocks is at least one per cent of the shortest distance to the foot of the continental slope (the "Gardiner" or sediment-thickness formula), or a line connecting points not more than sixty nautical miles from the foot of the slope (the "Hedberg" formula). The outer limit is subject to maximum constraints: it may not exceed **350 nautical miles** from the baselines, or **100 nautical miles from the 2,500-metre isobath** (Article 76(5)), with special rules for submarine ridges.

### The Nature of Coastal State Rights

Under Article 77, the coastal state exercises over the continental shelf **sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring it and exploiting its natural resources**. These rights are **exclusive** in the sense that, if the coastal state does not explore or exploit the shelf, no one may undertake these activities without its express consent (Article 77(2)). They **do not depend on occupation**, effective or notional, or on any express proclamation (Article 77(3)). The natural resources comprise the mineral and other non-living resources of the seabed and subsoil together with sedentary species (Article 77(4)).

These provisions codify the principle stated by the International Court of Justice in the *North Sea Continental Shelf* cases (1969), in which the Court held that the rights of the coastal state in respect of the shelf exist *ipso facto* and *ab initio*, by virtue of its sovereignty over the land, as an extension of it in the form of an exercise of sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring the seabed and exploiting its natural resources. The rights of the coastal state over the continental shelf do not affect the legal status of the superjacent waters or airspace (Article 78), and the exercise of these rights must not infringe or result in unjustifiable interference with navigation and other rights and freedoms of other states.

Under Article 81, the coastal state has the **exclusive right to authorise and regulate drilling** on the continental shelf for all purposes. Article 80 applies the regime of Article 60 on artificial islands, installations, and structures *mutatis mutandis* to those on the continental shelf. Article 85 preserves the right of the coastal state to exploit the subsoil by means of tunnelling, irrespective of the depth of water. Together, these provisions constitute the international legal foundation for national petroleum licensing systems offshore, examined in Chapter 5, and for the regulation of offshore installations, examined in section 20.4.

### Delineation of the Outer Continental Shelf

A coastal state intending to establish the outer limits of its continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles must submit information on the limits, with supporting scientific and technical data, to the **Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf** within ten years of the entry into force of the Convention for that state (Article 76(8) and Annex II, Article 4) — a deadline modified by a decision of the Meeting of States Parties in 2001, which allowed states to submit preliminary information to satisfy it. The Commission makes recommendations on matters related to the establishment of the outer limits, and the limits established by a coastal state on the basis of these recommendations are **final and binding** (Article 76(8)). The coastal state must deposit charts and information permanently describing the outer limits with the UN Secretary-General (Article 76(9)).

The Commission has received close to one hundred submissions, and its workload is such that some submissions will not be considered for many years. Under its Rules of Procedure, the Commission will not consider a submission concerning an area subject to a land or maritime dispute without the prior consent of all states that are parties to the dispute. The delineation of the outer limits, which concerns the boundary between the continental shelf and the Area, is distinct from **delimitation** between the continental shelves of opposite or adjacent states, addressed in section 20.8, and Article 76(10) provides that the provisions of the article are without prejudice to delimitation.

Many African coastal states, including Nigeria, have made submissions to the Commission, often with technical support from international programmes. **Nigeria** submitted information on the outer limits of its continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles in 2009, claiming an extended shelf in the Gulf of Guinea, and the submission was taken up for consideration by the Commission. The **Gulf of Guinea** is a region in which extended continental shelf claims overlap, requiring coordination among Nigeria, Benin, Togo, Ghana, São Tomé and Príncipe, Equatorial Guinea, and Cameroon. Since rights to the shelf exist *ipso facto*, a coastal state has sovereign rights over its extended continental shelf whether or not its outer limits have been established, but the establishment of final and binding limits provides legal certainty for investors in deepwater exploration.

### Relationship between the Continental Shelf and the EEZ

Within two hundred nautical miles, the seabed and subsoil are subject to both the continental shelf regime and the EEZ regime, with Article 56(3) providing that seabed rights in the EEZ are exercised in accordance with Part VI. The continental shelf regime is not dependent on a claim, while the EEZ must be proclaimed. Beyond two hundred nautical miles, the coastal state has rights over the seabed and subsoil of the extended continental shelf, but the superjacent waters are high seas. This raises particular issues where the extended continental shelf of one state lies within the EEZ of another — the "grey area" created by maritime delimitation in the *Bay of Bengal* cases (*Bangladesh/Myanmar*, ITLOS 2012; *Bangladesh v India*, Annex VII 2014), in which tribunals delimited the continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles in a manner that created an area where the shelf belonged to one state and the water column to another, and called on the parties to cooperate. In *Nicaragua v Colombia* (Question of the Delimitation of the Continental Shelf beyond 200 Nautical Miles, 2023), the International Court of Justice held that under customary international law a state's entitlement to a continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles from its baselines may not extend within two hundred nautical miles of the baselines of another state.

### Payments for Exploitation beyond 200 Nautical Miles

Article 82, a compromise between broad-margin states and states that favoured a narrower shelf, requires the coastal state to make **payments or contributions in kind** in respect of the exploitation of the non-living resources of the continental shelf beyond two hundred nautical miles. Payments are made annually with respect to all production at a site after the first five years of production. In the sixth year, the rate is one per cent of the value or volume of production at the site, rising by one per cent each year until the twelfth year, after which it remains at seven per cent. Production does not include resources used in connection with exploitation. A developing state that is a net importer of a mineral resource produced from its continental shelf is exempt in respect of that resource. Payments are made through the ISA, which distributes them to states parties on the basis of equitable sharing criteria, taking into account the interests and needs of developing states, particularly the least developed and landlocked among them.

Article 82 has yet to be applied in practice, but offshore production beyond two hundred nautical miles is approaching in several regions, and the ISA has undertaken work on its implementation, including on the definition of the "value" of production, the identification of the production site, and the procedures for distribution. Article 82 is significant for petroleum fiscal regimes, since it imposes on the coastal state an international obligation that will be passed through, in practice, to licensees in the design of production sharing contracts and licences for extended continental shelf areas.

## 20.4 Offshore Petroleum and Gas Development

### The Layered Legal Framework

Offshore petroleum and gas development is governed by several layers of law: the international law of the sea, which allocates jurisdiction and imposes obligations relating to navigation, safety, and environmental protection; international standards adopted by the International Maritime Organization and other bodies; regional agreements, particularly regional seas conventions; national petroleum legislation and licences or contracts; and, for floating facilities, the law of the flag state. Offshore operations present distinctive legal issues relating to the status and safety of installations, the jurisdiction applicable to persons and activities on them, the security of operations, and their decommissioning.

### National Legislation

Coastal states regulate offshore petroleum operations under legislation that applies to their territorial sea and continental shelf. The United States **Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act** of 1953 asserts federal jurisdiction over the outer continental shelf beyond state waters, applies federal law and, as surrogate federal law, the law of the adjacent state, and governs leasing by the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management and safety regulation by the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement — a separation of functions introduced after the *Deepwater Horizon* disaster of 2010, examined in Chapter 17. The United Kingdom's **Petroleum Act 1998** vests petroleum in the Crown and provides for licensing of the continental shelf, and the Continental Shelf Act 1964 applies civil and criminal law to installations. Norway's **Petroleum Activities Act** of 1996 governs licensing and operations on the Norwegian continental shelf. In **Nigeria**, the **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** governs upstream operations onshore, in shallow water, and in deep offshore areas, with the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission as regulator, and provides distinct fiscal terms for deep offshore and frontier acreage. The Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contract Act, as amended in 2019, governs the royalty terms of deep offshore production sharing contracts.

### Installations: Status, Safety, and Jurisdiction

Offshore installations range from fixed steel or concrete platforms to floating production, storage, and offloading vessels (FPSOs), drilling rigs, and subsea production systems connected to onshore or floating facilities. Under Articles 60 and 80, the coastal state has exclusive jurisdiction over installations on its continental shelf and in its EEZ, including with regard to safety, health, customs, fiscal, and immigration laws. Floating installations raise questions about the relationship between coastal state jurisdiction and **flag state** jurisdiction, since FPSOs and mobile drilling units are often registered as ships. The International Maritime Organization's **Code for the Construction and Equipment of Mobile Offshore Drilling Units** (MODU Code) establishes international standards, while coastal states apply their own safety regimes. The Nigerian fleet of FPSOs, including those at Bonga, Agbami, Akpo, and Egina, operates under Nigerian regulatory oversight while also subject to classification society and flag state requirements.

The **Piper Alpha** disaster in the United Kingdom North Sea in 1988, in which 167 people died, led to the Cullen Report and a fundamental shift to a goal-setting "safety case" regime, under which operators must demonstrate to the regulator that major accident hazards have been identified and risks reduced to as low as reasonably practicable. The *Deepwater Horizon* disaster led to similar reforms in the United States and to the European Union's **Offshore Safety Directive** (2013/30/EU), which requires reports on major hazards, independent verification, and the assessment of the financial capacity of licensees to meet liabilities. These regimes, examined further in Chapter 17, illustrate the transfer of regulatory models among jurisdictions.

### Safety Zones and Navigation

Coastal states establish **safety zones** of up to five hundred metres around installations, within which entry by unauthorised vessels is prohibited. In the *Arctic Sunrise* arbitration (*Netherlands v Russia*, Annex VII, 2015), arising from the boarding and detention by Russia of a Greenpeace vessel and its crew after a protest at the Prirazlomnaya oil platform in the Russian EEZ in the Pechora Sea in 2013, the tribunal held that Russia had breached its obligations to the Netherlands as flag state. It recognised that a coastal state may take measures to protect its sovereign rights and to prevent interference with installations, including the enforcement of safety zones, and that protest at sea is an internationally lawful use of the sea related to the freedom of navigation, but held that the boarding, seizure, and detention of the vessel were not justified on any of the grounds advanced by Russia. The award illustrates the balance between coastal state rights over offshore installations and the freedoms of other states.

### Maritime Security in the Gulf of Guinea

The Gulf of Guinea has been one of the world's principal areas of maritime insecurity, with piracy and armed robbery against ships, kidnapping of crews, crude oil theft, and attacks on offshore installations. Under UNCLOS, piracy is defined as illegal acts of violence or detention committed for private ends on the high seas or in the EEZ by the crew or passengers of a private ship against another ship (Article 101), and all states may seize pirate ships on the high seas and exercise jurisdiction over pirates (Article 105). Acts committed in the territorial sea are "armed robbery at sea", within the exclusive jurisdiction of the coastal state.

The regional response is founded on the **Yaoundé Code of Conduct** of 2013, adopted by ECOWAS, the Economic Community of Central African States, and the Gulf of Guinea Commission, which established an architecture of regional and zonal maritime security centres for information sharing and coordination. **Nigeria** enacted the **Suppression of Piracy and Other Maritime Offences Act 2019**, the first standalone anti-piracy legislation in West and Central Africa, which criminalises piracy and armed robbery at sea and provides for universal jurisdiction over piracy; prosecutions and convictions under the Act followed within a few years of its enactment. Nigeria also implemented the **Integrated National Security and Waterways Protection Infrastructure** (the "Deep Blue Project"), launched in 2021, comprising aircraft, vessels, and command and control facilities. Reported incidents of piracy in the Gulf of Guinea declined substantially from their peak in 2020, although insecurity, including crude oil theft from pipelines and offshore facilities, remains a serious concern for the petroleum industry. The protection of energy infrastructure against security threats is examined further in Chapter 25.

### Decommissioning

Article 60(3) requires that abandoned or disused installations be removed to ensure the safety of navigation, taking into account generally accepted international standards established by the competent international organisation, and that appropriate publicity be given to any installation not entirely removed. The **IMO Guidelines and Standards for the Removal of Offshore Installations and Structures** (1989) require the removal of installations in water depths of less than seventy-five metres and weighing less than four thousand tonnes in air, and of all installations placed after 1998 in water depths of less than one hundred metres and weighing less than four thousand tonnes, while permitting partial removal of larger structures subject to conditions. In the North-East Atlantic, **OSPAR Decision 98/3** prohibits the dumping or leaving wholly or partly in place of disused offshore installations, subject to derogations for the footings of large steel jackets and concrete installations — a regime that followed the controversy over the proposed deep-sea disposal of the *Brent Spar* storage buoy in 1995. The regulation of decommissioning, including its financial security, has been examined in Chapters 5 and 17. A growing question is whether offshore installations and pipelines may be repurposed for carbon capture and storage, hydrogen transport, or offshore renewable energy, rather than removed — an approach that raises issues of liability transfer and long-term stewardship.

### Offshore Carbon Storage and Other Marine Energy Activities

The legal framework for offshore activities is being extended to new uses. The storage of carbon dioxide in sub-seabed geological formations is regulated under the **London Protocol** (1996) to the London Dumping Convention, which was amended in 2006 to permit such storage subject to conditions and in 2009 to allow the cross-border export of carbon dioxide for storage — an amendment provisionally applied by agreement of the parties from 2019 pending its entry into force. National legislation, such as the EU CCS Directive (2009/31/EC), the UK Energy Act 2008, and Norway's regulations, governs the licensing of offshore storage sites; Norway's Northern Lights project began injecting carbon dioxide from European emitters in 2025. **Offshore wind**, including floating wind in deeper waters, **wave and tidal energy**, **ocean thermal energy conversion**, and **offshore hydrogen production** are governed by the coastal state's rights under Article 56(1)(a), national leasing regimes, and the due regard and navigation-related constraints of UNCLOS. **Hybrid projects**, connecting offshore wind farms in the EEZs of more than one state to multiple national grids, raise novel questions of jurisdiction, market regulation, and the allocation of costs and benefits, which the North Seas states and the European Union are addressing through cooperation agreements and the EU's Trans-European Networks for Energy Regulation.
