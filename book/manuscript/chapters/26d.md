## 26.7 Recognition and Enforcement of Arbitral Awards

### The New York Convention

The **Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards** (New York Convention), concluded in 1958, is the cornerstone of the international enforcement of non-ICSID awards. Its contracting states, now numbering more than 170, including Nigeria (which acceded in 1970 and gave effect to the Convention in the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1988, now the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023), undertake to recognise arbitration agreements (Article II) and to recognise and enforce foreign arbitral awards (Article III), subject only to the grounds for refusal in **Article V**. Many states, including Nigeria, have made the **reciprocity reservation**, applying the Convention only to awards made in the territory of other contracting states, and the **commercial reservation**, applying it only to differences arising out of legal relationships considered commercial under national law.

Under **Article V(1)**, recognition and enforcement may be refused, at the request of the party against whom the award is invoked, only if that party proves: (a) the **incapacity** of a party or the **invalidity of the arbitration agreement**; (b) that it was not given proper **notice** of the appointment of the arbitrator or the proceedings or was otherwise **unable to present its case**; (c) that the award deals with matters **beyond the scope** of the submission to arbitration; (d) that the composition of the tribunal or the procedure was **not in accordance with the agreement** of the parties or, failing such agreement, the law of the seat; or (e) that the award has **not yet become binding**, or has been **set aside or suspended** by a competent authority of the country in which, or under the law of which, it was made. Under **Article V(2)**, recognition and enforcement may also be refused if the competent authority finds that the subject matter is **not arbitrable** under its law or that recognition or enforcement would be contrary to its **public policy**. The grounds are exhaustive, the burden of proof lies on the resisting party, and courts have a discretion ("may be refused") to enforce even where a ground is established. The **pro-enforcement bias** of the Convention has been recognised by courts in many jurisdictions, and the public policy ground has generally been interpreted narrowly, as referring to the forum's most basic notions of morality and justice, or "international public policy".

### Enforcement of Annulled Awards

The effect of the setting aside of an award at the seat on its enforcement elsewhere is contested. Most jurisdictions treat an award set aside at the seat as having no legal existence, so that it cannot be enforced, applying Article V(1)(e). French courts, however, have held that an international award is not anchored in the legal order of the seat, and may be enforced in France notwithstanding its annulment at the seat, as in *Hilmarton* (1994) and *Putrabali* (2007). The US courts, in ***Corporación Mexicana de Mantenimiento Integral v Pemex-Exploración y Producción*** (Second Circuit, 2016), a dispute concerning offshore oil platform construction contracts, upheld the enforcement of an award set aside in Mexico on the ground that the annulment, based on legislation retroactively depriving the claimant of its remedy, violated basic notions of justice. The English courts, in *Yukos Capital v Rosneft* (2014), held that an English court is not bound to recognise a foreign annulment decision that offends basic principles of honesty, natural justice, and domestic concepts of public policy. The approach of these courts reflects concern about annulments procured through partiality or abuse by the courts of a state party.

### Sovereign Immunity from Enforcement and Execution

The enforcement of awards against **states and state entities** confronts the doctrine of **state immunity**. Under the **restrictive theory** of state immunity, now prevailing in most jurisdictions and reflected in the **UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and Their Property** (2004, not yet in force), the US **Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act 1976**, and the UK **State Immunity Act 1978**, states are not immune from jurisdiction in respect of commercial transactions and in proceedings relating to arbitration agreements to which they have agreed. However, immunity from **execution** against state property is broader: property used or intended for use for **governmental (non-commercial) purposes** — including diplomatic premises, military property, and, under many statutes, the property of central banks — is generally immune from measures of constraint, unless the state has expressly waived immunity from execution in respect of that property. The identification of commercial assets of a state that are available for execution, and the attribution of the assets of state-owned enterprises to the state, are therefore crucial to the enforcement of awards against states.

Award creditors in energy disputes have pursued creative enforcement strategies. The creditors of the *Yukos* awards sought to attach Russian state assets in several jurisdictions, including France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and the United States, with mixed results. The creditors of Venezuela, including ConocoPhillips and Crystallex, obtained from the US courts an order permitting the sale of the shares in **PDV Holding**, the parent company of **CITGO**, owned by PDVSA, on the basis that PDVSA was the **alter ego** of Venezuela under the US Supreme Court's decision in *First National City Bank v Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba* (*Bancec*, 1983), which establishes a presumption of the separate juridical status of state instrumentalities that may be rebutted where the state exercises extensive control over the entity; the court-supervised auction process resulted in the approval of a sale in 2025. Award creditors of Argentina, Kazakhstan, Spain, and other states have also pursued assets across jurisdictions. The ***Stati v Kazakhstan*** litigation, arising from an SCC award of around USD 500 million under the Energy Charter Treaty concerning oil and gas investments, involved attachment of assets in several countries, including assets of Kazakhstan's National Fund held by a custodian in the Netherlands and Belgium, while Kazakhstan's allegations that the award had been procured by fraud generated further litigation in several jurisdictions.

### Enforcement in Nigeria

In **Nigeria**, foreign arbitral awards may be enforced under the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023, which incorporates the New York Convention, by application to the Federal High Court or a State High Court, and ICSID awards under the ICSID (Enforcement of Awards) Act, by registration in the Supreme Court. The grounds for refusing recognition and enforcement under the 2023 Act mirror those in Article V of the New York Convention. Nigerian courts have generally adopted a supportive attitude to arbitration, though concerns persist about delays in court proceedings and the use of domestic litigation to frustrate arbitration, as in the *NNPC v Lutin Investments* (2006) and *Statoil v NNPC* (2013) litigation over the enforceability of arbitral awards and the jurisdiction of the Federal High Court under section 251 of the Constitution. Enforcement against the Federal Government and its agencies is further subject to section 84 of the **Sheriffs and Civil Process Act**, which requires the consent of the Attorney-General before execution against public funds in the custody of a public officer — a requirement that the Supreme Court has upheld, though its application to arbitral awards and to state-owned enterprises such as NNPC Limited, incorporated as a company under the PIA, has been debated. The P&ID case, examined in the casebox below, is the most significant arbitration involving Nigeria and has had profound implications for the country's approach to arbitration.

::: casebox {title="Case Study: The Federal Republic of Nigeria v Process & Industrial Developments Ltd (English Commercial Court, 2023)"}
**Background.** In 2010, the Nigerian Ministry of Petroleum Resources entered into a **Gas Supply and Processing Agreement** (GSPA) with Process & Industrial Developments Ltd (P&ID), a company incorporated in the British Virgin Islands, under which the government was to supply wet gas to a processing facility to be constructed by P&ID, which would return lean gas to the government free of charge for power generation. The GSPA provided for arbitration seated in London. The government did not supply the gas, and P&ID did not construct the facility. P&ID commenced arbitration, and in **2017** a tribunal awarded it damages of around **USD 6.6 billion**, representing the profits it would have earned over the twenty-year life of the agreement, with interest at seven per cent, so that by 2023 the total exceeded **USD 11 billion** — a sum representing a substantial share of Nigeria's foreign exchange reserves.

**Enforcement and challenge.** P&ID sought to enforce the award in England, and in 2019 the Commercial Court granted leave to enforce. Nigeria applied for an extension of time to challenge the award under section 68 of the Arbitration Act 1996 (serious irregularity), alleging that the GSPA had been procured by bribery, that P&ID had continued to bribe Nigerian officials during the arbitration, and that P&ID had improperly obtained Nigeria's privileged legal documents during the arbitration. In 2020, the court granted the extension, finding a strong prima facie case of fraud.

**Decision.** In **October 2023**, after a trial in which extensive evidence was heard, Robin Knowles J set aside the award under **section 68(2)(g)** of the Arbitration Act 1996, on the ground that it had been **obtained by fraud** and in a way **contrary to public policy**. The court found that P&ID had paid bribes to a Nigerian official involved in negotiating the GSPA, and continued to make payments during the arbitration to ensure the official's silence; that P&ID had presented false evidence to the tribunal; and that P&ID had improperly retained and used Nigeria's internal legal documents, which it had obtained during the arbitration. The court was also highly critical of the conduct of the arbitration process and the shortcomings of Nigeria's own legal representation and the failure of the tribunal and the arbitral process to detect the fraud, observing that the case raised serious questions about the capacity of arbitration to address fraud and corruption in disputes involving states. In 2024, the court ordered P&ID to pay a substantial part of Nigeria's costs.

**Significance.** The P&ID case is a landmark in the law of arbitration and corruption. It demonstrates that the courts of the seat will set aside awards obtained by fraud and corruption, even after long delay; it highlights the vulnerability of states with weak contract governance and inadequate legal representation to oppressive claims; and it has prompted reflection on the responsibilities of arbitrators, counsel, and institutions in detecting corruption, the role of third-party funders, the importance of transparency in state contracting, and the need for states to invest in their own capacity to negotiate contracts and conduct arbitrations. For Nigeria, the case has led to closer scrutiny of contracts entered into by ministries and agencies and of the dispute resolution clauses they contain.

## 26.8 Mediation, Negotiation, and Alternative Dispute Resolution

### The Spectrum of Dispute Resolution

Arbitration is not the only, nor always the most appropriate, method for resolving energy disputes. The **spectrum** of dispute resolution methods ranges from **negotiation**, in which the parties resolve the dispute themselves; through **mediation** or **conciliation**, in which a neutral third party assists the parties to reach a settlement; **expert determination**, in which an expert decides a technical issue, usually on a binding basis; and **dispute boards**, which resolve disputes during the performance of a construction contract; to **adjudicative** methods, including arbitration and litigation. The appropriate method depends on the nature of the dispute, the relationship between the parties, the importance of confidentiality and speed, and the need for a binding and enforceable outcome. Energy contracts commonly combine several methods in **multi-tiered clauses**, examined in Chapter 15.

### Negotiation and Renegotiation

Most energy disputes are resolved by **negotiation**, often in the shadow of arbitration. Long-term contracts frequently contain **renegotiation** or **hardship** clauses requiring the parties to negotiate in good faith to restore the economic equilibrium of the contract upon the occurrence of specified events, and **price review** clauses requiring negotiation of the contract price at specified intervals, with arbitration as a fallback if negotiation fails, as examined in Chapters 6 and 15. Investor–state disputes are also frequently settled: a significant proportion of ICSID cases are discontinued, many following settlement. Negotiated outcomes in energy disputes have included the restructuring of the Reko Diq project following the *Tethyan* award, the settlement between Barrick and Tanzania examined in Chapter 24, and the settlements between Repsol and Argentina over the expropriation of YPF. The structure of a negotiated settlement may include the payment of compensation in cash, bonds, or oil; the restructuring of the investment; the grant of new rights; or the withdrawal of claims in exchange for regulatory changes.

### Mediation and Conciliation

**Mediation** has become increasingly important in international commercial and investor–state disputes, though its use in energy disputes remains less common than arbitration. The advantages of mediation include its flexibility, confidentiality, lower cost, speed, and capacity to preserve relationships and produce creative solutions that are not available in adjudication. The **United Nations Convention on International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation** (**Singapore Convention on Mediation**), adopted in 2018 and in force since September 2020, provides a framework for the cross-border enforcement of settlement agreements resulting from mediation, analogous to the New York Convention for arbitral awards, subject to limited grounds for refusal. Nigeria signed the Singapore Convention in 2019, and the Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 contains provisions on mediation and the enforcement of mediated settlement agreements.

In the investor–state context, the **ICSID Mediation Rules** (2022) and the **ICSID Conciliation Rules** provide frameworks for mediation and conciliation, the **IBA Rules for Investor-State Mediation** (2012) offer a model procedure, and the **Energy Charter Secretariat** has promoted mediation of investment disputes, publishing a **Guide on Investment Mediation** in 2016. The **UNCITRAL Working Group III** on ISDS reform has developed provisions on mediation, including model clauses for treaties, as part of the reform package examined in Chapter 14. Some newer investment treaties, including the EU's treaties with Canada (CETA), Singapore, and Vietnam, and the **AfCFTA Investment Protocol** (2023), provide for or encourage mediation before or during arbitration.

### Expert Determination

**Expert determination** is widely used in the energy sector for technical disputes, such as disputes over the redetermination of tract participations under unitisation agreements, the measurement of gas quality and quantity, the calculation of price adjustments, the valuation of assets in pre-emption and default provisions, and the determination of reserves for financing purposes. The expert, who is selected for his or her technical expertise, decides the issue, often within a short timeframe, and the determination is usually final and binding, subject to limited challenge for fraud, manifest error, or the expert exceeding his or her mandate. The English courts have held that an expert determination may be challenged where the expert has departed from the instructions in a material respect, but not merely because the determination is wrong (*Jones v Sherwood Computer Services*, 1992). Expert determination is not governed by arbitration legislation or the New York Convention, so the determination must be enforced as a contractual obligation. The **ICC Rules for Expertise Administration** and the **ICC International Centre for ADR** provide institutional frameworks for expert determination.

### Dispute Boards

**Dispute boards**, consisting of one or three independent experts appointed at the beginning of a construction project to resolve disputes as they arise during performance, are widely used in large energy infrastructure projects, particularly under the **FIDIC** contracts, examined in Chapter 15. **Dispute Adjudication Boards** issue decisions that are binding unless and until revised by arbitration, and **Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Boards** under the 2017 FIDIC suite also assist in avoiding disputes. The binding force of dispute board decisions pending arbitration has been addressed in arbitral awards and by the courts, notably in the Singapore Court of Appeal's decisions in *PT Perusahaan Gas Negara (Persero) TBK v CRW Joint Operation* (2011 and 2015), arising from a gas pipeline project in Indonesia, in which the court ultimately upheld the enforcement of an interim award giving effect to a binding but non-final DAB decision.

### Inter-State Energy Disputes and Diplomatic Means

Inter-state energy disputes are often resolved by **negotiation** and **diplomatic means**. The settlement of maritime boundary disputes by negotiation and the conclusion of joint development agreements, examined in Chapter 20, the mediation of the EU in Russia–Ukraine gas transit negotiations in 2014 and 2019, and the facilitation by the United States of the maritime boundary agreement between Israel and Lebanon in 2022 illustrate the role of diplomacy. The conciliation mechanism of Article 7(7) of the Energy Charter Treaty for transit disputes, and **compulsory conciliation** under Annex V of UNCLOS, used for the first time in the *Timor Sea Conciliation* between Timor-Leste and Australia (2016–2018), which resulted in the Treaty establishing maritime boundaries in the Timor Sea in 2018, including provisions on the Greater Sunrise gas fields, demonstrate the potential of structured conciliation in resource disputes.

### Community Grievance Mechanisms

Disputes between energy companies and communities are increasingly addressed through **non-judicial grievance mechanisms**, including operational-level grievance mechanisms established by companies, the **National Contact Points** under the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, the **Compliance Advisor Ombudsman** of the International Finance Corporation and MIGA, and the **Independent Accountability Mechanisms** of development banks, as examined in Chapters 21 and 22. The **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights** set effectiveness criteria for such mechanisms: legitimacy, accessibility, predictability, equitability, transparency, rights-compatibility, being a source of continuous learning, and, for operational-level mechanisms, being based on engagement and dialogue. The **Hague Rules on Business and Human Rights Arbitration** (2019) offer a framework for the arbitration of business-related human rights disputes, including between companies and affected communities, though their use remains limited.
