## 2.6 Soft Law, Industry Standards, and Corporate Governance

### The Concept of Soft Law

"Soft law" refers to normative instruments that are not legally binding in themselves but that nonetheless influence the conduct of states and other actors and may acquire legal effect through incorporation, interpretation, or the formation of custom. Examples include General Assembly resolutions, declarations adopted at international conferences, codes of conduct, guidelines, recommendations of international organisations, and voluntary standards adopted by industry or multi-stakeholder initiatives. The concept is contested: some scholars reject it as a contradiction in terms, insisting on a binary distinction between law and non-law, while others regard it as an indispensable description of the graduated normativity characteristic of contemporary international governance.

Soft law is especially significant in energy and resource governance for several reasons. States are often reluctant to accept binding obligations in a field so closely tied to sovereignty and security. Many relevant actors — corporations, financial institutions, communities — are not subjects of international law in the traditional sense and cannot be parties to treaties. Technological and market conditions change rapidly, making flexible instruments preferable to treaties that are difficult to amend. And soft law can serve as a laboratory for norms that may later harden into binding law.

### Principal Soft Law Instruments

The principal soft law instruments relevant to energy and natural resources include:

- **UN General Assembly Resolution 1803 (XVII) (1962)** on permanent sovereignty over natural resources, which, although a resolution, is widely regarded as reflecting customary international law.
- **The Rio Declaration on Environment and Development (1992)**, whose principles — including prevention (Principle 2), precaution (Principle 15), the polluter-pays principle (Principle 16), and environmental impact assessment (Principle 17) — have been incorporated into numerous treaties and domestic laws.
- **The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011)**, establishing the "Protect, Respect and Remedy" framework.
- **The OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct**, revised most recently in 2023, which are backed by a system of National Contact Points that handle complaints ("specific instances") against enterprises.
- **The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007)**, which articulates the principle of free, prior, and informed consent.
- **The Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights (2000)**, a multi-stakeholder initiative addressing the use of public and private security forces by extractive companies.
- **The Natural Resource Charter**, setting out principles for the governance of natural resources for the benefit of citizens.
- **The Santiago Principles (2008)**, Generally Accepted Principles and Practices for sovereign wealth funds, many of which are funded by resource revenues.

### Industry Standards and Transnational Private Regulation

Beyond intergovernmental soft law lies a dense body of industry standards and private regulation. Technical standards developed by the American Petroleum Institute (API), the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) govern the design, construction, and operation of energy installations and are frequently incorporated by reference into regulation and contracts. Model contracts developed by industry associations, including the AIEN Model Joint Operating Agreement and Model Unitization and Unit Operating Agreement, standardise commercial relations. The International Association of Oil & Gas Producers (IOGP) publishes guidance on health, safety, and environmental management. In mining, the International Council on Mining and Metals (ICMM) has adopted Mining Principles that bind its member companies, and the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (2020) was developed following the catastrophic collapse of the Brumadinho tailings dam in Brazil in 2019.

Financial institutions exercise considerable regulatory influence through the conditions they attach to project finance. The IFC Performance Standards (2012) establish requirements on environmental and social risk management, labour, resource efficiency, community health and safety, land acquisition and involuntary resettlement, biodiversity, indigenous peoples, and cultural heritage. The Equator Principles, adopted by commercial banks, apply the IFC Performance Standards to project finance transactions above specified thresholds. Because many major energy and mining projects depend on international finance, these standards often impose more demanding requirements than domestic law.

### Corporate Governance as a Source of Norms

Corporate governance instruments — codes of conduct, sustainability policies, human rights policies, and climate commitments — are increasingly treated as sources of legal obligation. In *Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell*, the District Court of The Hague in 2021 relied on the UN Guiding Principles and other soft law instruments to give content to the unwritten standard of care under Dutch tort law and ordered Shell to reduce the net carbon dioxide emissions of the Shell group by 45 per cent by 2030 relative to 2019 levels.[^shell2021] On appeal, the Hague Court of Appeal in November 2024 set aside the specific reduction order, holding that there was insufficient consensus in climate science on a specific reduction percentage applicable to an individual company, while affirming that companies such as Shell have an obligation to contribute to the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions and that this obligation can be derived from the standard of care informed by human rights and soft law instruments.[^shell2024] The case illustrates how soft law can acquire legal force through domestic private law.

[^shell2021]: *Milieudefensie et al v Royal Dutch Shell plc*, District Court of The Hague, 26 May 2021, ECLI:NL:RBDHA:2021:5339.

[^shell2024]: *Shell plc v Milieudefensie et al*, Court of Appeal of The Hague, Judgment of 12 November 2024.

### The Hardening of Soft Law

Soft law norms can harden into binding law through several pathways: incorporation into treaties (the Rio principles in the UNFCCC and the Convention on Biological Diversity); incorporation into domestic legislation (the UN Guiding Principles in mandatory human rights due diligence statutes); incorporation into contracts (the IFC Performance Standards in loan agreements and host government agreements); judicial reliance (as in the *Shell* litigation); and the formation of customary international law. The study of these pathways is a rich field for doctoral research.

## 2.7 International Judicial and Arbitral Institutions

### The International Court of Justice

The International Court of Justice, the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, has decided numerous cases bearing on energy and natural resources, including maritime delimitation disputes involving offshore petroleum resources (*North Sea Continental Shelf*, 1969; *Land and Maritime Boundary between Cameroon and Nigeria*, 2002; *Maritime Delimitation in the Black Sea*, 2009; *Territorial and Maritime Dispute (Nicaragua v Colombia)*, 2012; *Maritime Delimitation in the Indian Ocean (Somalia v Kenya)*, 2021), disputes over shared watercourses and hydropower (*Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project*, 1997; *Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay*, 2010; *Dispute over the Status and Use of the Waters of the Silala*, 2022), and disputes concerning the protection of foreign investment (*Barcelona Traction*, 1970; *Elettronica Sicula SpA (ELSI)*, 1989). Its advisory opinion of 23 July 2025 on the *Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change* is of profound significance for energy law and is analysed in Chapter 18.

The Court's jurisdiction in contentious cases depends on the consent of states, given through special agreement, compromissory clauses in treaties, or optional clause declarations under Article 36(2) of its Statute. Because private parties have no standing, the Court plays a limited role in investor–state disputes, which are resolved principally through arbitration.

### The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea and Annex VII Tribunals

The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS), established under Annex VI of UNCLOS and seated in Hamburg, has jurisdiction over disputes concerning the interpretation or application of the Convention. Its Special Chamber decided the maritime boundary dispute between Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in 2017, a case driven by offshore petroleum discoveries, in which the Chamber also rejected Côte d'Ivoire's claim that Ghana had violated its sovereign rights by conducting oil activities in the disputed area.[^ghana] Its Seabed Disputes Chamber delivered an important advisory opinion in 2011 on the responsibilities of states sponsoring activities in the Area, holding that sponsoring states have obligations of due diligence and direct obligations, including the obligation to apply a precautionary approach.[^itlos2011] In 2024 the full Tribunal delivered an advisory opinion holding that anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions constitute pollution of the marine environment within the meaning of UNCLOS.

[^ghana]: *Dispute Concerning Delimitation of the Maritime Boundary between Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in the Atlantic Ocean (Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire)*, ITLOS Case No 23, Judgment of the Special Chamber, 23 September 2017.

[^itlos2011]: *Responsibilities and Obligations of States Sponsoring Persons and Entities with respect to Activities in the Area*, Advisory Opinion, ITLOS Seabed Disputes Chamber, 1 February 2011, ITLOS Reports 2011, p. 10.

Arbitral tribunals constituted under Annex VII of UNCLOS have decided several disputes with energy dimensions, including the *South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v China)* of 2016, which addressed the legal status of maritime features and the lawfulness of Chinese activities, including interference with Philippine petroleum exploration.

### The WTO Dispute Settlement System

The WTO Dispute Settlement Body, comprising panels and the Appellate Body, has decided several disputes concerning energy goods and renewable energy policies. Since December 2019, the Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals because the United States has blocked the appointment of new members. Many WTO members have joined the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA) under Article 25 of the Dispute Settlement Understanding as an interim substitute, but appeals "into the void" by members not party to the MPIA can prevent panel reports from being adopted. This crisis has weakened the enforcement of trade disciplines in the energy sector.

### Investment Arbitration Institutions

Investor–state disputes in the energy sector are resolved principally through arbitration under the auspices of:

- **ICSID**, established by the Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States 1965, with its own self-contained system of annulment rather than review by national courts, and enforcement provisions requiring contracting states to recognise awards as if they were final judgments of their own courts (Article 54);
- **the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA)** in The Hague, which administers many arbitrations conducted under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, including the *Yukos* arbitrations and numerous ECT cases;
- **the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce (SCC)**, designated as one of the forums for investor–state disputes under Article 26 of the ECT;
- **the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) International Court of Arbitration**, which administers many commercial and some investment disputes;
- **the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA)** and regional centres, including the Singapore International Arbitration Centre, the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration, and the Lagos Court of Arbitration.

The institutional framework, procedural rules, and practice of energy arbitration are examined in Chapter 26.

### Regional Courts and Human Rights Bodies

Regional courts and human rights bodies have become increasingly important forums for energy-related disputes. The Court of Justice of the European Union has decided cases of enormous significance for intra-EU investment arbitration (*Achmea*, 2018; *Komstroy*, 2021; *PL Holdings*, 2021) and energy market regulation. The European Court of Human Rights, in *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz v Switzerland* (2024), held that Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights encompasses a right to effective protection by state authorities from the serious adverse effects of climate change.[^klima] The African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, in *Social and Economic Rights Action Center (SERAC) and Center for Economic and Social Rights v Nigeria* (2001), found that Nigeria had violated the African Charter through the conduct of the state oil company and its failure to regulate the activities of oil companies in Ogoniland.[^serac] The ECOWAS Community Court of Justice, in *SERAP v Federal Republic of Nigeria* (2012), held Nigeria responsible for failing to protect the right to a satisfactory environment in the Niger Delta against the activities of oil companies.

[^klima]: *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz and Others v Switzerland*, App No 53600/20, European Court of Human Rights (Grand Chamber), Judgment of 9 April 2024.

[^serac]: *Social and Economic Rights Action Center (SERAC) and Center for Economic and Social Rights (CESR) v Nigeria*, Communication No 155/96, African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, Decision of 27 October 2001.

## 2.8 Conflicts Between Domestic and International Legal Obligations

### Monism, Dualism, and Their Limits

The relationship between international law and domestic law is traditionally described in terms of monism and dualism. Monist systems treat international law and domestic law as parts of a single legal order, such that treaties may have direct effect in domestic law upon ratification. Dualist systems treat the two as separate, such that treaties must be incorporated by legislation before they can be applied by domestic courts. Nigeria follows a dualist approach for treaties: section 12(1) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 provides that "no treaty between the Federation and any other country shall have the force of law except to the extent to which any such treaty has been enacted into law by the National Assembly". In *Abacha v Fawehinmi*, the Supreme Court of Nigeria held that the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, having been domesticated by the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (Ratification and Enforcement) Act, was part of Nigerian law, although subordinate to the Constitution.[^abacha] The United Kingdom is likewise dualist, whereas the Netherlands and many civil law states are closer to the monist model.

[^abacha]: *Abacha v Fawehinmi* (2000) 6 NWLR (Pt 660) 228 (Supreme Court of Nigeria).

### The International Law Perspective

From the perspective of international law, the classification of a state's domestic system is irrelevant to its international obligations. Article 27 of the VCLT provides that "a party may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty". Article 3 of ARSIWA provides that the characterisation of an act of a state as internationally wrongful is governed by international law and is not affected by its characterisation as lawful by internal law. Accordingly, a state cannot escape liability under an investment treaty by pointing to the lawfulness of its measures under its own constitution, nor can it rely on a domestic court decision annulling a contract as a defence if the decision itself amounts to a denial of justice.

### Typical Conflicts in the Energy Sector

Conflicts between domestic and international obligations arise in several recurring situations in the energy sector:

- **Legislative change and stabilisation.** A state enacts new fiscal or environmental legislation that is valid under its constitution but conflicts with stabilisation commitments in contracts or with investment treaty protections.
- **Judicial decisions.** Domestic courts annul or refuse to enforce contracts or arbitral awards, or issue anti-arbitration injunctions, in circumstances that international tribunals may regard as breaches of treaty obligations or of the New York Convention.
- **Constitutional limits on contracting.** Contracts concluded by state entities may be void under domestic law for failure to obtain required approvals, raising questions about whether the state can rely on its own law to avoid its obligations. Article 46 of the VCLT, by analogy, permits a state to invoke a violation of internal law regarding competence to conclude treaties only where the violation was manifest and concerned a rule of fundamental importance.
- **Climate and environmental obligations.** Domestic courts may order a state to adopt more stringent climate measures, as the Supreme Court of the Netherlands did in *State of the Netherlands v Urgenda Foundation* (2019),[^urgenda] potentially generating claims by investors whose investments are affected by the resulting measures.
- **Human rights obligations and investment protection.** A state's obligations to protect the rights of indigenous peoples or the right to water may conflict with commitments to investors.

[^urgenda]: *State of the Netherlands v Urgenda Foundation*, Supreme Court of the Netherlands, 20 December 2019, ECLI:NL:HR:2019:2007.

### The Intra-EU Investment Arbitration Conflict

The most dramatic recent example of conflict between legal orders arose from intra-EU investment arbitration under the ECT. In *Slovak Republic v Achmea BV* (2018), the Court of Justice of the European Union held that arbitration clauses in intra-EU BITs were incompatible with EU law because they removed disputes potentially concerning the interpretation or application of EU law from the EU judicial system.[^achmea] In *Republic of Moldova v Komstroy LLC* (2021), the Court extended this reasoning to the ECT, holding that Article 26 of the ECT must be interpreted as not applicable to disputes between a member state and an investor of another member state concerning an investment made by the latter in the first member state.[^komstroy]

[^achmea]: Case C-284/16, *Slowakische Republik v Achmea BV*, EU:C:2018:158 (CJEU, Grand Chamber, 6 March 2018).

[^komstroy]: Case C-741/19, *République de Moldavie v Komstroy LLC*, EU:C:2021:655 (CJEU, Grand Chamber, 2 September 2021).

Investment tribunals constituted under the ECT have overwhelmingly rejected the intra-EU objection, holding that they derive their jurisdiction from the ECT as a treaty of international law and that EU law cannot override the consent expressed in it. The result has been a direct conflict between legal orders: awards rendered against EU member states in favour of EU investors (many arising from Spain's withdrawal of renewable energy incentives) have been upheld by arbitral tribunals and enforced or recognised by courts in some non-EU jurisdictions such as the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom, while courts within the European Union have refused enforcement and the European Commission has treated payment of such awards as potentially constituting unlawful state aid. The European Union, Euratom, and a number of member states subsequently withdrew from the ECT. This saga is a vivid case study in legal pluralism and is examined further in Chapters 14 and 26.

::: casebox
**Factory at Chorzów (Germany v Poland), PCIJ, 1928**

*Facts.* Following the transfer of Upper Silesia to Poland after the First World War, Poland took possession of a nitrate factory at Chorzów owned by German companies, in breach of the Geneva Convention of 1922 concerning Upper Silesia. Germany brought proceedings seeking reparation.

*Holding.* The Permanent Court held that it is a principle of international law that the breach of an engagement involves an obligation to make reparation in an adequate form, and that reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act. It distinguished between lawful expropriation, which requires payment of the value of the undertaking at the time of dispossession plus interest, and unlawful expropriation, for which restitution in kind, or payment of a sum corresponding to the value that restitution would bear, is required — potentially including the value of the property at the date of the judgment rather than the date of the taking.

*Significance for energy law.* *Chorzów* remains the foundation of the law of reparation. Investment tribunals in energy cases — including *ADC v Hungary* (2006), *ConocoPhillips v Venezuela* (2019), and *Yukos Universal v Russia* (2014) — have relied on the distinction between lawful and unlawful expropriation to determine the valuation date and the measure of compensation, with major financial consequences.

::: summary
- The formal sources of international energy law are treaties, custom, and general principles, with judicial decisions and scholarly writings as subsidiary means of determining the law; no formal hierarchy exists, but treaties operate as *lex specialis* and peremptory norms prevail over all.
- Treaty interpretation under VCLT Articles 31–33 is central to the field, particularly in investment disputes; systemic integration under Article 31(3)(c) allows environmental and human rights obligations to inform the interpretation of investment treaties.
- Permanent sovereignty over natural resources is a principle of customary international law encompassing both rights and duties; it does not authorise a state to disregard its commitments, but affirms the state's ultimate authority subject to international law.
- The ILC Articles on State Responsibility govern attribution, breach, defences, and reparation; the attribution of national oil company conduct and the necessity defence have generated extensive energy jurisprudence.
- Jurisdiction is primarily territorial, but extraterritorial regulation through anti-corruption, sanctions, supply chain, and carbon border measures increasingly shapes energy activities.
- International organisations, soft law, industry standards, lender requirements, and corporate governance instruments constitute a significant layer of normative authority, which may harden into binding law.
- Conflicts between domestic and international obligations are resolved, from the international perspective, by the rule that internal law cannot justify non-performance; the intra-EU arbitration saga illustrates the limits of that rule when legal orders collide.
:::
