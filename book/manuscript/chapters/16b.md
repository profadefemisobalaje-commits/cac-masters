## 16.3 Sovereign Guarantees and Government Support Agreements

### Why Government Support Is Required

In many developing and emerging markets, the offtaker of a power project is a state-owned utility or single buyer whose creditworthiness is weak, the regulatory framework is untested, and currency convertibility cannot be assumed. In such conditions, lenders and investors will not finance projects on the strength of the project contracts alone, and require the host government to provide additional support. The forms of support, and the extent of the contingent liabilities they create for the state, are among the most important and contested issues in energy project finance.

### Forms of Government Support

Government support takes several forms, which are frequently combined.

**Implementation agreements and government support agreements.** An implementation agreement (IA) or government support agreement (GSA) is a contract between the host government and the project company under which the government undertakes obligations to facilitate the project: granting permits and licences, providing land, ensuring the availability of foreign exchange and the right to repatriate profits, granting tax and customs exemptions, protecting the project against discriminatory changes in law, and agreeing to international arbitration. Pakistan's IPP programme of the 1990s, under which implementation agreements were concluded with each IPP, established a widely copied template.

**Sovereign guarantees of payment obligations.** The government may guarantee the payment obligations of the state-owned offtaker under the PPA or of a state-owned fuel supplier, so that the project company can claim against the government if the offtaker fails to pay. Such guarantees transfer the offtaker's credit risk directly to the sovereign.

**Put and call option agreements.** A put and call option agreement (PCOA) gives the project company the right to require the government to purchase the project (the put option) on the occurrence of specified events — such as prolonged political force majeure, government or offtaker default, or change in law — at a price usually sufficient to repay outstanding debt and, depending on the event, to compensate equity. The government in turn has the right to purchase the project (the call option) in specified circumstances. PCOAs were used in Nigeria's Azura-Edo project and other IPPs, and are functionally similar to termination payment obligations in concession agreements, examined in Chapter 15.

**Letters of support or comfort.** A government may provide a letter of support expressing its support for the project and its intention to ensure that the state-owned offtaker meets its obligations, without assuming a legally binding guarantee. The legal effect of such letters depends on their wording; English courts have generally treated comfort letters as statements of present intention rather than contractual promises, unless the language indicates an intention to create legal relations.

**Liquidity support.** To address short-term payment delays, offtakers may be required to provide letters of credit, escrow accounts funded from designated revenue streams, or payment security mechanisms, often supported by multilateral guarantees, covering several months of payments.

### Multilateral and Bilateral Risk Mitigation

Multilateral and bilateral institutions provide instruments that cover risks which governments cannot credibly guarantee alone.

**The Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency.** MIGA was established by the Convention Establishing the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency, opened for signature in Seoul in 1985 and in force since 1988, as a member of the World Bank Group with the purpose of encouraging the flow of investment to developing member countries by issuing guarantees against non-commercial risks. Under Article 11 of the Convention, MIGA may cover the risks of currency transfer restriction, expropriation and similar measures, breach of contract by the host government (where the investor has no recourse to a judicial or arbitral forum, faces unreasonable delay, or cannot enforce a favourable decision), and war and civil disturbance; it also offers cover for the non-honouring of sovereign financial obligations. Upon paying a claim, MIGA is subrogated to the investor's rights against the host state, and disputes between MIGA and the host state are subject to negotiation and arbitration under the Convention. The deterrent effect of MIGA's involvement, arising from the host government's relationship with the World Bank Group, is a significant component of its value, and MIGA has historically paid relatively few claims, resolving many disputes through mediation.

**World Bank guarantees.** The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development and the International Development Association provide project-based guarantees — formerly described as partial risk guarantees and partial credit guarantees — which cover private lenders against the failure of a government or state entity to meet its contractual obligations, such as payment obligations under a PPA or termination payments, supported by a counter-guarantee and indemnity from the host government to the World Bank. The guarantees have been used for power projects in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Côte d'Ivoire, and elsewhere. In 2024, the World Bank Group announced the consolidation of its guarantee instruments into a single platform housed at MIGA, with a view to expanding their use.

**Other providers.** The African Development Bank provides partial risk guarantees; the African Trade and Investment Development Insurance (ATIDI, formerly the African Trade Insurance Agency) provides political risk insurance and liquidity products for IPPs in member states; GuarantCo, part of the Private Infrastructure Development Group, provides local currency guarantees; and national investment insurers and the private political risk insurance market, centred on Lloyd's of London, provide additional cover.

### Contingent Liabilities and Fiscal Risk

Government support creates **contingent liabilities** — obligations that crystallise only on the occurrence of future events — which may be large in relation to the state's fiscal capacity and are often not recorded in government budgets or debt statistics. Take-or-pay PPAs commit the state-owned offtaker to pay capacity charges whether or not the electricity is needed, and where demand projections prove optimistic, the resulting excess capacity charges become a substantial fiscal burden. Several states have experienced the consequences. In **Pakistan**, the accumulation of unpaid obligations through the electricity supply chain, known as "circular debt", reached very large proportions, leading to negotiations with IPPs in 2020 on tariff reductions and, in 2024, to the negotiated early termination of a number of older IPP contracts. In **Ghana**, the conclusion of numerous take-or-pay PPAs during a period of power shortages in the 2010s led to payments for capacity that was not used, and the government sought to renegotiate or terminate several contracts. Indian state distribution companies accumulated large arrears to generators, leading to the adoption of payment security mechanisms and rules on late payment surcharges.

The management of fiscal risks from energy projects has become a focus of international guidance. The IMF and the World Bank developed the **PPP Fiscal Risk Assessment Model** (P-FRAM) to help governments assess the fiscal costs and risks of public-private partnership projects, and the IMF's fiscal transparency standards call for the disclosure and management of contingent liabilities. Many states have adopted public finance legislation requiring the approval of guarantees by the ministry of finance or the legislature, the recording of contingent liabilities, and limits on the aggregate value of guarantees. In Nigeria, the Fiscal Responsibility Act 2007 and the Debt Management Office Establishment Act 2003 regulate public borrowing and guarantees, and the issuance of PCOAs and guarantees for IPPs has been subject to approval by the Federal Executive Council.

### Enforcing Government Support

The enforcement of sovereign guarantees and government support agreements raises the question of sovereign immunity, examined in Chapter 26. Government support agreements typically contain waivers of immunity from suit and from execution, and submission to international arbitration, often under the ICSID Convention. Under the United Kingdom's State Immunity Act 1978 and the United States' Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976, states are not immune in respect of commercial transactions and where they have submitted to arbitration, but immunity from execution protects state property not in use for commercial purposes, and central bank assets enjoy special protection. Investors holding awards against states must therefore identify commercial assets available for execution, a process that may be protracted, as illustrated by the *Karaha Bodas* case.

::: casebox {title="Karaha Bodas Co v Pertamina (1998–2007): Enforcing a Power Project Award Against a State Company"}
**Background.** In 1994, Karaha Bodas Company (KBC), a project company sponsored by United States investors, concluded a joint operation contract with Pertamina, the Indonesian state oil and gas company, for the development of a geothermal field in West Java, and an energy sales contract with PLN, the state electricity company, for the sale of electricity. During the Asian financial crisis, the Indonesian government issued presidential decrees in 1997 and 1998 suspending the project, together with numerous other infrastructure projects.

**The arbitration.** KBC commenced arbitration against Pertamina and PLN under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, seated in Geneva. In December 2000, the tribunal held that the suspension did not constitute force majeure excusing Pertamina and PLN, since the contracts allocated the risk of government action to the state companies, and awarded KBC approximately USD 261 million, comprising its expenditure and lost profits.

**Annulment and enforcement.** Pertamina's challenge to the award in Switzerland, the seat, was unsuccessful. Pertamina then obtained a judgment from a Jakarta district court in 2002 purporting to annul the award. KBC sought enforcement under the New York Convention in several jurisdictions, including the United States, Hong Kong, and Singapore. In the United States, the Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit upheld the enforcement of the award in 2003, holding that only the courts of the seat, Switzerland, had primary jurisdiction to set aside the award, and that the Indonesian annulment did not prevent enforcement; the court also held that the alleged irregularities did not justify refusing enforcement under Article V of the Convention. KBC subsequently pursued Pertamina's assets in several jurisdictions, and the award was ultimately satisfied.

**Significance.** The case illustrates the allocation of political risk to state-owned counterparties in energy contracts, the narrowness of the force majeure defence where the risk is within the state's sphere, the distinction between primary and secondary jurisdiction under the New York Convention, and the practical difficulties of enforcing awards against state entities. It also illustrates why lenders to emerging market energy projects place great weight on multilateral support, which provides a remedy that does not depend on the pursuit of state assets.

## 16.4 Petroleum and Mining Fiscal Regimes

### Objectives of Fiscal Design

A **fiscal regime** for the extractive sector comprises the set of legal instruments through which the state captures a share of the value of its natural resources: bonuses, rentals, royalties, income taxes, resource rent taxes, production sharing, state participation, withholding taxes, customs duties, and other charges. Fiscal design involves a balance among several objectives, which are frequently in tension:

- **revenue** — maximising the present value of government revenue over the life of the resource, rather than in a single year;
- **attractiveness** — ensuring that the regime provides investors with a return sufficient to attract the investment required, in competition with other jurisdictions;
- **progressivity** — increasing the government's share as the profitability of a project increases, so that the state shares in the upside of high prices or rich deposits;
- **neutrality** — minimising distortion of investment and production decisions, so that projects that are economic before tax remain economic after tax;
- **risk-sharing** — allocating the risks of price volatility and geological uncertainty between the state and investors;
- **stability and predictability** — providing a credible and stable framework for long-term investment;
- **administrative simplicity** — limiting the scope for disputes, avoidance, and corruption, and matching the capacity of the tax administration.

The economic justification for special fiscal treatment of the extractive sector lies in the existence of **economic rent** — the surplus of revenue over all costs of production, including a normal return on capital — which is generated by the scarcity and quality of natural resources. Because the state is usually the owner of subsoil resources, as examined in Chapter 2, it is entitled to capture a substantial share of this rent, and in principle a tax on pure economic rent does not affect investment decisions. In practice, however, rent is difficult to measure, investors and governments disagree about the appropriate normal return, and the time-inconsistency problem — the incentive for governments to increase their share once investments are sunk — creates the demand for stability examined in Chapters 14 and 15.

### The Principal Families of Fiscal Regime

Petroleum fiscal regimes are conventionally divided into two families, corresponding to the forms of host government agreement examined in Chapter 15.

**Concessionary (tax and royalty) regimes**, used in the United States, the United Kingdom, Norway, Canada, Australia, and many other states, grant the investor ownership of the petroleum produced, subject to the payment of royalties and taxes. The government's share is captured through royalties, corporate income tax, and frequently special petroleum taxes or resource rent taxes.

**Contractual regimes**, comprising production sharing contracts and service contracts, provide for the state to retain ownership of the petroleum, with the contractor receiving a share of production (under PSCs) or a fee (under service contracts) as compensation for its services and risk. The government's share is captured through royalties (where applicable), the state's share of profit oil, corporate income tax, and state participation.

The differences between these families are, in economic terms, less significant than they appear: any combination of fiscal instruments can be designed to produce a similar division of rent, and the choice between them is often driven by constitutional, political, and historical considerations, including the symbolic importance of state ownership of the resource in post-colonial states. ### Measuring and Comparing Fiscal Regimes

Fiscal regimes are compared using several measures. **Government take** is the government's share of the pre-tax net cash flow of a project, expressed as a percentage, and typically ranges from around fifty per cent to more than ninety per cent for petroleum projects. The **average effective tax rate** (AETR) measures the present value of government revenue as a share of the present value of pre-tax cash flow, while the **marginal effective tax rate** (METR) measures the effect of the regime on the return to marginal investment. Measures of **progressivity** examine how government take varies with prices and project profitability, and the **front-loading** of the regime measures how early in the project life the government receives revenue. Front-loaded instruments, such as bonuses and royalties, provide early and secure revenue for the state, but discourage marginal projects and transfer more risk to investors; profit-based instruments, such as resource rent taxes, are more neutral and progressive, but defer government revenue and expose the state to price and cost risks and to the manipulation of costs. The IMF's **Fiscal Analysis of Resource Industries** (FARI) model and the IMF's work on fiscal regimes for extractive industries provide widely used analytical frameworks.

### Comparative Petroleum Regimes

**Norway** applies the ordinary corporate income tax of twenty-two per cent together with a special petroleum tax, yielding a combined marginal rate of seventy-eight per cent, but allows generous deductions for investment costs and, since a reform adopted in 2022, operates the special tax as a cash-flow tax, under which investment costs are deducted immediately and the tax value of losses is refunded by the state. The regime is designed to be close to neutral, so that the state effectively shares in both the costs and the revenues of petroleum development, and is combined with direct state participation through the State's Direct Financial Interest, managed by Petoro, and the state's majority shareholding in Equinor.

**The United Kingdom** taxes petroleum production through ring-fence corporation tax and a supplementary charge on ring-fence profits. Petroleum revenue tax, which applied to older fields, has been set at zero since 2016. In response to high prices following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the government introduced an **Energy Profits Levy** in 2022, initially at twenty-five per cent and subsequently increased, which was extended to 2030 and is accompanied by an investment allowance. The levy raised questions about the stability of the regime and its effect on investment, and the government has consulted on a permanent mechanism to respond to future price shocks.

**Nigeria**, under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, replaced the petroleum profits tax for new and converted licences and leases with a combination of companies income tax, a **hydrocarbon tax** applicable to crude oil production from onshore and shallow water areas (but not to deep offshore and frontier acreage), royalties varying by terrain, production volume, and price, and limits on deductible costs through a cost price ratio. The Act provides incentives for gas development and for frontier exploration, and allows holders of existing licences to convert to the new regime on specified terms, as examined in Chapters 5 and 24. Production sharing contracts in the deep offshore remain subject to the Deep Offshore and Inland Basin Production Sharing Contracts Act, as amended in 2019, examined in Chapter 15.

**Australia** applies a profit-based Petroleum Resource Rent Tax to offshore projects, at a rate of forty per cent on profits above a threshold return, together with corporate income tax. The PRRT's treatment of LNG projects and the accumulation of large deductible carry-forward expenditures led to concerns that the tax was yielding little revenue from major LNG projects, and to reforms in 2023 that limited the use of deductions.

### Mining Fiscal Regimes

Mining fiscal regimes typically rely more heavily on royalties and corporate income tax than petroleum regimes, with resource rent taxes and state participation used in some jurisdictions. Mining royalties are often ad valorem — a percentage of the value of the mineral, such as three to five per cent for gold and copper — although profit-based and sliding-scale royalties linked to prices are increasingly common. The volatility of mineral prices and the long life of mines have led to frequent changes in mining fiscal regimes, and to disputes with investors.

Several recent reforms illustrate the trend towards increasing the state's share. The **Democratic Republic of Congo** adopted a revised Mining Code in 2018, which increased royalty rates, permitted the designation of "strategic substances" — including cobalt — subject to a royalty of ten per cent, introduced a tax on super-profits, and reduced the stability period for existing mining conventions, provoking strong objections from mining companies that had relied on the stability guarantees of the 2002 Code. **Chile**, the world's largest copper producer, enacted a new mining royalty law in 2023, applicable to large copper producers, combining an ad valorem component with a margin-based component, subject to a cap on the total tax burden. **Zambia** has repeatedly revised its mining royalty regime, oscillating between higher and lower rates in response to price changes and investor reactions, and in 2022–2023 adopted measures, including restoring the deductibility of mineral royalties for income tax purposes, intended to attract new investment. The **African Mining Vision**, adopted by African Union heads of state in 2009, calls for fiscal regimes that capture a fair share of resource rents for development, and the Intergovernmental Forum on Mining, Minerals, Metals and Sustainable Development has published guidance on mining fiscal policy.

### Fiscal Stability

Fiscal stability is the issue at which fiscal design and investment law intersect. Investors seek stability through stabilisation clauses in host government agreements and through the protection of investment treaties, while governments seek to preserve the ability to adjust fiscal terms in response to changing circumstances. The most common sources of dispute are increases in royalty or tax rates, the introduction of windfall taxes, and the withdrawal of incentives. Investment tribunals have generally treated the general power to tax as an incident of sovereignty, and many investment treaties exclude or limit the application of their protections to taxation measures, as in Article 21 of the Energy Charter Treaty. Nevertheless, tax measures may breach treaty obligations where they are confiscatory, discriminatory, or contrary to specific commitments, as in *Occidental v Ecuador* and the claims arising from Ecuador's windfall tax, examined in Chapter 14, and in the *Yukos* awards, where the tribunals held that the Russian tax measures against Yukos were not a bona fide exercise of the taxation power but a means of expropriating the company.
