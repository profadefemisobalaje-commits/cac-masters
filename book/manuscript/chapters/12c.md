## 12.4 Battery Storage and Grid Integration

### The Role of Electricity Storage

Electricity storage — the conversion of electrical energy into a form that can be stored and reconverted into electricity when needed — is increasingly central to the integration of variable renewable energy. Storage can shift energy from periods of surplus to periods of scarcity, provide fast-responding frequency regulation and other ancillary services, defer or replace investment in network reinforcement, provide backup supply, and enable consumers to increase their self-consumption of rooftop solar. **Pumped storage hydropower** remains the largest form of grid-scale storage globally, but **battery energy storage systems**, principally using lithium-ion technology, have grown dramatically as their costs have fallen, and are now deployed at grid scale in many markets. Other technologies include compressed air, flywheels, thermal storage, and, for long durations, hydrogen.

The rapid growth of battery storage has been illustrated by projects such as the Hornsdale Power Reserve in South Australia, commissioned in 2017 following a series of blackouts, which demonstrated the capability of large batteries to provide fast frequency response and reduced the costs of frequency control services in the Australian National Electricity Market. In sub-Saharan Africa, batteries are integral to solar mini-grids and stand-alone solar systems, examined in Chapter 10, and to the management of grids subject to frequent outages, including in Nigeria, where behind-the-meter batteries increasingly substitute for diesel generators.

### The Legal Characterisation of Storage

The principal legal difficulty presented by storage is that it does not fit within the categories of traditional electricity law. Storage consumes electricity when charging and produces it when discharging; it may be connected at transmission or distribution level or behind the customer's meter; and it may provide services to the market, to network operators, and to consumers. Early regulatory frameworks treated storage as generation (because it produces electricity), as consumption (because it draws electricity), or as both, with the result that storage could be subject to double charges — paying network charges and levies both when charging and when discharging — and to uncertainty about whether network operators could own it.

The European Union addressed these issues in the Clean Energy for All Europeans package. Article 2(59) of Directive (EU) 2019/944 defines "energy storage" in the electricity system as "deferring the final use of electricity to a moment later than when it was generated, or the conversion of electrical energy into a form of energy which can be stored, the storing of such energy, and the subsequent reconversion of such energy into electrical energy or use as another energy carrier". The definition recognises storage as a distinct activity, rather than as generation or consumption. Article 15(5) of the Directive requires member states to ensure that active customers who own energy storage facilities are not subject to double charges, including network charges, for stored electricity remaining within their premises or when providing flexibility services to system operators.

In Great Britain, storage was treated as a form of generation for licensing purposes, and the Energy Act 2023 amended the Electricity Act 1989 to define electricity storage expressly as a distinct subset of generation, providing legal clarity. In the United States, storage has been treated as a distinct resource for the purposes of wholesale market participation, as examined below. In Nigeria, the legal treatment of storage under the Electricity Act 2023 and NERC's regulations is still developing, and a comprehensive framework for the licensing, market participation, and network charging of storage has yet to be established.

### Ownership of Storage by Network Operators

Whether network operators — whose activities are regulated monopolies — may own and operate storage facilities is a significant question. Storage owned by network operators could defer network investment and support system security, but it could also distort competition in markets in which storage competes with generation and demand response to provide energy and flexibility services. The EU's unbundling principles, examined in Chapter 9, apply: Articles 36 and 54 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 provide that distribution and transmission system operators shall not own, develop, manage, or operate energy storage facilities, except where the facilities are "fully integrated network components" (such as capacitors that are used solely to maintain network stability) and the regulator has approved them, or where, following an open, transparent, and non-discriminatory tendering procedure, no other party has expressed interest in providing the storage services at reasonable cost, the facilities are necessary for the system operator's obligations, and the regulator has approved the derogation. The regulator must periodically reassess whether third parties are interested in owning the facilities, and if so, the system operator must phase out its storage activities.

### Market Participation

The ability of storage to participate in wholesale electricity markets on equal terms with other resources depends on market rules designed for generators and loads. In the United States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's **Order No 841** (2018) required regional transmission organisations and independent system operators to revise their market rules to establish participation models for electric storage resources, enabling them to provide all capacity, energy, and ancillary services that they are technically capable of providing, to be dispatched and to set market prices as both buyers and sellers, to account for their physical and operational characteristics, and to buy energy for later resale at the wholesale market price. In *National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners v FERC* 964 F.3d 1177 (DC Cir 2020), the Court of Appeals upheld Order No 841, rejecting the argument that FERC had exceeded its jurisdiction by preventing states from prohibiting storage resources connected at the distribution level from participating in wholesale markets, holding that the Order regulated practices directly affecting wholesale rates within FERC's jurisdiction under the Federal Power Act while preserving states' authority over the safety and reliability of the distribution system. **Order No 2222** (2020), examined in Chapter 9, extended wholesale market participation to aggregations of distributed energy resources, including behind-the-meter batteries.

Several jurisdictions have adopted **procurement mandates** for storage. California's Assembly Bill 2514 (2010) directed the California Public Utilities Commission to determine whether to adopt storage procurement targets, and the Commission subsequently required the state's investor-owned utilities to procure 1,325 MW of storage by 2020, which was followed by substantially larger procurement orders to maintain reliability as solar generation increased. Capacity markets, examined in Chapter 9, have also become an important source of revenue for storage, subject to "de-rating" factors that reflect the limited duration of battery storage.

### Safety, Siting, and End of Life

Large battery installations raise safety issues, principally the risk of thermal runaway leading to fires that are difficult to extinguish and may release toxic gases. A major fire at a large battery storage facility at Moss Landing, California, in January 2025 led to local evacuations and renewed scrutiny of battery safety standards and siting. Safety is addressed through fire codes and standards, including the United States National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 855 standard for the installation of stationary energy storage systems and certification standards such as UL 9540, and through planning and permitting requirements. The end-of-life management of batteries — including their collection, reuse in "second life" applications, and recycling — is governed in the European Union by the Batteries Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), examined in Chapter 8, which also imposes carbon footprint, recycled content, due diligence, and battery passport requirements on industrial batteries, including those used for grid storage.

## 12.5 Carbon Capture, Utilisation, and Storage

### The Role of CCUS

**Carbon capture, utilisation, and storage** (CCUS) comprises technologies that capture carbon dioxide from large point sources — such as power plants, cement and steel works, hydrogen production, and natural gas processing — or directly from the atmosphere, and either use it in products or store it permanently in deep geological formations. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has identified CCUS as a necessary component of many pathways limiting global warming to 1.5°C or 2°C, particularly for the abatement of emissions from industrial processes, such as cement production, for which there are few alternatives, and for the removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and direct air capture with carbon storage (DACCS). CCUS is nevertheless controversial: critics argue that it has a history of underperformance and cost overruns, that it may prolong the use of fossil fuels, and that much captured carbon dioxide has been used for enhanced oil recovery, which increases oil production.

Commercial-scale geological storage of carbon dioxide has been undertaken since 1996 at the **Sleipner** field in the Norwegian North Sea, where carbon dioxide separated from natural gas has been injected into a saline aquifer beneath the seabed, partly in response to Norway's carbon tax on offshore emissions. The **Northern Lights** project, part of Norway's Longship project, began injecting carbon dioxide transported by ship from industrial emitters in 2025, providing the first commercial cross-border carbon dioxide transport and storage service.

### The International Framework: The London Protocol

The injection of carbon dioxide into sub-seabed geological formations raised the question whether it constituted "dumping" prohibited by the international law of marine pollution. The 1996 **Protocol to the Convention on the Prevention of Marine Pollution by Dumping of Wastes and Other Matter** (the London Protocol) adopts a "reverse list" approach, prohibiting the dumping of all wastes or other matter except those listed in Annex 1. In 2006, the parties amended Annex 1 to include "carbon dioxide streams from carbon dioxide capture processes for sequestration", permitting the storage of carbon dioxide in sub-seabed geological formations, provided that the streams consist overwhelmingly of carbon dioxide, no wastes are added for the purpose of disposal, and the storage is subject to a permit and to the specific guidelines adopted by the parties. The **OSPAR Convention** for the protection of the marine environment of the North-East Atlantic was amended in 2007 to permit the storage of carbon dioxide in sub-soil geological formations.

Article 6 of the London Protocol prohibits the export of wastes or other matter to other countries for dumping or incineration at sea, which would have prevented the cross-border transport of carbon dioxide for sub-seabed storage. In 2009, the parties adopted an amendment to Article 6 permitting the export of carbon dioxide streams for storage, provided that an agreement or arrangement is entered into by the countries concerned, allocating permitting responsibilities. Because the 2009 amendment had not received sufficient acceptances to enter into force, the parties adopted a resolution in 2019 permitting its provisional application by parties that so declare, enabling cross-border projects such as Northern Lights to proceed on the basis of bilateral arrangements, such as those concluded between Norway and several European states.

### Domestic Storage Regimes

**The European Union.** Directive 2009/31/EC on the geological storage of carbon dioxide (the CCS Directive) establishes a comprehensive legal framework for storage in the territory of member states, including their exclusive economic zones and continental shelves. Member states retain the right to determine the areas from which storage sites may be selected, including the right not to allow any storage in parts or the whole of their territory. Exploration requires an exploration permit, and storage requires a storage permit, which may be issued only if the site has been characterised and assessed and found to present no significant risk of leakage or of environmental or health damage. Operators must monitor the storage site and take corrective measures in the event of leakage or significant irregularities, and must surrender emission allowances under the EU ETS for any leaked emissions. Following closure, responsibility for the site is transferred to the competent authority when all available evidence indicates that the stored carbon dioxide will be completely and permanently contained, a minimum period — normally not less than twenty years — has elapsed, a financial contribution has been made, and the site has been sealed and the injection facilities removed. Operators must provide financial security for their obligations. Captured and stored emissions are not counted as emissions under the EU ETS, providing an economic incentive for CCS in sectors covered by the ETS. The **Net-Zero Industry Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1735) set an objective of achieving an annual carbon dioxide injection capacity of at least fifty million tonnes in the Union by 2030, and imposes on holders of oil and gas production licences an obligation to contribute to that objective in proportion to their share of production.

**The United Kingdom.** The Energy Act 2008 established a licensing regime for offshore carbon dioxide storage, administered by the North Sea Transition Authority, and the Energy Act 2023 established a framework for the economic regulation of carbon dioxide transport and storage networks by Ofgem, together with revenue support mechanisms for industrial carbon capture, power with CCS, and hydrogen production.

**The United States.** The geological storage of carbon dioxide is regulated by the Environmental Protection Agency under the Safe Drinking Water Act's Underground Injection Control programme, which established in 2010 a dedicated "Class VI" well category for geological sequestration, with requirements relating to site characterisation, well construction, monitoring, financial responsibility, and post-injection site care; states may obtain primacy to administer the programme. The **section 45Q** tax credit, substantially enhanced by the Inflation Reduction Act, provides a credit per tonne of carbon oxide captured and stored or used, and has been a principal driver of CCUS investment.

### Pore Space and Long-Term Liability

Two legal questions are distinctive to onshore geological storage. The first concerns the **ownership of pore space** — the void spaces in subsurface rock formations in which carbon dioxide is stored. In common law jurisdictions where land ownership extends to the subsurface, the question arises whether pore space belongs to the surface owner or the mineral owner, where these have been severed. Several states of the United States, including North Dakota, Montana, and Wyoming, have enacted legislation vesting pore space in the surface owner, and provided for the "unitisation" or "amalgamation" of pore space, permitting storage operators to proceed with the consent of a specified proportion of owners, with compensation for non-consenting owners. In jurisdictions where the subsurface is owned by the state, as in most civil law and many developing countries, the state may grant storage rights through licences. In Nigeria, where minerals and petroleum are vested in the Federal Government, the ownership of pore space and the legal basis for storage licensing remain unaddressed, although the PIA's framework for petroleum operations might be adapted to regulate storage in depleted reservoirs.

The second concerns **long-term liability** for stored carbon dioxide, which must remain contained for centuries or millennia, far beyond the lifetime of the companies that inject it. Most regimes provide for the transfer of liability to the state after a period of post-closure monitoring, as in the EU CCS Directive, subject to conditions and financial contributions, and some, such as Australia's Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006, provide for the Commonwealth to assume long-term liability after a closing certificate is issued. The design of liability transfer must balance the need to provide investors with certainty against the risk of socialising the costs of leakage.

### CCUS in Climate Law and Carbon Markets

CCS was accepted as an eligible project activity under the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism by a decision of the parties in 2011, subject to modalities and procedures addressing site selection, monitoring, liability, and financial provision. Under the Paris Agreement, carbon dioxide removals, including through BECCS and DACCS, may generate credits under the Article 6.4 mechanism, subject to standards on removals adopted by its Supervisory Body, as examined in Chapter 18. The treatment of CCUS in climate law raises questions about the permanence of storage, the accounting for emissions from enhanced oil recovery, and the risk that reliance on future removals may delay emission reductions.
