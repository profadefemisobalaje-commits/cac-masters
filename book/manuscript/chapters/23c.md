## 23.5 Resource Revenue Distribution

### The Resource Curse

The paradox that countries rich in natural resources, particularly oil, gas, and minerals, have often experienced slower economic growth, greater inequality, more corruption, weaker democratic institutions, and a higher risk of civil conflict than countries with fewer resources is known as the **resource curse**. The term was popularised by the economist Richard Auty in 1993, and empirical work by Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner in the 1990s found a negative association between natural resource dependence and economic growth. Michael Ross's *The Oil Curse* (2012) documented the association between oil wealth and authoritarianism, conflict, and gender inequality, while Paul Collier's work linked resource dependence to the risk of civil war.

Several mechanisms have been identified: **Dutch disease**, in which a resource boom causes the real exchange rate to appreciate, harming the competitiveness of agriculture and manufacturing; **revenue volatility**, as commodity price swings produce boom-and-bust cycles in public spending; **rent-seeking and corruption**, as elites compete for control of resource rents; the **weakening of state–society accountability**, since governments funded by resource rents rather than taxation have less need to respond to citizens; and **conflict**, as resources finance armed groups or become objects of contestation between regions and groups. The resource curse is not inevitable: countries such as Norway, Botswana, Chile, and Malaysia have managed resource wealth relatively successfully, and the literature increasingly emphasises that the quality of institutions — including legal institutions governing revenue management — determines whether resources are a blessing or a curse.

### The Natural Resource Charter

The **Natural Resource Charter**, developed by economists and lawyers and now stewarded by the Natural Resource Governance Institute, sets out twelve precepts for the governance of natural resources for the benefit of citizens, covering the whole decision chain: the development strategy and the role of institutions; accountability and transparency; the exploration and licensing of resources; taxation; local impacts; the role of national resource companies; investment of revenues; smoothing of expenditure; the efficiency of public spending; private sector development; the role of companies; and the role of the international community. The second edition (2014) emphasises that the government should ensure that resource extraction yields the greatest possible benefit for citizens, taking into account the interests of future generations, and that the management of resource revenues should be transparent, accountable, and subject to public oversight.

### Fiscal Federalism and Derivation in Nigeria

In federal states, the distribution of resource revenues between the central government and sub-national units is a central question of constitutional law and politics, and in Nigeria it has been among the most contentious issues since independence. Under section 44(3) of the **Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999**, the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils, and natural gas is vested in the Government of the Federation. Revenues from petroleum accrue principally to the **Federation Account**, established by section 162(1), into which all revenues collected by the Government of the Federation are paid, subject to specified exceptions.

The Federation Account is shared among the federal, state, and local governments according to a **revenue allocation formula** prescribed by the National Assembly on the advice of the **Revenue Mobilisation Allocation and Fiscal Commission** (RMAFC). The vertical allocation formula in operation since the early 2000s allocates approximately 52.68 per cent to the federal government, 26.72 per cent to the states, and 20.60 per cent to local governments, with horizontal allocation among states and local governments based on factors including equality, population, landmass and terrain, social development, and internally generated revenue. The RMAFC has proposed revisions to the formula, which have been the subject of continuing debate.

Section 162(2) provides the **derivation principle**: the President shall table before the National Assembly proposals for revenue allocation, and in determining the formula, the National Assembly shall take into account the allocation principles especially those of population, equality of states, internal revenue generation, landmass, terrain, and population density, provided that the principle of derivation shall be constantly reflected in any approved formula as being **not less than thirteen per cent** of the revenue accruing to the Federation Account directly from any natural resources. The thirteen per cent derivation is paid to the oil-producing states. The history of the derivation principle reflects shifting political balances: under the regional constitutions of the 1950s and 1960s, derivation was fifty per cent or more, when the dominant export revenues came from agricultural produce of the regions; it was reduced progressively under military rule as oil became the dominant source of revenue, reaching as low as 1.5 per cent in the 1980s, before the 1999 Constitution restored a minimum of thirteen per cent.

The application of the derivation principle to offshore resources was addressed by the Supreme Court in ***Attorney-General of the Federation v Attorney-General of Abia State and 35 Others (No 2)*** (2002). The Court held that, for the purposes of derivation, the seaward boundary of a littoral state is the low-water mark of the land surface of the state or the seaward limit of inland waters within the state, so that revenues from offshore resources beyond that boundary do not accrue to the littoral states and are not subject to derivation. Following political agitation by the littoral states, the National Assembly enacted the **Allocation of Revenue (Abolition of Dichotomy in the Application of the Principle of Derivation) Act 2004**, which provides that the two-hundred-metre water depth isobath contiguous to a state is deemed to be part of that state for the purposes of computing derivation revenue, extending derivation to shallow offshore production but not to deep offshore production.

Oil-producing communities and states in the Niger Delta have long argued that the derivation principle does not adequately compensate them for the environmental and social costs of oil production or reflect their claims to the resources of their lands, giving rise to demands for **"resource control"** — the right of states and communities to own or control the resources in their territories, with payment of taxes or royalties to the federal government. The resource control agitation, advanced by governors of oil-producing states, ethnic nationality movements, and militant groups, has been a recurring theme of Nigerian politics and was debated in successive constitutional conferences, including the National Conference of 2014, without resolution.

### Special Institutions for the Niger Delta

In addition to derivation, the federal government has established special institutions to address the development of the Niger Delta. The **Niger Delta Development Commission** (NDDC), established by the Niger Delta Development Commission (Establishment, etc) Act 2000, is funded by contributions from the federal government, oil and gas companies (originally three per cent of their total annual budgets), and a share of the ecological fund, and is charged with formulating policies and implementing projects for the sustainable development of the region. The NDDC has been criticised for corruption, poor project execution, and political interference, documented in forensic audits. The **Ministry of Niger Delta Affairs**, established in 2008, and the **Presidential Amnesty Programme**, established in 2009 to disarm, demobilise, and reintegrate militants, are further elements of the institutional response. The **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** added **host communities development trusts**, funded by three per cent of the actual annual operating expenditure of upstream operators, to provide direct benefits to host communities, examined in Chapter 24. These overlapping mechanisms raise questions about coordination, accountability, and the direct benefit to citizens.

### Comparative Models of Revenue Sharing

Other resource-rich federations and decentralised states have adopted different models. In **Canada**, provinces own natural resources within their territories under section 109 of the Constitution Act 1867, as confirmed by the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 and section 92A, added in 1982, so that resource royalties accrue to the provinces, with equalisation payments from the federal government to less wealthy provinces. In the **United States**, resources on private land belong to the landowner, and royalties from federal lands are shared with states. In **Indonesia**, decentralisation laws allocate shares of resource revenues to producing regions, with special autonomy arrangements for Aceh and Papua. In **Iraq**, the Constitution of 2005 provides that oil and gas are owned by all the people of Iraq in all the regions and governorates, but the division of powers between the federal government and the Kurdistan Regional Government over oil has been a source of persistent dispute. The comparative experience suggests that the design of revenue-sharing arrangements must balance national cohesion, regional equity, compensation for the costs borne by producing areas, and incentives for good governance.

### Revenue Management Laws and Sovereign Wealth Funds

Many resource-rich states have adopted legal frameworks to manage resource revenues, including fiscal rules, stabilisation funds, savings funds, and transparency and oversight mechanisms.

**Norway**'s **Government Pension Fund Global**, which originated in the Petroleum Fund established in 1990, receives the state's net cash flow from petroleum activities and invests it abroad in equities, fixed income, real estate, and renewable energy infrastructure. Its value exceeds the equivalent of USD 1.8 trillion, making it the world's largest sovereign wealth fund. A **fiscal rule** limits the structural non-oil deficit financed from the fund to the expected real return, currently estimated at three per cent of the fund's value, so that petroleum wealth is converted into financial wealth that benefits future generations. The fund's ethical guidelines, administered by a Council on Ethics, provide for the exclusion of companies involved in specified activities, including thermal coal mining and power production above thresholds, and of companies whose conduct involves an unacceptable risk of serious human rights violations or severe environmental damage.

**Ghana**'s **Petroleum Revenue Management Act 2011** is widely regarded as a model in Africa. It requires all petroleum revenues to be paid into a Petroleum Holding Fund at the Bank of Ghana and allocates them according to a formula among the Ghana National Petroleum Corporation, the **Annual Budget Funding Amount** for public spending in priority areas, the **Ghana Stabilisation Fund**, to cushion the budget against revenue shortfalls, and the **Ghana Heritage Fund**, an endowment for future generations. It establishes the **Public Interest and Accountability Committee** (PIAC), a body representing civil society and professional groups, to monitor compliance and report publicly. Implementation has faced challenges, including the capping of the Stabilisation Fund and the use of revenues for debt service, but the Act's transparency and oversight provisions have been praised.

**Nigeria**'s experience has been more troubled. The **Excess Crude Account**, established administratively in 2004 to save oil revenues above a benchmark price, lacked a clear legal basis and was depleted through withdrawals agreed by the federal and state governments, particularly during the oil price collapse of 2014–2016. The **Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority (Establishment, etc) Act 2011** established the **Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority** (NSIA) to manage three funds: the **Stabilisation Fund**, the **Future Generations Fund**, and the **Nigeria Infrastructure Fund**. The NSIA has developed a reputation for professional management and transparency, but its capitalisation has been modest relative to the scale of Nigeria's oil revenues, reflecting the political difficulty of saving revenues in a federation with pressing current needs and constitutional obligations to share revenues among all tiers of government. The **Fiscal Responsibility Act 2007** introduced fiscal rules, including an oil price-based fiscal rule, though compliance has been inconsistent.

Other models include **Botswana**'s Pula Fund, built from diamond revenues, and its Sustainable Budget Index; **Chile**'s Economic and Social Stabilisation Fund, based on copper revenues and a structural balance rule; **Timor-Leste**'s Petroleum Fund, which finances most of the state budget; and the **Alaska Permanent Fund**, which pays an annual dividend to every resident of the state — a model that has inspired proposals for direct distribution of resource revenues to citizens ("oil-to-cash") as a means of strengthening accountability and reducing poverty.

The **Chad–Cameroon pipeline** project provides a cautionary tale. The World Bank's support for the project in 2000 was conditioned on Chad's adoption of a petroleum revenue management law (Law No. 001 of 1999), which required most direct revenues to be allocated to priority sectors and a Future Generations Fund and established an oversight committee. In 2006, Chad amended the law to increase discretionary spending and abolish the Future Generations Fund, and in 2008, after Chad failed to comply with its obligations, the World Bank withdrew from the project. The experience demonstrated the limits of externally imposed revenue management frameworks where domestic political commitment is lacking.

## 23.6 Intergenerational Equity

### The Principle

**Intergenerational equity** holds that each generation holds the earth and its resources in trust for future generations and has obligations to pass on a planet and resource base that is in no worse condition than it received. The principle was given its most influential legal articulation by Edith Brown Weiss in *In Fairness to Future Generations* (1989), which proposed three principles: **conservation of options**, requiring each generation to conserve the diversity of the natural and cultural resource base so as not to restrict the options available to future generations; **conservation of quality**, requiring each generation to maintain the quality of the planet so that it is passed on in no worse condition; and **conservation of access**, requiring each generation to provide its members with equitable rights of access to the legacy of past generations and to conserve this access for future generations.

The principle is reflected in the Brundtland Commission's definition of **sustainable development** as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (1987); in Principle 3 of the Rio Declaration (1992), which provides that the right to development must be fulfilled so as to equitably meet developmental and environmental needs of present and future generations; in Article 3(1) of the UNFCCC, which requires parties to protect the climate system for the benefit of present and future generations; and in the preamble to the Paris Agreement. Its legal status remains debated: it is widely invoked as a guiding principle, but its content and justiciability are uncertain, since future generations cannot be represented in the conventional sense, and their interests must be articulated by present persons and institutions.

### Judicial Recognition

Courts have increasingly invoked intergenerational equity, particularly in environmental and climate litigation. In ***Minors Oposa v Secretary of the Department of Environment and Natural Resources*** (1993), the Supreme Court of the Philippines recognised the standing of children to sue on their own behalf and on behalf of generations yet unborn to challenge timber licences, on the basis of the constitutional right to a balanced and healthful ecology and the concept of intergenerational responsibility. In ***Neubauer v Germany*** (2021), examined in Chapter 18, the Federal Constitutional Court held that Germany's Climate Protection Act violated fundamental rights by failing to provide sufficient specifications for emission reductions after 2030, thereby unconstitutionally shifting the burden of reductions to the future — an "intertemporal guarantee of freedom". In ***Future Generations v Ministry of the Environment*** (2018), the Supreme Court of Colombia recognised the Colombian Amazon as a subject of rights and ordered the government to adopt an intergenerational pact to reduce deforestation. The **advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice** of 23 July 2025, examined in Chapter 18, referred to intergenerational equity as a consideration relevant to the interpretation of states' climate obligations.

### Institutions for Future Generations

Several states have established institutions to represent the interests of future generations. **Wales**'s **Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015** requires public bodies to carry out sustainable development, defined by reference to seven well-being goals, and establishes a **Future Generations Commissioner** to promote the sustainable development principle and to review the work of public bodies. **Hungary** established a Parliamentary Commissioner for Future Generations in 2008, later incorporated as a deputy of the ombudsman. In 2023, a group of experts adopted the **Maastricht Principles on the Human Rights of Future Generations**, which articulate the obligations of states to respect, protect, and fulfil the human rights of future generations. At the international level, the **Pact for the Future**, adopted by the UN General Assembly in September 2024, included a **Declaration on Future Generations**, committing states to consider the interests of future generations in decision-making.

### Application to Non-Renewable Resources

The extraction of non-renewable resources raises distinctive questions of intergenerational equity, since resources consumed by one generation are unavailable to the next. Economists have proposed the **Hartwick rule**, under which intergenerational equity in consumption can be maintained if the rents from the extraction of non-renewable resources are invested in reproducible capital — physical, human, or financial — so that the total stock of capital is maintained. Sovereign wealth funds, such as Norway's fund and Ghana's Heritage Fund, examined in section 23.5, are institutional expressions of this approach, converting depleting natural assets into financial assets that benefit future generations. Investment in education, health, and infrastructure may serve the same purpose.

The energy transition adds a further dimension. Intergenerational equity requires not only that the wealth from resources be shared with future generations, but also that the present generation not impose on future generations the catastrophic costs of climate change. This implies that a substantial proportion of known fossil fuel reserves should remain unextracted — studies have estimated that large shares of oil, gas, and coal reserves would need to remain in the ground to meet the Paris temperature goals — and raises the prospect of **stranded assets** for producers. For resource-rich developing countries, this creates a dilemma: the window for monetising hydrocarbon resources may be closing, but their development may lock in emissions and expose future generations to the financial risks of stranded assets and the physical risks of climate change. In August 2023, voters in **Ecuador** approved, by around fifty-nine per cent, a referendum requiring the cessation of oil extraction in Block 43 (ITT) within the Yasuní National Park, an area of exceptional biodiversity and home to indigenous peoples living in voluntary isolation — a notable example of a democratic decision to leave proven reserves in the ground, although its implementation has been delayed. Proposals for a **Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty**, endorsed by a number of small island and other states, and the **Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance**, launched by Costa Rica and Denmark in 2021, seek to manage the phase-down of fossil fuel production in an equitable manner, including through support for developing producer states.

Intergenerational equity thus points in two directions for resource-rich developing countries such as Nigeria: towards the prudent management and saving of resource revenues for future generations, and towards a transition away from dependence on fossil fuel revenues in a manner that protects future generations from climate harm and fiscal shocks. Reconciling these demands, while meeting the urgent needs of the present generation — including the millions without access to electricity — is among the most difficult challenges of energy justice.
