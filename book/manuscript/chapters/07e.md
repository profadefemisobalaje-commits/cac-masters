## 7.8 International Standards for Responsible Mining

### The Proliferation of Standards

Mining is governed not only by domestic law and international treaties but by a dense and growing body of international standards developed by international financial institutions, industry associations, multi-stakeholder initiatives, and civil society. These standards address environmental and social performance, human rights, transparency, supply chain due diligence, and governance. Although most are not legally binding in themselves, they acquire legal significance in several ways: through incorporation into loan agreements and investment conditions; through incorporation into domestic law and licence conditions; through reference in contracts between companies and their customers; through their use by courts and tribunals as evidence of the standard of care or of good industry practice; and through the reputational and market pressures that lead companies to commit to them publicly. This section surveys the principal standards; supply chain due diligence standards specific to minerals, notably the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas, are examined in Chapter 8.

### International Finance Corporation Performance Standards and the Equator Principles

The **IFC Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability**, adopted by the International Finance Corporation (the private sector arm of the World Bank Group) and most recently revised in 2012, are the most influential environmental and social standards for project finance in emerging markets. They comprise eight standards: PS1 (Assessment and Management of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts); PS2 (Labor and Working Conditions); PS3 (Resource Efficiency and Pollution Prevention); PS4 (Community Health, Safety, and Security); PS5 (Land Acquisition and Involuntary Resettlement); PS6 (Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Management of Living Natural Resources); PS7 (Indigenous Peoples); and PS8 (Cultural Heritage). The Performance Standards are supported by Guidance Notes and by the World Bank Group's Environmental, Health, and Safety (EHS) Guidelines, including industry-specific guidelines for mining. Compliance with the Performance Standards is a condition of IFC financing, and the IFC's Compliance Advisor Ombudsman (CAO) receives complaints from communities affected by IFC-financed projects.

The **Equator Principles**, first adopted in 2003 by a group of commercial banks and now in their fourth iteration (EP4, effective from October 2020), commit participating financial institutions — over one hundred banks in more than thirty countries — to apply the IFC Performance Standards (and, in designated countries with robust governance, host country law) to project finance transactions above a specified threshold. EP4 strengthened requirements in relation to human rights, climate change risk assessment, and free, prior, and informed consent of indigenous peoples. Through the Equator Principles, the IFC Performance Standards have become a de facto global benchmark for the environmental and social performance of major mining projects, regardless of whether the IFC itself is involved.

The decision of the United States Supreme Court in *Jam v International Finance Corp* (2019), holding that international organisations such as the IFC enjoy only the same restrictive immunity from suit as foreign states under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, opened the possibility of litigation against the IFC in United States courts by communities harmed by projects it financed, although the subsequent proceedings in that case, concerning a coal-fired power plant in India, were dismissed on other grounds.[^jam]

[^jam]: *Jam v International Finance Corp*, 586 US 199 (2019).

### Industry Standards

The **International Council on Mining and Metals** (ICMM), whose members include most of the world's largest mining companies, has adopted Mining Principles comprising ten principles and associated performance expectations covering ethical business, decision-making, human rights, risk management, health and safety, environmental performance, conservation of biodiversity, responsible production, social performance, and stakeholder engagement. Members must implement the principles, report on their performance, and obtain independent assurance. ICMM has also adopted position statements on indigenous peoples and mining (committing members to work to obtain the consent of indigenous peoples for new projects), on climate change, on water stewardship, and on tailings governance, and co-convened the development of the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management discussed in section 7.4.

National industry initiatives include the Mining Association of Canada's **Towards Sustainable Mining** (TSM) programme, launched in 2004 and adopted by mining associations in several other countries, which requires member companies to assess and report their performance against protocols on tailings management, indigenous and community relationships, biodiversity, climate change, safety and health, crisis management, water stewardship, and preventing child and forced labour, with external verification.

### Multi-Stakeholder Standards

The **Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance** (IRMA), established in 2006 by a coalition of non-governmental organisations, affected communities, labour unions, mining companies, and purchasers of minerals, has developed the IRMA Standard for Responsible Mining, which provides for independent third-party audits of mine sites against comprehensive requirements covering business integrity, planning for positive legacies, social responsibility, and environmental responsibility. IRMA is distinctive in its equal governance by six stakeholder houses and in its requirement for site-level rather than corporate-level assessment. Purchasers of minerals, including automotive and electronics manufacturers, increasingly require their suppliers to undergo IRMA audits.

The **Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative** (EITI), launched in 2003, requires implementing countries to publish information on the extractive sector value chain, including the award of contracts and licences, beneficial ownership, production, revenue collection, revenue allocation, and social and economic spending, and to reconcile company payments with government receipts. Nigeria implements the EITI through the Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (NEITI) Act 2007, which makes Nigeria one of the few countries to have given EITI a statutory basis, and NEITI publishes regular audits of the oil, gas, and solid minerals sectors. The EITI is examined further in Chapter 16 in the context of revenue transparency.

The **Kimberley Process Certification Scheme**, established in 2003 following United Nations General Assembly resolutions on "conflict diamonds" that financed civil wars in Angola, Sierra Leone, Liberia, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, requires participating states to ensure that shipments of rough diamonds are accompanied by government-validated certificates attesting that they are conflict-free, and to trade rough diamonds only with other participants. The Kimberley Process has been criticised for its narrow definition of conflict diamonds, which is limited to diamonds used by rebel movements to finance wars against legitimate governments and does not extend to human rights abuses by governments or security forces, and for its consensus-based decision-making, which has blocked proposals for reform.

The **Voluntary Principles on Security and Human Rights**, adopted in 2000 by the governments of the United States and the United Kingdom, extractive companies, and non-governmental organisations, provide guidance to extractive companies on maintaining the safety and security of their operations within a framework that ensures respect for human rights. They address risk assessment, relations with public security forces (police and military), and relations with private security providers, and they have been incorporated into some host government agreements, security arrangements, and financing conditions.

### Regional Frameworks

At the regional level, the **Africa Mining Vision** (2009) and its Action Plan provide a policy framework for African mining that emphasises linkages between mining and the broader economy, beneficiation and value addition, artisanal mining, human and institutional capacity, and the management of mineral revenues. The African Minerals Development Centre, established to support implementation, has developed guidance on Country Mining Visions. In West Africa, the **ECOWAS Directive on the Harmonisation of Guiding Principles and Policies in the Mining Sector** (C/DIR.3/05/09), adopted in 2009, requires member states to harmonise their mining laws in accordance with common principles concerning human rights, transparency, environmental protection, community development, and the rights of local communities, including requirements for free, prior, and informed consent of local communities before exploration and prior to each subsequent phase of mining operations. The ECOWAS Directive is notable as one of the first binding regional instruments to require community consent in mining, although its implementation in member states has been uneven. The **ECOWAS Mining and Minerals Development Policy** of 2011 elaborates these principles.

### Evaluating the Standards Landscape

The proliferation of standards raises questions of legitimacy, coherence, and effectiveness. Standards developed by industry associations may be criticised as self-serving or lacking independent verification; multi-stakeholder standards may be more credible but are costly to implement; and the coexistence of multiple overlapping standards imposes compliance burdens and may confuse stakeholders. Standards are, moreover, most effective in relation to large companies with reputational exposure and access to international capital markets, and least effective in relation to state-owned enterprises, companies from jurisdictions with weaker accountability mechanisms, and the informal sector. Their legal status is ambiguous: they are not law in the traditional sense, yet they shape behaviour, inform legal standards, and may become binding through contract and regulation. The relationship between such transnational private regulation and formal law, analysed in Chapter 2, is one of the most important areas for doctoral research in natural resources law.

::: casebox
**Reko Diq: From the Supreme Court of Pakistan to ICSID (2013–2022)**

*Facts.* In 1993, the Balochistan Development Authority concluded the Chagai Hills Exploration Joint Venture Agreement (CHEJVA) with BHP Minerals for exploration of the Reko Diq area. BHP's interest was later acquired by Tethyan Copper Company, owned jointly by Barrick Gold and Antofagasta. Tethyan discovered a world-class copper-gold deposit and, after completing a feasibility study, applied in 2011 for a mining lease, which the Balochistan authorities refused.

*Domestic proceedings.* In January 2013, the Supreme Court of Pakistan, in proceedings brought by public interest petitioners, declared the CHEJVA void *ab initio* on the ground that it had been concluded in violation of the applicable mining rules and in excess of the authority of the officials who signed it.

*International proceedings.* Tethyan commenced ICSID arbitration under the Australia–Pakistan bilateral investment treaty. In its 2017 decision on jurisdiction and liability, the tribunal held that the refusal of the mining lease was motivated by the provincial government's intention to develop the project itself and breached the fair and equitable treatment standard and the prohibition of unlawful expropriation; it rejected Pakistan's allegations that the investment had been procured through corruption, finding them unproven. In July 2019, the tribunal awarded damages of approximately USD 4.1 billion plus interest, bringing the total to approximately USD 5.9 billion. Pakistan's application for annulment was pending when, in 2022, the parties reached a settlement under which Barrick would develop the project with the federal and Balochistan governments and Pakistani state-owned enterprises, and the award would be discharged. The Supreme Court of Pakistan, in an advisory opinion in December 2022, held that the new arrangements were not contrary to law or to its 2013 judgment.

*Significance.* The case illustrates the interaction between domestic and international legal orders in mining disputes: a domestic judgment invalidating a mining agreement did not prevent an international tribunal from finding the state liable for breaching treaty protections. It raises questions about the valuation of undeveloped mining projects, the role of corruption allegations in investment arbitration, and the use of settlements to convert awards into renegotiated partnerships.
:::

::: summary
- Mineral ownership is vested in the state in most jurisdictions; in Nigeria, section 44(3) of the Constitution and section 1 of the Minerals and Mining Act 2007 vest minerals in the Federal Government, while South Africa's MPRDA treats minerals as the common heritage of the people under state custodianship.
- Mining titles follow the mining life cycle — reconnaissance, exploration, mining — and are allocated by first-come, first-served, competitive tender, or discretion, ideally through a transparent electronic cadastre.
- Security of tenure, linking exploration to the right to mine, is central to investment; its denial has generated major awards, including *Crystallex* and *Tethyan Copper*.
- Mining agreements and stability clauses have been challenged by waves of resource nationalism, as in the DRC's 2018 Code and Tanzania's 2017 legislation; corruption can vitiate titles, as in the Simandou dispute.
- Environmental obligations include EIA, water and biodiversity protection, and tailings governance, transformed by the Brumadinho and Mariana disasters and the GISTM; social obligations include cultural heritage protection, highlighted by Juukan Gorge.
- Community development agreements, mandated by section 116 of the Nigerian Act, and revenue sharing mechanisms seek to share mining benefits with affected communities, but face challenges of representation, capacity, and enforceability.
- Mine closure law requires closure planning, financial assurance, and completion criteria to prevent public liability for abandoned mines.
- Artisanal and small-scale mining is best addressed through formalisation; the Minamata Convention requires action plans to reduce mercury use in ASGM.
- International standards — IFC Performance Standards, Equator Principles, ICMM, IRMA, EITI, Kimberley Process, Voluntary Principles, and the ECOWAS Mining Directive — increasingly shape mining practice and law.
:::
