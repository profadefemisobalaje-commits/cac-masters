## 18.2 Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement

### The Kyoto Protocol

The **Kyoto Protocol**, adopted at COP3 in 1997 and in force from 2005, gave effect to the "top-down" approach of setting legally binding, quantified emission limitation and reduction commitments for developed countries. Under Article 3 and Annex B, developed country parties undertook to reduce their aggregate emissions of six greenhouse gases by at least five per cent below 1990 levels in the first commitment period of 2008 to 2012, with individual targets ranging from an eight per cent reduction for the European Community to a ten per cent increase for Iceland. Developing countries, including China, India, Brazil, and the members of OPEC, had no quantified commitments.

The Protocol introduced three **flexibility mechanisms** to enable parties to meet their targets at lower cost: **international emissions trading** among Annex B parties (Article 17); **joint implementation**, under which Annex B parties could earn credits from emission reduction projects in other Annex B parties (Article 6); and the **Clean Development Mechanism** (CDM), under which Annex B parties could earn certified emission reductions from projects in developing countries that contributed to sustainable development (Article 12). The CDM registered several thousand projects, many in the energy sector, including renewable energy, energy efficiency, and the capture of industrial gases and landfill methane, and established an international infrastructure for the monitoring, reporting, and verification of emission reductions. It was also criticised for the questionable additionality of many projects, the concentration of projects in a few large developing countries, and the environmental integrity of credits from some project types. The Protocol's compliance mechanism, examined in Chapter 17, was the most rigorous of any MEA.

The Protocol's effectiveness was limited by its architecture. The United States did not ratify it; Canada withdrew in 2011; and because developing countries had no targets, the Protocol covered a declining share of global emissions as emissions from emerging economies grew. The **Doha Amendment** of 2012, establishing a second commitment period from 2013 to 2020, attracted limited participation and entered into force only on the last day of the period, at the end of 2020.

### From Copenhagen to Paris

The failure of the Copenhagen conference of 2009 to agree a comprehensive successor to Kyoto marked the end of the top-down approach. The **Copenhagen Accord**, which was "taken note of" rather than adopted by the COP, introduced a "bottom-up" approach under which countries — developed and developing — would pledge their own mitigation targets and actions, and articulated the goal of limiting warming to below 2°C and the commitment of developed countries to mobilise USD 100 billion a year by 2020 for climate action in developing countries. The **Cancún Agreements** of 2010 formalised these elements within the UNFCCC, and the **Durban Platform** of 2011 launched negotiations on "a protocol, another legal instrument or an agreed outcome with legal force under the Convention applicable to all Parties", to be adopted in 2015. In the lead-up to Paris, parties submitted "intended nationally determined contributions", which formed the basis of the new agreement.

### The Paris Agreement

The **Paris Agreement** was adopted at COP21 on 12 December 2015 and entered into force on 4 November 2016, after reaching the threshold of ratification by at least fifty-five parties accounting for at least fifty-five per cent of global emissions. It is a treaty within the meaning of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, but it combines legally binding procedural obligations with non-binding or discretionary substantive elements, in a design intended to secure universal participation — including that of the United States, whose administration sought to avoid the need for Senate approval — while creating a framework for progressively increasing ambition.

**The purpose and goals (Article 2).** The Agreement aims to strengthen the global response to climate change by holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C (Article 2(1)(a)); increasing the ability to adapt to the adverse impacts of climate change and fostering climate resilience and low greenhouse gas emissions development (Article 2(1)(b)); and making finance flows consistent with a pathway towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development (Article 2(1)(c)). The Agreement is to be implemented to reflect equity and the principle of CBDR-RC, "in the light of different national circumstances" (Article 2(2)). Subsequent decisions of the CMA, notably the Glasgow Climate Pact of 2021, resolved to pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C, and the International Court of Justice in its 2025 advisory opinion described 1.5°C as having become the parties' agreed primary temperature goal.

**Mitigation (Article 4).** To achieve the temperature goal, parties aim to reach global peaking of greenhouse gas emissions as soon as possible and to undertake rapid reductions thereafter, so as to achieve a balance between anthropogenic emissions by sources and removals by sinks — net zero — in the second half of this century (Article 4(1)). Each party "shall prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions that it intends to achieve", and "shall pursue domestic mitigation measures, with the aim of achieving the objectives of such contributions" (Article 4(2)). Each successive NDC "will represent a progression beyond the Party's then current nationally determined contribution and reflect its highest possible ambition, reflecting its common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances" (Article 4(3)). Developed countries "should continue taking the lead by undertaking economy-wide absolute emission reduction targets", while developing countries "should continue enhancing their mitigation efforts" and are encouraged to move over time towards economy-wide targets (Article 4(4)). NDCs are communicated every five years (Article 4(9)) and recorded in a public registry (Article 4(12)).

**Sinks and forests (Article 5)** and **cooperative approaches (Article 6)**, which provide for international transfers of mitigation outcomes and a new crediting mechanism, examined in section 18.5.

**Adaptation (Article 7)** establishes the global goal on adaptation of enhancing adaptive capacity, strengthening resilience, and reducing vulnerability to climate change, and requires each party to engage in adaptation planning processes.

**Loss and damage (Article 8)** recognises the importance of averting, minimising, and addressing loss and damage associated with the adverse effects of climate change, while the decision adopting the Agreement provides that Article 8 does not involve or provide a basis for any liability or compensation — a provision insisted upon by developed countries.

**Finance, technology, and capacity building (Articles 9–11)** provide that developed country parties "shall provide financial resources to assist developing country Parties with respect to both mitigation and adaptation", and encourage other parties to provide support voluntarily.

**Transparency (Article 13)** establishes an **enhanced transparency framework**, under which each party must regularly provide a national inventory report and the information necessary to track progress in implementing and achieving its NDC, subject to technical expert review and a facilitative, multilateral consideration of progress. Parties submitted their first biennial transparency reports under the framework by the end of 2024.

**The global stocktake (Article 14)** requires the CMA periodically to take stock of the implementation of the Agreement to assess the collective progress towards achieving its purpose and long-term goals, in order to inform parties in updating and enhancing their actions and support. The first global stocktake was concluded in 2023, and subsequent stocktakes will take place every five years.

**Compliance (Article 15)** establishes a mechanism to facilitate implementation and promote compliance, which is expert-based, facilitative, transparent, non-adversarial, and non-punitive.

### The Legal Character of the Paris Agreement

The Paris Agreement's legal character has been described as a "hybrid" of binding and non-binding elements. Its procedural obligations — to prepare, communicate, and maintain NDCs, to report on progress, and to participate in review — are legally binding obligations of result, expressed in mandatory language ("shall"). By contrast, the content of NDCs is nationally determined, and there is no legal obligation to achieve them; Article 4(2) requires parties to "pursue" domestic measures "with the aim of" achieving them, an obligation of conduct rather than result. The expectations of progression and highest possible ambition in Article 4(3) are expressed in the language of "will", which is less clearly mandatory. The Agreement relies on the combination of transparency, peer pressure, the global stocktake, and the five-yearly cycle of NDCs — the **ambition cycle** or "ratchet" mechanism illustrated in Figure 18.1 — to drive increasing ambition over time.

The question whether the Agreement's substantive provisions impose enforceable standards on parties' discretion in setting their NDCs was addressed by the International Court of Justice in its 2025 advisory opinion, examined in section 18.4, which held that the discretion of parties in preparing their NDCs is limited, and that NDCs must, taken together, be capable of achieving the temperature goal.

### The Paris Rulebook and Subsequent Decisions

The detailed rules for implementing the Agreement — the "Paris Rulebook" — were largely agreed at COP24 in **Katowice** in 2018, with the rules for Article 6 completed at **Glasgow** in 2021 and further operational details agreed at **Baku** in 2024. Key decisions since Paris include the following:

- the **Glasgow Climate Pact** (2021), which called on parties to accelerate efforts towards the phasedown of unabated coal power and the phase-out of inefficient fossil fuel subsidies — the first explicit reference to fossil fuels in a COP decision — and requested parties to revisit and strengthen their 2030 targets;
- the **Sharm el-Sheikh** decisions (2022), which established new funding arrangements, including a fund, for responding to loss and damage;
- the **UAE Consensus** following the first global stocktake at Dubai (2023), which called on parties to contribute to global efforts including tripling renewable energy capacity globally and doubling the global average annual rate of energy efficiency improvements by 2030, accelerating efforts towards the phasedown of unabated coal power, and "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner, accelerating action in this critical decade, so as to achieve net zero by 2050 in keeping with the science", and recognised that transitional fuels can play a role in facilitating the energy transition while ensuring energy security;
- the **New Collective Quantified Goal** on climate finance adopted at Baku (2024), under which developed countries are to take the lead in mobilising at least USD 300 billion a year by 2035 for developing countries, within a broader call for all actors to scale up finance to at least USD 1.3 trillion a year by 2035 — a figure widely criticised by developing countries as inadequate.

COP30, held in Belém, Brazil, in November 2025, was intended to mark the submission of a new round of NDCs and a shift towards implementation. Its formal outcome did not include an agreed roadmap for transitioning away from fossil fuels, despite the support of a substantial group of parties, and the presidency indicated that work on such roadmaps would be taken forward outside the formal negotiations. The difficulty of translating the Dubai language on fossil fuels into concrete commitments illustrates the continuing influence of producer states and the limits of consensus decision-making.

## 18.3 Nationally Determined Contributions

### Content and Form

Nationally determined contributions are the central instrument of the Paris Agreement. Each party determines the content of its NDC, which may take various forms: economy-wide absolute emission reduction targets relative to a base year (the form expected of developed countries); targets relative to a "business as usual" projection; emissions intensity targets, relative to gross domestic product; peaking years; or sectoral policies and actions, such as renewable energy or forestry targets. Many developing country NDCs distinguish between **unconditional** contributions, which the party will implement with its own resources, and **conditional** contributions, which depend on the provision of international finance, technology, and capacity building. Decisions of the CMA require parties to provide information to facilitate clarity, transparency, and understanding (ICTU) of their NDCs, including the reference point, time frames, scope and coverage, planning processes, assumptions and methodological approaches, and how the party considers that its NDC is fair and ambitious.

Parties submitted their first NDCs around the time of the Agreement's entry into force, updated or enhanced NDCs in 2020–2021, and were expected to submit a new round of NDCs, containing targets for 2035, by early 2025, although many parties submitted late. The synthesis reports prepared by the UNFCCC secretariat and the annual Emissions Gap Reports of UNEP have consistently found that the aggregate effect of NDCs falls far short of what is required to meet the Paris temperature goal; UNEP's 2024 report estimated that full implementation of then-current unconditional NDCs would limit warming to around 2.6–2.8°C this century, and that continuation of current policies would result in warming of around 3°C — the so-called **ambition gap** and **implementation gap**.

### The Energy Content of NDCs

Because energy accounts for the largest share of emissions, NDCs are in substance energy policy documents. Typical energy measures include targets for the share of renewable energy in electricity generation, energy efficiency targets, coal phase-out dates, the electrification of transport, the reduction of gas flaring and methane emissions, and the expansion of clean cooking. NDCs therefore provide a reference point for the interpretation of national energy legislation and for the assessment of the consistency of energy projects with national climate commitments, including in EIA, as examined in Chapter 17, and in litigation, as examined in section 18.6.

### Nigeria's NDC and Climate Legislation

Nigeria submitted its first NDC in 2015 and an updated NDC in 2021, which committed to an unconditional reduction of twenty per cent in greenhouse gas emissions below business-as-usual levels by 2030, and a conditional reduction of forty-seven per cent subject to international support, with measures including the reduction of gas flaring, the expansion of off-grid solar, improved energy efficiency, clean cooking, and reductions in methane emissions from the oil and gas sector. Nigeria prepared a further NDC in 2025, setting targets for 2035, as part of the third round of contributions.

In 2021, Nigeria enacted the **Climate Change Act**, which provides a framework for achieving low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development. The Act sets a target date for achieving net zero emissions between 2050 and 2070; establishes the **National Council on Climate Change**, chaired by the President, with responsibility for coordinating climate policy and administering a Climate Change Fund; requires the Federal Ministry of Environment to set a carbon budget for successive five-year periods; requires the development of a National Climate Change Action Plan; imposes climate-related obligations on ministries, departments, agencies, and private entities with fifty or more employees; and provides for the development of mechanisms for carbon emission trading and carbon taxation. The **Energy Transition Plan**, launched by the federal government in 2021, sets out a pathway to net zero emissions in the energy sector by 2060, emphasising the role of natural gas as a transition fuel, the expansion of renewable energy, and universal access to modern energy by 2030. The relationship between these instruments and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, which promotes the development of petroleum resources, illustrates the tension that many developing producer states face between climate commitments and the development of hydrocarbon resources, examined further in section 18.7 and Chapter 24.

### National Climate Framework Laws

Climate framework laws, which establish long-term targets, carbon budgets, institutional arrangements, and accountability mechanisms, have been adopted by a growing number of states. The **United Kingdom's Climate Change Act 2008**, the first such law, originally set a target of an eighty per cent reduction in emissions below 1990 levels by 2050, amended in 2019 to net zero; established a system of five-yearly legally binding carbon budgets set fifteen years in advance; created the independent Climate Change Committee to advise on budgets and report on progress; and requires the government to publish proposals and policies for meeting the budgets. In *R (Friends of the Earth Ltd) v Secretary of State for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy* [2022] EWHC 1841 (Admin), the High Court held that the government's net zero strategy did not comply with its statutory obligations because it lacked sufficient information on the quantified effects of its policies, and in a further decision in 2024 the court held that the replacement Carbon Budget Delivery Plan was also unlawful, as the minister had not been given sufficient information about the risks to the delivery of policies.

The **European Climate Law** (Regulation (EU) 2021/1119) makes binding the Union's objective of climate neutrality by 2050 and its target of a net domestic reduction of at least fifty-five per cent below 1990 levels by 2030, implemented through the "Fit for 55" package of legislation, including reforms of the EU Emissions Trading System, the Renewable Energy Directive, and the Energy Efficiency Directive; the Commission has proposed a 2040 target of a ninety per cent net reduction. Germany's Federal Climate Change Act of 2019 was amended in 2021 following the decision of the Federal Constitutional Court in *Neubauer*, examined in section 18.6. In Africa, **Kenya's Climate Change Act 2016**, amended in 2023 to regulate carbon markets, and **South Africa's Climate Change Act 2024** provide framework legislation, the latter providing for sectoral emission targets and carbon budgets for companies.
