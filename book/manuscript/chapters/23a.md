## Introduction

The preceding chapters have examined the legal frameworks governing the production, trade, and regulation of energy and natural resources, and the protection of the environment and human rights. This chapter asks a more fundamental question: are the benefits and burdens of energy and resource systems distributed justly, and how can law contribute to making them more just? The question is pressing. Hundreds of millions of people, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa, lack access to electricity; more than two billion lack access to clean cooking fuels and technologies, with grave consequences for health, especially for women and children; and many households, in rich and poor countries alike, cannot afford the energy services they need. At the same time, the communities that host oil fields, mines, and power plants often bear their environmental and social costs without sharing equitably in their benefits, and the revenues from natural resources have frequently enriched elites rather than improving the lives of citizens — the phenomenon known as the "resource curse". The energy transition, while necessary to avert dangerous climate change, raises its own questions of justice: who will pay for it, who will lose their livelihoods, who will benefit from new industries, and whose lands and waters will be used for renewable energy and transition minerals?

**Energy justice** has emerged over the past decade and a half as a framework for addressing these questions, drawing on philosophy, law, economics, and the social sciences. It complements the more established concepts of environmental justice and climate justice and offers analytical tools — the "tenets" of distributional, procedural, and recognition justice, supplemented by restorative and intergenerational dimensions — that can be applied to evaluate and design energy law and policy. This chapter examines the theoretical foundations of energy justice (section 23.1) and its principal tenets (section 23.2), before applying them to energy poverty and universal access (section 23.3), gender and social equity (section 23.4), resource revenue distribution (section 23.5), intergenerational equity (section 23.6), the Sustainable Development Goals (section 23.7), and the design of equitable energy regulatory systems (section 23.8).

## 23.1 Theoretical Foundations of Energy Justice

### The Emergence of Energy Justice Scholarship

Concerns about the fairness of energy systems are not new — debates about the affordability of electricity, the rights of communities affected by dams and mines, and the distribution of oil revenues are as old as the modern energy industry — but the articulation of **energy justice** as a distinct conceptual framework dates from the early 2010s. Scholars such as Kirsten Jenkins, Darren McCauley, Raphael Heffron, Benjamin Sovacool, and Michael Dworkin developed the concept to bring the insights of justice theory to bear on energy policy, which had been dominated by technical and economic analysis focused on security, cost, and, increasingly, carbon emissions. In an influential formulation, McCauley, Heffron, Stephan, and Jenkins (2013) proposed a "triumvirate of tenets" — distributional, procedural, and recognition justice — drawn from environmental justice scholarship. Sovacool and Dworkin, in *Global Energy Justice* (2014), defined energy justice as a global energy system that fairly disseminates both the benefits and costs of energy services and one that has representative and impartial energy decision-making, and proposed a set of principles — availability, affordability, due process, transparency and accountability, sustainability, intragenerational equity, intergenerational equity, and responsibility — to guide policy. Later scholarship added **restorative justice**, concerned with repairing harms caused by energy activities, and **cosmopolitan justice**, concerned with obligations that extend beyond national borders.

Energy justice is both a **conceptual tool** for analysing how energy systems distribute benefits and burdens, an **analytical tool** for researchers to understand how values are incorporated into energy systems, and a **decision-making tool** for policymakers and lawyers seeking to design fairer laws and institutions. Its relationship to law is direct: the legal rules governing access to energy, tariff-setting, licensing, land acquisition, environmental assessment, revenue distribution, and participation are among the principal means by which the distribution of benefits and burdens is determined, and legal processes are among the principal means by which injustice may be contested.

### Philosophical Foundations

Energy justice draws on several traditions of political philosophy.

**Distributive justice** theories address the fair distribution of goods and burdens in society. John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) proposed that principles of justice are those that free and rational persons would choose behind a "veil of ignorance", not knowing their place in society, and argued that they would choose equal basic liberties for all and the "difference principle", under which social and economic inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society. Applied to energy, a Rawlsian approach would prioritise the energy needs of the poorest and evaluate energy policies by their effects on the least advantaged. Utilitarian approaches, by contrast, evaluate policies by their aggregate welfare effects, underlying much cost-benefit analysis in energy policy, while libertarian approaches emphasise property rights and voluntary exchange, informing market-based models of energy regulation.

The **capabilities approach**, developed by Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum, focuses not on resources or welfare as such but on what people are actually able to do and to be — their capabilities to achieve functionings they have reason to value, such as being well nourished, healthy, educated, and able to participate in social life. Energy is not valued for its own sake but for the capabilities it enables: cooking, heating, lighting, refrigeration, communication, mobility, education, and productive work. The capabilities approach has been influential in understanding energy poverty as a deprivation of capabilities rather than merely a lack of access to particular fuels or a high ratio of expenditure to income, and it informs the multidimensional measurement of energy access.

The **politics of recognition**, associated with Nancy Fraser, Axel Honneth, and Iris Marion Young, holds that justice requires not only the fair distribution of goods but also the recognition of the equal status and distinct identities of social groups, and that misrecognition — cultural domination, non-recognition, and disrespect — is itself a form of injustice. Fraser's account of justice as **parity of participation** integrates redistribution, recognition, and representation. Applied to energy, recognition justice draws attention to groups whose needs and perspectives are ignored or devalued in energy decision-making — indigenous peoples, women, the rural poor, people with disabilities, and minorities.

### Environmental Justice

**Environmental justice** emerged as a social movement in the United States in the 1980s, when civil rights activists protested against the disproportionate siting of hazardous waste facilities in African American and low-income communities — notably in Warren County, North Carolina, in 1982. The United Church of Christ's report *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States* (1987) documented a strong correlation between race and the location of hazardous waste sites. Executive Order 12898 of 1994 directed federal agencies to identify and address the disproportionately high and adverse human health or environmental effects of their programmes on minority and low-income populations. In 2021 and 2023, the federal government adopted further environmental justice measures, including the **Justice40 Initiative**, which aimed to direct forty per cent of the overall benefits of certain federal investments in climate and clean energy to disadvantaged communities; these measures were revoked in January 2025. Environmental justice scholarship, notably the work of Robert Bullard and David Schlosberg, has developed the distributional, procedural, and recognition dimensions on which energy justice draws.

In the Global South, environmental justice movements have often arisen in response to resource extraction — the "environmentalism of the poor" described by Joan Martínez-Alier — including the struggles of the Ogoni and other communities of the Niger Delta against the impacts of oil production, examined in Chapters 17 and 21. The **Environmental Justice Atlas**, a global database, has documented thousands of environmental conflicts, a large proportion of them related to fossil fuels, mining, and energy infrastructure.

### Climate Justice

**Climate justice** addresses the distribution of the burdens of climate change and of the costs of mitigation and adaptation. It highlights that those who have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions — the poor, developing countries, small island states, and future generations — are often the most vulnerable to climate impacts, while the historical responsibility for emissions lies predominantly with industrialised countries. Climate justice is reflected in the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the provisions on climate finance and loss and damage, and in the concept of just transition, examined in Chapter 18. The tension between the development needs of energy-poor countries and the imperative of decarbonisation — the "energy trilemma" of security, equity, and sustainability, as framed by the World Energy Council — is a central concern of energy justice in Africa, where proponents of the right to development argue that countries with low historical emissions should be permitted to develop their fossil fuel resources, including natural gas, to meet their energy needs, while others argue that renewable energy offers a more just and sustainable path.

### Energy Justice and Law

The relationship between energy justice and law operates at several levels. Law **embodies** distributional choices — for example, in the allocation of resource ownership, the design of tariffs and subsidies, and the sharing of revenues. Law **structures** procedures — determining who participates in decisions, what information is available, and what remedies exist. Law **recognises** or fails to recognise particular groups and interests — for example, through the recognition of customary land rights, indigenous peoples, and gender equality. And law provides **avenues for contestation** — through litigation, complaints mechanisms, and political advocacy. Scholars such as Heffron have argued that energy law should be understood as a discipline whose purpose includes the achievement of energy justice, and have proposed that the principles of energy justice be incorporated into legislation, regulatory mandates, and judicial reasoning.

## 23.2 Distributional, Procedural, and Recognition Justice

### Distributional Justice

**Distributional justice** concerns the allocation of the benefits and burdens of energy systems. The benefits include access to energy services, employment, revenues, infrastructure, and economic development; the burdens include pollution, health risks, landscape impacts, displacement, the costs of energy, and the risks of accidents. Distributional injustice occurs when these are allocated unequally in ways that cannot be justified — for example, when pollution from power plants and refineries is concentrated in poor or minority neighbourhoods, when communities hosting oil fields remain without electricity or clean water, or when the costs of the energy transition fall disproportionately on low-income households.

Distributional questions arise at multiple scales. **Globally**, energy consumption per capita varies enormously: the average American consumes many times as much electricity as the average Nigerian, and the richest tenth of the world's population is responsible for around half of consumption-based emissions. **Nationally**, energy access, quality, and affordability vary between urban and rural areas, regions, and income groups; in Nigeria, for example, access to grid electricity is concentrated in urban areas and the south, and the quality of supply varies widely among tariff bands, examined in section 23.8. **Locally**, the siting of energy infrastructure determines which communities bear its impacts.

Legal instruments that address distributional justice include tariff structures and subsidies, examined in section 23.8; universal service obligations and rural electrification programmes, examined in section 23.3; revenue-sharing arrangements, examined in section 23.5; benefit-sharing mechanisms for host communities, such as Nigeria's host communities development trusts, examined in Chapter 24; siting regulations and environmental standards; and compensation regimes. Distributional analysis is also increasingly incorporated into environmental and regulatory impact assessment, for example through distributional impact assessments of carbon pricing and other climate policies.

### Procedural Justice

**Procedural justice** concerns the fairness of the processes by which energy decisions are made. It requires that affected people have access to information, opportunities to participate meaningfully in decision-making, impartial decision-makers, and access to remedies. Procedural injustice occurs when decisions are taken without consultation, when information is withheld, when participation is tokenistic, when decision-makers are captured by interested parties, or when affected people lack access to justice.

The legal foundations of procedural justice in environmental and energy matters are well established. **Principle 10 of the Rio Declaration** (1992) provides that environmental issues are best handled with the participation of all concerned citizens at the relevant level, and that each individual shall have appropriate access to information, the opportunity to participate in decision-making processes, and effective access to judicial and administrative proceedings, including redress and remedy. These "three pillars" of environmental democracy are given binding form in the **Aarhus Convention** (1998), which applies in Europe and Central Asia and whose Compliance Committee has addressed numerous energy-related cases, including those concerning nuclear power, renewable energy planning, and national energy policies, and the **Escazú Agreement** (2018) in Latin America and the Caribbean. Procedural justice is also embodied in the requirements of environmental impact assessment, examined in Chapter 17, the principle of free, prior, and informed consent, examined in Chapter 21, the procedural requirements of administrative law governing energy regulators, and rights of access to information, such as Nigeria's **Freedom of Information Act 2011**.

Procedural justice in energy regulation involves the transparency and participation of regulatory processes, such as tariff reviews. The **Electricity Act 2023** of Nigeria and its predecessor require the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission to consult stakeholders in tariff-setting and rule-making, and NERC holds public hearings and consultations on major regulatory decisions; consumers may lodge complaints with distribution companies and, on appeal, with NERC's **Forum Offices**, consumer dispute resolution bodies located throughout the country. The effectiveness of such procedures depends on the capacity of consumers and civil society to participate, the availability of information in accessible forms, and the responsiveness of regulators.

### Recognition Justice

**Recognition justice** concerns whether the identities, needs, values, and knowledge of different groups are acknowledged and respected in energy systems. Misrecognition may take the form of the failure to recognise that particular groups exist or are affected, the disregard of their distinctive needs, the devaluation of their knowledge and cultural values, or stereotyping and discrimination. It is often a precondition of distributional and procedural injustice: groups that are not recognised are unlikely to be consulted or to have their needs met.

Examples of misrecognition in energy systems include the failure to recognise the customary land rights of rural communities and indigenous peoples in the siting of energy projects, examined in Chapter 21; the neglect of the distinct energy needs of women, who in many developing countries bear the burden of collecting fuel and cooking with polluting fuels, examined in section 23.4; the failure to account for the needs of people with disabilities, older people, and people with medical conditions dependent on electricity in the design of disconnection rules and tariffs; and the treatment of informal settlements as "illegal" and therefore ineligible for formal electricity connections. Legal instruments addressing recognition include the recognition of customary and indigenous rights, anti-discrimination law, the collection of disaggregated data, gender impact assessments, and protections for vulnerable customers in energy regulation, such as rules prohibiting the disconnection of vulnerable customers during winter or extreme heat.

### Restorative Justice

**Restorative justice**, a later addition to the energy justice framework, concerns the repair of harms caused by energy activities to people and the environment. It draws on the restorative justice tradition in criminal law, which emphasises repairing the harm caused by wrongdoing and restoring relationships, rather than merely punishing offenders. In energy, restorative justice is concerned with the remediation of pollution, the compensation of victims, the rehabilitation of mining and oil-producing areas, and the transition of communities dependent on declining industries. Legal instruments include environmental liability regimes, remediation obligations, decommissioning and mine closure requirements, compensation funds, and just transition programmes. The legacy of oil pollution in the Niger Delta, and the slow progress of the remediation of Ogoniland under the **Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project** established in 2016 following the UNEP Environmental Assessment of 2011, examined in Chapter 17, illustrates both the need for restorative justice and the difficulties of achieving it.

### Applying the Tenets: The Energy Transition

The tenets of energy justice can be applied together to evaluate energy policies and projects. Consider the deployment of a large solar park in a rural area. A **distributional** analysis would ask who benefits from the electricity and revenues — whether local communities gain access to electricity, employment, and a share of revenues, or whether the power is exported to urban centres or other countries — and who bears the burdens, such as the loss of grazing or agricultural land. A **procedural** analysis would ask whether affected communities were consulted, given adequate information, and able to influence the project's design and location, and whether they have access to grievance mechanisms. A **recognition** analysis would ask whether the customary land rights of local people, including women and pastoralists, were recognised, and whether their knowledge of the land was respected. A **restorative** analysis would ask how any harms will be remedied and how the site will be restored at the end of its life. The analysis may reveal that a project that appears beneficial in aggregate terms — reducing emissions and expanding generation capacity — nonetheless perpetuates injustice, and may suggest legal and design changes, such as community benefit-sharing, local ownership, or alternative sites.

The energy transition makes such analysis especially important. Studies of the transition have identified risks of "green grabbing" — the appropriation of land and resources for environmental ends — and of "sacrifice zones" in regions where transition minerals are extracted; of regressive effects of carbon pricing and of the costs of renewable energy support schemes passed through to electricity bills; of the concentration of the benefits of rooftop solar, electric vehicles, and energy efficiency subsidies among wealthier households; and of the displacement of workers and communities dependent on fossil fuel industries. A just transition, examined in Chapter 18 and in section 23.8, requires that these risks be identified and addressed through law and policy.
