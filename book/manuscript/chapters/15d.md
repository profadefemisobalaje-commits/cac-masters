## 15.6 Change-in-Law and Stabilisation Clauses: Drafting and Operation

### From Investment Protection to Contractual Drafting

Section 14.6 examined stabilisation clauses from the perspective of international investment law: their legal effect, their relationship with the sovereign's right to regulate, and their treatment by investment tribunals. This section examines change-in-law and stabilisation provisions from the perspective of the drafter and the contract manager: how the clauses are structured, what events they cover, what thresholds and procedures apply, and what remedies they provide. In practice, the effectiveness of contractual protection against regulatory change depends far more on careful drafting than on abstract debates about the validity of freezing clauses under international law.

### Defining "Change in Law"

The definition of the triggering event is the foundation of any change-in-law clause. Key drafting issues include the following.

**What counts as "law".** The definition typically covers legislation, subordinate legislation, regulations, decrees, and binding decisions of governmental authorities, and may extend to changes in the interpretation or application of law by courts or competent authorities, to the imposition of new requirements in permits, and to changes in tax law. Whether the definition includes changes in **industry standards**, **international obligations**, or the **policies** of regulators that are not legally binding is frequently contested.

**Which jurisdiction.** The clause usually covers changes in the law of the host state, but may also extend to changes in other relevant jurisdictions, such as the jurisdiction of equipment suppliers or financiers.

**Discriminatory and general changes.** Many PPAs and concession agreements distinguish between **discriminatory** (or specific) changes in law, which affect the project, the project company, or projects of the same type specifically, and **general** changes in law, which affect all businesses equally. Discriminatory changes are typically borne by the state counterparty, while general changes may be borne by the private party, at least within limits, on the theory that a general change is a normal business risk which the private party can price into its tariff and which affects all competitors equally. This distinction reflects the influence of the regulatory taking and non-discrimination doctrines examined in Chapter 14.

**Foreseeability.** Changes that were announced, published in draft, or reasonably foreseeable at the date of the contract may be excluded, placing on the private party the risk of changes it should have anticipated.

**Exclusions.** Changes in the rate of general income or corporate taxes are sometimes excluded, as are changes required by international obligations or by the private party's own breach.

### Thresholds and Procedures

Change-in-law clauses typically apply only where the change has a material effect, defined by reference to a financial threshold — for example, an increase in costs or decrease in revenue exceeding a specified amount per year, or a percentage of the project's revenue — so as to avoid disputes over trivial effects. The affected party must give notice of the change and its effect, supported by evidence, within specified time limits, and the parties are required to negotiate in good faith to agree on the appropriate adjustment, with recourse to expert determination or arbitration if they fail to agree. Mitigation obligations require the affected party to take reasonable steps to minimise the effect of the change.

### Remedies

The remedies available under change-in-law clauses take several forms, corresponding to the categories of stabilisation clause identified in section 14.6:

- **compensation** by the state or state-owned counterparty, through a lump sum payment or periodic payments;
- **tariff adjustment**, under which the tariff payable under a PPA or concession is adjusted to restore the private party's economic position;
- **extension** of the term of the concession or licence, to allow the private party to recover additional costs over a longer period;
- **relief** from the obligation to comply with the change, which is unusual and difficult to enforce;
- **termination**, with a termination payment, where the change is so severe that the project cannot continue.

The standard of restoration is critical. Clauses may require that the private party be restored to the position it would have occupied had the change not occurred, or to the same **economic equilibrium**, measured by reference to the project's financial model, its equity internal rate of return, or its debt service coverage ratios. Lenders typically require that change-in-law protection preserve the project's ability to service debt, and the financial model agreed at financial close is frequently used as the reference point for determining compensation.

### Political Force Majeure and Buy-Out Obligations

In PPAs and concession agreements with state counterparties in developing countries, change-in-law provisions are often combined with **political force majeure** provisions and **put option** or **buy-out** obligations, under which the host government is obliged to purchase the project for a specified price — usually sufficient to repay outstanding debt and, depending on the event, to provide a return on equity — if a political force majeure event continues beyond a specified period or a change in law makes the project unviable. These provisions are typically supported by government guarantees or put and call option agreements, and their credibility depends on the fiscal capacity of the host government, an issue examined further in Chapter 16.

### Guidance and Best Practice

The World Bank's **Guidance on PPP Contractual Provisions**, published in 2017 and revised in 2019, provides recommended approaches to change-in-law, force majeure, termination payments, and related provisions in public-private partnership contracts, including power and energy projects. The Guidance recommends that the allocation of change-in-law risk reflect the capacity of each party to control and manage the risk, that discriminatory changes generally be borne by the public party, and that the allocation of general change-in-law risk depend on the maturity of the market and the capacity of the private party to pass costs through to users. The **Principles for Responsible Contracts**, developed by the UN Special Representative on Business and Human Rights in 2011 and endorsed by the Human Rights Council, provide that stabilisation clauses should not prevent the state from implementing laws, regulations, or policies in a non-discriminatory manner in order to meet its human rights obligations, and that any compensation provisions should be carefully drafted to ensure that the protection of investors does not interfere with the state's legitimate efforts to fulfil those obligations. This principle reflects the shift in drafting practice, noted in section 14.6, from freezing clauses towards economic equilibrium clauses that preserve regulatory space while protecting the investor's economic position.

### Climate Policy and Change-in-Law Risk

The energy transition has brought change-in-law clauses to the centre of contractual risk allocation. Carbon taxes, emissions trading schemes, accelerated phase-out dates for coal and other fossil fuel plants, methane regulations, and the European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism, examined in Chapter 13, may all impose substantial costs on energy projects and trigger change-in-law protections. For long-term contracts entered into today, the foreseeability of climate policy measures is a key issue: given the commitments made under the Paris Agreement, examined in Chapter 18, a tribunal may consider that the introduction of carbon pricing or fossil fuel phase-out was reasonably foreseeable and therefore excluded from change-in-law protection, or that the investor assumed the risk. Drafters increasingly address climate measures expressly, either allocating their cost to a particular party or providing for specific adjustment mechanisms, rather than leaving the issue to general change-in-law provisions.

## 15.7 Liability Allocation, Indemnities, and Security Instruments

### Knock-for-Knock Indemnities

The offshore petroleum industry has developed a distinctive approach to the allocation of liability for personal injury and property damage among the many contractors and subcontractors working on an installation: the **knock-for-knock** (or reciprocal) indemnity. Under a knock-for-knock arrangement, each party bears responsibility for injury to its own personnel and damage to its own property, regardless of fault, and indemnifies the other parties against claims in respect of them. Thus, if a drilling contractor's employee is injured through the negligence of the operator, the drilling contractor indemnifies the operator against the employee's claim. The rationale is that the arrangement avoids the cost and complexity of determining fault among multiple parties in a complex operating environment, allows each party to insure its own people and property without duplicating cover, and reduces disputes. Knock-for-knock indemnities are usually extended to the parties' respective "groups", including affiliates, co-venturers, and subcontractors, through mutual hold-harmless deeds, and may cover pollution and well control liabilities, typically allocating to the operator responsibility for pollution emanating from the reservoir and to the contractor responsibility for pollution emanating from its own equipment above the surface.

The enforceability of indemnities that cover the indemnified party's own negligence depends on clear drafting. Under English law, applying the principles derived from *Canada Steamship Lines Ltd v The King* [1952] AC 192, an indemnity will cover the indemnified party's negligence only if it does so expressly or by necessary implication, although modern courts approach the issue as one of ordinary contractual interpretation. In the United States, several states have enacted **anti-indemnity statutes** limiting indemnities for the indemnified party's own negligence in oilfield contracts, which may affect knock-for-knock arrangements.

### The Deepwater Horizon Litigation

The 2010 Deepwater Horizon disaster in the Gulf of Mexico, which killed eleven workers and caused the largest marine oil spill in United States history, tested the indemnity provisions of the drilling contract between BP and Transocean, the owner and operator of the drilling rig. The contract allocated to BP responsibility for pollution originating below the surface of the water, and to Transocean responsibility for pollution originating above the surface. In 2012, the federal district court held that BP was obliged to indemnify Transocean for third-party compensatory damages arising from pollution originating below the surface, even if the pollution resulted from Transocean's own gross negligence, although the indemnity did not extend to punitive damages or civil penalties under the Clean Water Act, which public policy prevented from being indemnified. The decision illustrates both the strength of carefully drafted pollution indemnities and their limits where public policy intervenes. The litigation also demonstrated the scale of liability that can arise from a major incident: BP's total costs exceeded USD 60 billion, including civil and criminal penalties, compensation, and clean-up costs.

### Exclusion of Consequential Loss

Energy contracts commonly exclude liability for "consequential" or "indirect" losses, often defined to include loss of production, loss of profit, and business interruption. The interpretation of such exclusions has generated difficulties. Under the traditional English approach, derived from *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854), "consequential loss" was interpreted as referring to losses falling within the second limb of the remoteness rule — losses that do not arise naturally from the breach, but are recoverable only if they were within the contemplation of the parties at the time of contracting because of special circumstances. This interpretation meant that an exclusion of "consequential loss" might not exclude loss of profit or loss of production arising directly from the breach. More recent decisions have emphasised that the meaning of such exclusions depends on the ordinary meaning of the words in their context, and the industry practice is to define the excluded losses expressly, listing loss of production, loss of profit, and loss of use, and to apply the exclusion on a mutual basis, so that each party bears its own consequential losses regardless of fault. In *Transocean Drilling UK Ltd v Providence Resources plc* [2016] EWCA Civ 372, the Court of Appeal upheld a mutual exclusion of consequential loss in a drilling contract, which excluded the operator's claim for the spread costs incurred during a period of delay caused by the contractor's equipment failure, emphasising that the clause formed part of a coherent allocation of risk agreed between commercial parties of equal bargaining power, and should be given its natural meaning.

### Liability Caps and Carve-Outs

Energy contracts frequently limit each party's aggregate liability to a specified amount, such as a percentage of the contract price, the value of the work, or available insurance, with exceptions for liabilities that the parties consider should be unlimited. Typical carve-outs from liability caps and consequential loss exclusions include liability for fraud, **gross negligence or wilful misconduct**, death and personal injury, breaches of confidentiality, and the knock-for-knock indemnities themselves. The scope of these carve-outs is often heavily negotiated, and their interpretation, particularly the meaning of "gross negligence", frequently becomes the central issue in disputes following major incidents.

### Security Instruments

Energy contracts rely on various security instruments to secure performance and payment obligations.

**Parent company guarantees.** Where a contracting party is a subsidiary or special purpose vehicle, the counterparty may require a guarantee from its parent company, under which the parent undertakes to perform or procure performance of the subsidiary's obligations. Host governments commonly require parent company guarantees from licensees and contractors under PSCs to secure work programme commitments and decommissioning obligations, as examined in Chapter 5.

**Performance bonds and on-demand guarantees.** EPC contracts and other construction contracts typically require contractors to provide performance bonds, advance payment guarantees, and retention bonds issued by banks. The critical distinction is between **conditional** (or accessory) guarantees, under which the guarantor is liable only if the principal is in breach of the underlying contract, and **on-demand** (or independent) guarantees, under which the guarantor must pay on presentation of a complying demand, without inquiring into whether the underlying obligation has been breached. In *Edward Owen Engineering Ltd v Barclays Bank International Ltd* [1978] QB 159, the Court of Appeal held that a bank which has given an on-demand performance guarantee must honour it according to its terms, and is not concerned with whether the beneficiary is entitled to the payment as between itself and the principal, subject only to the exception of clear fraud of which the bank has notice. The autonomy of on-demand guarantees makes them a powerful tool for beneficiaries, but also exposes contractors to the risk of abusive calls, and courts in some jurisdictions, such as Singapore, permit injunctions restraining calls on grounds of unconscionability as well as fraud.

**Standby letters of credit.** Standby letters of credit perform a similar function to on-demand guarantees and are common in United States practice and in energy trading. They are typically governed by the ICC's **Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600)** or the **International Standby Practices (ISP98)**, while on-demand guarantees are commonly subject to the ICC's **Uniform Rules for Demand Guarantees (URDG 758)**, which set out the rules governing demands, examination, and payment.

**Payment security under PPAs.** Independent power producers selling to state-owned utilities in developing countries frequently require payment security in the form of letters of credit, escrow accounts, sovereign guarantees, or partial risk guarantees from multilateral institutions, as examined in Chapter 16.

### Insurance

Insurance complements contractual risk allocation. Energy projects commonly carry construction all risks insurance, operational property damage and business interruption insurance, third-party liability insurance, control of well insurance (for drilling operations), marine insurance, and political risk insurance. The interaction between insurance and contractual indemnities is critical: indemnities are only as valuable as the financial capacity of the indemnifying party, and insurance provides the funds to meet them. Contracts typically require each party to insure the risks it has assumed, to name the other parties as co-insureds, and to waive the insurers' rights of subrogation against them, thereby preventing an insurer that has paid a claim from recovering it from another party whom the indemnity scheme was designed to protect.
