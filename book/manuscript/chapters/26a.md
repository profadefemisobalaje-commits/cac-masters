## Introduction

The energy sector generates disputes of exceptional value, complexity, and duration. Long-term contracts for the exploration and production of petroleum, the construction of power plants and LNG terminals, the sale of gas and electricity, and the transit of energy across borders are exposed to shifts in prices, technology, regulation, and politics over decades. Investments are large, immobile, and vulnerable to host state action once made. Parties come from different legal systems and are often reluctant to submit to each other's courts; states and state-owned enterprises are frequently involved. These features explain why **international arbitration** has become the predominant method for resolving energy disputes. Energy and natural resources disputes consistently account for a substantial share of the caseloads of the major arbitral institutions: cases concerning oil, gas, and mining, together with electric power and other energy, account for around two-fifths of the historical caseload of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID), and they represent and a significant proportion of cases at the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA), and the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce (SCC). Many of the largest awards in the history of arbitration — *Yukos*, *ConocoPhillips v Venezuela*, *Process and Industrial Developments v Nigeria* (subsequently set aside), and the Gazprom-related awards — have arisen from energy disputes.

Earlier chapters have examined the substantive law applied in energy disputes: the protection of investments under treaties, including the institutional overview of investor–state dispute settlement and its reform in Chapter 14; the drafting and interpretation of energy contracts, including dispute resolution clauses, in Chapter 15; and the state–state disputes over maritime boundaries and shared resources in Chapter 20. This chapter focuses on the **process** of energy dispute resolution, from the perspective of the practitioner and the scholar of procedure. It examines the nature and classification of energy disputes (section 26.1), international commercial arbitration (section 26.2), the conduct of investment treaty arbitration (section 26.3), the ICSID and UNCITRAL frameworks (section 26.4), jurisdiction, admissibility, and applicable law (section 26.5), evidence, expert testimony, and damages (section 26.6), the recognition and enforcement of awards (section 26.7), and mediation, negotiation, and alternative dispute resolution (section 26.8). Litigation before national courts, including the jurisdiction of courts over transnational energy disputes and the enforcement of foreign judgments, is examined in Chapter 27.

## 26.1 Nature and Classification of Energy Disputes

### Why Energy Disputes Are Distinctive

Energy disputes share characteristics that shape the choice and design of dispute resolution mechanisms. First, they typically arise from **long-term relationships** — production sharing contracts of twenty to thirty years, gas sales agreements of twenty years or more, power purchase agreements of fifteen to twenty-five years — in which the parties may need to continue performing while the dispute is resolved, and in which the preservation of the relationship has value. Second, they involve **high stakes**: the amounts in dispute frequently run to hundreds of millions or billions of dollars, justifying substantial investment in legal representation and expert evidence. Third, they are **technically complex**, involving questions of geology, reservoir engineering, construction, pricing formulae, accounting, and valuation that require specialist expertise. Fourth, they frequently involve **states and state entities**, raising issues of sovereign immunity, public policy, and the interaction of contract and public law. Fifth, they are **politically sensitive**, touching on national sovereignty over resources, public revenues, energy prices, and environmental and social concerns, and attracting public and media attention. Sixth, they are **multi-jurisdictional**, involving parties, assets, and operations in several countries and requiring enforcement across borders.

### Classification by Parties

Energy disputes may be classified by the parties involved:

1. **Commercial disputes between private parties**, such as disputes between joint venture partners under joint operating agreements, between buyers and sellers under LNG or crude oil sales contracts, between project owners and EPC contractors, or between operators and service providers. These are generally resolved by international commercial arbitration or national litigation.
2. **Disputes between investors and states or state entities**, which may be contractual (arising from a concession, PSC, or PPA with the state or a state entity), treaty-based (arising from the alleged breach of an investment treaty), or both. These may be resolved by commercial arbitration under the contract, investment treaty arbitration under ICSID or UNCITRAL rules, or national courts.
3. **Disputes between states**, such as disputes over maritime boundaries and shared resources, transit, or the interpretation of treaties, resolved by negotiation, the International Court of Justice, the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, arbitration under Annex VII of UNCLOS, inter-state arbitration under bilateral treaties, or the WTO dispute settlement system.
4. **Disputes involving communities and civil society**, such as claims by communities affected by oil pollution against companies, or challenges to project approvals, generally resolved by national courts, non-judicial grievance mechanisms, or human rights bodies, as examined in Chapters 21 and 27.

### Classification by Subject Matter

By subject matter, recurring categories of energy disputes include: (a) **price review and price reopener disputes** under long-term gas contracts, examined in Chapter 6, which became the most common category of gas disputes in Europe from the late 2000s as oil-indexed contract prices diverged from hub prices; (b) **take-or-pay and delivery disputes**; (c) **cost recovery and profit oil disputes** under PSCs, including disputes over the allocation of costs and the computation of the state's share; (d) **fiscal and tax disputes**, including challenges to windfall taxes and the application of stabilisation clauses, examined in Chapters 14 and 16; (e) **expropriation and nationalisation**; (f) **termination and licence revocation disputes**; (g) **construction disputes** over delay, defects, variations, and cost overruns in energy infrastructure projects; (h) **joint venture disputes** over operatorship, sole risk, default, and pre-emption rights; (i) **unitisation and boundary disputes**; (j) **regulatory disputes**, including challenges to tariff decisions and the withdrawal of renewable energy incentives, of which the Spanish renewable energy arbitrations under the Energy Charter Treaty are the leading example; and (k) **environmental and climate-related disputes**, including claims arising from fossil fuel phase-out measures and, increasingly, counterclaims by states for environmental harm.

### Classification by Legal Basis and Forum

Energy disputes may also be classified by their legal basis — contract, treaty, statute, or general international law — and by the forum: national courts, international commercial arbitration, investment treaty arbitration, inter-state adjudication, or alternative mechanisms such as expert determination and mediation. The same dispute may involve several legal bases and fora. The expropriation of an oil investment, for example, may give rise to a contractual claim under the PSC's arbitration clause, a treaty claim under a BIT, proceedings in the host state's courts, and enforcement proceedings in several jurisdictions, raising questions about parallel proceedings, *lis pendens*, *res judicata*, and "fork-in-the-road" clauses, addressed in section 26.5.

### Why Arbitration Predominates

Arbitration's predominance in energy disputes reflects its advantages over national litigation for transnational disputes: **neutrality**, allowing parties to avoid the courts of either party's state; **party autonomy**, allowing parties to choose arbitrators with relevant expertise, the procedure, the seat, and the language; **enforceability**, through the New York Convention of 1958, which has more than 170 contracting states, and the ICSID Convention, which provides for the enforcement of ICSID awards as if they were final judgments of national courts; **confidentiality**, which is valued in commercial disputes although increasingly limited in investor–state disputes; and **finality**, with limited grounds for challenge. Its disadvantages include cost, duration, the limited availability of appeal, the difficulty of joining third parties, and, in investor–state arbitration, concerns about legitimacy examined in Chapter 14.

## 26.2 International Commercial Arbitration

### The Legal Framework

International commercial arbitration rests on a layered legal framework: the **arbitration agreement**, which is the foundation of the tribunal's jurisdiction; the **arbitration rules** chosen by the parties, whether institutional (such as the ICC, LCIA, SCC, Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC), or Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre rules) or ad hoc (such as the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules); the **law of the seat** (*lex arbitri*), which governs the arbitral procedure and the supervisory jurisdiction of the courts; the **law governing the arbitration agreement**; the **law governing the substance** of the dispute; and the **international conventions** governing recognition and enforcement, principally the **New York Convention**.

Many states have adopted arbitration legislation based on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration** (1985, amended 2006), which establishes principles including the separability of the arbitration agreement, the competence of the tribunal to rule on its own jurisdiction (*Kompetenz-Kompetenz*), the limitation of court intervention, equal treatment of the parties, and exhaustive grounds for setting aside awards. **Nigeria** was among the first states to adopt the Model Law, in the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1988**, which was repealed and replaced by the **Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023**. The 2023 Act modernises Nigerian arbitration law, introducing provisions on emergency arbitrators, third-party funding (with disclosure obligations), the joinder and consolidation of proceedings, the immunity of arbitrators, an **Award Review Tribunal** that parties may opt into to review awards on the grounds for setting aside, limitation periods that exclude the period of the arbitration, and mediation, and it confirms that awards made in Nigeria shall be recognised and enforced. The **English Arbitration Act 1996**, amended by the **Arbitration Act 2025** — which introduced a default rule that the law governing the arbitration agreement is the law of the seat, a statutory duty of disclosure for arbitrators, a power to make awards on a summary basis, and clarified the framework for jurisdictional challenges — governs arbitrations seated in London, the most popular seat for energy arbitrations, alongside Paris, Geneva, Stockholm, Singapore, and New York.

### The Arbitration Agreement

The arbitration agreement must be valid, cover the dispute, and be capable of being performed. In energy contracts, arbitration agreements are often embedded in **multi-tiered dispute resolution clauses**, which require the parties to negotiate, escalate the dispute to senior management, or submit it to mediation or expert determination before commencing arbitration, as examined in Chapter 15. The enforceability of such pre-arbitral steps has been addressed by courts in several jurisdictions: the English courts have held that a clause requiring "friendly discussions" for a specified period before arbitration was enforceable as a condition precedent (*Emirates Trading Agency v Prime Mineral Exports*, 2014), while later holding that non-compliance with pre-arbitral steps goes to admissibility rather than jurisdiction, and is therefore for the tribunal rather than the courts (*NWA v NVF*, 2021; *Republic of Sierra Leone v SL Mining*, 2021). The doctrine of **separability**, recognised in *Fiona Trust & Holding Corp v Privalov* (House of Lords, 2007), which also established a presumption that rational businessmen intend all disputes arising out of their relationship to be decided by the same tribunal, ensures that the arbitration agreement survives allegations that the main contract is invalid.

The **law governing the arbitration agreement** may differ from the law of the main contract and the law of the seat. In ***Enka Insaat ve Sanayi AS v OOO Insurance Company Chubb*** (UK Supreme Court, 2020), a dispute arising from the construction of a power plant in Russia, the Supreme Court held that, where the parties have not specified the law of the arbitration agreement, the law chosen for the main contract will generally apply to the arbitration agreement, and in the absence of such a choice, the arbitration agreement is governed by the law with which it is most closely connected, generally the law of the seat. The Arbitration Act 2025 has since reversed this approach for arbitrations seated in England, providing that the law of the seat applies unless the parties expressly agree otherwise. In ***Kabab-Ji SAL v Kout Food Group*** (UK Supreme Court, 2021), the Supreme Court held that the validity of an arbitration agreement at the enforcement stage is governed by the law chosen by the parties for the main contract, which in that case was English law, and refused enforcement of a French-seated award, while the French courts upheld it — illustrating the potential for divergent outcomes.

### Arbitral Institutions and the Energy Sector

The **ICC International Court of Arbitration**, founded in 1923, administers the largest number of international commercial arbitrations, and energy and construction disputes form a significant share of its caseload. Distinctive features of ICC arbitration include the **Terms of Reference**, which define the issues and procedure at the outset, and the Court's **scrutiny** of draft awards. The **LCIA** is favoured for disputes under English law, including many energy and commodity disputes; the **SCC** has historically been prominent in East–West energy disputes, including those involving Russia and the former Soviet states, and in Energy Charter Treaty cases; **SIAC** and the **HKIAC** have grown rapidly as Asian seats; and the **Permanent Court of Arbitration** (PCA) in The Hague administers many investor–state and inter-state arbitrations under the UNCITRAL Rules. In Africa, the **Lagos Court of Arbitration**, the **Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration**, the **Kigali International Arbitration Centre**, the **Arbitration Foundation of Southern Africa**, and the **Common Court of Justice and Arbitration** (CCJA) of OHADA, which serves the seventeen member states of the Organisation for the Harmonisation of Business Law in Africa, are developing as regional fora. The under-representation of African seats, institutions, and arbitrators in disputes involving African parties — despite the large number of energy and mining disputes involving African states — has been a persistent concern, addressed by initiatives such as the African Promise, launched in 2019 to improve the representation of African arbitrators.

### The Role of National Courts

National courts at the seat of arbitration play a **supportive** and **supervisory** role. In their supportive role, courts may appoint arbitrators where the parties' mechanism fails, grant interim measures in support of arbitration, compel the attendance of witnesses, and enforce arbitration agreements by staying court proceedings brought in breach of them, as required by Article II(3) of the New York Convention. Courts may also grant **anti-suit injunctions** restraining a party from pursuing proceedings in breach of an arbitration agreement, a power exercised by the English courts, including in support of foreign-seated arbitrations. In ***UniCredit Bank GmbH v RusChemAlliance LLC*** (UK Supreme Court, 2024), the Supreme Court upheld an anti-suit injunction restraining a Russian company from pursuing proceedings in Russia, under the exclusive jurisdiction conferred on Russian courts in sanctions-related disputes by Article 248.1 of the Russian Arbitrazh Procedure Code, in breach of a Paris-seated ICC arbitration agreement governed by English law, illustrating the role of courts in protecting arbitration from the fragmentation described in Chapter 25. In their supervisory role, courts at the seat may set aside awards on limited grounds, generally those in Article 34 of the Model Law, which mirror the grounds for refusing enforcement in Article V of the New York Convention, examined in section 26.7.

### Confidentiality and Transparency

Commercial arbitration is generally **private**, in that hearings are not open to the public, and in many jurisdictions it is also **confidential**, in that the parties are under an implied or express obligation not to disclose the proceedings and award. The scope of confidentiality varies: English law recognises an implied duty of confidentiality subject to exceptions, while Australian law, in *Esso Australia Resources v Plowman* (High Court of Australia, 1995), a case arising from gas supply arbitrations, held that confidentiality is not an essential attribute of arbitration, and that the public interest may require disclosure where a public authority is a party. The tension between confidentiality and the public interest is particularly acute in energy disputes involving states and public revenues, and the transparency of contract-based disputes involving states has been increasingly questioned, as the *P&ID* case, examined in section 26.7, illustrates.
