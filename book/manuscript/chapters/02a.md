## Introduction

The preceding chapter described international energy and natural resources law as a pluralist field drawing upon multiple bodies of law. This chapter examines the materials from which that law is made — its sources — and the general principles and institutions through which it operates. It begins with the formal sources of international law identified in Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice and considers how each functions in the energy context. It then examines the principle that more than any other defines the field: permanent sovereignty over natural resources. The chapter proceeds to the law of state responsibility, the principles of jurisdiction, and the growing role of international organisations, soft law, and industry standards. It concludes with an analysis of the institutions that adjudicate energy disputes and of the persistent problem of conflicts between domestic and international legal obligations.

A preliminary observation is in order. Students trained in a domestic legal system sometimes approach international law expecting a hierarchy of norms comparable to that of a national constitution, with clearly defined law-making institutions and a supreme court at the apex. International law does not work in that way. It is a decentralised system in which the subjects of the law — principally states — are also its makers, its interpreters, and, to a considerable extent, its enforcers. The sources of international law are therefore best understood not as a hierarchy but as a set of recognised processes through which states, and increasingly other actors, generate obligations. This decentralisation is particularly pronounced in the energy field, where there is no single governing treaty and where much of the normative work is done by contracts, standards, and the decisions of arbitral tribunals.

## 2.1 Treaties, Custom, and General Principles of International Law

### Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice

Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice directs the Court, in deciding disputes in accordance with international law, to apply:

> (a) international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the contesting states;
> (b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law;
> (c) the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations;
> (d) subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.

Although Article 38 is formally a direction to the Court, it is universally regarded as an authoritative statement of the sources of international law. The archaic reference to "civilized nations" is today understood to refer to the community of nations generally. Article 38 does not establish a formal hierarchy among treaties, custom, and general principles, although in practice a treaty, as *lex specialis*, will usually prevail over a conflicting rule of custom as between the parties to it, subject always to the overriding force of peremptory norms (*jus cogens*).

### Treaties

Treaties are the most important source of international energy law in terms of volume and specificity. The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties 1969 (VCLT) defines a treaty as "an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation". Treaties relevant to energy and natural resources may be classified as follows.

**Multilateral treaties of general application** that apply to energy and resource activities without being specifically directed at them. These include the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea 1982 (UNCLOS), the WTO agreements, the ICSID Convention 1965, the New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards 1958, the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change 1992 (UNFCCC), the Paris Agreement 2015, and the Convention on Biological Diversity 1992.

**Multilateral treaties specific to energy.** The Energy Charter Treaty 1994 is the principal example of a multilateral treaty dedicated to energy. Others include the Statute of the International Atomic Energy Agency 1956, the Agreement on an International Energy Program 1974 (which established the IEA), the Statute of the International Renewable Energy Agency 2009, and the suite of nuclear conventions on safety, security, liability, and waste management considered in Chapter 11.

**Regional treaties**, including the Treaty Establishing the Energy Community 2005, the ECOWAS Energy Protocol 2003 (modelled on the ECT), and the energy provisions of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, notably Article 194.

**Bilateral treaties**, including more than two thousand bilateral investment treaties, tax treaties, maritime boundary agreements, joint development agreements, unitisation agreements for transboundary reservoirs, and intergovernmental agreements for cross-border pipelines and interconnectors.

### Treaty Interpretation

Because so much of the field turns on the interpretation of treaties — investment treaties in particular — a doctoral candidate must have a thorough command of the rules of interpretation in Articles 31 to 33 of the VCLT, which reflect customary international law. Article 31(1) provides that a treaty "shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose". Article 31(3) requires account to be taken, together with the context, of subsequent agreements between the parties regarding interpretation, subsequent practice establishing agreement regarding interpretation, and "any relevant rules of international law applicable in the relations between the parties". Article 32 permits recourse to supplementary means of interpretation, including the preparatory work of the treaty and the circumstances of its conclusion, to confirm the meaning resulting from Article 31 or to determine the meaning when that interpretation leaves it ambiguous or obscure or leads to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result.

Three interpretive issues recur in energy disputes. The first is the weight to be given to the *object and purpose* of investment treaties: tribunals that emphasise the promotion and protection of investment, often stated in treaty preambles, have tended to adopt expansive interpretations of investor protections, while others insist that the object and purpose include a balance between investment protection and the host state's right to regulate. The second is the use of *subsequent agreements and practice*, including joint interpretive statements issued by treaty parties, such as the interpretive note issued by the NAFTA Free Trade Commission in 2001 limiting the minimum standard of treatment to the customary international law minimum standard. The third is *systemic integration* under Article 31(3)(c), which permits tribunals to take account of environmental, human rights, and climate obligations in interpreting investment treaties.

### Customary International Law

Customary international law arises from a general practice of states accepted as law (*opinio juris*). Both elements must be established: practice that is sufficiently widespread, representative, and consistent, and the acceptance of that practice as legally required or permitted. The International Law Commission's *Draft Conclusions on Identification of Customary International Law*, adopted in 2018, provide authoritative guidance on the evidence of each element.

Customary international law performs several functions in the energy field. It supplies rules of general application where no treaty exists, such as the rules on the protection of foreign property and the standard of compensation for expropriation, the obligation to prevent significant transboundary harm, and the rules on state responsibility. It fills gaps in treaty regimes and informs their interpretation. And in some areas — notably the minimum standard of treatment of aliens — treaty provisions expressly incorporate customary standards.

The identification of custom in the energy field is often difficult. Investment treaties are numerous and broadly similar in content, prompting debate over whether they have generated or codified customary rules. In *Barcelona Traction*, the International Court of Justice observed that, in the field of foreign investment, the law had developed through bilateral and multilateral agreements rather than general rules,[^bt] and this remains a contested question. The dominant view is that the proliferation of BITs has not of itself transformed their substantive standards into custom, because states conclude such treaties to obtain reciprocal benefits rather than out of a sense of legal obligation, although the treaties may serve as evidence of the content of the customary minimum standard.

[^bt]: *Barcelona Traction, Light and Power Company, Limited (Belgium v Spain)*, Second Phase, Judgment, ICJ Reports 1970, p. 3, paras 89–90.

### General Principles of Law

General principles of law recognised by the community of nations serve primarily to fill gaps in treaty and customary law. The International Law Commission adopted *Draft Conclusions on General Principles of Law* in 2023, distinguishing between principles derived from national legal systems and principles that may be formed within the international legal system. General principles of particular importance in the energy field include good faith, *pacta sunt servanda*, the prohibition of abuse of rights, the principle of unjust enrichment, estoppel, the principle that no one may profit from his own wrong, the obligation to make reparation for injury caused by a wrongful act, and the procedural principles of due process and equality of arms.

General principles played a central role in the arbitral jurisprudence of the concession era. In the *Abu Dhabi* arbitration of 1951, Lord Asquith of Bishopstone, acting as umpire, declined to apply the law of Abu Dhabi on the ground that no settled body of legal principles existed there that could be applied to the construction of modern commercial instruments, and instead applied principles "rooted in the good sense and common practice of the generality of civilised nations — a sort of 'modern law of nature'".[^abudhabi] In *Sapphire International Petroleums v National Iranian Oil Company* (1963), the arbitrator similarly applied general principles of law.[^sapphire] Such reasoning reflected attitudes towards non-Western legal systems that are now rightly regarded as unacceptable, and modern tribunals generally apply the law chosen by the parties or the law of the host state, supplemented where appropriate by international law.

[^abudhabi]: *Petroleum Development (Trucial Coast) Ltd v Sheikh of Abu Dhabi*, Award of September 1951, (1952) 1 ICLQ 247.

[^sapphire]: *Sapphire International Petroleums Ltd v National Iranian Oil Company*, Award of 15 March 1963, (1967) 35 ILR 136.

### Subsidiary Means: Judicial Decisions and Teachings

Judicial decisions and the teachings of highly qualified publicists are subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law. There is no formal doctrine of binding precedent in international law; Article 59 of the ICJ Statute provides that decisions of the Court have no binding force except between the parties and in respect of the particular case. Nevertheless, the decisions of the ICJ, the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, the WTO Appellate Body, and investment tribunals carry significant persuasive authority, and tribunals routinely cite earlier awards. In investment arbitration, a *jurisprudence constante* has emerged on many issues, although significant divergences persist. The influence of scholarly writing is also considerable: the works of authors such as Nico Schrijver on permanent sovereignty, Christoph Schreuer on the ICSID Convention, and Philippe Sands on environmental law are frequently cited by tribunals.

## 2.2 Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources

### Origins and Development

The principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources (PSNR) is the cornerstone of international resource law. It emerged in the 1950s from the efforts of newly independent and developing states to secure control over their natural wealth, which in many cases remained subject to concessions granted by colonial administrations or on terms that the host states regarded as inequitable. The principle was first articulated in General Assembly Resolution 523 (VI) of 1952 on integrated economic development and commercial agreements, and Resolution 626 (VII) of the same year, which affirmed the right of peoples "freely to use and exploit their natural wealth and resources". A Commission on Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources was established in 1958, and its work culminated in Resolution 1803 (XVII) of 1962.

The principle was incorporated into common Article 1(2) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights 1966, which provides:

> All peoples may, for their own ends, freely dispose of their natural wealth and resources without prejudice to any obligations arising out of international economic co-operation, based upon the principle of mutual benefit, and international law. In no case may a people be deprived of its own means of subsistence.

Article 21 of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights 1981 is more emphatic still, providing that "all peoples shall freely dispose of their wealth and natural resources", that this right "shall be exercised in the exclusive interest of the people", and that "in no case shall a people be deprived of it". The provision also obliges states parties to "undertake to eliminate all forms of foreign economic exploitation particularly that practised by international monopolies so as to enable their peoples to fully benefit from the advantages derived from their national resources".

### Content of the Principle

PSNR comprises a bundle of rights and, increasingly, duties. The principal rights include:

- the right to possess, use, and dispose of natural resources;
- the right to determine freely the conditions of exploration, development, and disposition of resources;
- the right to regulate foreign investment and to supervise the activities of foreign investors;
- the right to nationalise or expropriate foreign property, subject to the payment of appropriate compensation;
- the right to settle disputes in accordance with national law, subject to agreements to the contrary;
- the right to manage resources in pursuance of national environmental and developmental policy.

Schrijver's influential study demonstrated that the principle has evolved from a primarily rights-based claim asserted by developing states against foreign interests into a more balanced concept that also encompasses duties: the duty to exercise sovereignty in the interest of national development and the well-being of the people; the duty to respect the rights and interests of indigenous peoples; the duty to observe international agreements in good faith; the duty to treat foreign investors fairly; and the duty to manage resources sustainably and to avoid transboundary environmental harm.[^schrijver]

[^schrijver]: Nico Schrijver, *Sovereignty over Natural Resources: Balancing Rights and Duties* (Cambridge University Press 1997), especially chs 9–10.

### Legal Status

The legal status of PSNR has been considered by the International Court of Justice. In *Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (Democratic Republic of the Congo v Uganda)*, the Court recognised that Resolution 1803 and the related instruments reflected customary international law in respect of the principle of permanent sovereignty, although it held that the principle was not applicable to the specific situation of looting, pillage, and exploitation of natural resources by members of the army of a state militarily intervening in another state, which fell instead to be addressed under the law of belligerent occupation and international humanitarian law.[^drc] In its 2022 judgment on reparations in the same case, the Court awarded the Democratic Republic of the Congo a global sum in respect of damage to natural resources.

[^drc]: *Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (Democratic Republic of the Congo v Uganda)*, Judgment, ICJ Reports 2005, p. 168, para 244; Reparations, Judgment, ICJ Reports 2022, p. 13.

It is generally accepted that the core of PSNR — the right of states to control and regulate their natural resources, and to expropriate foreign property for a public purpose on payment of compensation — forms part of customary international law. More contested are the claims, associated with the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States, that compensation is to be determined solely by domestic law and that disputes are to be resolved exclusively by domestic courts. The arbitral tribunal in *Texaco v Libya* held that Resolution 1803, adopted by a large majority including both developed and developing states, reflected customary law, whereas the relevant provisions of the Charter, adopted over the opposition of the principal capital-exporting states, did not.

### PSNR and Contractual Commitments

The relationship between PSNR and contractual commitments is one of the field's central problems. Can a state, in the exercise of its sovereignty, bind itself not to exercise its sovereign powers in the future — for example, by agreeing to a stabilisation clause that freezes the applicable law? The arbitral jurisprudence has generally answered this question in the affirmative, on the ground that the power to enter into binding commitments is itself an attribute of sovereignty. As the sole arbitrator in *Texaco v Libya* put it, "the State, by entering into an international agreement with a private person, has not alienated its sovereignty but has made use of it".[^texaco2] On this view, PSNR does not authorise a state to disregard its contractual obligations; it merely affirms the state's ultimate authority over its resources, which must be exercised in accordance with international law, including the obligation to pay compensation for any taking.

[^texaco2]: *Texaco Overseas Petroleum Company v Libyan Arab Republic*, Award of 19 January 1977, (1979) 53 ILR 389, para 68.

The *Aminoil* tribunal adopted a more nuanced position. It accepted that stabilisation clauses were valid but held that, in the absence of an express prohibition on nationalisation of particular duration, the clause in the Aminoil concession did not prohibit nationalisation as such; rather, its effect was to be taken into account in determining the compensation payable. The tribunal also emphasised that the concession had undergone significant evolution over time, through renegotiation and the acceptance by the company of changes in the fiscal regime, such that the contract had been transformed and its original stabilisation clause could not be read in isolation from that evolution. The approach of *Aminoil* — treating stabilisation clauses as relevant to compensation rather than as an absolute bar to sovereign action — has been influential in later practice and is examined further in Chapters 14 and 15.

::: keypoint
Permanent sovereignty over natural resources is not an unlimited licence to disregard commitments. In its modern form it is a principle of balanced rights and duties: the state controls its resources and may regulate and expropriate them in the public interest, but it must exercise that control in good faith, for the benefit of its people, in accordance with its international obligations, and subject to the payment of compensation where property is taken.
:::
