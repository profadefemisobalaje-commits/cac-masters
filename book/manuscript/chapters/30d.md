## 30.6 Emerging International Disputes and Regulatory Conflicts

### The Shifting Landscape of Energy Disputes

The categories of energy disputes examined in Chapter 26 — price reviews, expropriations, fiscal disputes, construction claims — will persist, but the forces examined in this chapter are generating new categories of disputes and regulatory conflicts. Anticipating these disputes is important for practitioners advising clients, for states designing laws and treaties, and for scholars analysing the evolution of the field.

### Transition-Related Investment Disputes

**Disputes arising from climate and transition measures** are likely to increase. Investors in fossil fuel assets may bring claims against states that phase out coal, ban new oil and gas exploration, revoke licences, or impose windfall taxes, as in the claims under the Energy Charter Treaty and investment treaties examined in Chapters 14 and 26, including *Rockhopper v Italy* (2022), in which a tribunal awarded compensation for the refusal of an offshore production concession following a ban on drilling near the coast, an award subsequently annulled in 2025. Investors in renewable energy may bring claims when states reduce incentives, as in the Spanish cases. States may increasingly invoke their climate obligations, clarified by the ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion, as defences, and the interpretation of investment treaties in the light of the Paris Agreement and the right to regulate will be contested. The withdrawal of the EU and several member states from the Energy Charter Treaty, and the modernisation of its investment provisions to allow the exclusion of fossil fuel investments, examined in Chapter 14, will reduce but not eliminate such claims, given the treaty's twenty-year sunset clause.

### Trade Disputes over Green Industrial Policy

**Trade disputes** over green industrial policies and carbon border measures are likely to multiply. The EU's **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** (Regulation (EU) 2023/956), whose definitive period began in January 2026, examined in Chapter 13, has been criticised by India, China, South Africa, and other states as protectionist and inconsistent with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, and its consistency with WTO law may be tested. The local content and subsidy provisions of the US **Inflation Reduction Act**, partially curtailed in 2025, China's support for its clean energy industries, and the countervailing and anti-dumping duties imposed by the United States, the European Union, and others on Chinese solar panels, batteries, and electric vehicles — including the EU's countervailing duties on Chinese electric vehicles imposed in 2024, which China challenged at the WTO — illustrate the tension between the WTO's rules on subsidies and non-discrimination and states' pursuit of green industrialisation. **Export restrictions** on critical minerals, examined in Chapter 25, may also generate disputes, as the EU's challenge to Indonesia's nickel export ban illustrates. The paralysis of the WTO Appellate Body and the rise of national security justifications complicate the resolution of these disputes.

### Critical Minerals Disputes

The scramble for **critical minerals** is generating disputes between investors and host states over resource nationalism, licence revocations, and renegotiations, as in the disputes arising from Mali's 2023 mining code and its conflict with Barrick over the Loulo-Gounkoto complex, which led to the suspension of operations, the detention of employees, and ICSID proceedings before a settlement in late 2025; Panama's closure of the Cobre Panamá copper mine following the Supreme Court's declaration in November 2023 that the law approving the concession contract was unconstitutional, which led to arbitration claims by First Quantum; and the review of mining contracts in Niger, Burkina Faso, and the DRC. Disputes between consumer states over **supply chain** measures, including export controls and investment screening, and disputes concerning **deep seabed mining** under UNCLOS are also likely.

### Climate Liability and Loss and Damage

**Climate liability disputes** — claims against fossil fuel companies for climate damages, against states for inadequate climate action, and potentially between states for transboundary climate harm — are likely to grow, building on the jurisprudence examined in Chapters 18 and 27, the ICJ advisory opinion, and the advisory opinions of the **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea** (May 2024), which held that anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions constitute pollution of the marine environment under UNCLOS, and of the **Inter-American Court of Human Rights** (July 2025), which recognised a right to a healthy climate. The operationalisation of the **Fund for responding to Loss and Damage**, established at COP27 and COP28, may provide a non-adversarial channel for addressing climate harms, but will not eliminate litigation. **Inter-state** climate litigation, long discussed, may become a reality, as small island states and others explore claims against major emitters in the light of the ICJ's clarification of the law.

### Disputes over Digital Energy and AI

Disputes concerning **data, AI, and digital energy systems** are emerging: disputes over access to energy data, liability for algorithmic failures, cyber incidents and their insurance coverage, intellectual property in energy technologies, and the treatment of digital assets in energy markets. Disputes may also arise over the **electricity supply** of data centres, including the allocation of grid capacity and the costs of network reinforcement between data centre operators and other consumers, and over co-location arrangements in which data centres are connected directly to power plants, an issue addressed by FERC in proceedings concerning co-located load in 2024–2025.

### Regulatory Conflicts in a Fragmented Order

Beyond formal disputes, the coming decades are likely to see increasing **regulatory conflicts** — clashes between the regulatory requirements of different jurisdictions that impose inconsistent obligations on companies operating across borders. Examples include conflicts between sanctions regimes and blocking statutes; between the extraterritorial due diligence obligations of EU law and the laws of producer states; between ESG requirements in some US states and anti-ESG laws in others; between data localisation requirements and cross-border data flows; and between the EU's carbon border measures and the carbon pricing policies of trading partners. Managing such conflicts will require international cooperation, mutual recognition, and the development of principles of **comity** and **interoperability**, and will place demands on lawyers capable of navigating multiple legal orders.

## 30.7 Unresolved Questions in Global Resource Governance

### The Enduring Questions

Despite a century of development, international energy and natural resources law leaves fundamental questions unresolved. Some are perennial; others are new; all will shape the field's future. This section identifies a selection of these questions, not to answer them, but to frame them for the research agenda that follows.

### Sovereignty and Global Commons

1. **Sovereignty and the climate.** How should the permanent sovereignty of states over their natural resources be reconciled with the global imperative to limit the extraction and combustion of fossil fuels? Does the principle of permanent sovereignty now carry duties as well as rights, including duties to use resources in a manner consistent with the protection of the climate system? The ICJ's 2025 advisory opinion suggests that it does, but the content of these duties and their relationship with the right to development remain contested.
2. **Common heritage beyond national jurisdiction.** How should the resources of areas beyond national jurisdiction — the deep seabed, the high seas, outer space, and perhaps the Arctic and Antarctic — be governed? Will the common heritage principle survive unilateral challenges, and how will the benefits of these resources be shared?

### Equity and Justice

3. **Equity in the transition.** How should the burdens and benefits of the energy transition be shared among states — between historical emitters and developing countries, between fossil fuel producers and consumers, and between mineral-producing and technology-producing states? What does "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances" require in relation to fossil fuel production and transition minerals?
4. **Communities and benefit-sharing.** How can the rights of indigenous peoples and local communities, including free, prior, and informed consent, be given effect in the development of transition minerals and renewable energy projects, which increasingly affect their lands? Is there an emerging right to share in the benefits of resource development?
5. **Energy access as a right.** Is there, or should there be, a human right to energy, and what would it require of states and companies?

### Investment and Regulation

6. **Investment protection and the right to regulate.** How should investment law balance the protection of investors against arbitrary state conduct with states' freedom to pursue climate and transition policies? Will investor–state arbitration be reformed, replaced, or abandoned?
7. **Stability and change.** How can legal frameworks provide the stability investors need while adapting to rapid technological, market, and climate change? Are stabilisation clauses compatible with the energy transition?

### Institutions and Order

8. **The architecture of governance.** Will global energy governance converge towards more inclusive multilateral institutions, or fragment further into competing blocs and clubs? What role should international law play in a multipolar energy order?
9. **Corporate accountability.** How should transnational corporations in the energy and mining sectors be held accountable for human rights and environmental harms across their value chains? Will a binding international instrument on business and human rights, negotiated under the auspices of the UN Human Rights Council since 2014, be concluded and, if so, with what effect?
10. **Technology and law.** How should law govern the technologies of the transition — AI, digital platforms, hydrogen, carbon removal, nuclear innovation, and perhaps geoengineering — whose risks and benefits are uncertain?

## 30.8 A Doctoral Research Agenda for the Next Generation

### Framing the Agenda

The unresolved questions identified above, and the analysis throughout this book, suggest a research agenda for the next generation of doctoral scholars in international energy and natural resources law. The agenda below is organised by theme and is intended to stimulate, not to limit, the formulation of original research questions. Each chapter of this book concludes with specific doctoral research questions, and Appendix T provides further research topics and emerging scholarly debates.

### Themes for Doctoral Research

**1. The law of managed decline and just transition.** Research is needed on the legal frameworks for phasing out fossil fuel production and use, including restrictions on new licensing, decommissioning, compensation, and just transition measures for workers and communities, with particular attention to producer economies in Africa. How can legal frameworks enable producer states such as Nigeria to diversify their economies while managing declining revenues?

**2. Transition minerals and resource sovereignty.** The governance of critical minerals offers a fertile field for research on value addition, export restrictions, fiscal regimes, community rights, environmental regulation, supply chain due diligence, and the interaction of producer and consumer state regulation. Comparative studies of African mining codes, empirical studies of their implementation, and analyses of the compatibility of resource nationalist measures with trade and investment law are needed.

**3. Investment law and the climate.** Research on the reform of investment treaties, the interpretation of investment obligations in the light of climate obligations, the treatment of transition measures in arbitration, and alternatives to investor–state arbitration remains urgent.

**4. Energy access and energy justice in Africa.** Legal research on frameworks for universal energy access — mini-grids, off-grid solar, clean cooking, tariff design, subsidy reform, and the decentralisation of electricity regulation, as under Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023 — can make a direct contribution to development.

**5. The implementation of Nigerian energy reforms.** The Petroleum Industry Act 2021, the Electricity Act 2023, the Climate Change Act 2021, and the tax reforms of 2025 require doctrinal analysis, empirical evaluation, and comparative assessment, including studies of host community development trusts, the commercialisation of NNPC Limited, the new regulators, and state electricity markets.

**6. Digital energy law.** The legal implications of AI, data, platforms, and cyber security for energy systems are an emerging field, in which doctoral research can help shape nascent regulation.

**7. Climate liability and litigation.** Research on attribution, causation, corporate and state liability, remedies, and the role of courts in climate governance will remain central, as will research on the ICJ advisory opinion and its implementation.

**8. Resources beyond national jurisdiction.** The governance of deep seabed minerals, the implementation of the BBNJ Agreement, the Arctic, and outer space resources raise questions of common heritage, environmental protection, and benefit-sharing.

**9. Regional integration and African energy law.** The AfCFTA and its investment protocol, regional power pools, the African Single Electricity Market, ECOWAS energy instruments, and regional approaches to minerals and climate offer opportunities for research on regional integration as a tool of energy governance.

**10. Theory and method.** The field needs theoretical work that explains and evaluates its evolution — drawing on TWAIL, energy justice, political economy, regulatory theory, and transnational law — and methodological innovation, including empirical and interdisciplinary research.

### The Responsibility of the Scholar

The next generation of scholars in energy and natural resources law will work at the intersection of some of the most consequential questions of the century: how to power the world's growing population while protecting the climate; how to govern the resources on which the transition depends; how to share the burdens and benefits of change fairly; and how to maintain a rules-based international order in a fragmented world. Their research will be read by courts, legislatures, regulators, companies, and communities. This places on them a responsibility to conduct research of the highest rigour and integrity, to engage with the realities of the sector and the experiences of those affected by it, to speak truthfully about evidence and uncertainty, and to bring the insights of scholarship to bear on the design of better laws and institutions.
