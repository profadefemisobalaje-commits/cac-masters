### Choosing and Designing Dispute Resolution Mechanisms

The design of dispute resolution mechanisms for energy contracts and treaties requires careful attention to the likely types of disputes and the interests of the parties. Practitioners advising states and investors should consider: (a) the **types of dispute** likely to arise and the methods suited to each — expert determination for technical and valuation issues, dispute boards for construction disputes, mediation for relational disputes, and arbitration for disputes requiring a binding and enforceable decision; (b) the **sequencing** of methods in multi-tiered clauses, with clear time limits and triggers to avoid uncertainty about whether conditions precedent have been satisfied; (c) the **seat and institution** of arbitration, taking into account the supportiveness of the courts of the seat, the neutrality of the venue, and the cost; (d) the **enforceability** of the outcome, including the waiver of sovereign immunity from jurisdiction and execution in contracts with states; (e) the **joinder and consolidation** of related disputes under interconnected contracts, such as the PSC, the JOA, and the gas sales agreement, through compatible dispute resolution clauses; (f) **transparency** and the public interest in disputes involving states and public resources; and (g) the **capacity** of the parties, particularly states, to participate effectively — a lesson starkly illustrated by the P&ID case. For African states, investment in specialised government legal teams, the development of local arbitral institutions and expertise, and participation in regional initiatives to strengthen arbitration capacity are essential.

## Conclusion

International arbitration is the backbone of energy dispute resolution, providing a neutral, flexible, and enforceable forum for disputes of exceptional value and complexity. Its effectiveness depends on a robust legal infrastructure — the New York and ICSID Conventions, modern arbitration laws such as Nigeria's Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 and England's Arbitration Act 2025, institutional rules, and supportive national courts — and on the competence and integrity of arbitrators, counsel, and experts. The process raises distinctive issues: the strategic structuring of investments and claims, the limits of consent and the rejection of abuse of process, the interaction of contract and treaty, the quantification of damages in long-lived energy projects, and the enforcement of awards against sovereign assets. Recent developments — the revision of the ICSID Rules in 2022, the UNCITRAL Code of Conduct for arbitrators, the growth of third-party funding and transparency, the conflict between intra-EU awards and EU law, the fragmentation resulting from sanctions and Russian anti-arbitration injunctions, and the setting aside of the P&ID award for fraud — show a system in evolution. Alongside arbitration, negotiation, mediation, expert determination, and dispute boards offer valuable tools, increasingly supported by the Singapore Convention. For African states and practitioners, the challenge is to build the capacity to participate effectively and on equal terms in the resolution of the disputes arising from their resources. Chapter 27 turns to the role of national and international courts in energy and environmental litigation.

::: summary
- Energy disputes are distinctive in their long-term relational context, high stakes, technical complexity, state involvement, political sensitivity, and multi-jurisdictional character; they may be classified by parties, subject matter, legal basis, and forum, and arbitration predominates because of its neutrality, flexibility, and enforceability.
- International commercial arbitration rests on the arbitration agreement, institutional or ad hoc rules, the law of the seat, and the New York Convention; Nigeria's Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023 and England's Arbitration Act 2025 modernise the framework, and national courts support and supervise arbitration, including through anti-suit injunctions.
- Investment treaty arbitration proceeds through notice and cooling-off, commencement, constitution of the tribunal, procedural orders, written and oral phases, and award, with distinctive issues of arbitrator independence, bifurcation, preliminary objections, provisional measures, transparency, and third-party funding.
- The ICSID Convention provides a delocalised system with jurisdiction under Article 25, annulment under Article 52, and enforcement under Article 54 subject to immunity from execution; UNCITRAL arbitration has a legal seat and relies on the New York Convention.
- Jurisdiction depends on consent, protected investors, and protected investments; abuse of process limits treaty planning; corruption may defeat claims; contract and treaty claims are distinct; and applicable law depends on the forum and basis of the claim, with growing attention to environmental counterclaims and climate obligations.
- Evidence is governed by tribunal discretion guided by the IBA Rules; expert evidence is central; compensation follows the Chorzów Factory principle of full reparation for unlawful acts, using DCF, market, asset-based, or loss-of-opportunity methods, with interest and costs often decisive.
- Awards are enforced under the New York Convention, subject to exhaustive grounds for refusal, and under the ICSID Convention; enforcement against states confronts immunity from execution; the P&ID case illustrates the setting aside of awards obtained by fraud.
- Negotiation, mediation (supported by the Singapore Convention), expert determination, dispute boards, conciliation, and grievance mechanisms complement arbitration, and dispute resolution clauses should be designed to match methods to disputes.
:::
