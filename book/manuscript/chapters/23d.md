## 23.7 Sustainable Development Goals and Energy Policy

### From the MDGs to the SDGs

The **Millennium Development Goals** (2000–2015) did not include a goal on energy, an omission widely regarded as a significant gap given the role of energy in achieving the goals on poverty, health, education, and gender equality. In 2011, the UN Secretary-General launched the **Sustainable Energy for All** (SEforALL) initiative, with three objectives for 2030: ensuring universal access to modern energy services, doubling the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency, and doubling the share of renewable energy in the global energy mix. The UN General Assembly declared 2012 the International Year of Sustainable Energy for All and 2014–2024 the UN Decade of Sustainable Energy for All. These efforts laid the foundation for the inclusion of energy in the **2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development**, adopted by the General Assembly in September 2015 (Resolution 70/1), comprising seventeen **Sustainable Development Goals** (SDGs) and 169 targets.

### SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy

**SDG 7** aims to "ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all". Its targets are:

- **Target 7.1**: by 2030, ensure universal access to affordable, reliable, and modern energy services;
- **Target 7.2**: by 2030, increase substantially the share of renewable energy in the global energy mix;
- **Target 7.3**: by 2030, double the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency;
- **Target 7.a**: by 2030, enhance international cooperation to facilitate access to clean energy research and technology, and promote investment in energy infrastructure and clean energy technology; and
- **Target 7.b**: by 2030, expand infrastructure and upgrade technology for supplying modern and sustainable energy services for all in developing countries, in particular least developed countries, small island developing states, and landlocked developing countries.

Progress is monitored through indicators, including the proportion of the population with access to electricity (7.1.1), the proportion of the population with primary reliance on clean fuels and technology (7.1.2), the renewable energy share in total final energy consumption (7.2.1), energy intensity (7.3.1), international financial flows to developing countries in support of clean energy (7.a.1), and installed renewable energy-generating capacity in developing countries per capita (7.b.1). The annual *Tracking SDG 7* reports have consistently found that the world is **off track** to achieve SDG 7 by 2030: on current trends, hundreds of millions of people, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, will still lack electricity in 2030, and around 1.8 billion will lack clean cooking; renewable energy deployment, while growing rapidly in electricity, is slower in heating and transport; and international public financial flows to developing countries for clean energy have declined from their peak in 2016 and are highly concentrated in a few countries.

### The SDGs as an Integrated Framework

The SDGs are designed to be "integrated and indivisible", and SDG 7 is closely linked to other goals. Energy access contributes to **ending poverty** (SDG 1), **food security** (SDG 2, through irrigation, processing, and cold storage), **health** (SDG 3, through clean cooking and electrified health facilities), **education** (SDG 4, through lighting and digital learning), **gender equality** (SDG 5), **clean water and sanitation** (SDG 6, through pumping and treatment), **decent work and economic growth** (SDG 8), **industry, innovation, and infrastructure** (SDG 9), **reduced inequalities** (SDG 10), **sustainable cities** (SDG 11), and **climate action** (SDG 13). The relationships are not always synergistic: the expansion of energy access through fossil fuels may conflict with climate action; large hydropower may conflict with the protection of ecosystems and the rights of displaced people (SDGs 6, 15, and 16); and the extraction of transition minerals may conflict with goals on health, water, and peace. The concept of **policy coherence for sustainable development** (SDG target 17.14) requires governments to identify and manage such trade-offs.

### The Legal Status of the SDGs

The SDGs are not legally binding. They are political commitments adopted by a General Assembly resolution, to be implemented through national policies and voluntary reporting — through **Voluntary National Reviews** presented at the UN **High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development**. Nigeria has presented Voluntary National Reviews, including in 2017 and 2020, which addressed energy access among national priorities. Nonetheless, the SDGs have legal significance in several respects: they inform the interpretation of legal obligations, including human rights obligations, whose realisation the SDGs are expressly intended to advance; they have been incorporated into national development plans, legislation, and regulatory mandates; they are used as benchmarks by international financial institutions, development partners, and investors, including through sustainable finance taxonomies and SDG-linked bonds; and they influence the conduct of companies through sustainability reporting and voluntary commitments. Courts have occasionally referred to the SDGs in interpreting constitutional and statutory provisions on development and the environment.

### International Commitments after the SDGs

Subsequent international commitments have built on SDG 7. At the **UN High-Level Dialogue on Energy** in September 2021, the first summit-level meeting on energy under the UN General Assembly in four decades, governments and other stakeholders announced **Energy Compacts** — voluntary commitments to accelerate progress on SDG 7. At **COP28** in Dubai in 2023, parties to the Paris Agreement, in the outcome of the first global stocktake, called on parties to contribute to global efforts including **tripling renewable energy capacity globally and doubling the global average annual rate of energy efficiency improvements by 2030**, and **transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner**, examined in Chapter 18. The **African Union's Agenda 2063** identifies energy as a priority for the continent's development, and the **Africa Renewable Energy Initiative**, launched at COP21, aimed to install substantial new renewable capacity in Africa. The **Nairobi Declaration** adopted at the Africa Climate Summit in 2023 called for increasing Africa's renewable generation capacity from 56 GW in 2022 to at least 300 GW by 2030.

### SDG 7 and Nigerian Energy Policy

Nigeria's energy policies are framed by reference to SDG 7. The **Energy Transition Plan** (2021) aims to achieve universal access to modern energy by 2030 and net zero emissions in the energy sector by 2060, with natural gas playing a transitional role, including in clean cooking through LPG. The **National Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency Policy** (2015) set targets for renewable energy and efficiency. The Electricity Act 2023 requires the federal government to adopt a national electricity policy addressing, among other matters, renewable energy and rural electrification, and contains provisions on renewable energy, energy efficiency, and the obligations of licensees in respect of renewable energy. The **Mission 300 national energy compact** presented by Nigeria in 2025 sets out commitments to expand access, including through distributed renewable energy, and to improve the financial viability of the electricity sector. The gap between these commitments and outcomes — in a country where access has grown slowly and the grid remains unreliable — underscores the importance of implementation, institutional capacity, financing, and governance, examined in Chapter 24.

## 23.8 Designing Equitable Energy Regulatory Systems

### Principles of Equitable Regulatory Design

How can the principles of energy justice be translated into the design of energy regulatory systems? Drawing on the preceding analysis, several principles may be identified:

1. **Universal service**: regulatory frameworks should aim to ensure that all people have access to affordable, reliable, and modern energy services, through obligations on licensees, dedicated funds, and enabling frameworks for decentralised solutions.
2. **Affordability with financial sustainability**: tariffs should reflect the efficient costs of supply, to ensure the financial viability of the sector and attract investment, while protecting poor and vulnerable consumers through targeted measures.
3. **Fair allocation of costs and benefits**: the costs of policies, including the energy transition, should be allocated in ways that do not unduly burden the poor, and the benefits should be shared broadly, including with host communities.
4. **Meaningful participation**: regulatory processes should be transparent and open to the participation of consumers, communities, and civil society, with access to information and remedies.
5. **Recognition of vulnerability and diversity**: regulation should identify and protect vulnerable groups and take account of gender and other dimensions of disadvantage.
6. **Accountability**: regulators, utilities, and governments should be accountable for their performance, through independent regulation, performance standards, reporting, and judicial review.

### Tariff Design

**Tariff design** is perhaps the most consequential regulatory instrument for energy justice. Regulators must balance the objective of **cost-reflectivity** — tariffs that recover the efficient costs of generation, transmission, and distribution, including a reasonable return on investment — with the objective of **affordability**. Where tariffs are set below cost for political reasons, utilities accumulate losses, investment stagnates, supply deteriorates, and the burden falls on the state budget or, ultimately, on consumers through unreliable supply and costly self-generation — an outcome that is often more regressive than cost-reflective tariffs with targeted support.

Instruments for protecting the poor within cost-reflective tariff structures include **lifeline tariffs**, under which a minimum block of consumption is provided at a low price, with higher prices for consumption above that block (**increasing block tariffs**); **social tariffs** for eligible low-income households, identified through social registries; **cross-subsidies** from commercial and industrial consumers or high-consumption households to low-consumption households; **connection subsidies** to reduce the upfront cost of connection, which is often a greater barrier to access than the tariff itself; and **direct cash transfers** to compensate households for tariff increases. Each instrument has advantages and disadvantages: lifeline tariffs may be poorly targeted where poor households share connections, and cross-subsidies may burden industries and encourage them to leave the grid. Experience suggests that the design of support should be based on data about the consumption patterns and characteristics of poor households.

In **Nigeria**, NERC sets tariffs through a **Multi-Year Tariff Order** (MYTO) methodology. Since 2020, Nigeria has adopted a **service-based tariff** system, under which customers are classified into bands (A to E) according to the average hours of supply they receive, with higher tariffs for customers receiving more hours of supply. In April 2024, NERC approved a substantial increase in the tariff for **Band A** customers, receiving at least twenty hours of supply per day, removing the subsidy for this band, while maintaining subsidised tariffs for other bands; the tariff was subsequently adjusted. The approach seeks to align tariffs with service quality and to target subsidies at customers receiving poorer service, but it has raised concerns about the accuracy of band classifications, the enforcement of supply commitments, and the burden on small businesses. Metering remains a significant challenge: a large proportion of customers are unmetered and subject to **estimated billing**, which has been a major source of consumer grievance and which NERC has sought to address through capped estimated billing and metering programmes, including the Meter Asset Provider regulations and the National Mass Metering Programme.

### Universal Service Obligations and Rural Electrification

Regulatory frameworks may impose **universal service obligations** on distribution licensees, requiring them to connect customers within their franchise areas on request, subject to conditions, and to meet quality-of-service standards. Where grid extension is uneconomic, frameworks should enable decentralised solutions through mini-grid and off-grid regulation, as examined in section 23.3, and provide public funding through rural electrification funds, results-based financing, and concessional finance. The integration of grid and off-grid planning, through least-cost electrification plans, helps to avoid conflicts between grid extension and mini-grid investments. Under Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023, the Rural Electrification Agency, the Rural Electrification Fund, and the power of states to regulate electricity within their territories create a multilevel framework for universal access, whose effectiveness will depend on coordination among federal and state institutions.

### Community Energy and Benefit-Sharing

**Community energy** — renewable energy projects owned or controlled by local communities, cooperatives, or municipalities — offers a means of sharing the benefits of the energy transition more widely and increasing public acceptance. **Germany**'s energy transition was driven in its early stages by citizens' energy cooperatives and local ownership, supported by feed-in tariffs. In **Denmark**, legislation required that a share of new wind projects be offered to local residents. The European Union's revised **Renewable Energy Directive** (Article 22) and the **Electricity Market Directive** (Directive (EU) 2019/944, Article 16) require member states to provide enabling frameworks for **renewable energy communities** and **citizen energy communities**, respectively, including rights to produce, consume, store, and sell renewable energy and to share it within the community. **Scotland** has adopted good practice principles under which developers of onshore wind projects are expected to provide community benefits of £5,000 per megawatt per year, together with opportunities for shared ownership.

In developing countries, benefit-sharing mechanisms for energy projects include community development agreements, revenue-sharing with local governments and communities, community equity in projects, local employment and procurement requirements, and community funds. South Africa's **Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme** (REIPPPP), examined in Chapter 10, required bidders to commit to economic development obligations, including local community ownership of at least a minimum percentage of the project company and spending on socio-economic and enterprise development in communities within a specified radius of the project. Such mechanisms can contribute to distributional justice, but their effectiveness depends on governance, transparency, and the capacity of community structures to manage benefits, as the experience of community trusts in the REIPPPP and of Nigeria's host communities development trusts, examined in Chapter 24, illustrates.

### Just Transition Mechanisms

The **just transition** — ensuring that the transition to a low-carbon economy is fair to workers, communities, and countries dependent on fossil fuels — requires legal and policy mechanisms to manage its distributional consequences, as examined in Chapter 18. These include social dialogue with workers and communities; retraining and redeployment programmes; social protection; economic diversification in affected regions; the remediation and repurposing of fossil fuel sites; and the allocation of carbon pricing revenues to support affected households and regions. The **Just Energy Transition Partnerships** (JETPs) — financing arrangements between groups of developed countries and multilateral institutions and emerging economies, beginning with **South Africa** in 2021 (an initial USD 8.5 billion), followed by **Indonesia** (USD 20 billion) and **Viet Nam** (USD 15.5 billion) in 2022 and **Senegal** (EUR 2.5 billion) in 2023 — illustrate an international dimension of just transition, though questions have been raised about the balance between grants and loans, the participation of affected communities, and the withdrawal of the United States from these partnerships in 2025. South Africa's **Presidential Climate Commission** adopted a **Just Transition Framework** in 2022, setting out principles of distributive, restorative, and procedural justice to guide the transition — a notable example of the incorporation of energy justice concepts into national policy.

The **revenue recycling** of carbon pricing illustrates how regulatory design can address distributional concerns. Canada's federal carbon pricing system returned most of the proceeds of its fuel charge directly to households through quarterly rebates, so that most lower-income households received more in rebates than they paid in increased costs; nonetheless, the consumer fuel charge became politically contentious and was set to zero in April 2025. The European Union's **Social Climate Fund** is designed to address the distributional effects of its second emissions trading system for buildings and road transport. These examples illustrate that the political sustainability of climate policies depends on their perceived fairness.

### Regulatory Institutions and Accountability

Finally, the achievement of energy justice depends on the quality of regulatory institutions. **Independent regulators**, insulated from short-term political pressures and subject to transparent procedures, can set cost-reflective tariffs while protecting consumers, enforce quality-of-service standards, and resolve disputes, but they require adequate resources, technical capacity, and legitimacy. Regulatory decisions should be subject to **accountability** through legislative oversight, judicial review, and public reporting. Consumer advocacy bodies, such as the United Kingdom's Citizens Advice, which has statutory functions as the energy consumer advocate, can strengthen the representation of consumers, especially vulnerable consumers, in regulatory processes. In Nigeria, the **Electricity Act 2023** reinforces NERC's consumer protection mandate and requires state electricity regulators to observe similar principles, while NERC's Forum Offices and customer service standards provide mechanisms for consumer redress. Strengthening these institutions, and ensuring that energy justice considerations are embedded in their mandates, is among the most important tasks for energy law reform in Nigeria and elsewhere.

## Conclusion

Energy justice offers a framework for evaluating whether energy and resource systems distribute benefits and burdens fairly, whether decisions are made through fair processes, whether the needs and identities of all groups are recognised, and whether harms are remedied. Its theoretical foundations in distributive justice, the capabilities approach, the politics of recognition, and environmental and climate justice provide a rich vocabulary for legal analysis and reform. Applied to the realities of the energy sector, it highlights the scandal of energy poverty, which leaves hundreds of millions without electricity and billions without clean cooking; the gendered burdens of energy deprivation; the distribution of resource revenues, which in Nigeria has been shaped by constitutional battles over derivation and resource control; and the obligations owed to future generations, both to share resource wealth and to avert catastrophic climate change.

The Sustainable Development Goals, and SDG 7 in particular, provide a widely accepted policy framework, but progress remains off track, especially in sub-Saharan Africa. Achieving energy justice requires the translation of its principles into the concrete design of legal and regulatory instruments: tariffs that are financially sustainable while protecting the poor; universal service frameworks that enable grid and off-grid solutions; benefit-sharing and community energy mechanisms; just transition policies that protect workers and communities; and regulatory institutions that are independent, competent, and accountable. For Nigeria, where the paradox of energy poverty amid resource abundance is acute, energy justice is not an abstract ideal but a practical imperative of law and policy, to which Chapter 24 returns in examining the Nigerian energy and resource legal framework in comparative perspective.
