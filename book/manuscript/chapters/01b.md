### Phase Four: Liberalisation, Investment Protection, and Market Integration (1980–2005)

The fourth phase was characterised by a reversal of the statist tendencies of the preceding period. The collapse of oil prices in the mid-1980s, the debt crisis in the developing world, the end of the Cold War, and the ascendancy of market-oriented economic policies led many states to reopen their resource sectors to foreign investment. Production sharing contracts — first used in Indonesia in the 1960s — became the dominant contractual form in many developing producer states, allowing governments to retain formal ownership of petroleum while engaging foreign companies as contractors entitled to a share of production. Mining codes across Africa and Latin America were rewritten, often with the support of international financial institutions, to attract foreign capital through security of tenure, fiscal stability, and liberal repatriation of profits.

At the same time, a dense network of bilateral investment treaties (BITs) emerged. From a few hundred in the 1980s, the number of BITs grew to more than two thousand by the early 2000s. These treaties conferred upon foreign investors substantive protections — fair and equitable treatment, full protection and security, protection against expropriation without compensation, national and most-favoured-nation treatment — and, crucially, the right to bring claims directly against host states before international arbitral tribunals, typically under the auspices of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) established by the Washington Convention of 1965. The extension of investor–state arbitration transformed the enforcement of investor rights by removing the need for diplomatic protection by the investor's home state.

In the energy sector specifically, the Energy Charter Treaty (ECT), signed in Lisbon in December 1994 and in force since April 1998, established a multilateral framework for investment protection, trade, transit, and energy efficiency among the states of Europe and the former Soviet Union, together with a number of states in Asia. The ECT became the most frequently invoked investment treaty in the world, a fact that would later make it a central battleground in debates over the compatibility of investment protection with climate policy.

The liberalisation of domestic energy markets proceeded in parallel. In the electricity and gas sectors, the United Kingdom privatised its state-owned utilities in the late 1980s and 1990s; the United States introduced open access to transmission networks through Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order No. 888 of 1996; and the European Union adopted successive legislative packages, beginning with the first Electricity Directive of 1996, designed to create an internal energy market through the unbundling of vertically integrated utilities and the introduction of competition in generation and supply.

### Phase Five: Resource Nationalism, Sustainability, and the Energy Transition (2005–present)

The fifth phase, which continues today, is marked by a complex and sometimes contradictory set of trends.

First, the commodity price boom of the 2000s prompted a revival of resource nationalism. Venezuela, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Russia, among others, increased state participation, renegotiated contracts, or expropriated foreign interests, generating a new wave of investment arbitrations, several of which resulted in awards of billions of United States dollars. Resource nationalism also took subtler forms, including increased royalties and windfall taxes, local content requirements, export restrictions on unprocessed minerals, and mandatory state participation in new projects.

Second, the international community increasingly emphasised transparency, good governance, and the equitable distribution of resource revenues. The Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (EITI), launched in 2002 and now implemented in more than fifty countries, requires the disclosure of payments by companies to governments and of revenues received by governments from the extractive sector. Supply chain due diligence standards for conflict minerals, notably the OECD *Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas*, sought to address the link between mineral extraction and armed conflict. The United Nations *Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights*, endorsed by the Human Rights Council in 2011, articulated the responsibility of business enterprises, including extractive companies, to respect human rights.

Third, and most consequentially, climate change has moved from the margins to the centre of energy law. The Paris Agreement of 2015 set the objective of holding the increase in global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C. At the twenty-eighth Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (COP28) in Dubai in 2023, the parties for the first time called in the outcome of the first global stocktake for "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner". Climate litigation has proliferated in domestic courts, regional human rights bodies, and international tribunals, culminating in advisory opinions from the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (2024), the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2025), and the International Court of Justice (2025) on the obligations of states in respect of climate change. These developments are examined in Chapter 18.

Fourth, the energy transition has created new legal frontiers: the regulation of renewable energy, hydrogen, energy storage, carbon capture and storage, and offshore wind; the governance of critical minerals essential to batteries, electric vehicles, and grid infrastructure; and the management of stranded assets and the decommissioning of fossil fuel infrastructure. Simultaneously, geopolitical events — notably the Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 and the resulting disruption of European gas supply — have restored energy security to the forefront of policy and have demonstrated that the transition will be shaped as much by security considerations as by environmental ones.

::: keypoint
The history of resource governance is not a linear progression from one paradigm to another. Elements of each phase coexist in contemporary law. Classical concession-style licences remain in use in many jurisdictions; the doctrine of permanent sovereignty retains its normative force; investment treaties continue to protect investors even as they are reformed or terminated; and the imperatives of the energy transition are layered on top of these older structures rather than replacing them. A doctoral analysis must therefore be historically literate: it must recognise which layer of the law a given rule belongs to and how that rule has been reinterpreted over time.

## 1.3 Internationalisation of Energy Markets and Resource Regulation

### The Global Character of Energy Markets

Energy markets are among the most internationalised of all economic sectors. Crude oil is a global commodity, traded on international exchanges and priced by reference to benchmark grades such as Brent and West Texas Intermediate. A substantial proportion of the world's oil production crosses at least one international border before it is consumed, often passing through maritime chokepoints such as the Strait of Hormuz and the Strait of Malacca. Natural gas markets, historically regional because of the constraints of pipeline transport, have become increasingly integrated through the growth of the liquefied natural gas (LNG) trade, which permits gas to be shipped across oceans and redirected in response to price signals. Coal is traded internationally in large volumes. Electricity, while still predominantly national, is increasingly traded across borders through interconnectors and regional power pools. The supply chains for renewable energy technologies — solar panels, wind turbines, batteries — are global, with manufacturing concentrated in a small number of states.

The internationalisation of markets has legal consequences. Domestic regulatory decisions in one state — on export restrictions, subsidies, environmental standards, or sanctions — can have profound effects on prices and supplies in others. Commercial disputes arising from cross-border transactions require rules on applicable law, jurisdiction, and the enforcement of judgments and awards. Infrastructure that crosses borders, such as pipelines and interconnectors, requires intergovernmental agreements to address transit, tariffs, and the allocation of regulatory authority.

### Mechanisms of Internationalisation

The internationalisation of energy and resource regulation has proceeded through several mechanisms.

**Treaty-making.** States have concluded treaties addressing particular aspects of energy and resource activities: investment protection (BITs and the ECT), trade (the WTO agreements and regional trade agreements), transboundary pipelines (for example, the intergovernmental agreements underlying the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline and the West African Gas Pipeline), nuclear safety and liability, the law of the sea, and environmental protection.

**Institutional cooperation.** International organisations such as the International Energy Agency (IEA), the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), OPEC, and the Gas Exporting Countries Forum provide forums for the coordination of national policies, the exchange of information, and, in some cases, the adoption of binding standards. Regional bodies — the Energy Community in South-East Europe, the West African Power Pool, the Southern African Power Pool — promote regional market integration.

**Regulatory convergence.** States often borrow regulatory models from one another, whether through voluntary emulation, the conditions attached to development finance, or the technical assistance of international organisations. The spread of independent regulatory agencies, of unbundling requirements in electricity, and of model petroleum agreements reflects this process of regulatory transplantation, which is examined in Chapter 24.

**Private ordering.** The standard contracts produced by industry associations — the AIEN Model Joint Operating Agreement, the International Federation of Consulting Engineers (FIDIC) suites of construction contracts, and the master agreements used in energy trading — create transnational norms that operate across jurisdictions.

**Extraterritorial regulation.** Some states regulate conduct outside their territory through legislation with extraterritorial effect. Examples include anti-corruption statutes such as the United States Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 and the United Kingdom Bribery Act 2010, sanctions regimes targeting the energy sectors of particular states, and supply chain due diligence obligations such as those contained in the European Union's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive and Batteries Regulation. These are discussed in Chapters 2 and 22.

### The Limits of Internationalisation

Notwithstanding these developments, energy and resource regulation remains in important respects national. There is no comprehensive multilateral energy treaty of universal application; the ECT, the most ambitious attempt to create such an instrument, never attracted the participation of major producers and consumers such as the United States, China, Saudi Arabia, or Brazil, and its membership has contracted in recent years following withdrawals by the European Union and several of its member states. Petroleum, mining, and electricity remain subject primarily to domestic legislation, and the doctrine of permanent sovereignty ensures that the fundamental decisions about whether, when, and on what terms to develop natural resources are reserved to the state in which they are located. The internationalisation of energy law is thus partial, uneven, and contested — characteristics that make it a fertile subject for doctoral research.

## 1.4 Public Law, Private Law, and Transnational Legal Relations

### The Public–Private Divide in Energy and Resource Law

One of the distinctive features of energy and resource law is the extent to which it straddles the conventional divide between public and private law. On the public side, the state acts as sovereign: it owns or controls subsurface resources, grants licences, sets fiscal terms, regulates conduct, protects the environment, and resolves disputes through its courts. On the private side, the state frequently acts as a commercial party: it enters into contracts with investors, participates in joint ventures through national oil or mining companies, sells resources on international markets, and borrows money to finance its share of project costs.

The dual character of the state — as sovereign regulator and as contracting party — generates persistent legal difficulties. When a state that has entered into a contract with a foreign investor subsequently changes its tax laws or environmental regulations in a manner that adversely affects the investor, is it acting as a sovereign exercising its regulatory powers, or as a contracting party in breach of its contractual obligations? The answer may determine whether the investor has a remedy, which tribunal has jurisdiction, and what law applies. The distinction between acts *jure imperii* and acts *jure gestionis*, familiar from the law of state immunity, and the distinction in investment law between a mere breach of contract and a breach of treaty, are both attempts to grapple with this problem.

### The State Contract

The "state contract" — a contract between a state (or a state entity) and a foreign private party — is the central legal instrument of international resource development. Petroleum concessions, production sharing contracts, mining agreements, host government agreements for pipelines, and power purchase agreements with state-owned utilities are all species of state contract.

The legal status of state contracts has been debated for more than a century. Three principal positions may be distinguished.

The first position holds that state contracts are governed exclusively by the domestic law of the host state. On this view, the state retains its sovereign power to modify the contract by legislation, and the investor's protection lies only in such guarantees as domestic law provides. This position was associated with the Calvo doctrine in Latin America and with the claims of the New International Economic Order.

The second position holds that state contracts may be "internationalised" — that is, subjected to international law or to general principles of law — by the choice of the parties, by the inclusion of stabilisation and arbitration clauses, or by the nature of the contract as an economic development agreement. This position found its fullest expression in the award of Sole Arbitrator René-Jean Dupuy in *Texaco v Libya*, which held that the concession contracts at issue had been internationalised and that Libya's nationalisation measures were a breach of its contractual obligations under international law.[^texaco]

[^texaco]: *Texaco Overseas Petroleum Company and California Asiatic Oil Company v Government of the Libyan Arab Republic*, Award on the Merits of 19 January 1977, (1978) 17 ILM 1; (1979) 53 ILR 389.

The third position, now widely accepted, holds that the contract is governed by the law chosen by the parties (often the host state's law, sometimes supplemented by international law or general principles), but that the investor may also enjoy independent protection under international law — most importantly through investment treaties — which may be invoked even where the contract itself is governed by domestic law. The distinction between contract claims and treaty claims, articulated by the *ad hoc* Committee in *Compañía de Aguas del Aconquija SA and Vivendi Universal v Argentina* (*Vivendi I*), is now a cornerstone of investment arbitration: "whether there has been a breach of the BIT and whether there has been a breach of contract are different questions".[^vivendi]

[^vivendi]: *Compañía de Aguas del Aconquija SA and Vivendi Universal v Argentine Republic*, ICSID Case No ARB/97/3, Decision on Annulment, 3 July 2002, para 96.

### Transnational Legal Relations

Beyond the public–private divide lies a further category of relations that is neither purely public nor purely private, neither purely national nor purely international. Philip Jessup, writing in 1956, coined the term "transnational law" to include "all law which regulates actions or events that transcend national frontiers", embracing both public and private international law as well as "other rules which do not wholly fit into such standard categories".[^jessup] Energy and resource activities exemplify transnational legal relations. A single offshore gas project may involve a host government agreement with the state, a production sharing contract with the national oil company, a joint operating agreement among the co-venturers, engineering contracts with international contractors, LNG sales agreements with buyers in several countries, financing agreements with commercial banks and export credit agencies, and an investment treaty between the host state and the home states of the investors. Each relationship is governed by a different combination of national and international rules, and disputes may be resolved in different forums.

[^jessup]: Philip C. Jessup, *Transnational Law* (Yale University Press 1956), p. 2.

The transnational character of these relations has three important implications. First, it means that no single legal order governs the project as a whole; the applicable law must be determined relationship by relationship. Second, it creates the risk of conflicting obligations, as when an environmental obligation imposed by domestic law conflicts with a stabilisation clause in a contract, or a sanctions regime imposed by one state conflicts with a contractual obligation to supply. Third, it confers significant influence upon private actors — companies, banks, industry associations, arbitrators — in the creation and interpretation of norms, raising questions of legitimacy and accountability that recur throughout this book.

## 1.5 The Relationship Between Energy Law and International Economic Law

### Energy as an Exception in Trade Law

The relationship between energy law and international economic law is close but historically uneasy. When the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was negotiated in 1947, energy products were not excluded from its scope, but in practice trade in oil and gas was largely left outside the multilateral trading system. Many major producers were not parties to the GATT; the trade in crude oil was conducted largely within vertically integrated companies; and energy policy was regarded as so closely bound up with national security that states were unwilling to subject it to multilateral discipline. The GATT security exception in Article XXI, which permits measures "relating to fissionable materials or the materials from which they are derived" and measures "taken in time of war or other emergency in international relations", reinforced this sense of energy as a domain of sovereign prerogative.

The establishment of the WTO in 1995 and the accession of major energy producers — including Saudi Arabia in 2005 and the Russian Federation in 2012 — brought energy trade more firmly within the multilateral framework. WTO disputes have since addressed export restrictions on raw materials (*China — Raw Materials* and *China — Rare Earths*), local content requirements in renewable energy programmes (*Canada — Renewable Energy/Feed-in Tariff Program* and *India — Solar Cells*), and the European Union's energy market regulation (*EU — Energy Package*). These disputes are examined in Chapter 13.

### Investment Law as the Primary Interface

If trade law has historically played a limited role in energy, investment law has played a central one. Energy and mining projects are capital-intensive, long-lived, and dependent on the stability of the host state's legal and fiscal regime; they are therefore particularly vulnerable to what economists call the "obsolescing bargain" — the tendency for the host state's bargaining power to increase once the investor's capital has been sunk. Investment treaties and investor–state arbitration were designed in large part to mitigate this risk. Statistics compiled by ICSID consistently show that the oil, gas, and mining sector, together with electric power and other energy, account for a larger share of registered cases than any other economic sector.

The obsolescing bargain theory, associated with the work of Raymond Vernon, explains much of the dynamic of resource governance. At the time of the initial investment decision, the host state competes with other states for scarce capital and expertise and must offer attractive terms. Once the investment has been made, however, the investor's assets are immobile and the host state may seek to renegotiate the terms in its favour — through increased taxation, contract renegotiation, or expropriation. Investors anticipate this risk and demand protections: stabilisation clauses, international arbitration, investment treaty coverage, and political risk insurance. The law of international energy investment can be understood, in significant part, as an attempt to manage the obsolescing bargain.

### Convergence and Tension

The relationship between energy law and international economic law is characterised by both convergence and tension. Convergence occurs where economic law supports energy policy objectives — for example, where investment protection encourages the capital flows necessary for the energy transition. Tension occurs where economic law constrains the regulatory autonomy that states need to pursue energy and climate objectives — for example, where investors challenge the phase-out of coal-fired power or the withdrawal of renewable energy subsidies, or where trade rules are invoked against local content requirements designed to build domestic manufacturing capacity. The phase-out of coal-fired power in the Netherlands gave rise to ECT claims by RWE and Uniper (the latter subsequently withdrawn), and the retroactive reduction of renewable energy incentives in Spain generated dozens of ECT arbitrations. The resulting debates about regulatory chill, the right to regulate, and the reform of investment treaties are examined in Chapter 14.
