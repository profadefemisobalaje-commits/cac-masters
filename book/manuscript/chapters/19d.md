## 19.7 Water Rights, Human Rights, and Public Access

### The Human Right to Water and Sanitation

Access to safe drinking water and sanitation is fundamental to human life, health, and dignity, yet according to the World Health Organization and UNICEF, around two billion people lack safely managed drinking water services and several billion lack safely managed sanitation. The international recognition of a human right to water has developed progressively. Although the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966) does not mention water expressly, the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, in its **General Comment No 15** (2002), interpreted the rights to an adequate standard of living (Article 11) and to health (Article 12) as including the right to water, defined as the right of everyone to sufficient, safe, acceptable, physically accessible, and affordable water for personal and domestic uses. Water is expressly mentioned in the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (Article 14(2)(h)), the Convention on the Rights of the Child (Article 24(2)(c)), and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (Article 28(2)(a)). In 2010, the UN General Assembly adopted **Resolution 64/292**, recognising the right to safe and clean drinking water and sanitation as a human right that is essential for the full enjoyment of life and all human rights, by a vote of 122 to none with 41 abstentions, and the Human Rights Council affirmed the right in Resolution 15/9 later that year. Since 2015, the General Assembly and the Human Rights Council have recognised the rights to water and to sanitation as distinct rights. Sustainable Development Goal 6 aims to ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all by 2030.

The right entails obligations to **respect** (refraining from interfering with existing access, for example through arbitrary disconnections or the pollution of water sources), to **protect** (preventing third parties, including companies, from interfering with the right), and to **fulfil** (taking steps, to the maximum of available resources, to achieve progressively the full realisation of the right). General Comment No 15 identifies a minimum core obligation to ensure access to the minimum essential amount of water sufficient for personal and domestic uses and to prevent disease, and emphasises that priority in the allocation of water must be given to the right to water for personal and domestic uses, and to the water resources required to prevent starvation and disease.

### Constitutional Rights to Water

Numerous states have recognised the right to water in their constitutions. Section 27(1)(b) of the **Constitution of South Africa** provides that everyone has the right to have access to sufficient water, and requires the state to take reasonable legislative and other measures, within its available resources, to achieve the progressive realisation of the right. In *Mazibuko v City of Johannesburg* [2009] ZACC 28, the Constitutional Court upheld the City's policy of providing a free basic water supply of six kilolitres per household per month and the installation of prepaid water meters in Soweto, holding that the right did not entitle the applicants to a specific quantity of water and that the City's policy was reasonable, while emphasising the importance of the right and the obligation of the state to review its policies — a decision criticised by some commentators for its deference to the executive. Article 43(1)(d) of the **Constitution of Kenya** 2010 guarantees the right to clean and safe water in adequate quantities, and the constitutions of Uganda, Ecuador, Bolivia, Uruguay, and many other states contain comparable provisions. In Nigeria, the Constitution does not expressly guarantee a right to water, but the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy direct the state to ensure suitable and adequate shelter, food, and other necessities, and courts and commentators have argued that access to water is an aspect of the rights to life and dignity, as examined in Chapter 17 in relation to environmental rights.

### Water for Energy and the Right to Water

The allocation of water to energy and extractive uses may conflict with the human right to water, particularly in water-scarce regions. Mining and petroleum operations may compete with communities for limited water resources, contaminate drinking water sources, or lower water tables; hydropower reservoirs may displace communities and alter downstream flows on which riverine populations depend; and large-scale green hydrogen production, which requires substantial quantities of water for electrolysis, has raised concerns in arid regions, including in Namibia, Morocco, and parts of the Middle East, where proposals for desalination have been advanced to avoid competition with domestic and agricultural uses. The UN Special Rapporteur on the human rights to safe drinking water and sanitation has reported on the effects of extractive industries and large infrastructure on the rights to water and sanitation, emphasising the priority of personal and domestic uses, the need for human rights impact assessment, and the participation of affected communities. In Chile, the extensive use of water by the copper and lithium mining industries in the arid north has been contested by indigenous communities and farmers, and reforms of the Water Code in 2022 established that water is a national public good for public use, prioritised human consumption, and limited the duration of new water rights. The **UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights**, examined in Chapter 21, require companies to respect the right to water, including through due diligence on their water use and pollution.

### Indigenous Peoples and Water

Indigenous peoples frequently have special relationships with water bodies, which may be of spiritual, cultural, and economic significance. The **UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples** (2007) recognises the right of indigenous peoples to maintain and strengthen their distinctive spiritual relationship with their traditionally owned or otherwise occupied and used lands, territories, waters, and coastal seas (Article 25), and requires states to consult and cooperate in good faith with indigenous peoples to obtain their free and informed consent prior to the approval of any project affecting their lands or territories and other resources, particularly in connection with the development, utilisation, or exploitation of mineral, water, or other resources (Article 32(2)). In some jurisdictions, rivers have been recognised as legal persons, in part to give effect to indigenous relationships with them: New Zealand's **Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017** recognised the Whanganui River as a legal person, with guardians appointed by the Crown and the Whanganui iwi, and courts in Colombia, India, and Bangladesh have recognised rivers as subjects of rights. The opposition of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe to the Dakota Access oil pipeline in the United States, on the ground that it threatened the tribe's water supply and sacred sites, illustrated the intersection of indigenous rights, water, and energy infrastructure; in 2020, a federal court vacated an easement for the pipeline for failure to prepare an adequate environmental impact statement, although the pipeline continued to operate. Free, prior, and informed consent is examined in detail in Chapter 21.

### Public Access and Participation

The governance of water resources increasingly emphasises public participation and access to information, in line with Principle 10 of the Rio Declaration and the Aarhus and Escazú Conventions, examined in Chapter 17. **Integrated water resources management** (IWRM), endorsed by the Dublin Statement on Water and Sustainable Development (1992), promotes the coordinated development and management of water, land, and related resources, and the participation of users, planners, and policy-makers at all levels, including through catchment management agencies and water user associations. The Dublin Statement's principle that water has an economic value in all its competing uses, and should be recognised as an economic good, has been influential but controversial, particularly where it has been associated with the privatisation of water services and the introduction of cost-recovery tariffs. The tension between water as an economic good and water as a human right — reflected in disputes over water privatisation in Cochabamba, Bolivia, in 2000, and in investment arbitrations arising from water concessions in Argentina and Tanzania, examined in Chapter 14 — remains a central theme of water governance.

## 19.8 International Water Dispute Resolution

### The Nature of Water Disputes

Disputes over shared waters arise from competing claims to the quantity of water, from the effects of infrastructure such as dams and diversions, from pollution, from the operation of reservoirs, and from boundaries defined by rivers. They are frequently embedded in broader political relationships and may be linked to questions of national security, food security, and energy security. Despite the frequent prediction of "water wars", research has found that interstate cooperation over shared waters has historically been far more common than violent conflict, and that states have concluded hundreds of water treaties. Nevertheless, water disputes can be protracted and bitter, and the prevention and resolution of such disputes is a central function of international water law.

### Treaty-Based Mechanisms

Water treaties commonly provide graduated mechanisms for dispute resolution, beginning with consultation and negotiation within joint commissions, and proceeding to more formal mechanisms. The **Indus Waters Treaty**, examined in section 19.5, provides a three-tier system distinguishing questions, differences, and disputes, with recourse to a neutral expert or a court of arbitration. Article 33 of the **UN Watercourses Convention** provides that, if the parties cannot reach agreement by negotiation, they may jointly seek the good offices of, or request mediation or conciliation by, a third party, or submit the dispute to arbitration or the International Court of Justice; if, after six months, they have not settled the dispute, any party may require the establishment of an impartial **fact-finding commission**, whose report the parties must consider in good faith. Parties may declare their acceptance of the compulsory jurisdiction of the ICJ or arbitration. Basin agreements in Africa, including the Niger Basin Water Charter and the SADC Revised Protocol, provide for dispute resolution within their institutions, with recourse to regional courts or arbitration; the SADC Protocol provides for disputes to be referred to the SADC Tribunal, whose functioning was suspended in 2010 and subsequently restricted to disputes between states.

### International Courts and Tribunals

The International Court of Justice has decided several water disputes: the *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* case (1997), examined in section 19.3; the *Kasikili/Sedudu Island* case (*Botswana/Namibia*, 1999), concerning a boundary defined by the main channel of the Chobe River; *Pulp Mills* (2010), examined in Chapter 17; the *Certain Activities* and *Construction of a Road* cases (2015) concerning the San Juan River; the *Dispute regarding Navigational and Related Rights* (*Costa Rica v Nicaragua*, 2009); and the *Silala* case (2022), in which the Court found that many of the parties' claims had become moot because their positions had converged during the proceedings, while confirming the applicable customary principles. Arbitral tribunals, often administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration, have decided disputes including *Lac Lanoux* (1957), the *Indus Waters Kishenganga* arbitration (2013), and the arbitration concerning the *Iron Rhine* railway (*Belgium/Netherlands*, 2005), which applied principles of environmental law to the reactivation of a railway through a nature reserve.

### Diplomatic and Facilitated Processes

Many water disputes are addressed through diplomatic and facilitated processes rather than adjudication. The World Bank played a central role in the negotiation of the Indus Waters Treaty and in the appointment of the neutral expert and court of arbitration under it; the African Union, the United States, and the World Bank facilitated negotiations on the GERD; and the Nile Basin Initiative provided a forum for dialogue among Nile riparians. Basin organisations, such as the OMVS and the Niger Basin Authority, provide institutional settings for the prevention of disputes through joint planning, data sharing, and the joint review of proposed projects. Mediation and conciliation, which allow parties to explore solutions based on benefit-sharing rather than legal entitlements alone, are well suited to water disputes, in which the parties' long-term relationship and interdependence make purely adversarial outcomes unattractive.

### Water and Investment Disputes

Water disputes also arise in investor-state arbitration, where investors in water concessions or hydropower projects have challenged government measures affecting their investments. Disputes arising from the privatisation of water and sanitation services in Argentina, including *Azurix Corp v Argentina* (2006), *Suez, Sociedad General de Aguas de Barcelona SA and Vivendi Universal SA v Argentina* (2010), and *Urbaser SA v Argentina* (2016) — in which the tribunal accepted that it had jurisdiction over a counterclaim by Argentina based on the human right to water, although it rejected the counterclaim on the merits — and *Biwater Gauff (Tanzania) Ltd v Tanzania* (2008), illustrate the interaction between investment protection and the human right to water, examined in Chapter 14. Hydropower investors have brought claims arising from tariff disputes, delays, and terminations, and the *Mambilla* arbitration in Nigeria, examined in section 19.5, illustrates the significance of commercial arbitration in hydropower development.

### Water, Armed Conflict, and Infrastructure

The protection of water resources and installations in armed conflict is addressed by international humanitarian law, including Article 54 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977), which prohibits attacking, destroying, removing, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population, including drinking water installations and supplies and irrigation works, and Article 56, which provides special protection for works and installations containing dangerous forces, namely dams, dykes, and nuclear electrical generating stations. The destruction of the **Kakhovka dam** on the Dnipro River in Ukraine in June 2023, during the Russian occupation of the area, caused extensive flooding, loss of life, environmental damage, and the loss of water supply for irrigation and for the cooling of the Zaporizhzhia nuclear power plant, illustrating the catastrophic consequences of attacks on water and energy infrastructure. The ILC's Principles on the Protection of the Environment in Relation to Armed Conflicts (2022) and the **Geneva List of Principles on the Protection of Water Infrastructure** (2019) provide further guidance. The protection of energy infrastructure in armed conflict is examined further in Chapter 25.

### Conclusion

International water law has developed a coherent set of principles — equitable and reasonable utilisation, the obligation not to cause significant harm, prior notification and consultation, environmental protection, and cooperation — which are reflected in the UN Watercourses Convention, the UNECE Water Convention, and customary international law. Its effectiveness depends on their application through basin agreements and institutions, and on the willingness of states to cooperate. For energy law, water law matters because hydropower, thermal generation, petroleum, mining, and hydrogen production all depend on water, and because the development of shared rivers for hydropower is among the most consequential and contentious decisions that states make. In a warming world, in which the availability of water is becoming less predictable, the importance of effective legal frameworks for shared waters will only increase.
