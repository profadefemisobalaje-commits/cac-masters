## 14.6 Stabilization Clauses and Regulatory Change

### The Problem of Regulatory Change

The obsolescing bargain manifests itself most frequently not in outright expropriation but in regulatory and fiscal change: increases in taxes and royalties, the imposition of windfall levies, the reform of tariff regimes, the tightening of environmental and local content requirements, and the renegotiation of contracts. Such changes may be justified by legitimate public interests, by changes in economic circumstances, or by the inadequacy of the original bargain, but they may also be opportunistic attempts to appropriate the returns on sunk investments. Investors seek protection against adverse regulatory change through two principal instruments: **contractual stabilisation clauses**, and the **treaty standards** examined in sections 14.3 to 14.5, particularly FET and the umbrella clause. This section examines their interaction. The drafting and contractual operation of stabilisation and change-in-law clauses are examined from a contractual perspective in Chapter 15.

### Types of Stabilisation Clauses

Stabilisation clauses in host government contracts take several forms. **Freezing clauses** provide that the law applicable to the contract, or to the investor's activities, is the law in force at the date of the contract, and that subsequent changes in law shall not apply to the investor without its consent. **Consistency clauses** provide that the investment is governed by the terms of the contract, and that legislation inconsistent with the contract shall not apply. **Economic equilibrium clauses** do not prevent changes in law from applying to the investor, but require the state to restore the economic equilibrium of the contract — through compensation, tariff adjustment, or other means — if changes in law adversely affect the investor's economic position. **Hybrid clauses** combine elements of these approaches. Contemporary practice has shifted from freezing clauses towards economic equilibrium clauses, which are regarded as more compatible with state sovereignty and with the state's ongoing need to regulate.

The legal effect of stabilisation clauses was considered in the classic oil nationalisation arbitrations, examined in Chapter 3. In *Texaco Overseas Petroleum Co v Libya* (1977), the sole arbitrator held that stabilisation clauses in Libya's concessions were binding, and that Libya's nationalisation breached them. In *AGIP SpA v People's Republic of the Congo* (ICSID, 1979), the tribunal held that the stabilisation clauses in the agreement were binding under international law and that their breach engaged the state's responsibility. In *Kuwait v American Independent Oil Co (Aminoil)* (1982), the tribunal held that the stabilisation clause in the concession did not prohibit nationalisation in the absence of an express prohibition for a limited period, but that its existence was relevant to the determination of compensation, reflecting the investor's legitimate expectations.

### Stabilisation Clauses and Treaty Standards

Investment treaties interact with stabilisation clauses in several ways.

**Umbrella clauses.** An umbrella clause — such as the last sentence of Article 10(1) of the ECT, requiring each contracting party to "observe any obligations it has entered into with an Investor or an Investment of an Investor" — may elevate the breach of a contractual obligation, including a stabilisation clause, into a breach of the treaty, enabling the investor to bring a treaty claim. The scope of umbrella clauses has been contested. In *SGS Société Générale de Surveillance SA v Pakistan* (ICSID, 2003), the tribunal adopted a narrow interpretation, holding that the umbrella clause did not elevate every breach of contract into a breach of the treaty. In *SGS v Philippines* (ICSID, 2004), a differently constituted tribunal adopted a broader interpretation, holding that the umbrella clause made it a breach of the BIT for the host state to fail to observe binding commitments, including contractual commitments, which it had assumed with regard to specific investments, although the investor could not rely on the clause to bypass the contractual forum selection clause. Subsequent tribunals have generally held that umbrella clauses cover obligations specifically entered into with the investor, including stabilisation commitments, but differ on whether they cover obligations entered into by state entities with separate legal personality and on the effect of contractual forum clauses.

**Legitimate expectations.** A stabilisation clause is the clearest form of specific commitment by a host state, and its existence may support the investor's legitimate expectation that the regulatory framework will not change to its detriment, so that a breach of the clause may also breach the FET standard. In *Duke Energy Electroquil Partners and Electroquil SA v Ecuador* (ICSID, 2008), the tribunal held that Ecuador had breached the FET standard by failing to honour guarantees in a power purchase agreement, including the guarantee of payment, on which the investors had legitimately relied. Conversely, as noted in section 14.3, in the absence of a stabilisation clause or specific commitment, tribunals have generally held that investors cannot legitimately expect that the legal framework will not change, as in *Parkerings*. The existence or absence of stabilisation therefore significantly affects the outcome of FET claims arising from regulatory change.

### The Argentine Gas Cases and the Necessity Defence

The economic crisis in Argentina in 2001–2002 gave rise to a wave of investment arbitrations, many involving energy investors, particularly in the gas transportation and distribution sector, which had been privatised in the 1990s under a regulatory framework that guaranteed tariffs calculated in US dollars and adjusted in accordance with the United States producer price index. In response to the crisis, Argentina's Emergency Law of January 2002 abolished the convertibility of the peso with the US dollar, converted tariffs into pesos at a rate of one to one, and froze tariff adjustments. Investors claimed that the measures breached the FET standard, the umbrella clause, and the expropriation provisions of the US–Argentina BIT and other treaties.

The tribunals generally held that Argentina had breached the FET standard by dismantling the tariff guarantees on which the investors had relied, but differed on whether Argentina could rely on the defence of necessity, either under customary international law, as codified in Article 25 of the International Law Commission's Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts, or under Article XI of the US–Argentina BIT, which provided that the treaty did not preclude measures necessary for the maintenance of public order or the protection of a party's essential security interests. In *CMS Gas Transmission Co v Argentina* (ICSID, 2005), the tribunal rejected the necessity defence, holding that the crisis was not so severe as to threaten the state's existence, that Argentina had contributed to it, and that the measures adopted were not the only means available, and awarded around USD 133 million. In *LG&E Energy Corp v Argentina* (ICSID, 2006), by contrast, the tribunal accepted the necessity defence under Article XI for the period of the most acute crisis, from December 2001 to April 2003, holding that Argentina was exempted from liability for that period. The annulment committee in *CMS* (2007) criticised the tribunal's conflation of Article XI with customary necessity but declined to annul the award on that ground; in *Sempra Energy International v Argentina* (2010) and *Enron Corp v Argentina* (2010), annulment committees annulled the awards for failure to apply Article XI properly. In *Continental Casualty Co v Argentina* (ICSID, 2008), the tribunal interpreted Article XI by reference to the WTO jurisprudence on necessity, holding that most of Argentina's measures were justified.

The Argentine gas cases illustrate the significance of emergency and necessity defences in energy disputes arising from economic crises, and the inconsistency of arbitral reasoning, which has been a principal ground of criticism of the investment arbitration system.

### Stabilisation and the Right to Regulate

Stabilisation clauses have been criticised for constraining the ability of host states — particularly developing states — to adopt new environmental, social, and human rights protections. A study conducted in 2008 for the International Finance Corporation and the United Nations Special Representative of the Secretary-General on Business and Human Rights, *Stabilization Clauses and Human Rights*, found that stabilisation clauses in contracts with non-OECD host states were more likely to be broad freezing clauses that could exempt investors from new social and environmental laws, whereas those in OECD states more commonly limited stabilisation to fiscal matters or excluded laws of general application. The *Principles for Responsible Contracts*, issued by the Special Representative in 2011 and welcomed by the Human Rights Council, provide in Principle 4 that contractual stabilisation clauses, if used, should be carefully drafted so that any protections for investors against future changes in law do not interfere with the state's bona fide efforts to implement laws, regulations, or policies in a non-discriminatory manner in order to meet its human rights obligations.

Contemporary practice increasingly limits stabilisation to fiscal matters, excludes changes in law of general application relating to health, safety, environment, and human rights, provides for economic equilibrium rather than freezing, and limits the duration of stabilisation. The host government agreements for the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline, concluded in 2000, contained broad economic equilibrium clauses which were criticised by civil society organisations; in response, the project sponsors executed a unilateral **Human Rights Undertaking** in 2003, committing not to invoke the economic equilibrium provisions in relation to the host states' regulation of health, safety, environment, and human rights in a manner consistent with international standards.

### Windfall Taxes and the 2022 Energy Crisis

The energy price crisis of 2022 led many states to impose windfall taxes on the profits of energy companies, including the EU's temporary **solidarity contribution** on fossil fuel companies under Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854, examined in Chapter 9, and the United Kingdom's Energy Profits Levy. These measures generated legal challenges both before national and EU courts — including actions by ExxonMobil subsidiaries before the General Court challenging the legality of the solidarity contribution — and under investment treaties, including ECT claims by the Klesch group against the European Union, Germany, and Denmark. These challenges raise questions concerning the competence of the European Union to impose such measures, the application of the ECT's taxation carve-out, the effect of stabilisation commitments, and the legitimacy of windfall taxation in the face of extraordinary profits.

## 14.7 Investor–State Dispute Settlement

### Arbitration Without Privity

The distinctive feature of modern investment treaties is the provision for **investor–state dispute settlement** (ISDS), which enables an investor of one contracting party to bring a claim directly against the other contracting party before an international arbitral tribunal, without the need for the investor's home state to espouse the claim through diplomatic protection. The state's consent to arbitration is expressed in the treaty as a standing offer to arbitrate with all qualifying investors; the investor accepts the offer by commencing arbitration, thereby perfecting the agreement to arbitrate. This model, described by Jan Paulsson as **"arbitration without privity"**, was first applied in *Asian Agricultural Products Ltd v Sri Lanka* (ICSID, 1990). It transformed investment protection from a matter of interstate diplomacy into a system of enforceable investor rights.

### Arbitral Forums

Investment treaties typically offer investors a choice among several arbitral forums. The **International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes** (ICSID), established by the Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States (the ICSID Convention, 1965), is the principal institution, administering the majority of known investment treaty cases. ICSID arbitration is delocalised from national legal systems: ICSID awards are subject only to the Convention's internal annulment procedure (Article 52), not to review by national courts at the seat of arbitration, and must be recognised and enforced by all contracting states as if they were final judgments of their own courts (Article 54). The **ICSID Additional Facility** Rules apply where one of the parties is not a contracting state or a national of a contracting state. Arbitration under the **UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules**, often administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration, and arbitration under the rules of the **Stockholm Chamber of Commerce** (SCC), which is expressly provided for in the ECT, are subject to the law of the seat of arbitration and to setting aside proceedings in its courts, and their awards are enforced under the **New York Convention** on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (1958). The procedural frameworks of ICSID and UNCITRAL arbitration are examined in Chapter 26.

### Preconditions and Parallel Proceedings

Investment treaties often impose preconditions on the investor's right to arbitrate, including a period of amicable negotiation (a "cooling-off" period, typically three to six months), the pursuit of domestic remedies for a specified period (as in the eighteen-month requirement at issue in *Maffezini*), or an election between domestic courts and international arbitration (a **"fork-in-the-road"** clause), under which the investor's choice to submit a dispute to domestic courts precludes subsequent arbitration of the same dispute. Tribunals have generally applied fork-in-the-road clauses narrowly, requiring that the dispute submitted to domestic courts be the same as that submitted to arbitration — in the sense of having the same fundamental basis, as held in *Pantechniki SA v Albania* (ICSID, 2009). Some treaties, notably the US and Canadian models and NAFTA, adopt a **"no U-turn"** or waiver approach, requiring the investor to waive the right to initiate or continue domestic proceedings with respect to the measure as a condition of arbitration.

Energy disputes frequently involve both contract claims, arising under a concession, PSC, or PPA with a forum selection clause, and treaty claims. In *Compañía de Aguas del Aconquija SA and Vivendi Universal v Argentina* (ICSID, annulment decision 2002), the annulment committee drew a fundamental distinction between contract claims and treaty claims, holding that whether there has been a breach of the BIT and whether there has been a breach of contract are different questions, each determined by reference to its own proper law, and that a contractual forum selection clause does not preclude a tribunal from considering a treaty claim, even where the treaty claim requires consideration of the contract. This distinction underpins the ability of energy investors to bring treaty claims notwithstanding contractual dispute resolution clauses.

### State Counterclaims and Investor Obligations

The traditional asymmetry of investment treaties — which confer rights on investors and obligations on states — has been tempered by the increasing acceptance of **counterclaims** by host states. In the energy sector, Ecuador successfully brought counterclaims for environmental damage against oil companies in *Burlington Resources Inc v Ecuador* (ICSID, decision on counterclaims 2017), in which the tribunal awarded Ecuador around USD 40 million for environmental remediation and infrastructure damage, and in *Perenco Ecuador Ltd v Ecuador* (ICSID), in which the tribunal found Perenco liable for environmental harm in the blocks it had operated. In *Urbaser SA v Argentina* (ICSID, 2016), the tribunal accepted jurisdiction over a counterclaim alleging that the investor had violated the human right to water, holding that investors may be subject to obligations under international law, although it dismissed the counterclaim on the merits because the human right to water imposed obligations on states rather than investors in the circumstances. The inclusion of investor obligations in recent treaties, such as the Morocco–Nigeria BIT and the ECOWAS Supplementary Act, may provide a firmer basis for counterclaims in the future.

### Transparency and Third-Party Participation

Investment arbitration, modelled on confidential commercial arbitration, was initially criticised for its lack of transparency, given that it concerns public measures and public funds. The **UNCITRAL Rules on Transparency in Treaty-based Investor-State Arbitration** (2014) provide for the publication of documents, open hearings, and submissions by third parties (*amicus curiae*) and non-disputing treaty parties, and the **United Nations Convention on Transparency in Treaty-based Investor-State Arbitration** (the Mauritius Convention, 2014, in force 2017) extends their application to existing treaties. The ICSID Arbitration Rules, amended in 2022, provide for the publication of awards and the participation of non-disputing parties. Transparency has also been addressed through the disclosure of **third-party funding**, which is increasingly common in investment arbitration, including energy disputes, and raises issues of conflicts of interest and security for costs.

### Legitimacy and State Responses

ISDS has been subjected to sustained criticism on several grounds: the inconsistency of arbitral decisions, illustrated by the divergent outcomes in the Argentine gas cases and the renewable energy cases; the lack of an appellate mechanism; concerns about the independence and impartiality of arbitrators, who are appointed by the parties and may act alternately as counsel and arbitrator ("double-hatting"), as illustrated by the annulment of the *Eiser* award, examined in Chapter 10; the costs of proceedings; the size of awards, which may represent a significant burden on the public finances of developing states; and the potential for "regulatory chill", whereby states refrain from adopting legitimate regulation for fear of claims.

States have responded in diverse ways. Bolivia (2007), Ecuador (2009), and Venezuela (2012) denounced the ICSID Convention, although Ecuador rejoined in 2021; Honduras denounced it in 2024. Ecuador's Constitution of 2008 prohibits the state from entering into treaties that cede sovereign jurisdiction to international arbitration in contractual or commercial disputes between the state and private parties, and a 2024 referendum rejected a proposal to remove the prohibition. South Africa terminated many of its BITs from 2012 and enacted the Protection of Investment Act 2015, which provides for domestic protection and state-to-state arbitration. India terminated most of its BITs in 2017 and adopted a new model BIT in 2015 that narrows the protections offered and requires the exhaustion of local remedies. The European Union has pursued the replacement of ISDS by an **Investment Court System** in its trade agreements and the establishment of a multilateral investment court, examined in section 14.8.
