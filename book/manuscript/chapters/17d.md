## 17.6 Marine Pollution and Offshore Operations

### Scope of this Section

The protection of the marine environment is governed by Part XII of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), which establishes the general obligation of states to protect and preserve the marine environment and specific obligations in respect of different sources of pollution. The general framework of Part XII, the new Agreement on Marine Biological Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction, and the environmental regulation of deep-seabed mining are examined in Chapter 20. This section focuses on a narrower question of particular importance to energy law: the international, regional, and national rules governing pollution arising from offshore petroleum operations — exploration, production, and the associated transport of hydrocarbons — and the lessons drawn from major offshore incidents.

### The Absence of a Global Offshore Regime

Article 208 of UNCLOS requires coastal states to adopt laws and regulations to prevent, reduce, and control pollution of the marine environment arising from or in connection with seabed activities subject to their jurisdiction and from artificial islands, installations, and structures under their jurisdiction, which shall be no less effective than international rules, standards, and recommended practices, and requires states to endeavour to harmonise their policies at the appropriate regional level. Unlike shipping, however, offshore petroleum activities are not governed by a comprehensive global convention. The International Maritime Organization's conventions apply principally to ships, and their application to offshore installations is partial.

The **International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships** (MARPOL 73/78) applies to fixed and floating platforms in certain respects, including their discharges of machinery-space oily water and garbage, but does not regulate discharges arising directly from the exploration, exploitation, and associated offshore processing of seabed mineral resources. The **International Convention on Oil Pollution Preparedness, Response and Co-operation** (OPRC, 1990) requires operators of offshore units to have oil pollution emergency plans, coordinated with national systems, and to report oil pollution incidents without delay, and requires parties to establish national systems for responding to oil pollution incidents and to cooperate in response. The **London Convention** (1972) and its **Protocol** (1996) regulate the dumping of wastes at sea, including the disposal of offshore platforms, and the Protocol was amended in 2006 to permit the storage of carbon dioxide in sub-seabed geological formations, as examined in Chapter 12.

The **civil liability conventions** for oil pollution — the International Convention on Civil Liability for Oil Pollution Damage (CLC, 1992), the International Oil Pollution Compensation Fund Convention (1992) and its Supplementary Fund Protocol (2003), and the Bunkers Convention (2001) — apply to pollution from ships, including tankers carrying oil in bulk, but not, in general, to pollution from fixed offshore installations, although their application to floating production, storage, and offloading units (FPSOs) depends on whether such units are "ships" within the meaning of the conventions, a question that has been debated. Following the Montara blowout in 2009, Indonesia proposed that the IMO develop a convention on liability and compensation for transboundary pollution damage from offshore oil exploration and exploitation, but the IMO Legal Committee concluded that there was no compelling need for a global convention and instead developed guidance to assist states wishing to conclude bilateral or regional agreements.

### Regional Regimes

In the absence of a global regime, the most developed rules governing offshore pollution are found in regional seas agreements.

**The North-East Atlantic.** The **OSPAR Convention** for the Protection of the Marine Environment of the North-East Atlantic (1992) contains, in Annex III, specific provisions on the prevention and elimination of pollution from offshore sources, and the OSPAR Commission has adopted binding decisions and recommendations on matters including the discharge of oil in produced water, the use and discharge of offshore chemicals, and drill cuttings. **OSPAR Decision 98/3** on the Disposal of Disused Offshore Installations, adopted following the controversy over Shell's proposal in 1995 to dispose of the Brent Spar oil storage buoy in the deep Atlantic, prohibits the dumping and leaving wholly or partly in place of disused offshore installations, subject to derogations for certain large steel structures and concrete installations, and is a key instrument in decommissioning law, as examined in Chapter 5.

**The Mediterranean.** The **Offshore Protocol** to the Barcelona Convention, adopted in 1994 and in force since 2011, regulates exploration and exploitation of the continental shelf and seabed, including authorisation, the use and discharge of harmful substances, safety measures, contingency planning, and the removal of installations.

**The Gulf.** The Kuwait Regional Convention for Cooperation on the Protection of the Marine Environment from Pollution (1978) is supplemented by a Protocol concerning Marine Pollution resulting from Exploration and Exploitation of the Continental Shelf (1989).

**West and Central Africa.** The **Abidjan Convention** for Cooperation in the Protection, Management and Development of the Marine and Coastal Environment of the Atlantic Coast of the West, Central and Southern Africa Region (1981), to which Nigeria is a party, has been supplemented by an Additional Protocol concerning Environmental Norms and Standards for Offshore Oil and Gas Exploration and Exploitation Activities, adopted at Malabo in 2019, which addresses EIA, the management of discharges and wastes, emergency preparedness, decommissioning, and liability, and represents an important development for a region in which offshore production is expanding rapidly, including in the Gulf of Guinea and off Senegal, Mauritania, and Namibia.

### Lessons from Major Offshore Incidents

**Deepwater Horizon (2010).** The blowout of the Macondo well in the Gulf of Mexico, examined in Chapter 15 in relation to contractual indemnities, killed eleven workers and released several million barrels of oil over eighty-seven days. The National Commission on the BP Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill and Offshore Drilling found that the disaster resulted from systemic failures of risk management by the companies involved and of regulatory oversight by the federal regulator, the Minerals Management Service, whose functions as a revenue collector, a promoter of leasing, and a safety regulator were in conflict. The United States responded by dividing those functions among separate agencies — the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement, and the Office of Natural Resources Revenue — and by adopting new rules on well control and safety management systems. BP pleaded guilty to criminal charges and, in 2015, agreed to a settlement with the federal government and the Gulf states of approximately USD 20.8 billion, including civil penalties under the Clean Water Act and natural resource damages under the Oil Pollution Act of 1990.

**Montara (2009).** The blowout of a well on the Montara platform in the Timor Sea, off the north-west coast of Australia, released oil for more than ten weeks, some of which reached waters off Indonesia. A Commission of Inquiry found serious deficiencies in the operator's well integrity practices and in regulatory oversight, leading to the establishment of a national offshore petroleum safety and environmental regulator (NOPSEMA). In 2021, in a class action brought by Indonesian seaweed farmers, the Federal Court of Australia held in *Sanda v PTTEP Australasia (Ashmore Cartier) Pty Ltd* that the operator owed a duty of care to the farmers and had breached it, causing damage to their seaweed crops, and the case was subsequently settled. The case is significant as an example of transboundary civil liability for offshore pollution pursued in the courts of the operator's state.

**The European Union response.** In the light of Deepwater Horizon, the European Union adopted the **Offshore Safety Directive** (Directive 2013/30/EU), which requires operators to prepare reports on major hazards and internal emergency response plans, to demonstrate their technical and financial capacity, including the capacity to meet liabilities, before being licensed, and requires Member States to establish independent competent authorities separate from the authorities responsible for licensing and economic development — reflecting the lesson of the conflict of functions in the United States.

### Offshore Pollution in Nigeria

Nigeria's offshore sector has experienced significant pollution incidents. In December 2011, an oil spill at the Bonga deepwater field, operated by Shell Nigeria Exploration and Production Company, released an estimated forty thousand barrels of oil during a cargo transfer operation to a tanker. The National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency (NOSDRA) proposed a very large fine and sought compensation, and the matter became the subject of protracted litigation and regulatory dispute. The incident highlighted gaps in the legal framework for offshore pollution, including the limited penalties under NOSDRA's legislation and the absence of clear standards for natural resource damage assessment.

### Operational Discharges, Gas Flaring, and Methane

Beyond accidents, offshore and onshore petroleum operations generate routine discharges and emissions that are regulated by national law and regional standards: produced water, drilling muds and cuttings, chemicals, sewage, and air emissions. **Gas flaring** — the burning of associated gas produced with oil, for which no market or infrastructure exists — wastes a valuable resource, emits greenhouse gases and pollutants, and has long been a major environmental and health problem in the Niger Delta. Nigeria has prohibited routine flaring since the Associated Gas Re-injection Act of 1979, but successive deadlines for ending flaring were not met, and penalties were long set at levels too low to deter the practice. The **Flare Gas (Prevention of Waste and Pollution) Regulations 2018** established a commercial framework for the capture and sale of flare gas and increased penalties, and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 provides for the elimination of routine flaring subject to exemptions, and for penalties to be paid into an environmental remediation fund. The World Bank's **Zero Routine Flaring by 2030** initiative, launched in 2015, has been endorsed by numerous governments and companies.

**Methane** emissions from oil and gas operations — through venting, flaring, and leaks — are a major contributor to climate change, given methane's high short-term warming potential. The **Global Methane Pledge**, launched at COP26 in 2021, commits participants to reduce global methane emissions by at least thirty per cent from 2020 levels by 2030. The European Union's **Methane Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1787) requires operators to measure, report, and verify methane emissions, to detect and repair leaks, and to cease routine venting and flaring, and will progressively apply methane intensity requirements to imports of oil, gas, and coal into the Union — an extraterritorial dimension with significant implications for exporting states, including Nigeria, Algeria, and other African suppliers.

## 17.7 Environmental Liability and Remediation

### State Responsibility for Environmental Damage

Under the law of state responsibility, a state that commits an internationally wrongful act causing environmental damage is obliged to make full reparation. The assessment of compensation for environmental damage, which may not have a market value, presents particular difficulties, which the International Court of Justice addressed for the first time in 2018.

In the compensation phase of *Certain Activities Carried Out by Nicaragua in the Border Area* (*Costa Rica v Nicaragua*), the Court held that damage to the environment, and the consequent impairment or loss of the ability of the environment to provide goods and services, is compensable under international law, and that compensation may include indemnification for the impairment or loss of environmental goods and services in the period prior to recovery and payment for the restoration of the damaged environment. Rejecting both the parties' proposed methodologies, the Court adopted an "overall assessment" of the impairment of environmental goods and services, and awarded Costa Rica compensation totalling approximately USD 379,000, including USD 120,000 for the impairment or loss of environmental goods and services. The amount was modest, but the recognition of pure environmental damage as compensable was a significant development. In *Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo* (*DRC v Uganda*), the Court in 2022 awarded the DRC reparations of USD 325 million, including USD 60 million for damage related to natural resources, including the illegal exploitation of minerals, timber, and wildlife.

The **United Nations Compensation Commission**, established by the Security Council in 1991 to process claims arising from Iraq's invasion and occupation of Kuwait, awarded more than USD 5 billion for environmental damage, including damage caused by the deliberate release of oil and the setting on fire of more than seven hundred oil wells, and established a follow-up programme to monitor the use of the awards for environmental remediation. Its practice remains the most extensive international precedent for the assessment of large-scale environmental damage arising from the energy sector.

### Civil Liability Regimes

Because the activities causing environmental damage are usually conducted by private operators, the principal international mechanism for compensation is the imposition of civil liability on operators through treaties that harmonise national law. The civil liability conventions for oil pollution from ships, examined in section 17.6, and for nuclear damage, examined in Chapter 11, share common features: **strict liability** of a designated person (the shipowner or the operator of the nuclear installation), regardless of fault, subject to limited defences; **channelling** of liability to that person; **limitation** of liability to specified amounts; **compulsory insurance** or other financial security; and, in the case of oil pollution and nuclear damage, additional tiers of compensation from industry-funded or state-funded sources. Civil liability conventions for other hazardous activities, including the 1993 Lugano Convention on Civil Liability for Damage resulting from Activities Dangerous to the Environment and the 1999 Basel Protocol on Liability and Compensation, have not entered into force, reflecting the reluctance of states to accept harmonised liability for activities other than shipping and nuclear energy.

### National Liability Regimes

National environmental liability regimes take several forms. In the **United States**, the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980 (CERCLA, or "Superfund") imposes strict, joint and several, and retroactive liability for the costs of cleaning up hazardous substance releases on current and former owners and operators of facilities, generators of hazardous substances, and transporters, and the Oil Pollution Act of 1990 imposes strict liability on responsible parties for oil spills, including liability for **natural resource damages** assessed by federal and state trustees. In the **European Union**, the Environmental Liability Directive (Directive 2004/35/EC) establishes an administrative regime under which operators of listed dangerous activities are strictly liable to prevent and remedy environmental damage to protected species and habitats, water, and land, through **primary remediation** (restoring the damaged resources to baseline condition), **complementary remediation** (compensating for incomplete primary remediation), and **compensatory remediation** (compensating for interim losses pending recovery). Unlike CERCLA, the Directive does not give private parties a right to compensation, but permits affected persons and environmental organisations to request action by the competent authority.

### Oil Pollution in the Niger Delta

The Niger Delta has experienced decades of pollution from oil spills, caused by equipment failure and corrosion of ageing pipelines, operational failures, sabotage, and the theft of crude oil ("bunkering") and illegal refining. The legal framework for liability and remediation comprises several elements.

**Statutory compensation.** The **Oil Pipelines Act** of 1956 requires the holder of a pipeline licence to pay compensation to any person suffering damage as a consequence of a breakage of or leakage from the pipeline, except where the damage is caused by the claimant's own default or the malicious act of a third person — an exception that has generated extensive dispute about whether spills were caused by sabotage. Nigerian courts have also applied common law torts, including negligence, nuisance, and the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*.

**Regulation.** The **National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency (Establishment) Act** 2006 established NOSDRA, with responsibility for the detection of and response to oil spills, and regulations made under it govern spill reporting, clean-up, remediation, and damage assessment. The **Environmental Guidelines and Standards for the Petroleum Industry in Nigeria** (EGASPIN), first issued by the Department of Petroleum Resources in 1991 and revised subsequently, set standards for discharges and remediation, including target and intervention values for soil and groundwater contamination. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 requires holders of licences and leases to contribute to an **environmental remediation fund** for the rehabilitation of environmental damage, and to establish decommissioning and abandonment funds, as examined in Chapters 5 and 24.

**The Ogoniland assessment and HYPREP.** In 2011, the United Nations Environment Programme published its **Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland**, following an invitation from the Nigerian government. The report found widespread and severe contamination of land, groundwater, and surface water, including drinking water contaminated with benzene at levels far exceeding World Health Organization guidelines, and concluded that the restoration of the environment could take twenty-five to thirty years. It recommended the establishment of an Ogoniland Environmental Restoration Authority and an initial capital fund of USD 1 billion, contributed by the oil industry operators and the government, for the first five years. The government established the **Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project** (HYPREP) to implement the recommendations, relaunched in 2016, but progress in remediation has been slow and has been criticised by communities and civil society.

**Transnational litigation.** The difficulty of obtaining redress in Nigerian courts has led communities to bring claims against Shell and its Nigerian subsidiary in the courts of the United Kingdom and the Netherlands. In *Bodo Community v Shell Petroleum Development Co of Nigeria Ltd*, concerning two major spills from the Trans-Niger Pipeline in 2008, Shell's Nigerian subsidiary admitted liability under the Oil Pipelines Act and settled the claims in 2015 for GBP 55 million. In *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* [2021] UKSC 3, the United Kingdom Supreme Court held that it was arguable that the parent company owed a duty of care to communities affected by spills from its subsidiary's operations, allowing the claims to proceed in England. In *Oguru and Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell plc*, the Court of Appeal of The Hague held in January 2021 that Shell's Nigerian subsidiary was liable for spills affecting two villages, rejecting the defence of sabotage as unproven, and that the parent company was obliged to ensure the installation of a leak detection system; the claims were subsequently settled. These cases, which raise questions of parent company liability and jurisdiction examined in Chapters 22 and 27, have been accompanied by the divestment of Shell's onshore Nigerian business to a consortium of predominantly Nigerian companies, completed in 2025, raising concerns about responsibility for legacy pollution and the adequacy of funds for remediation and decommissioning.

### Remediation Standards and Financial Assurance

The effectiveness of liability regimes depends on remediation standards and the availability of funds. Key legal issues include the standard to which contaminated sites must be remediated — whether to background conditions, to standards appropriate for the intended use, or to risk-based targets; the allocation of responsibility for historical pollution where responsible parties no longer exist or are insolvent; and the requirement of **financial assurance** — bonds, guarantees, insurance, or dedicated funds — to ensure that funds are available for remediation, decommissioning, and mine closure, even if the operator becomes insolvent or divests its assets. The divestment of ageing onshore and shallow water petroleum assets by international oil companies to smaller operators, in Nigeria and elsewhere, has heightened concern that liabilities may be transferred to entities lacking the resources to meet them, and has led regulators to scrutinise the decommissioning and remediation provisions of transactions. The catastrophic failures of tailings dams at Mariana (2015) and Brumadinho (2019) in Brazil, examined in Chapter 7, and the resulting settlements — including a settlement concluded in October 2024 of approximately BRL 170 billion in respect of the Mariana disaster — illustrate the scale of liabilities that may arise from mining and the importance of financial assurance and robust safety regulation.
