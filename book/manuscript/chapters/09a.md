## Introduction

Electricity is the energy carrier of modern life and, increasingly, of the energy transition. It powers homes, hospitals, schools, factories, and digital infrastructure; its reliable supply is a precondition of economic development and of the realisation of numerous human rights. The decarbonisation of energy systems depends overwhelmingly on electricity: on the replacement of fossil-fuelled generation with renewable and other low-carbon sources, and on the electrification of transport, heating, and industrial processes that currently rely on direct combustion of fossil fuels. The International Energy Agency has described the current period as the beginning of an "age of electricity", in which global electricity demand is growing faster than overall energy demand, driven by industrial growth, air conditioning, electric vehicles, and data centres. At the same time, around seven hundred million people, the large majority of them in sub-Saharan Africa, still lack access to electricity, and hundreds of millions more have access only to unreliable supply.

The law of electricity has historically been predominantly domestic. Electricity systems were built as national or regional monopolies, regulated by domestic statutes and agencies, and international law played a limited role. That position has changed. Cross-border electricity trade is expanding through regional power pools and interconnected markets; foreign investment in generation and networks is protected by investment treaties and has given rise to numerous arbitrations; international development institutions have shaped the reform of electricity sectors across the developing world; the European Union has built a supranational electricity market governed by detailed legislation; and international climate law is driving the transformation of generation. International electricity law, as treated in this chapter, therefore comprises both the comparative law of domestic electricity regulation — the models and techniques that states have adopted and that circulate among them — and the international and regional legal frameworks that govern cross-border trade, investment, and cooperation.

This chapter begins with the legal structure of electricity industries (section 9.1) and the regulation of generation, transmission, and distribution (section 9.2). It then examines electricity market liberalisation (section 9.3), grid access and third-party rights (section 9.4), and power purchase agreements (section 9.5). It concludes with cross-border electricity trading (section 9.6), consumer protection and energy affordability (section 9.7), and market abuse, competition, and regulatory enforcement (section 9.8). Throughout, particular attention is paid to Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023, which fundamentally restructured the legal framework of the Nigerian electricity supply industry, and to the regional framework of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS). Renewable energy law is examined in Chapter 10, and the technologies of the future grid — storage, smart grids, and digital infrastructure — in Chapter 12.

## 9.1 Legal Structure of Electricity Industries

### The Physical Characteristics of Electricity

The legal structure of electricity industries is shaped by the physical characteristics of electricity, which distinguish it from every other commodity. Four characteristics are particularly important.

First, **electricity cannot be economically stored in large quantities**, at least with technologies widely deployed until recently. Supply and demand must therefore be balanced continuously, second by second, across the entire interconnected system. If generation falls short of demand, the frequency of the system falls, and if the imbalance is not corrected quickly, protective devices disconnect generators and loads, potentially causing a cascading blackout. The need for continuous balancing requires a central coordinator — the system operator — with authority to dispatch generation, procure reserves, and take emergency action. The development of battery storage at scale, examined in Chapter 12, is beginning to change this characteristic, but the need for real-time balancing remains fundamental.

Second, **electricity flows according to the laws of physics, not the terms of contracts**. Electricity injected into a meshed network flows along all available paths in inverse proportion to their impedance (Kirchhoff's laws), so that a contract for the sale of electricity from a generator in one location to a consumer in another does not determine the physical path that the electricity takes. This "loop flow" phenomenon means that transactions between two parties affect the network capacity available to others, including in neighbouring countries, and complicates the allocation of transmission rights and costs.

Third, **demand for electricity is highly variable and, in the short term, relatively insensitive to price**. Demand varies by time of day, day of the week, and season, and most consumers historically had no means of observing or responding to real-time prices. Generation capacity must therefore be sufficient to meet peak demand plus a reserve margin, and much capacity is used only for a few hours a year.

Fourth, **the networks through which electricity is transmitted and distributed are natural monopolies**, as in the gas sector (examined in Chapter 6). It would be inefficient to duplicate transmission lines or distribution networks, and network owners therefore enjoy market power that must be regulated.

### The Electricity Value Chain

The electricity supply industry comprises four principal functions:

1. **Generation** — the production of electricity in power stations using fossil fuels, nuclear energy, hydropower, wind, solar, geothermal, biomass, or other sources.
2. **Transmission** — the transport of electricity at high voltage over long distances from generators to load centres, and the operation of the interconnected system.
3. **Distribution** — the transport of electricity at lower voltages from the transmission system to end-users.
4. **Supply** (or retail) — the sale of electricity to end-users, including metering, billing, and customer service.

To these should be added **system operation** — the real-time balancing of supply and demand and the dispatch of generation, which may be performed by the transmission owner or by an independent entity — and **market operation** — the administration of wholesale markets, settlement, and related functions.

### Models of Industry Structure

The legal structure of electricity industries can be classified according to the degree of vertical integration and competition. A widely used typology, derived from the work of Sally Hunt and Graham Shuttleworth, distinguishes four models.

**Model 1: Vertically integrated monopoly.** A single utility — state-owned or privately owned but regulated — owns and operates generation, transmission, distribution, and supply within its territory, with no competition at any level. This was the dominant model worldwide until the 1980s and remains common in many developing countries and in parts of the United States.

**Model 2: Single buyer.** The integrated utility, or a separate entity, acts as the sole purchaser of electricity from generators, including independent power producers (IPPs) that sell under long-term power purchase agreements, and resells it to distributors or consumers. Competition exists only for the right to build new generation, typically through competitive procurement of PPAs. The single buyer model was widely adopted in Asia and Africa in the 1990s as a means of attracting private investment in generation without full liberalisation.

**Model 3: Wholesale competition.** Generators compete to sell electricity to distribution companies and large consumers through a wholesale market, which may be organised as a mandatory pool, a power exchange, or bilateral contracts, with non-discriminatory access to the transmission network. Distribution companies retain monopoly supply to small consumers.

**Model 4: Retail competition.** All consumers, including households, may choose their supplier, and distribution networks are open to competing retailers. This is the model required by EU law and adopted in the United Kingdom, Australia, parts of the United States, and other jurisdictions.

These models are ideal types; real electricity industries frequently combine elements of several. Many developing countries, including Nigeria, have adopted a transitional model in which a liberalised framework exists in law but the market operates in practice through regulated contracts and administered prices, pending the development of the conditions for effective competition.

### Ownership

Electricity industries may be state-owned, privately owned, or mixed. State ownership was justified historically by the natural monopoly characteristics of networks, the strategic importance of electricity, the need for very large investments, and the goal of universal access. Privatisation, beginning in Chile in the 1980s and in England and Wales with the Electricity Act 1989, was justified by the expected efficiency gains of private ownership, the need to mobilise private capital, and the fiscal pressures facing governments. The results of privatisation have been mixed, and in recent years some jurisdictions have taken steps towards renationalisation or increased state ownership, as in France, where the state acquired full ownership of Électricité de France (EDF) in 2023, and in Germany, where municipalities have repurchased distribution networks.

### The Nigerian Electricity Supply Industry: Structural Evolution

The Nigerian electricity supply industry illustrates the evolution from vertically integrated monopoly to an unbundled, privatised, and increasingly decentralised structure. From 1972, electricity was supplied by the National Electric Power Authority (NEPA), a vertically integrated state monopoly that became a byword for unreliable supply. The National Electric Power Policy of 2001 and the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005 (EPSRA) provided for the unbundling of NEPA into the Power Holding Company of Nigeria (PHCN), and subsequently into eighteen successor companies — six generation companies (GenCos), eleven distribution companies (DisCos), and the Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN) — and established the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC) as an independent regulator. In November 2013, the federal government completed the privatisation of the GenCos and DisCos, while retaining ownership of TCN, whose management was contracted for a period to a private operator. The Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Plc (NBET) was established as a transitional bulk trader, purchasing power from GenCos and IPPs under PPAs and reselling it to DisCos under vesting contracts.

The privatised industry has faced severe challenges: insufficient gas supply to thermal power stations; transmission constraints; high technical and commercial losses; tariffs below cost-recovery levels, resulting in large subsidy obligations and a liquidity crisis in which DisCos failed to remit full payment for energy received; widespread estimated billing and inadequate metering; and the inability of many DisCos to attract investment. Several DisCos were taken over by their creditors, including the Asset Management Corporation of Nigeria (AMCON), following defaults on acquisition financing.

### The Electricity Act 2023 and Constitutional Decentralisation

Two legal developments in 2023 transformed the structure of the Nigerian electricity industry. First, the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria (Fifth Alteration) Acts of 2023 amended the Exclusive and Concurrent Legislative Lists to empower state Houses of Assembly to make laws with respect to the generation, transmission, and distribution of electricity in areas covered by the national grid within the state, extending the existing state competence over areas not covered by the national grid. Secondly, the Electricity Act 2023, signed into law in June 2023, repealed EPSRA and established a new framework that recognises the competence of states to regulate electricity markets within their territories. Under the Act, a state that establishes its own electricity market and regulatory body assumes regulatory oversight of the market within the state, and NERC must transfer regulatory functions over that market to the state regulator. By 2025, a number of states — beginning with Enugu, followed by Ekiti, Ondo, Imo, Oyo, Edo, Kogi, Ogun, Niger, Plateau, and others — had enacted electricity laws and established state regulators, and NERC had transferred regulatory oversight to them.

The Electricity Act 2023 also provides for the establishment of the Nigerian Independent System Operator (NISO), separating system operation from the transmission business of TCN; for the promotion of renewable energy and a requirement that generation companies include renewable sources in their generation mix; for the development of a competitive wholesale market; for the protection of consumers; and for integrated resource planning. The constitutional and statutory decentralisation of electricity regulation in Nigeria is one of the most significant recent developments in African electricity law, and raises novel questions concerning the relationship between federal and state regulation, the regulation of interstate transmission and trading, and the coherence of the national market. These questions are examined in section 9.2 and in Chapter 24.
