## Introduction

The decarbonisation of the energy system requires not only the replacement of fossil-fuelled electricity generation with renewable and other low-carbon sources, examined in Chapters 10 and 11, but also the transformation of the infrastructure, markets, and technologies through which energy is converted, stored, transported, and used. Electricity from variable wind and solar resources must be stored or balanced; sectors that are difficult to electrify — steel, chemicals, fertilisers, shipping, and aviation — require low-carbon fuels and feedstocks; residual emissions from industrial processes must be captured and stored; offshore resources must be harnessed and connected; and an increasingly decentralised and digitalised energy system must be managed securely. Hydrogen, electricity storage, carbon capture and storage, offshore renewable technologies, and smart grids are the principal technological responses to these challenges.

These emerging technologies pose a common legal problem: the law was designed for an energy system with different technologies, actors, and boundaries. Electricity law presupposed a clear separation between generation, transmission, distribution, and consumption, which storage, which can act as each in turn, does not respect. Gas law was designed for natural gas, and its application to hydrogen raises questions of classification, network regulation, and safety. Mining and petroleum law regulates the extraction of substances from the subsurface, and must be adapted to regulate the injection of carbon dioxide into it. The law of the sea was developed for navigation, fishing, and seabed resources, and must accommodate large-scale offshore energy infrastructure. And energy regulation was designed for a system of large, centrally controlled assets, not a system of millions of connected devices exchanging data. The legal response has been a combination of the adaptation of existing frameworks, the creation of new regimes, and the use of experimental instruments such as regulatory sandboxes.

This chapter examines hydrogen production and regulatory classification (section 12.1), hydrogen production pathways and their legal definition (section 12.2), hydrogen certification and cross-border trade (section 12.3), battery storage and grid integration (section 12.4), carbon capture, utilisation, and storage (section 12.5), offshore renewable energy technologies (section 12.6), smart grids, digital infrastructure, and cybersecurity (section 12.7), and regulatory sandboxes and technological innovation (section 12.8).

## 12.1 Hydrogen Production and Regulatory Classification

### The Role of Hydrogen in the Energy Transition

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, but it occurs on Earth almost entirely in compounds, principally water and hydrocarbons, from which it must be separated using energy. It is therefore an energy carrier rather than a primary energy source. Hydrogen has been produced and used for more than a century as an industrial feedstock, principally in oil refining and in the production of ammonia for fertilisers and methanol, and global production amounts to around one hundred million tonnes per year, almost all of it produced from natural gas and coal without carbon capture, resulting in substantial greenhouse gas emissions.

The interest in hydrogen as an instrument of decarbonisation rests on its potential, when produced with low emissions, to replace fossil fuels in applications that are difficult to electrify directly: as a feedstock and reducing agent in steel production (replacing coking coal), as a feedstock in ammonia and methanol production, as a fuel for shipping (directly or through derivatives such as ammonia and methanol), aviation (through synthetic fuels), and heavy road transport, and as a medium for the long-duration storage of energy. Its role in other applications, such as residential heating and light passenger vehicles, is contested, because direct electrification is generally more efficient. Many governments have adopted national hydrogen strategies, and several resource-rich states, including Australia, Chile, Morocco, Namibia, Egypt, Oman, and Saudi Arabia, aspire to become exporters of hydrogen and its derivatives.

The legal framework for hydrogen is at an early stage of development. Hydrogen raises legal questions at each stage of its value chain: production (permitting, electricity and water supply, and the definition of "renewable" or "low-carbon" hydrogen); conversion (into ammonia, methanol, or other carriers); transport and storage (by pipeline, ship, or truck, and in underground salt caverns or depleted reservoirs); trade (certification and the treatment of hydrogen under trade law); and end use (safety standards, demand-side mandates, and support schemes).

### The Problem of Classification

The threshold legal question is how hydrogen is to be classified. Existing legal categories — "gas", "natural gas", "fuel", "chemical", "dangerous substance", and "energy product" — have been defined with other substances in mind, and the classification of hydrogen determines which regulatory regimes apply to it.

**Hydrogen as a gas.** In some jurisdictions, hydrogen falls within the definition of "gas" for the purposes of gas legislation, bringing hydrogen networks within existing frameworks of licensing, network regulation, and safety. The United Kingdom's Gas Act 1986 defines "gas" broadly to include any substance in a gaseous state consisting wholly or mainly of methane, ethane, propane, butane, hydrogen, or carbon monoxide, among other things, so that the conveyance of hydrogen is, in principle, within the licensing regime. In other jurisdictions, gas legislation is limited to "natural gas" or "methane", and hydrogen is excluded.

**Hydrogen as a chemical and dangerous substance.** Hydrogen is a flammable gas with a wide flammability range and low ignition energy, and is subject to the legislation governing dangerous substances, industrial safety, and the prevention of major accidents. In the European Union, the **Seveso III Directive** (Directive 2012/18/EU) on the control of major-accident hazards involving dangerous substances applies to establishments where hydrogen is present above specified thresholds, and the **ATEX Directives** (Directives 2014/34/EU and 1999/92/EC) govern equipment and workplaces in potentially explosive atmospheres. Hydrogen is also classified under chemicals legislation, including the EU's REACH Regulation, and under the international rules on the transport of dangerous goods.

**Hydrogen in the EU gas market framework.** The European Union adopted the **Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package** in 2024, comprising Directive (EU) 2024/1788 on common rules for the internal markets for renewable gas, natural gas and hydrogen, and Regulation (EU) 2024/1789 on the internal markets for renewable gas, natural gas and hydrogen. The package establishes, for the first time, a dedicated regulatory framework for hydrogen networks, distinct from that for natural gas. It provides for the vertical unbundling of hydrogen network operators from hydrogen production and supply, using models analogous to those for gas and electricity examined in Chapters 6 and 9; for regulated third-party access to hydrogen networks from 2033, with negotiated access permitted until then; for the certification of hydrogen network operators; for the establishment of a European Network of Network Operators for Hydrogen (ENNOH); for integrated network planning; and for rules on the blending of hydrogen into natural gas networks, under which transmission operators must accept flows with up to two per cent hydrogen by volume at interconnection points. The package also defines "low-carbon hydrogen" — hydrogen whose energy content is derived from non-renewable sources and which meets a greenhouse gas emissions reduction threshold of seventy per cent compared with fossil fuel — and provides for its certification.

**Hydrogen pipelines in the United States.** The regulation of hydrogen pipelines in the United States illustrates the difficulties of fitting hydrogen into existing categories. Hydrogen pipelines are subject to safety regulation by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration, but the federal authority responsible for their economic regulation is unclear: the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's jurisdiction under the Natural Gas Act extends to "natural gas", which may not include pure hydrogen, while the Surface Transportation Board's residual jurisdiction over pipelines transporting commodities other than water, gas, or oil may extend to hydrogen. Blends of hydrogen with natural gas transported in natural gas pipelines are generally considered to remain within FERC's jurisdiction.

### Production Permitting

The production of hydrogen by electrolysis requires an electrolyser — industrial equipment that uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen — together with a supply of electricity and water. Electrolysers are typically permitted as industrial installations, subject to land use, environmental, and major-accident hazard requirements. Large electrolysers above specified thresholds may be subject to the EU's Industrial Emissions Directive and environmental impact assessment, and RED III requires member states to ensure that permit-granting procedures for renewable hydrogen production and for associated infrastructure are streamlined. Production from natural gas with carbon capture requires, in addition, the permitting of capture facilities and carbon dioxide transport and storage, examined in section 12.5.

**Water.** Electrolysis requires around nine litres of purified water per kilogram of hydrogen, in addition to cooling water. Although the water requirements of hydrogen production are modest relative to agriculture, large-scale production in water-scarce regions — including many of the regions with the best solar resources, such as North Africa, the Middle East, Namibia, and Australia — may require desalination, raising issues of water rights, the disposal of brine, and the allocation of scarce water between export industries and local needs. Water allocation law, examined in Chapter 10, therefore forms part of the legal framework for hydrogen.

**Land and benefit-sharing.** Export-scale hydrogen projects in developing countries involve the allocation of large areas of land for renewable generation, electrolysers, and port facilities, raising issues of land rights, environmental protection, and the sharing of benefits with host states and communities. Namibia's flagship green hydrogen project in the Tsau //Khaeb National Park near Lüderitz, for which the government concluded a feasibility and implementation agreement with a private developer and acquired an option to take an equity stake, has attracted debate about the location of an industrial project in a national park, the terms of the state's participation, and the distribution of benefits. These questions echo those examined in the context of extractive industries in Chapters 5 and 7, and resource nationalism and local content in the hydrogen sector are emerging themes of energy law in Africa.

**Nigeria.** Nigeria does not yet have a dedicated legal framework for hydrogen. Hydrogen production from natural gas is undertaken incidentally in refining and in the fertiliser industry, and the PIA's provisions on gas utilisation and midstream licensing may apply to some hydrogen-related activities. Nigeria's abundant natural gas, solar resources, and existing fertiliser and ammonia industries, together with the Energy Transition Plan's identification of hydrogen as a potential decarbonisation pathway, suggest that the development of a hydrogen strategy and legal framework is a subject of growing interest.
