## Introduction

Renewable energy has moved from the margins to the centre of energy law. In the space of two decades, wind and solar power have been transformed from expensive niche technologies dependent on generous subsidies into the cheapest sources of new electricity generation in most of the world. Renewable sources now account for the large majority of new generating capacity installed globally each year, and at the 2023 United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP28) in Dubai, the parties to the Paris Agreement called for a tripling of global renewable energy capacity and a doubling of the global average annual rate of energy efficiency improvements by 2030. Renewable energy is also central to the achievement of universal energy access: decentralised solar systems and mini-grids offer the most cost-effective means of electrifying many of the rural communities in sub-Saharan Africa that the grid has not reached.

The law has been both a driver and a constraint of this transformation. Legal instruments — renewable energy targets, feed-in tariffs, renewable portfolio standards, auctions, tax credits, priority grid access, and permitting regimes — created the markets that drove down costs through deployment and learning. At the same time, legal obstacles — lengthy and uncertain permitting, conflicts over land and resources, inadequate grid access, regulatory instability, and opposition from affected communities — have slowed deployment. And the retroactive reduction of renewable support schemes by several European governments in the aftermath of the financial crisis generated one of the largest waves of investment treaty arbitration in history, testing the limits of investor protection against regulatory change.

The legal issues raised by renewable energy differ from those of fossil fuels in important ways. Renewable resources — sunlight, wind, flowing water, geothermal heat, and biomass — are not "owned" in the same way as subsurface minerals, and their legal status has required new conceptual frameworks. Renewable projects have low operating costs but high upfront capital costs, so their economics depend critically on the cost of capital and therefore on regulatory stability. Many renewable resources are variable, requiring new approaches to grid integration and market design. And the land-intensive character of solar and wind energy, the impacts of hydropower on rivers, and the sustainability concerns surrounding bioenergy generate conflicts with other land uses, biodiversity, and the rights of local and indigenous communities.

This chapter examines the legal foundations of renewable energy development (section 10.1), the licensing and land rights issues of solar energy (section 10.2), wind energy permitting and environmental compliance (section 10.3), hydropower law and water resource allocation (section 10.4), geothermal and biomass energy regulation (section 10.5), renewable energy incentives and support mechanisms (section 10.6), renewable energy certificates and attribute markets (section 10.7), and renewable energy investment disputes (section 10.8). The integration of renewable energy into electricity markets is addressed in Chapter 9, the emerging technologies of storage, hydrogen, and offshore renewables in Chapter 12, and the transboundary dimensions of hydropower in Chapter 19.

## 10.1 Legal Foundations of Renewable Energy Development

### Defining Renewable Energy

Legal definitions of renewable energy matter because they determine eligibility for support schemes, targets, priority access, and certificates. Most definitions enumerate the sources considered renewable. Article 2(1) of the EU Renewable Energy Directive (Directive (EU) 2018/2001, as amended by Directive (EU) 2023/2413) defines "energy from renewable sources" as energy from renewable non-fossil sources, namely wind, solar (solar thermal and solar photovoltaic) and geothermal energy, osmotic energy, ambient energy, tide, wave and other ocean energy, hydropower, biomass, landfill gas, sewage treatment plant gas, and biogas. The Statute of the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), adopted in 2009, defines renewable energy in Article III to include all forms of energy produced from renewable sources in a sustainable manner, including bioenergy, geothermal energy, hydropower, ocean energy (including tidal, wave, and ocean thermal energy), solar energy, and wind energy.

Definitions raise contested questions at the margins. Is large hydropower renewable, given its environmental and social impacts? Is biomass renewable if its harvesting causes deforestation or releases more carbon than it saves over relevant time horizons? Is waste incineration renewable? Should nuclear energy, which is low-carbon but not renewable, be included in "clean energy" targets? Different legal systems answer these questions differently, and the trend has been to supplement definitions with sustainability criteria, as in the EU's sustainability and greenhouse gas saving criteria for biomass, examined in section 10.5, and to adopt broader "clean" or "low-carbon" categories alongside "renewable" categories.

### International Foundations

There is no comprehensive international treaty on renewable energy. The international legal foundations of renewable energy are found in several instruments.

**Climate change law.** The UNFCCC, the Kyoto Protocol, and the Paris Agreement, examined in Chapter 18, provide the overarching framework. Although the Paris Agreement does not specifically mention renewable energy in its operative provisions, the nationally determined contributions of most parties include renewable energy targets or measures, and the COP28 decision on the first global stocktake called on parties to contribute to tripling renewable energy capacity globally by 2030. Article 4(1)(c) of the UNFCCC commits all parties to promote and cooperate in the development, application, and diffusion of technologies that control, reduce, or prevent anthropogenic emissions of greenhouse gases in all relevant sectors, including energy.

**The Sustainable Development Goals.** SDG 7, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 2015, calls for access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all by 2030, with target 7.2 calling for a substantial increase in the share of renewable energy in the global energy mix.

**IRENA.** The Statute of the International Renewable Energy Agency, which entered into force in 2010, established IRENA as an intergovernmental organisation to promote the widespread and increased adoption and sustainable use of all forms of renewable energy. IRENA's institutional role is examined in Chapter 4.

**The International Solar Alliance.** The Framework Agreement on the Establishment of the International Solar Alliance, launched by India and France at COP21 in 2015 and in force since 2017, establishes a treaty-based organisation of countries committed to promoting solar energy, particularly in countries located between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, through the aggregation of demand, the mobilisation of finance, and capacity building. Nigeria is a party.

**The Energy Charter Treaty.** Article 19 of the ECT requires contracting parties to strive to minimise harmful environmental impacts of energy activities, including by "promoting and using renewable energy sources", and the ECT's investment protection provisions have been the basis of most renewable energy investment arbitrations, examined in section 10.8.

### Regional and Domestic Targets

Renewable energy targets are the most widespread legal instrument of renewable energy policy, adopted by almost every country. Targets may be expressed as shares of final energy consumption, electricity generation, or installed capacity, and may be binding or indicative.

The European Union has adopted binding targets for renewable energy since the Renewable Energy Directive of 2009 (Directive 2009/28/EC), which set a binding target of twenty per cent of gross final energy consumption by 2020, broken down into binding national targets. The recast Directive of 2018 set a binding Union-level target of at least thirty-two per cent by 2030, without binding national targets, relying on national energy and climate plans and a governance mechanism. Directive (EU) 2023/2413 ("RED III") raised the binding Union target to at least 42.5 per cent by 2030, with an indicative aspiration of forty-five per cent, and introduced sub-targets for transport, buildings, industry, and district heating and cooling, including a binding target for the use of renewable fuels of non-biological origin (principally renewable hydrogen) in industry.

In Africa, the ECOWAS Renewable Energy Policy, adopted in 2013 and implemented through the ECOWAS Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency (ECREEE) in Praia, Cape Verde, set targets for increasing the share of renewable energy in the regional electricity mix, including large hydropower, and member states adopted National Renewable Energy Action Plans. The African Union's Agenda 2063 and the Africa Renewable Energy Initiative (AREI), launched at COP21, aimed to install substantial new renewable capacity across the continent.

### The Nigerian Framework

Nigeria's legal and policy framework for renewable energy has developed incrementally. The **National Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency Policy** (NREEEP), approved in 2015, set out the government's objectives for renewable energy, and the **Nigeria Renewable Energy Master Plan** set targets for the contribution of renewables to electricity supply. NERC's **Feed-in Tariff Regulations for Renewable Energy Sourced Electricity** (2015) provided for feed-in tariffs for small hydropower, wind, solar, and biomass, and required DisCos to procure a proportion of their electricity from renewable sources, although implementation was limited. In 2016, NBET signed PPAs with fourteen solar IPPs for a combined capacity of around 1,125 MW, but most of these projects failed to reach financial close, owing largely to disagreements over tariffs, the absence of adequate payment security, and the liquidity crisis in the electricity market.

The **Rural Electrification Agency** (REA), established under EPSRA and continued under the Electricity Act 2023, administers the Rural Electrification Fund and implements programmes, including the World Bank-supported Nigeria Electrification Project and the Distributed Access through Renewable Energy Scale-up (DARES) programme, that have supported the deployment of solar mini-grids and stand-alone solar systems. The **Climate Change Act 2021** set a target of net zero emissions between 2050 and 2070 and established the National Council on Climate Change, and the **Energy Transition Plan** of 2022 set out a pathway to net zero by 2060, with a substantial expansion of solar capacity. The **Electricity Act 2023** contains specific provisions on renewable energy, including obligations on the regulator to promote renewable energy, provision for renewable purchase obligations, incentives for renewable energy, and the treatment of renewable energy in integrated resource planning. These developments are examined further in Chapter 24.

### The Legal Status of Renewable Resources

A distinctive conceptual question is whether, and by whom, renewable resources can be owned. Wind and sunlight are generally regarded as *res communes* — things common to all, incapable of ownership in their natural state. The legal question therefore concerns not ownership of the resource itself, but rights to access and use it: rights to use land on which solar panels and wind turbines are located, protection against interference with access to the resource (for example, by structures that shade solar panels or obstruct wind flows), and the allocation of offshore areas for renewable development. Some jurisdictions have legislated to protect access to solar or wind resources: several states of the United States have enacted "solar access" laws permitting the creation of solar easements or protecting solar installations from shading, and the law of nuisance and easements may be invoked to protect access.

Offshore, the coastal state's sovereign rights in the exclusive economic zone under Article 56(1)(a) of UNCLOS extend expressly to "the production of energy from the water, currents and winds", providing the international legal basis for offshore wind and ocean energy development, examined in Chapters 12 and 20. Water used for hydropower and geothermal resources are, by contrast, subject to well-developed legal regimes of public ownership and allocation, examined in sections 10.4 and 10.5.

## 10.2 Solar Energy Licensing and Land Rights

### The Solar Revolution

Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy has experienced the most dramatic cost reductions of any energy technology: the cost of solar modules has fallen by around ninety per cent over the past decade, and utility-scale solar is now among the cheapest sources of new electricity in most regions. Solar energy is deployed at every scale: utility-scale solar farms of hundreds or thousands of megawatts; commercial and industrial installations on rooftops and land; residential rooftop systems; solar mini-grids serving rural communities; and stand-alone solar home systems and appliances. Each scale raises different legal issues.

### Licensing and Permitting of Utility-Scale Solar

Utility-scale solar projects typically require a generation licence (where the capacity exceeds the licensing threshold), land rights, planning or land use permission, environmental assessment, grid connection, and, for projects selling to a utility, a PPA. The environmental impacts of solar farms are generally modest compared with other generation technologies, but they include land use change, habitat loss, visual impacts, glare, and the use of water for cleaning panels in arid areas. Large solar projects typically require an EIA, and in some jurisdictions they benefit from streamlined permitting. Under the EU's RED III, member states must designate "renewables acceleration areas" in which renewable projects benefit from simplified and accelerated permitting, including exemption from project-level environmental assessment where a strategic environmental assessment has been carried out, and must ensure that permit-granting procedures for projects in such areas do not exceed twelve months for onshore projects. RED III also provides that the planning, construction, and operation of renewable energy plants are presumed to be in the overriding public interest for the purposes of certain environmental assessments under EU nature conservation law, until climate neutrality is achieved.

In Nigeria, utility-scale solar projects above the licensing threshold require a generation licence from NERC (or, where applicable, a state regulator), an EIA approved by the Federal Ministry of Environment, land rights secured under the Land Use Act, and a grid connection agreement with TCN or a DisCo. Projects intended to sell to the grid require a PPA with a buyer — historically NBET, and increasingly DisCos, eligible customers, or traders.

### Land Rights for Solar Projects

Utility-scale solar projects are land-intensive, requiring around one to two hectares per megawatt, depending on technology and location. Developers must secure long-term rights to the land for the life of the project (typically twenty-five to thirty-five years), sufficient to satisfy lenders. The form of these rights depends on the land tenure system: freehold purchase, long-term leasehold, rights of occupancy, easements, or concessions over public land.

In jurisdictions with customary land tenure, such as most of sub-Saharan Africa, securing land rights for solar projects raises distinctive challenges. Land may be held communally by families, clans, or communities under customary law, often without formal registration; rights may be overlapping and contested; and the identification of the persons entitled to consent to a transfer may be uncertain. In Nigeria, the **Land Use Act 1978** vests all land in each state in the governor, who may grant statutory rights of occupancy over urban land, while local governments may grant customary rights of occupancy over non-urban land. Holders of customary rights may transfer their interests only with the consent of the governor (for statutory rights) or the local government (for customary rights), and the requirement of governor's consent has been a notorious source of delay and cost. Developers of solar projects in Nigeria must therefore negotiate with customary landholders and communities, obtain consent to the transfer or grant of rights of occupancy, and register their interests, while managing the risk of competing claims.

The **Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure of Land, Fisheries and Forests** (VGGT), endorsed by the Committee on World Food Security in 2012, and **IFC Performance Standard 5** on land acquisition and involuntary resettlement provide international standards for responsible land acquisition, emphasising the recognition of legitimate tenure rights (including customary rights), consultation, the avoidance of involuntary resettlement where possible, and fair compensation. Large solar projects in Africa, such as the Noor Ouarzazate complex in Morocco, which was built on land acquired from a local collective of the Ait Oukrour Toundout community under Moroccan law on collective lands, have attracted scrutiny of the adequacy of consultation and compensation. These issues are examined further in Chapter 21.

### Agrivoltaics and Competing Land Uses

The land requirements of solar energy create potential conflicts with agriculture, particularly in densely populated areas or regions with high-quality agricultural land. Agrivoltaics — the co-location of solar panels and agricultural production, for example by mounting panels on elevated structures over crops or grazing land — offers a means of reducing conflict, and some jurisdictions have adopted specific legal frameworks for agrivoltaic projects. France's Law No 2023-175 on the acceleration of renewable energy production defined agrivoltaic installations and established conditions under which they may be located on agricultural land, requiring that they provide a direct service to agricultural production, such as improving agronomic potential or protection against hazards, and do not significantly reduce agricultural output.

### Distributed Solar and Mini-Grids

Distributed solar — rooftop systems and small ground-mounted installations — raises different legal issues: the right of consumers to install generation and connect it to the grid; net metering and compensation for exports; building regulations and fire safety standards; and the treatment of distributed generation in network tariffs. In Nigeria, NERC's Regulations on Embedded Generation (2012) and its regulatory instruments on net metering govern the connection of distributed generation to distribution networks and the treatment of exports.

**Solar mini-grids** are particularly important in Nigeria and other countries with low rates of electrification. The NERC **Mini-Grid Regulations** (2016, revised 2023) distinguish between isolated mini-grids (not connected to the distribution network) and interconnected mini-grids (connected to an existing distribution network, often to serve underserved customers), set thresholds for permits and registration, allow mini-grid operators to charge cost-reflective tariffs approved by the regulator, and address the consequences of the arrival of the main grid, including options for the mini-grid to convert to a small power distributor or to be compensated for its assets. These provisions are critical to investor confidence, because the risk of grid arrival rendering the mini-grid uneconomic is one of the principal risks of mini-grid investment.

**Stand-alone solar systems** and solar home systems, often sold on "pay-as-you-go" terms using mobile money, have brought electricity to millions of households in sub-Saharan Africa. Their legal issues include consumer protection (product quality standards, warranties, transparent pricing, and responsible debt collection), the regulation of mobile payments, import duties and taxes (several countries have exempted solar products from import duties and value added tax), and the management of electronic waste from end-of-life batteries and panels. The quality standards developed by the World Bank Group's Lighting Global programme (now VeraSol) have been widely adopted as a reference.
