## 22.3 Anti-Bribery and Anti-Corruption Frameworks

### Corruption in the Energy and Extractive Sectors

Corruption — the abuse of entrusted power for private gain — is a pervasive risk in the energy and extractive sectors. The OECD's *Foreign Bribery Report* (2014), analysing concluded enforcement actions under the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, found that the extractive sector accounted for the largest share of foreign bribery cases, around one-fifth of the total, followed by construction, transportation and storage, and information and communication. The risks arise at every stage of the value chain: in the allocation of licences and contracts, where discretionary decisions by officials are worth billions; in the award of service and supply contracts; in the obtaining of permits, customs clearances, and visas; in the marketing of state crude oil and the import of petroleum products; in the administration of fuel subsidies; in local content requirements and the selection of local partners; and in the management of revenues by NOCs and governments. The consequences include the diversion of public revenues, the distortion of investment decisions, the undermining of regulation, including safety and environmental regulation, and the entrenchment of political elites — contributing to the "resource curse" examined in Chapter 23.

### International and Regional Conventions

The international legal framework against corruption comprises several treaties:

- The **OECD Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions** (1997), in force since 1999, with forty-six parties including all OECD members and several non-members such as Argentina, Brazil, Bulgaria, Peru, Russia (whose participation was suspended in 2022), and South Africa — the only African party. It requires parties to criminalise the bribery of foreign public officials, to establish the liability of legal persons, and to impose effective, proportionate, and dissuasive sanctions. Implementation is monitored through peer review by the OECD Working Group on Bribery, and the **2021 Recommendation** of the OECD Council on further combating bribery addresses the demand side, whistleblower protection, and non-trial resolutions.
- The **United Nations Convention against Corruption** (UNCAC, 2003), in force since 2005, with around 190 parties, including Nigeria, is the only global legally binding anti-corruption instrument. It addresses prevention (including codes of conduct, public procurement, and the independence of anti-corruption bodies), criminalisation (including bribery of national and foreign public officials, embezzlement, trading in influence, abuse of functions, illicit enrichment, and money laundering), international cooperation, and **asset recovery**, establishing the return of assets as a fundamental principle (Chapter V). An Implementation Review Mechanism, established in 2009, conducts peer reviews.
- The **African Union Convention on Preventing and Combating Corruption** (2003), in force since 2006, requires parties to criminalise a range of corrupt practices, including illicit enrichment, and to adopt measures on political party funding and access to information. The **ECOWAS Protocol on the Fight against Corruption** (2001) addresses corruption in the West African region.
- The **Inter-American Convention against Corruption** (1996) and the Council of Europe's **Criminal Law Convention on Corruption** (1999), monitored by the Group of States against Corruption (GRECO), are regional instruments of similar purpose.

### The US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act

The **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act** of 1977 (FCPA), enacted in the aftermath of revelations of widespread corporate bribery abroad, was the first national law to criminalise the bribery of foreign public officials, and its vigorous enforcement since the mid-2000s has made it the most significant anti-bribery law in practice. It has two sets of provisions:

- The **anti-bribery provisions** prohibit issuers of securities registered in the United States, domestic concerns, and other persons acting within US territory from corruptly offering, paying, promising, or authorising the payment of money or anything of value to a foreign official, foreign political party, or candidate, or to any person knowing that it will be passed on to such persons, in order to influence an official act, induce a breach of lawful duty, secure an improper advantage, or obtain or retain business. Officials of state-owned enterprises, including NOCs, are "foreign officials". The provisions contain a narrow exception for **facilitating payments** to expedite routine governmental action, and affirmative defences for payments lawful under the written laws of the foreign country and for reasonable and bona fide expenditure directly related to product promotion or contract performance.
- The **accounting provisions** require issuers to make and keep books and records that accurately and fairly reflect their transactions and to devise and maintain a system of internal accounting controls. These provisions, enforced by the Securities and Exchange Commission, apply regardless of whether bribery is proved and have been used extensively.

The FCPA is enforced criminally by the Department of Justice and civilly by the SEC. Its jurisdictional reach is broad, extending to foreign companies listed in the United States and to conduct involving the use of US interstate commerce, including US dollar transactions and emails passing through US servers. Many of the largest resolutions have involved energy and resource companies. In **February 2025**, the President issued an executive order pausing new FCPA investigations and enforcement actions pending a review of enforcement policy, and the Department of Justice issued revised guidelines in June 2025, prioritising cases connected with cartels and transnational criminal organisations, cases that harm the competitiveness of US companies, and cases involving serious misconduct rather than routine business practices. The long-term effects of the change on enforcement in the energy sector remain to be seen, but the statute itself, and the limitation periods applicable to past conduct, remain in force, and other jurisdictions have continued to enforce their own laws.

::: casebox {title="The Bonny Island LNG Bribery Scheme"}
**Enforcement actions concerning the TSKJ joint venture and Nigeria LNG (United States and other jurisdictions, 2008–2011).**

**Facts.** Between 1995 and 2004, a joint venture known as **TSKJ**, comprising Technip of France, Snamprogetti Netherlands (then a subsidiary of Eni of Italy), Kellogg Brown & Root (KBR, then a subsidiary of Halliburton of the United States), and JGC Corporation of Japan, was awarded engineering, procurement, and construction contracts worth more than USD 6 billion to build liquefied natural gas facilities on Bonny Island, Nigeria, for Nigeria LNG Limited, a company in which the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation held a forty-nine per cent interest. US authorities established that the joint venture had paid around USD 180 million in bribes to Nigerian government officials, at various levels, to obtain the contracts, channelled through two agents — a United Kingdom solicitor and a Japanese trading company — under sham consultancy agreements.

**Resolutions.** In 2009, KBR pleaded guilty to FCPA violations, and KBR and Halliburton paid a combined total of USD 579 million to resolve criminal and civil charges — then the largest FCPA resolution by a US company. Technip paid USD 338 million in 2010, Snamprogetti and Eni paid USD 365 million in 2010, and JGC paid USD 218.8 million in 2011. The UK solicitor who acted as an agent was extradited to the United States, pleaded guilty, and was sentenced to imprisonment, and a former KBR chief executive pleaded guilty and was sentenced to imprisonment. In Nigeria, the federal government brought charges against companies and individuals and reached settlements with several of the companies, which agreed to pay fines in Nigeria.

**Significance.** The case — with total resolutions in the United States of around USD 1.5 billion — illustrated the vulnerability of large energy infrastructure projects to corruption, the use of agents and consultancy arrangements to disguise bribes, the exposure of joint venture partners to liability for a common scheme, and the extraterritorial reach of US enforcement. It also prompted the Nigerian authorities to seek accountability at home, raising questions about the allocation of penalties between the home states of the companies and the state whose officials were bribed — a theme in debates about the return of penalties to affected countries.

### The UK Bribery Act 2010

The **Bribery Act 2010** of the United Kingdom, in force since July 2011, is widely regarded as among the strictest anti-bribery laws. It creates offences of bribing another person (section 1), being bribed (section 2), and bribing a foreign public official (section 6), which apply to private-to-private bribery as well as bribery of officials and contain no exception for facilitating payments. Section 7 creates a strict liability corporate offence of **failure to prevent bribery**: a relevant commercial organisation — a body incorporated in or carrying on business in the United Kingdom — is guilty of an offence if a person associated with it (including employees, agents, and subsidiaries performing services for it) bribes another person intending to obtain or retain business or an advantage in the conduct of business for the organisation. The only defence is to prove that the organisation had in place **adequate procedures** designed to prevent persons associated with it from undertaking such conduct. The Ministry of Justice's Guidance (2011) identifies six principles for adequate procedures, examined in section 22.8.

The Bribery Act has been enforced principally through **deferred prosecution agreements** (DPAs), introduced by the Crime and Courts Act 2013, examined in section 22.7. In 2022, the Serious Fraud Office secured a conviction of **Glencore Energy UK** for bribery in connection with oil operations in Africa, including in Nigeria, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Equatorial Guinea, and South Sudan, resulting in a penalty of around £281 million, following Glencore's guilty pleas in the United States and Brazil earlier that year in resolutions exceeding USD 1.1 billion. The **Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023** extended the "failure to prevent" model to a new offence of **failure to prevent fraud** by large organisations, in force from September 2025, and reformed the identification doctrine for attributing criminal liability to companies, making it easier to attribute the conduct of senior managers to the company for economic crimes.

### Other Jurisdictions

France's **Sapin II Law** (2016) established the French Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA), which oversees mandatory compliance programmes for large companies, and introduced the *convention judiciaire d'intérêt public* (CJIP), a non-trial resolution similar to a DPA. Brazil's **Clean Company Act** (2013), adopted in the context of the *Lava Jato* investigation, imposes strict civil and administrative liability on companies for corrupt acts against foreign or domestic public administrations. Commodity trading companies have been the subject of a series of enforcement actions for bribery in connection with the purchase of crude oil and products from NOCs, including resolutions by **Vitol** (2020), **Glencore** (2022), **Trafigura** (2024), and **Gunvor** (2024) in the United States, Brazil, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom, highlighting the risks of the oil trading business.

### Nigerian Anti-Corruption Law

Nigeria's anti-corruption framework comprises several statutes and institutions. The **Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Act 2000** established the **Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission** (ICPC) and criminalises bribery, the corrupt demanding of benefits by officials, fraudulent acquisition of property, and related offences. The **Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (Establishment) Act 2004** established the **Economic and Financial Crimes Commission** (EFCC), with broad powers to investigate and prosecute economic and financial crimes, including money laundering and advance fee fraud. The **Code of Conduct Bureau and Tribunal Act** and the Fifth Schedule to the Constitution require public officers to declare their assets and prohibit conflicts of interest. The **Money Laundering (Prevention and Prohibition) Act 2022** and the **Proceeds of Crime (Recovery and Management) Act 2022** strengthened the anti-money laundering and asset recovery framework, partly in response to evaluations by the Financial Action Task Force, which placed Nigeria on its list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring in 2023 — a listing from which Nigeria was removed in October 2025 after completing its action plan.

Corruption in Nigeria's petroleum sector has been documented in numerous official reports, including the reports of the **Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative** (NEITI), the Ribadu-led Petroleum Revenue Special Task Force (2012), and the investigations into the administration of the fuel subsidy regime, which revealed large-scale fraud in subsidy claims in 2011–2012. The **OPL 245** affair — concerning the award of a prolific offshore block to Shell and Eni in 2011 for USD 1.3 billion, most of which was paid via a government escrow account to Malabu Oil and Gas, a company associated with a former petroleum minister — led to prosecutions in Italy, in which the companies and individual defendants were acquitted in 2021, a decision that became final in 2022, and to proceedings in Nigeria and an arbitration brought by Eni against Nigeria under the Netherlands–Nigeria bilateral investment treaty. The affair illustrates the complexity of corruption cases involving licence awards, the difficulty of proof across jurisdictions, and the interaction of criminal, civil, and investment proceedings. A former minister of petroleum resources was charged in the United Kingdom in 2023 with bribery offences in connection with the award of oil contracts, in proceedings that were continuing.

### Asset Recovery

The recovery and return of the proceeds of corruption is an important dimension of anti-corruption law for resource-rich states. Nigeria has been a leading participant in asset recovery, notably with respect to funds attributed to the former military head of state General Sani Abacha. Under agreements with Switzerland, the United States, Jersey, and others, and with the involvement of the World Bank in monitoring, several hundred million dollars have been returned to Nigeria since the early 2000s, including USD 322 million returned by Switzerland under an agreement of 2017 and disbursed through a conditional cash transfer programme, and USD 308 million returned by the United States and Jersey under an agreement of 2020 for infrastructure projects. The **Global Forum on Asset Recovery Principles** (2017) provide guidance on the transparent and accountable return of assets.

## 22.4 Beneficial Ownership and Corporate Transparency

### The Problem of Anonymous Ownership

Anonymous companies, trusts, and other legal arrangements are used to conceal the identity of those who ultimately own or control assets, facilitating corruption, money laundering, tax evasion, and sanctions evasion. In the extractive sector, investigations have repeatedly found that licences and contracts were awarded to companies whose undisclosed beneficial owners included politically exposed persons — public officials, their family members, and close associates. The Natural Resource Governance Institute's analysis of such cases has identified the use of secret ownership as a central mechanism of extractive sector corruption. **Beneficial ownership transparency** — the identification and disclosure of the natural persons who ultimately own or control legal entities — is therefore a key anti-corruption measure.

### International Standards

The **Financial Action Task Force** (FATF), the intergovernmental body that sets international standards on anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism, requires countries to ensure that competent authorities have access to adequate, accurate, and up-to-date information on the beneficial ownership and control of legal persons (**Recommendation 24**) and legal arrangements such as trusts (**Recommendation 25**). Recommendation 24 was revised in 2022 to require countries to use a multi-pronged approach including a public authority or body holding beneficial ownership information, such as a central register, and Recommendation 25 was revised in 2023. Financial institutions and designated non-financial businesses and professions must identify and verify the beneficial owners of their customers as part of customer due diligence, and apply enhanced due diligence to politically exposed persons.

The **Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative** (EITI), launched in 2003, is a global standard for the disclosure of information along the extractive industry value chain, implemented by more than fifty countries through multi-stakeholder groups of government, companies, and civil society. Under the **EITI Standard**, implementing countries must disclose information on the legal framework and fiscal regime, licence allocations, contracts, production, exports, state participation, revenue collection, revenue allocation, and social and economic spending. Since January 2020, the Standard has required implementing countries to request, and companies to disclose, the **beneficial owners** of companies that apply for or hold participating interests in exploration or production licences or contracts, including the identity of any politically exposed persons. Since January 2021, it has required the disclosure of the full text of contracts and licences granted, entered into, or amended from that date. The 2023 EITI Standard added requirements concerning the energy transition, gender, and anti-corruption.

**Nigeria** was among the first countries to implement the EITI, enacting the **Nigeria Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative Act 2007**, which made NEITI a statutory body and requires audits of the oil, gas, and mining sectors — the first EITI implementing country to give the initiative a legal foundation. NEITI's audits have identified significant revenue discrepancies and unremitted funds and have contributed to policy reform. NEITI has published a register of beneficial owners in the extractive sector and has advocated contract transparency. **CAMA 2020** requires companies to disclose **persons with significant control** — those holding, directly or indirectly, at least a prescribed percentage of shares or voting rights, or otherwise exercising significant influence or control — to the Corporate Affairs Commission, and to maintain a register of such persons, and the Commission launched a public electronic register of persons with significant control. The **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** contains requirements for the disclosure of information on the beneficial owners of applicants for and holders of petroleum licences and leases, and for the publication of licences, leases, and contracts, building on NEITI's work.

### National Registers and the Debate over Public Access

The **United Kingdom** introduced a register of **people with significant control** in 2016, making it the first G20 country to establish a public central register of beneficial ownership, and later a register of overseas entities owning UK property under the Economic Crime (Transparency and Enforcement) Act 2022. The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduced identity verification for directors and persons with significant control. The **European Union**'s Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive (2018) required member states to provide public access to beneficial ownership registers, but in ***WM and Sovim SA v Luxembourg Business Registers*** (Joined Cases C-37/20 and C-601/20, November 2022), the Court of Justice of the European Union held that general public access constituted a serious interference with the rights to privacy and the protection of personal data under the Charter of Fundamental Rights that was not limited to what was strictly necessary. The EU's anti-money laundering package of 2024 provides for access by persons with a legitimate interest, including journalists and civil society organisations. In the **United States**, the **Corporate Transparency Act** of 2021 required many companies to report beneficial ownership information to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, but following litigation, FinCEN issued an interim final rule in March 2025 exempting US companies and US persons from reporting requirements, limiting the obligation to certain foreign companies registered to do business in the United States.

### Payments-to-Governments Reporting

Complementing EITI, several jurisdictions require extractive companies to publish the payments they make to governments on a country-by-country and project-by-project basis, enabling citizens to follow the money. The **EU Accounting Directive** (2013/34/EU, Chapter 10) and the **Transparency Directive**, as amended in 2013, require large EU and EU-listed extractive and logging companies to report payments to governments above a threshold, broken down by country and project, including taxes, royalties, dividends, signature and production bonuses, licence fees, and infrastructure payments. The United Kingdom implemented these requirements through the Reports on Payments to Governments Regulations 2014, and Norway and Canada adopted similar rules — Canada through the Extractive Sector Transparency Measures Act 2015. In the United States, **section 1504 of the Dodd-Frank Act** directed the SEC to adopt payment disclosure rules; after its first rule was vacated by a court in 2013 and its second was disapproved by Congress in 2017 under the Congressional Review Act, the SEC adopted a third rule in December 2020, with a narrower definition of "project" and broader exemptions.

### Commodity Trading Transparency

Payments by commodity traders to governments and NOCs for the purchase of crude oil, which may represent the largest single revenue stream for some producer governments, were initially outside the scope of payment reporting rules. The EITI encourages the disclosure of revenues from the sale of the state's share of production, and the **Swiss** government, home to many major commodity traders, has considered but not yet adopted mandatory disclosure of payments by traders. Several trading companies voluntarily disclose payments to governments. The series of enforcement actions against traders for bribery, noted in section 22.3, has increased pressure for greater transparency in this segment of the oil value chain.
