## 9.6 Cross-Border Electricity Trading

### Benefits and Challenges

Interconnecting national electricity systems offers substantial benefits. Interconnection enables countries to share generation reserves, reducing the capacity each must maintain to ensure security of supply; to exploit differences in generation costs, resource endowments, and demand patterns, so that low-cost hydropower in one country can displace expensive thermal generation in another; to integrate variable renewable generation over larger areas, smoothing fluctuations in wind and solar output; and to provide mutual assistance in emergencies. For small developing countries, regional integration can make possible the development of large, low-cost generation projects that would be too large for any single national market.

Cross-border electricity trade nonetheless faces significant obstacles: the physical characteristics of electricity, which require close coordination of system operation across borders; differences in market structures, regulatory frameworks, and technical standards; concerns about dependence on foreign supply and the loss of national control; the creditworthiness of buyers; and the need to agree on the allocation of interconnector costs and capacity. The legal frameworks for cross-border trade range from bilateral agreements between utilities, through regional power pools with common rules, to fully integrated supranational markets.

### The European Union Internal Electricity Market

The European Union has developed the world's most advanced framework for cross-border electricity trade. Regulation (EU) 2019/943 and the network codes and guidelines adopted under it establish common rules for cross-border trading, capacity allocation, and congestion management. The **Capacity Allocation and Congestion Management Guideline** (Regulation (EU) 2015/1222) established **single day-ahead coupling** (SDAC) and **single intraday coupling** (SIDC), under which cross-border transmission capacity is allocated implicitly together with energy through a common price coupling algorithm (EUPHEMIA), maximising the use of interconnectors and producing price convergence across bidding zones when capacity is not congested. Regulation (EU) 2019/943 requires TSOs to make available for cross-border trade at least seventy per cent of the transmission capacity of interconnectors and internal critical network elements, subject to transitional arrangements. ACER oversees the implementation of these rules, decides on certain cross-border matters where national regulators cannot agree, and monitors the market. The EU has also set an interconnection target of fifteen per cent of installed generation capacity for each member state by 2030.

Following the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union, Great Britain left the EU's internal electricity market and market coupling arrangements. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement of 2020 contains an energy title that provides for cooperation on electricity trading over interconnectors and commits the parties to develop new arrangements for the efficient use of interconnectors, although the more efficient implicit allocation of capacity has not yet been restored. The episode illustrates both the depth of integration achieved within the EU and the costs of exit.

### The West African Power Pool

The **West African Power Pool** (WAPP) was established by Decision A/DEC.5/12/99 of the ECOWAS Authority of Heads of State and Government in 1999, and its Articles of Agreement were adopted in 2006, establishing it as a specialised institution of ECOWAS. The WAPP's mission is to integrate the national power systems of the fourteen continental ECOWAS member states into a unified regional electricity market, with the objective of providing reliable and affordable electricity. Its members are the national utilities, transmission companies, and generation companies of member states. The WAPP has developed a master plan of priority regional generation and transmission projects, including the **CLSG interconnection** linking Côte d'Ivoire, Liberia, Sierra Leone, and Guinea, the **OMVG interconnection** linking the Gambia River Basin Development Organisation states (Gambia, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, and Senegal), and the **North Core** project linking Nigeria, Niger, Benin, Togo, and Burkina Faso.

The **ECOWAS Regional Electricity Regulatory Authority** (ERERA), established in 2008 by a Supplementary Act of the ECOWAS Authority, is the regional regulator, responsible for regulating cross-border electricity exchanges among member states, approving regional market rules, setting tariffs for regional transmission, and resolving disputes. The **ECOWAS Regional Electricity Market** was launched in phases, with the first phase commencing in 2018 on the basis of bilateral contracts and regional market rules approved by ERERA, and later phases envisaging a day-ahead market. The legal framework is supported by the ECOWAS Energy Protocol of 2003, examined in Chapter 6, and by the ECOWAS directive on the organisation of the regional electricity market.

Nigeria has historically been a significant exporter of electricity to neighbouring states, supplying Benin, Togo, and Niger under bilateral contracts. In August 2023, following the military coup in Niger and the imposition of sanctions by ECOWAS, Nigeria suspended electricity supply to Niger, illustrating the vulnerability of cross-border electricity trade to political crises and the use of energy interdependence as an instrument of regional pressure. Supply was restored in 2024 after ECOWAS lifted most sanctions. Nigeria's own domestic supply shortages have periodically generated political pressure to reduce exports, and the payment performance of foreign buyers has been a recurrent concern.

### Other Regional Power Pools

The **Southern African Power Pool** (SAPP), established in 1995 under an intergovernmental memorandum of understanding among member states of the Southern African Development Community (SADC), was the first regional power pool in Africa. It operates competitive day-ahead, intraday, and forward markets alongside bilateral contracts, and the **Regional Electricity Regulators Association of Southern Africa** (RERA) facilitates regulatory harmonisation. The **Eastern Africa Power Pool** (EAPP), established in 2005, and the **Central African Power Pool** (CAPP), established in 2003, are at earlier stages of development, while the **Comité Maghrébin de l'Électricité** coordinates North African interconnection.

At the continental level, the **African Single Electricity Market** (AfSEM), launched by the African Union in 2021 with the support of the European Union, aims to integrate the regional power pools into a single continental market by 2040, through a continental transmission master plan, harmonised regulatory frameworks, and continental market rules. The **Continental Power System Master Plan**, adopted in 2023, identifies priority interconnections. The AfSEM is linked to the African Continental Free Trade Area, which may provide a legal framework for trade in electricity services.

Elsewhere, the **ASEAN Power Grid**, established under ASEAN agreements and memoranda of understanding, has developed through bilateral interconnections and the Lao PDR–Thailand–Malaysia–Singapore Power Integration Project, the first multilateral electricity trade in ASEAN. The **Gulf Cooperation Council Interconnection Authority** (GCCIA) operates an interconnection linking the six GCC states, used principally for mutual support in emergencies. In South Asia, India's cross-border electricity trade with Bhutan, Nepal, and Bangladesh is governed by bilateral agreements and Indian regulations on cross-border trade.

### International Law and Cross-Border Electricity

The international law governing cross-border electricity trade is relatively undeveloped. Electricity is generally treated as a good for the purposes of GATT 1994, although the classification of electricity transmission and distribution services under the General Agreement on Trade in Services is uncertain. The Energy Charter Treaty's transit provisions, examined in Chapter 6, apply to electricity as well as to gas. Regional frameworks such as the ECOWAS Energy Protocol and the EU legislation provide the most developed rules. The legal issues raised by long-distance interconnections — such as the proposed Xlinks project to transmit solar and wind power from Morocco to Great Britain through submarine cables, and the proposed interconnections between North Africa and Europe — include the regulatory treatment of interconnectors, the allocation of costs and capacity, security of supply, and the law of submarine cables, examined in Chapter 20.

## 9.7 Consumer Protection and Energy Affordability

### Electricity as an Essential Service

Electricity is an essential service, without which households cannot light their homes, cook, heat or cool their dwellings, store food and medicines, communicate, study, or work. Access to affordable and reliable electricity is closely connected to the realisation of human rights, including the rights to an adequate standard of living, to housing, to health, and to education, as examined in Chapter 23. Sustainable Development Goal 7 calls for universal access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy by 2030. Electricity consumers are, moreover, in a structurally weak position relative to suppliers: they depend on a continuous supply that they cannot easily replace, they face complex contracts and tariffs, and in many markets they have little or no choice of supplier. Electricity law therefore contains extensive provisions for consumer protection, which have become more important as liberalisation has exposed consumers to market risks and as the energy price crisis of 2021–2023 created severe affordability pressures.

### Consumer Rights in EU Law

Directive (EU) 2019/944 establishes a comprehensive set of consumer rights, including: the right to a contract with specified information; the right to switch supplier within three weeks (and, from 2026, within twenty-four hours) free of charge; the right to clear billing information, at least once a year based on actual consumption; the right to a dynamic electricity price contract for consumers with smart meters; the right to request a smart meter; access to out-of-court dispute settlement mechanisms; and protection for vulnerable customers and against disconnection. Member states must define the concept of vulnerable customers, which may refer to energy poverty, and ensure that the rights and obligations linked to vulnerable customers are applied, including measures to prohibit disconnection in critical times. The 2024 market design reform strengthened these rights, including by giving consumers the right to fixed-price, fixed-term contracts and requiring suppliers to have appropriate hedging strategies to limit the risk that supplier failures harm consumers, and by establishing a framework for suppliers of last resort.

### Price Regulation for Households

Many jurisdictions regulate retail electricity prices for households, either through comprehensive tariff regulation (in non-liberalised systems) or through price caps in liberalised markets. In Great Britain, the Domestic Gas and Electricity (Tariff Cap) Act 2018 required Ofgem to impose a cap on standard variable and default tariffs, which has been adjusted periodically by reference to wholesale and other costs. During the 2022 crisis, the cap rose dramatically, and the government introduced the **Energy Price Guarantee**, which limited the unit rates paid by households below the level of the cap, with the government compensating suppliers for the difference. In the European Union, the Court of Justice held in *Federutility* (Case C-265/08, 2010) that public intervention in the price of supply of natural gas to final consumers is compatible with the internal market provided it pursues a general economic interest, is proportionate, and complies with conditions of transparency, non-discrimination, and limited duration; the reasoning has been applied to electricity, and the 2019 Directive codified the conditions under which member states may regulate prices for households and microenterprises, including energy poor and vulnerable households.

### Consumer Protection in Nigeria

In Nigeria, consumer protection in the electricity sector has centred on metering, billing, quality of supply, and complaint handling. A large proportion of electricity consumers have historically been unmetered and subjected to **estimated billing**, which consumers regard as arbitrary and excessive. NERC has adopted a series of measures to address the metering gap, including the Meter Asset Provider Regulations (2018, revised), which permit independent meter asset providers to finance and install meters; the National Mass Metering Programme, launched in 2020; and orders capping estimated bills for unmetered customers by reference to the consumption of metered customers on the same feeder. The NERC Customer Service Standards of Performance for Distribution Companies, and the Customer Complaints Handling Standards and Procedures, establish timelines for addressing complaints, connecting new customers, and restoring supply, with compensation for failures. Consumers may escalate complaints to NERC Forum Offices, which operate as a quasi-judicial dispute resolution mechanism. The Electricity Act 2023 contains provisions on consumer protection and empowers the regulator to establish rules on customer rights and obligations, and the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018 applies to the electricity sector.

The service-based tariff structure introduced by NERC, in which tariffs vary according to the hours of supply received by each feeder band, links consumer payments to service quality, and NERC has imposed sanctions on DisCos that failed to deliver the minimum hours of supply to Band A feeders. The effectiveness of these measures depends on the regulator's capacity to monitor service delivery and enforce compliance.

### Disconnection and the Right to Electricity

The disconnection of electricity supply for non-payment raises acute questions of consumer protection and human rights. Many jurisdictions restrict disconnection: prohibiting it during winter months or extreme heat, for households with vulnerable members (such as those dependent on medical equipment), or without adequate notice and opportunity to pay. In South Africa, the courts have developed a body of jurisprudence on the procedural fairness of electricity disconnections by municipalities. In *Joseph v City of Johannesburg* (2009), the Constitutional Court held that tenants who were not parties to the supply contract between their landlord and the municipality were nonetheless entitled to procedural fairness, including adequate notice, before their supply was disconnected, because the municipality's obligation to provide basic services gave rise to a public law relationship with the users of those services.[^joseph] The Court's reasoning rests on the constitutional duty of local government to ensure the provision of services to communities in a sustainable manner.

[^joseph]: *Joseph v City of Johannesburg* [2009] ZACC 30, 2010 (4) SA 55 (CC).

The prolonged "load shedding" — scheduled rotational power cuts — imposed by South Africa's state utility Eskom because of generation shortfalls also gave rise to litigation. In *United Democratic Movement v Eskom Holdings SOC Ltd* (2023), the High Court in Pretoria held that the state's failure to address the impact of load shedding on hospitals, clinics, schools, and police stations infringed constitutional rights, and ordered the Minister of Public Enterprises to take steps to ensure sufficient supply to such facilities. The case illustrates the potential of constitutional litigation to translate the human rights dimensions of electricity supply into enforceable obligations, a theme examined in Chapters 23 and 27.

### Energy Poverty and Social Tariffs

Energy poverty — the inability of households to secure adequate energy services at affordable cost — affects households in both developed and developing countries. Legal and policy responses include social or "lifeline" tariffs, under which low-income or low-consumption households pay reduced prices for an initial block of consumption; direct income support; energy efficiency programmes targeting low-income households; and protections against disconnection. Nigeria's tariff structure has included a lifeline tariff for residential customers with very low consumption. The EU's Social Climate Fund, established by Regulation (EU) 2023/955 to address the social impacts of the extension of carbon pricing to buildings and road transport, will support vulnerable households from 2026. The design of social tariffs must balance the goal of protecting vulnerable consumers against the risks of subsidising consumption by better-off households and undermining the financial viability of utilities. Energy poverty and universal access are examined further in Chapter 23.
