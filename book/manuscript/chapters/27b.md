## 27.3 Judicial Review of Licensing Decisions

### Licensing Decisions as a Focus of Litigation

The grant, renewal, transfer, and revocation of licences, leases, and consents for energy and resource projects are among the most frequently litigated decisions in energy law. Licensing decisions determine who may explore for and produce resources, build power plants and pipelines, and operate in regulated markets; they shape the environmental and social impacts of projects; and they allocate valuable rights. They may be challenged by unsuccessful applicants, by licence holders whose licences are revoked or conditioned, by communities affected by projects, and by environmental organisations seeking to prevent fossil fuel development. The grounds of review — summarised in Figure 27.1 — and the procedures for challenge determine the extent to which courts can hold licensing authorities to account.

### The Grounds of Judicial Review

In the common law tradition, the grounds of judicial review were classified by Lord Diplock in ***Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service*** (1985) as **illegality**, **irrationality**, and **procedural impropriety**, with the possibility of adding **proportionality**. **Illegality** encompasses acting outside the powers conferred by statute, misdirection in law, failing to take into account relevant considerations or taking into account irrelevant ones, and using powers for an improper purpose. **Irrationality**, or *Wednesbury* unreasonableness (*Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation*, 1948), refers to a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have reached it. **Procedural impropriety** covers failure to observe statutory procedures and breaches of the rules of natural justice, including the right to be heard and the rule against bias. The doctrine of **legitimate expectation** protects expectations generated by a public authority's clear and unambiguous representations or established practice, procedurally and, in some circumstances, substantively, unless frustrating the expectation is justified by an overriding public interest. Where fundamental rights are engaged, courts apply a **proportionality** analysis. Civil law systems apply analogous grounds through administrative courts, often with a more developed proportionality doctrine.

In **Nigeria**, judicial review of administrative action is available in the Federal High Court and State High Courts, and the courts apply grounds derived from English administrative law, as well as constitutional grounds, including the right to fair hearing under section 36 of the Constitution. The revocation of oil mining leases and licences has been challenged in several cases, and the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 specifies the grounds and procedures for the revocation of licences and leases, including the requirement of notice and an opportunity to remedy defaults, providing a statutory basis for review. The controversy over the **OPL 245** block, examined in Chapter 22, generated litigation in Nigeria, Italy, the United Kingdom, and the United States, as well as arbitration, illustrating the multiple legal consequences of a contested licensing decision.

### Environmental Assessment and Licensing

Many of the most significant licensing challenges concern the adequacy of **environmental impact assessment**, examined in Chapter 17. Courts have quashed consents where the assessment failed to consider relevant environmental effects, alternatives, or cumulative impacts, or where the public was not adequately consulted. The question whether the assessment of fossil fuel projects must include **downstream (scope 3) emissions** from the combustion of the extracted fuel has become a central issue in climate-related licensing litigation.

In ***R (Finch) v Surrey County Council*** (UK Supreme Court, June 2024), the Supreme Court held, by a majority of three to two, that the environmental impact assessment for a project to extend oil production from a well site in Surrey was required, under the Town and Country Planning (Environmental Impact Assessment) Regulations 2017, implementing the EU EIA Directive, to assess the greenhouse gas emissions from the combustion of the oil to be produced, because those emissions were an inevitable consequence of the project and their extent could be readily estimated. The grant of planning permission was therefore unlawful. *Finch* has had far-reaching consequences. In September 2024, the government conceded a challenge to the consent for a new coal mine in **Cumbria** (Whitehaven), and the High Court quashed it. In **January 2025**, the Court of Session in Scotland held, in *Greenpeace Ltd v Advocate General* and a related case brought by Uplift, that the development consents for the **Rosebank** and **Jackdaw** oil and gas fields in the North Sea were unlawful because the environmental assessments had not addressed scope 3 emissions, and set them aside, while permitting work to continue pending reconsideration. The government issued new guidance in June 2025 on the assessment of downstream emissions for offshore projects, under which the operators submitted revised environmental statements for reconsideration.

Courts in other jurisdictions have reached similar conclusions. In ***Gloucester Resources Ltd v Minister for Planning*** (Land and Environment Court of New South Wales, 2019), Preston CJ upheld the refusal of consent for the Rocky Hill open-cut coal mine, finding that its scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions would contribute to climate change and that the mine was in the "wrong place at the wrong time". In ***Earthlife Africa Johannesburg v Minister of Environmental Affairs*** (High Court of South Africa, 2017), the court held that the environmental authorisation for the Thabametsi coal-fired power station was unlawful because the authority had failed to consider a climate change impact assessment, which it held to be a relevant consideration under the National Environmental Management Act. In Norway, the Oslo District Court in 2024 invalidated the approval of three oil and gas fields for failure to assess combustion emissions, and the question was referred to the EFTA Court, which in 2025 confirmed that combustion emissions must be assessed under the EIA Directive. By contrast, courts in the United States have reached varying conclusions on the scope of NEPA reviews, and the Supreme Court in *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v Eagle County* (2025), examined in Chapter 24, emphasised deference to agencies in defining the scope of their environmental reviews and held that NEPA does not require agencies to assess the effects of separate upstream and downstream projects.

### Consultation and Indigenous Rights

Licensing decisions may also be challenged for failure to consult affected communities and indigenous peoples. In ***Santos NA Barossa v Tipakalippa*** (Full Federal Court of Australia, 2022), the court upheld the decision setting aside the approval of an environment plan for offshore drilling because the regulator could not have been satisfied that the operator had consulted all relevant persons, including the traditional owners of the Tiwi Islands, whose functions, interests, or activities might be affected. In ***Sustaining the Wild Coast NPC v Minister of Mineral Resources and Energy*** (High Court of South Africa, Makhanda, 2022), the court set aside the exploration right granted to Shell and Impact Africa for seismic surveys off the Wild Coast of the Eastern Cape, on the grounds that the consultation process had been inadequate, particularly with the affected communities whose customary rights and spiritual and cultural relationship with the ocean were at stake, and that the decision-maker had failed to consider relevant factors, including the climate impacts of oil and gas development. Courts in Canada have repeatedly quashed approvals of pipelines and energy projects for failure to fulfil the Crown's **duty to consult and accommodate** indigenous peoples, as in the Federal Court of Appeal's decision in ***Tsleil-Waututh Nation v Canada*** (2018) quashing the approval of the Trans Mountain pipeline expansion, which was subsequently re-approved after further consultation. These cases, examined in Chapter 21, demonstrate the importance of consultation as a ground of review in resource licensing.

### Remedies in Licensing Challenges

The remedies available in licensing challenges include **quashing orders** setting aside the decision, **mandatory orders** requiring the authority to perform its duty, **prohibiting orders**, **declarations**, and, in some systems, **suspension** of the decision pending judgment. Courts have discretion to refuse relief, for example where the outcome would inevitably have been the same, or to suspend the effect of a quashing order to allow the authority to remedy the defect, as in the Rosebank and Jackdaw litigation. The **timing** of challenges is important: judicial review claims must generally be brought promptly, and in England within six weeks for planning decisions, and courts may refuse relief if a claimant has delayed. Licensing challenges also raise issues of **costs**, which may deter claimants; the **Aarhus Convention**, examined in Chapter 21, requires that procedures in environmental matters be not "prohibitively expensive", leading to costs-capping rules in the United Kingdom and other parties.

## 27.4 Environmental and Climate Litigation

### Scope of this Section

Chapter 18 examined climate litigation as a source and driver of climate law, surveying the landmark cases against governments and corporations and their substantive holdings. This section focuses on the **litigation process** — the procedural and evidential questions that determine whether environmental and climate claims succeed: standing, the choice of legal basis and forum, evidence and causation, the role of science, and litigation strategy.

### Typology of Environmental and Climate Cases

Environmental and climate cases in the energy sector may be classified as: (a) **framework cases** challenging the overall adequacy of governmental climate policies, such as *Urgenda*, *Neubauer*, and *KlimaSeniorinnen*; (b) **project cases** challenging the approval of specific fossil fuel projects, such as *Finch* and the Rosebank litigation; (c) **corporate cases** seeking to impose obligations on companies to reduce emissions, such as ***Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell***, in which the Hague District Court ordered Shell in 2021 to reduce its global emissions by forty-five per cent by 2030, a decision reversed by the Hague Court of Appeal in November 2024, which held that Shell has a duty to reduce emissions but that there was insufficient consensus to impose a specific percentage reduction; (d) **damages cases** seeking compensation for climate harms from fossil fuel producers, such as the claims brought by US states and municipalities against oil companies under state law, and ***Lliuya v RWE***, in which a Peruvian farmer sought a contribution from RWE towards the costs of protecting his home from a glacial lake outburst flood; (e) **"greenwashing" cases** challenging misleading environmental claims, examined in Chapter 22; (f) **disclosure and fiduciary duty cases**, such as *ClientEarth v Shell's Directors* (2023), examined in Chapter 22; and (g) **pollution cases** seeking remedies for local environmental harm, such as the oil pollution cases in the Niger Delta.

### Standing

**Standing** (*locus standi*) — the right to bring a claim — is a threshold barrier in environmental litigation, because environmental harms are often diffuse, affecting the public at large rather than particular individuals. Approaches to standing vary widely. In the **United States**, Article III of the Constitution requires a plaintiff to show an injury in fact that is concrete and particularised, fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, and likely to be redressed by a favourable decision (*Lujan v Defenders of Wildlife*, 1992), a requirement that has defeated many climate claims, although in *Massachusetts v EPA* (2007) the Supreme Court accepted that a state had standing given its "special solicitude" as a sovereign and the injury to its coastal land. In the **United Kingdom**, a claimant for judicial review must have a "sufficient interest" in the matter, which courts have interpreted liberally to allow environmental organisations to bring public interest claims (*R v Inspectorate of Pollution, ex p Greenpeace (No 2)*, 1994). The **Aarhus Convention** requires parties to ensure that members of the public concerned, including environmental NGOs meeting requirements under national law, have access to justice in environmental matters. The European Court of Human Rights in *KlimaSeniorinnen* developed a distinctive approach, recognising the standing of associations representing persons affected by climate change while imposing stringent requirements for individual victim status.

In **Nigeria**, the traditional restrictive approach to standing, articulated in ***Adesanya v President of the Federal Republic of Nigeria*** (1981), required a claimant to show that his civil rights and obligations had been or were in danger of being violated, which often defeated public interest environmental claims. The Supreme Court's decision in ***Centre for Oil Pollution Watch v NNPC*** (2018) marked a decisive shift, holding that an NGO dedicated to environmental protection had standing to sue for the remediation of an oil spill affecting a community's water supply, and emphasising that environmental protection is a matter of public interest and that the restrictive approach would leave environmental wrongs without remedy. The Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules 2009 also liberalise standing in human rights matters. At the regional level, the **ECOWAS Community Court of Justice** held in ***SERAP v Nigeria*** (2012) that an NGO could bring a claim concerning the failure of the Nigerian government to prevent and remedy oil pollution in the Niger Delta, finding a violation of the right to a satisfactory environment under Article 24 of the African Charter and ordering the government to take measures to restore the environment and prevent future damage.

### Evidence, Causation, and Attribution Science

Environmental and climate litigation raises formidable questions of **evidence and causation**. In pollution cases, claimants must prove that the defendant's activities caused the pollution and that the pollution caused the claimed harm — often difficult where there are multiple potential sources, as in the Niger Delta, where spills may result from operational failures, corrosion, or sabotage and theft. The **burden of proof** of the cause of spills has been a central issue: under the Nigerian Oil Pipelines Act, an operator is not liable for damage caused by the malicious act of a third person, and companies have frequently attributed spills to sabotage, a claim contested by communities. In ***Four Nigerian Farmers and Milieudefensie v Shell*** (Hague Court of Appeal, January 2021), examined in section 27.8, the court held that, where Shell's Nigerian subsidiary alleged sabotage, it bore the burden of proving sabotage beyond reasonable doubt, and that it had failed to do so in respect of two of the spills. Joint fact-finding and independent investigations of spills, as recommended by the UNEP **Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland** (2011), can reduce disputes over causation.

In climate cases, the causal chain between a defendant's emissions and particular harms is long and diffuse. **Attribution science** — the scientific study of the extent to which climate change has influenced specific extreme weather events and the contribution of particular emitters to climate change — has advanced rapidly and is increasingly invoked in litigation. Studies attributing a quantifiable share of historical emissions to specific fossil fuel producers ("carbon majors"), and studies estimating the influence of climate change on the probability and intensity of particular events, provide a basis for claims that particular companies have contributed to particular harms. In ***Lliuya v RWE***, the Higher Regional Court of Hamm held in **May 2025**, after taking expert evidence including a site inspection in Peru, that the claim failed because the risk to the claimant's property from a glacial lake outburst flood was too low to justify relief, but it accepted in principle that a major emitter could be liable under German law for its proportionate contribution to climate-related risks — a significant recognition of the legal relevance of attribution. Courts in the United States have grappled with the removal of state law climate claims to federal court and with defences of federal displacement and pre-emption, and the progress of these cases through state courts will test the use of attribution evidence at trial.

### The Role of Science and Expert Evidence

Environmental and climate litigation relies heavily on **scientific evidence** — on the reports of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, which courts in *Urgenda*, *Neubauer*, and *KlimaSeniorinnen* treated as authoritative statements of the scientific consensus; on carbon budgets; on environmental monitoring and modelling; and on expert testimony on pollution, health impacts, and remediation. The treatment of scientific evidence raises questions about the expertise of courts, the admissibility of expert evidence (governed in the United States by the *Daubert* standard), and the use of court-appointed experts. The **precautionary principle**, examined in Chapter 17, may affect the burden and standard of proof where there is scientific uncertainty about environmental risks.

### Litigation Strategy

Strategic environmental and climate litigation is often pursued by NGOs and communities as part of broader campaigns for legal and policy change, with objectives that include not only winning particular cases but also establishing legal principles, generating public attention, influencing corporate and governmental behaviour, and building movements. Litigation strategy involves choices about the **claimants** (individuals, communities, NGOs, youth, or local governments), the **defendants** (governments, regulators, parent companies, subsidiaries, financiers, or directors), the **legal basis** (constitutional rights, human rights treaties, tort, administrative law, company law, consumer protection, or securities law), the **forum** (domestic courts, regional human rights courts, UN treaty bodies, OECD National Contact Points, or international courts), and the **remedies** (declarations, injunctions, damages, or orders for policy change). The proliferation of climate cases — more than 2,500 cases have been recorded worldwide by the Sabin Center for Climate Change Law at Columbia University — and their diffusion across jurisdictions reflect a transnational network of litigants, lawyers, and scientists. At the same time, companies and governments have also used litigation strategically, including through **strategic lawsuits against public participation** (SLAPPs) against activists and NGOs, as in the defamation and racketeering claims brought by Energy Transfer against Greenpeace in North Dakota, which resulted in a jury verdict of more than USD 660 million against Greenpeace entities in March 2025, later reduced by the court, and which Greenpeace International has challenged in the Netherlands under the EU's **Anti-SLAPP Directive** (Directive (EU) 2024/1069).
