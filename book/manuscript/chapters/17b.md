## 17.2 Prevention, Precaution, and the Polluter-Pays Principle

### The Role of Principles

International environmental law is characterised by the prominence of principles — general norms that guide the interpretation and development of more specific rules, and that may themselves have binding force. The Rio Declaration articulates many of these principles, including the sovereign right to exploit resources and the responsibility not to cause transboundary harm (Principle 2), the right to development (Principle 3), the integration of environmental protection into development (Principle 4), common but differentiated responsibilities (Principle 7), public participation and access to information and justice (Principle 10), the precautionary approach (Principle 15), the internalisation of environmental costs and the polluter-pays principle (Principle 16), and environmental impact assessment (Principle 17). The legal status of these principles varies: some, such as prevention and environmental impact assessment, are now established rules of customary international law, while others, such as precaution and polluter-pays, are more contested as general obligations, although they are incorporated in numerous treaties and national laws. The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, which is central to the climate regime, is examined in Chapter 18.

### The Principle of Prevention

The **principle of prevention** requires states to take measures to prevent significant harm to the environment, both within and beyond their territories. In its transboundary dimension, derived from *Trail Smelter* and Principle 21 of the Stockholm Declaration, it is firmly established as customary international law. In *Pulp Mills*, the International Court of Justice described the principle as having its origins in the due diligence that is required of a state in its territory, and held that a state is obliged to use all the means at its disposal to avoid activities which take place in its territory, or in any area under its jurisdiction, causing significant damage to the environment of another state.

The obligation of prevention is an obligation of **due diligence** — of conduct rather than of result. A state does not breach the obligation merely because harm occurs; it breaches the obligation if it fails to take the measures that could reasonably be expected of it to prevent the harm. In *Pulp Mills*, the Court explained that due diligence entails not only the adoption of appropriate rules and measures, but also a certain level of vigilance in their enforcement and the exercise of administrative control applicable to public and private operators, such as the monitoring of activities undertaken by such operators. The standard of due diligence is variable: it depends on the nature of the activity, the degree of risk, the state of scientific knowledge and technology, and the capacity of the state, and it becomes more demanding as the risk increases. In its 2011 Advisory Opinion on the responsibilities of states sponsoring activities in the Area, the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea described due diligence as a variable concept which may change over time as measures considered sufficiently diligent at a certain moment become not diligent enough in light of new scientific or technological knowledge. In its 2024 Advisory Opinion on climate change, the Tribunal held that the due diligence required of states in relation to greenhouse gas emissions is "stringent", given the high risks of serious and irreversible harm.

The **International Law Commission's Draft Articles on Prevention of Transboundary Harm from Hazardous Activities** (2001) codify the obligation of prevention and its procedural corollaries: the obligation to take all appropriate measures to prevent significant transboundary harm or to minimise the risk thereof; the requirement of prior authorisation of hazardous activities; the assessment of risk; notification and information of potentially affected states; consultation on preventive measures; and the exchange of information. These obligations apply directly to energy and extractive activities with transboundary risks, such as nuclear power plants near borders, oil and gas installations in shared seas, mines and tailings facilities on shared rivers, and hydroelectric dams.

### The Precautionary Principle

The **precautionary principle**, or precautionary approach, addresses the problem of scientific uncertainty. Principle 15 of the Rio Declaration provides that, in order to protect the environment, the precautionary approach shall be widely applied by states according to their capabilities, and that where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. The principle thus permits — and in stronger formulations requires — action to prevent harm before the harm is scientifically established.

The precautionary approach is incorporated in numerous treaties, including the UNFCCC (Article 3(3)), the Convention on Biological Diversity (preamble), the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (2000), the UN Fish Stocks Agreement (1995), and regional agreements such as the OSPAR Convention, examined in section 17.6. In the European Union, Article 191(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union provides that Union policy on the environment shall be based on the precautionary principle, and the Court of Justice has applied the principle extensively, holding that where there is uncertainty as to the existence or extent of risks to human health or the environment, protective measures may be taken without having to wait until the reality and seriousness of those risks become fully apparent.

The status of the principle in general international law remains debated. The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, in the *Southern Bluefin Tuna* provisional measures order (1999), held that the parties should act with "prudence and caution", and in its 2011 Advisory Opinion, the Seabed Disputes Chamber observed that the precautionary approach had been incorporated into a growing number of international instruments, initiating a trend towards making it part of customary international law, and held that it is an integral part of the general obligation of due diligence. The International Court of Justice, in *Pulp Mills*, accepted that a precautionary approach may be relevant in the interpretation and application of a treaty, but held that it does not operate as a reversal of the burden of proof. Within the World Trade Organization, the Appellate Body in *EC – Hormones* (1998) declined to decide whether the principle had become customary law and held that it did not override the specific provisions of the Agreement on Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures, although the SPS Agreement itself reflects precautionary elements.

In the energy sector, the precautionary principle has been invoked in support of restrictions on hydraulic fracturing (fracking), deep-sea mining, and the siting of nuclear facilities. France prohibited hydraulic fracturing for the exploration and production of hydrocarbons by a law of 2011, whose constitutionality was upheld by the Conseil constitutionnel in 2013; several other European states and sub-national governments adopted moratoria or bans; and the United Kingdom imposed a moratorium on fracking in England in 2019, following seismic events at a site in Lancashire, which was briefly lifted and then reinstated in 2022. The calls by numerous states for a precautionary pause on deep-sea mining, examined in Chapter 20, are a further application of the principle.

### The Polluter-Pays Principle

The **polluter-pays principle** requires that the costs of pollution prevention, control, and remediation be borne by the polluter rather than by society at large. It was first articulated as an economic principle by the OECD in its 1972 Recommendation on Guiding Principles concerning International Economic Aspects of Environmental Policies, which aimed to prevent governments from subsidising pollution control in ways that would distort international trade. Principle 16 of the Rio Declaration provides that national authorities should endeavour to promote the internalisation of environmental costs and the use of economic instruments, taking into account the approach that the polluter should, in principle, bear the cost of pollution, with due regard to the public interest and without distorting international trade and investment.

The polluter-pays principle is applied through several legal mechanisms: environmental taxes and charges, such as carbon taxes and effluent charges; emissions trading schemes, examined in Chapter 18; liability regimes that require polluters to pay for remediation and compensation, examined in section 17.7; financial security requirements, such as decommissioning and mine closure bonds; and the removal of subsidies for polluting activities, including fossil fuel subsidies. In the European Union, the principle is enshrined in Article 191(2) TFEU and implemented through the Environmental Liability Directive (Directive 2004/35/EC). In *Raffinerie Mediterranee (ERG) SpA v Ministero dello Sviluppo economico* (Case C-378/08, 2010), the Court of Justice held that, under the Directive, the competent authority may presume a causal link between operators and pollution on the basis of the proximity of their installations to the pollution and the correspondence between the pollutants found and the substances used by the operators, provided it has plausible evidence.

In the energy sector, the principle underlies obligations to decommission offshore installations and to rehabilitate mine sites at the operator's expense, examined in Chapters 5 and 7, and the debate over whether fossil fuel producers should contribute to the costs of climate change, examined in Chapter 18. Its application in developing countries is complicated by weak enforcement, the insolvency or departure of responsible operators, and the existence of historical or "orphan" pollution for which no solvent polluter can be identified.

### Other Principles

Several other principles are relevant to energy and resource activities. The principle of **intergenerational equity** — that the present generation holds the natural environment in trust for future generations — has been invoked in national courts, notably by the Supreme Court of the Philippines in *Minors Oposa v Secretary of the Department of Environment and Natural Resources* (1993), in which the Court recognised the standing of children to sue on behalf of themselves and future generations to challenge timber licences. The principle of **integration**, reflected in Principle 4 of the Rio Declaration, requires environmental protection to constitute an integral part of the development process. The principle of **public participation**, reflected in Principle 10, has been given binding form in the UNECE **Aarhus Convention** on Access to Information, Public Participation in Decision-making and Access to Justice in Environmental Matters (1998) and the Latin American and Caribbean **Escazú Agreement** (2018), which entered into force in 2021 and is the first treaty to include specific provisions on the protection of environmental human rights defenders. The principle of **non-regression**, which holds that environmental protection should not be weakened once achieved, has been recognised in some national and regional legal systems.

## 17.3 Environmental Impact Assessment

### The Function of Environmental Impact Assessment

**Environmental impact assessment** (EIA) is a procedure for identifying, predicting, and evaluating the likely environmental effects of a proposed activity before a decision is taken on whether, and on what conditions, to authorise it. EIA originated in the United States with the **National Environmental Policy Act of 1969** (NEPA), which required federal agencies to prepare an environmental impact statement for major federal actions significantly affecting the quality of the human environment, and has since been adopted in virtually every legal system. EIA is the principal legal tool through which environmental considerations are integrated into decisions on energy and extractive projects, and it frequently becomes the focus of litigation by communities and environmental organisations seeking to challenge projects.

The EIA process typically comprises the following stages, illustrated in Figure 17.1: **screening**, to determine whether an activity requires EIA; **scoping**, to identify the issues and alternatives to be addressed; the preparation of an **environmental impact statement** or report by the proponent, describing the project, the baseline environment, the likely effects, alternatives, and mitigation measures; **public participation** and consultation with affected communities and authorities; **review** of the statement by the competent authority, sometimes with the assistance of an independent panel; the **decision** whether to authorise the project, with conditions; and **monitoring** and follow-up of the project's actual effects. Many jurisdictions now require **environmental and social impact assessment** (ESIA), which integrates the assessment of social effects, including effects on livelihoods, health, cultural heritage, and human rights, and international lenders require ESIA in accordance with IFC Performance Standard 1 for the projects they finance, as examined in Chapter 16.

### EIA in International Law

At the international level, Principle 17 of the Rio Declaration provides that EIA, as a national instrument, shall be undertaken for proposed activities that are likely to have a significant adverse impact on the environment and are subject to a decision of a competent national authority. The **Espoo Convention on Environmental Impact Assessment in a Transboundary Context** (1991), adopted under the auspices of the UN Economic Commission for Europe, requires parties to assess the environmental impact of listed activities — including crude oil refineries, large thermal power stations, nuclear installations, large dams, oil and gas pipelines, and major mining — likely to cause significant adverse transboundary impact, to notify and consult potentially affected states, and to allow the public of affected states to participate. Its **Protocol on Strategic Environmental Assessment** (Kyiv, 2003) extends assessment to plans and programmes, including energy plans. The Espoo Convention's Implementation Committee has addressed numerous cases concerning energy projects, including nuclear power plants and the lifetime extension of nuclear reactors, which it has held may require transboundary assessment.

In *Pulp Mills*, the International Court of Justice held that it may now be considered a requirement under general international law to undertake an environmental impact assessment where there is a risk that the proposed industrial activity may have a significant adverse impact in a transboundary context, in particular on a shared resource. The Court held that the content of the assessment is to be determined by each state in its domestic legislation or in the authorisation process, having regard to the nature and magnitude of the proposed development and its likely adverse impact on the environment, as well as to the need to exercise due diligence; that the assessment must be conducted prior to the implementation of the project; and that, once operations have started, continuous monitoring of the project's effects on the environment shall be undertaken throughout its life. In *Certain Activities* and *Construction of a Road* (2015), the Court clarified that the obligation applies generally to proposed activities that may have a significant adverse impact in a transboundary context, and that a state must first ascertain, through a preliminary assessment, whether there is such a risk; if so, it must conduct a full EIA and, where the EIA confirms the risk, notify and consult the potentially affected state in good faith. The African Convention on the Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources, as revised at Maputo in 2003, also requires parties to ensure that activities likely to affect natural resources are subject to adequate impact assessment.

### EIA in National Law

In the **European Union**, the EIA Directive (Directive 2011/92/EU, as amended by Directive 2014/52/EU) requires assessment of projects listed in Annex I — including crude oil refineries, thermal power stations of 300 MW or more, nuclear power stations, the extraction of petroleum and natural gas above specified thresholds, large pipelines, and large dams — and screening of projects listed in Annex II. The 2014 amendments required the assessment of effects on climate, including greenhouse gas emissions and vulnerability to climate change, and on biodiversity. The Strategic Environmental Assessment Directive (Directive 2001/42/EC) applies to plans and programmes, including energy plans.

In **Nigeria**, the **Environmental Impact Assessment Act** of 1992 requires the assessment of the environmental effects of public or private projects before decisions are taken, and lists mandatory study activities, including petroleum exploration and production, refineries, pipelines, power stations, and mining. The Act is administered by the Federal Ministry of Environment, and provides that no project to which it applies shall be undertaken without the prior consideration of its environmental effects. The Court of Appeal's decision in *Oronto Douglas v Shell Petroleum Development Co Ltd* (1999), recognising the standing of an environmental activist to challenge a failure to comply with the Act in relation to an LNG project, was an early landmark in the enforcement of EIA requirements in Nigeria, although enforcement of the Act has remained uneven, and the relationship between federal EIA requirements and the environmental provisions of the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, including the environmental management plans required by the Act, raises questions of coordination.

In the **United States**, the scope of NEPA review has been narrowed. In *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v Eagle County* (2025), concerning a railway line intended to transport crude oil from Utah's Uinta Basin, the Supreme Court held that courts should afford substantial deference to agencies in determining the scope of NEPA review, and that an agency is not required to assess the environmental effects of separate upstream or downstream projects that it does not regulate, such as oil production and refining, merely because they are foreseeable consequences of the project under review. The decision, together with the rescission of the regulations of the Council on Environmental Quality implementing NEPA in 2025, has reduced the role of NEPA in the review of fossil fuel infrastructure.

### Assessing Downstream Emissions

The question whether the EIA of a fossil fuel extraction project must assess the greenhouse gas emissions that will result from the combustion of the extracted product — so-called "downstream" or "scope 3" emissions — has become one of the most significant issues in energy law. The United Kingdom Supreme Court answered it in the affirmative.

::: casebox {title="R (Finch) v Surrey County Council [2024] UKSC 20"}
**Facts.** Surrey County Council granted planning permission for the expansion of an onshore oil extraction site at Horse Hill, near Gatwick Airport, permitting the drilling of four new wells and the production of oil over a period of twenty-five years. The environmental statement assessed the greenhouse gas emissions from the construction and operation of the site, but not the emissions from the combustion of the oil once refined and used, on the ground that those emissions were not effects of the project. Sarah Finch, on behalf of a local campaign group, sought judicial review of the permission.

**The issue.** The question was whether, under the EIA Regulations implementing the EIA Directive, which require the assessment of the "direct and indirect significant effects" of a project, the combustion emissions were effects of the project that had to be assessed.

**The decision.** By a majority of three to two, the Supreme Court held that the permission was unlawful. Lord Leggatt, giving the leading judgment, held that it was inevitable that the oil extracted would be refined and burned, and that the resulting emissions were therefore effects of the project. The question whether an effect is an effect of the project is a question of causation, and the combustion emissions were clearly caused by the extraction, since the oil would not otherwise be produced; the fact that the oil would be refined elsewhere did not break the chain of causation. The council's conclusion that the combustion emissions did not need to be assessed was therefore legally flawed. The Court emphasised that the purpose of EIA is to ensure that decisions are taken with full knowledge of their environmental consequences, and that the assessment of combustion emissions did not determine whether the project should be approved, which remained a matter for the decision-maker. The dissenting justices considered that the extent of the project's effects was a matter of evaluative judgment for the planning authority.

**Aftermath.** The decision has had major consequences for oil and gas development in the United Kingdom. In January 2025, the Court of Session in Scotland held that the consents granted for the Rosebank and Jackdaw offshore fields were unlawful because the assessments had not considered combustion emissions, while permitting preparatory work to continue pending fresh decisions, and the government subsequently issued guidance on the assessment of such emissions. Courts in other jurisdictions have reached similar conclusions; in Norway, the Oslo District Court held in 2024 that approvals of several offshore oil field developments were invalid for failure to assess combustion emissions, and the EFTA Court subsequently confirmed that such emissions fall within the scope of the EIA Directive as applied in the European Economic Area. The contrast with the narrowing of NEPA review in the United States illustrates divergent judicial approaches to the role of EIA in addressing climate change.

### Strategic Environmental Assessment

**Strategic environmental assessment** (SEA) applies assessment to policies, plans, and programmes, such as national energy plans, petroleum licensing rounds, and renewable energy zoning, before individual projects are proposed. SEA allows the cumulative effects of multiple projects, alternatives at the level of policy, and the consistency of plans with climate and biodiversity objectives to be considered at a stage when the range of options is still open. Several resource-rich developing countries, including Uganda for the Albertine Graben petroleum region, have undertaken SEAs of their extractive sectors, and SEA is increasingly used in the planning of offshore wind zones, examined in Chapter 20.

### Effectiveness and Limitations

EIA is an essentially procedural tool: it requires that environmental effects be identified and considered, but it does not in itself require that projects with adverse effects be refused. Its effectiveness depends on the quality and independence of the assessment, which is usually prepared by consultants engaged by the proponent; the genuineness of public participation; the capacity of the reviewing authority; and the enforcement of conditions and monitoring. Common weaknesses in practice include inadequate baseline data, the failure to consider alternatives or cumulative effects, consultation that is late or superficial, and the absence of follow-up. Courts have played an important role in enforcing EIA requirements, and the developments in the assessment of downstream emissions illustrate how judicial interpretation can transform the significance of a procedural requirement.
