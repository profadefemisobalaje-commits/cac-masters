## 4.7 Independent Regulators and Administrative Accountability

### The Rise of the Independent Regulator

At the national level, the most significant institutional development in energy governance over the past four decades has been the spread of independent regulatory agencies. Before the liberalisation era, the electricity and gas sectors in most countries were dominated by vertically integrated, state-owned monopolies that were, in effect, self-regulating, subject to ministerial oversight. The petroleum sector was typically regulated by a ministry, sometimes acting through a national oil company that combined commercial and regulatory functions.

The liberalisation and privatisation of energy markets, beginning in the United Kingdom, Chile, and New Zealand in the 1980s and spreading globally in the 1990s, required new regulatory institutions. Where private companies own and operate natural monopoly networks, or where competition is introduced into generation and supply, an independent regulator is needed to set tariffs, ensure non-discriminatory network access, license market participants, monitor market conduct, and protect consumers. The model of the independent regulatory agency, long established in the United States in the form of state public utility commissions and the Federal Power Commission (later the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission), was transplanted across the world, frequently with the encouragement of the World Bank and other development institutions.

In Nigeria, the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005 established the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC), now continued under the Electricity Act 2023. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 established the NUPRC and the NMDPRA as regulators separate from the commercial NNPC Ltd, replacing the Department of Petroleum Resources, the Petroleum Products Pricing Regulatory Agency, and the Petroleum Equalisation Fund (Management) Board.

### The Rationale for Independence

Several justifications have been advanced for the independence of energy regulators.

**Credible commitment.** Energy infrastructure is capital-intensive and long-lived, and investors require assurance that tariffs will allow them to recover their investment and earn a reasonable return. Political control of tariffs creates a risk that governments will hold down prices for electoral reasons, expropriating investors' sunk capital. Delegating tariff-setting to an independent regulator with a statutory mandate and secure tenure is a means by which governments can credibly commit not to behave opportunistically.

**Expertise.** Energy regulation is technically complex, requiring expertise in economics, engineering, accounting, and law. Specialised agencies can develop and retain such expertise more effectively than ministries.

**Separation of roles.** Where the state owns energy companies, independence separates the state's role as owner from its role as regulator, reducing conflicts of interest and promoting a level playing field between state-owned and private participants. The separation of the regulatory and commercial functions of national oil companies is a central theme of petroleum sector reform.

**Insulation from capture.** Independence is also intended to protect regulators from capture by the industries they regulate, although independence from government does not of itself guarantee independence from industry.

### Elements of Independence

The independence of a regulator depends on several institutional features:

- **Legal foundation**: establishment by statute with a clearly defined mandate, rather than by executive decision;
- **Appointment and tenure**: appointment through a transparent process, fixed terms of office, and protection against removal except for defined cause;
- **Financial autonomy**: funding through levies on regulated entities or secure budgetary allocations, rather than discretionary appropriations;
- **Decision-making autonomy**: authority to take decisions without ministerial direction on individual matters, subject to general policy guidance;
- **Organisational autonomy**: control over staffing and internal organisation.

EU law imposes detailed independence requirements on national regulatory authorities. Under Article 57 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 on common rules for the internal market for electricity, member states must guarantee the independence of regulatory authorities and ensure that they exercise their powers impartially and transparently; regulatory staff and management must act independently from any market interest and must not seek or take direct instructions from any government or other public or private entity when carrying out regulatory tasks. In *Commission v Germany* (2021), the Court of Justice held that Germany had failed to fulfil its obligations by conferring on the federal government powers to determine tariff methodologies that EU law reserved exclusively to the national regulatory authority.[^comger]

[^comger]: Case C-718/18, *European Commission v Federal Republic of Germany*, EU:C:2021:662 (CJEU, 2 September 2021).

### The Governance of National Oil Companies

National oil companies (NOCs) control the majority of the world's proven oil and gas reserves and are therefore among the most significant institutions in global energy governance, even though they are corporate entities rather than regulators. Their governance raises distinctive legal questions concerning the separation of commercial, policy, and regulatory functions; the relationship between the NOC and the state treasury; transparency and accountability; and the application of corporate law to entities whose shareholder is the state.

The "Norwegian model" is frequently cited as a benchmark. Under this model, policy functions are exercised by the Ministry of Energy, regulatory and resource management functions by a specialised agency (the Norwegian Offshore Directorate, as the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate was renamed in 2024), and commercial functions by a company operating on market terms (Equinor, formerly Statoil, partially listed since 2001), with the state's direct financial interests in petroleum licences managed separately by a wholly state-owned company, Petoro. Comparative research has questioned whether the Norwegian separation of functions is a necessary condition of good NOC performance, observing that it presupposes strong institutional capacity, and that some NOCs perform well under integrated models while some separated models perform poorly.

Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021 moved decisively towards a separation of functions. Section 53 provides for the incorporation of NNPC Limited under the Companies and Allied Matters Act, with ownership of its shares vested in the Federal Government and held by the Ministry of Finance Incorporated and the Ministry of Petroleum Resources on behalf of the Federation. NNPC Ltd is to operate as a commercial entity without recourse to government funding and to pay dividends to its shareholders, and the Act contemplates that its shares may in future be offered to the public. Regulatory functions previously exercised by NNPC divisions and the Department of Petroleum Resources were transferred to the NUPRC and the NMDPRA. Whether this formal separation will produce the commercial discipline and transparency its drafters intended depends on implementation, including the robustness of corporate governance at NNPC Ltd, the publication of audited accounts, and the treatment of legacy obligations such as fuel subsidy arrears.

### Administrative Accountability

Independence must be balanced by accountability. An independent regulator exercises significant public power — it sets prices paid by millions of consumers, grants and revokes licences, and imposes penalties — and in a democratic system such power must be subject to mechanisms of accountability. These mechanisms include:

- **Legislative oversight**: reporting obligations to the legislature, appearances before parliamentary committees, and legislative approval of budgets;
- **Judicial review**: the right of affected persons to challenge regulatory decisions before courts or specialised tribunals on grounds of illegality, irrationality, and procedural impropriety, examined further in Chapter 27;
- **Procedural requirements**: obligations to consult on proposed decisions, to give reasons, to publish decisions, and to act transparently;
- **Appeals**: specialised appeal mechanisms, such as the Competition and Markets Authority's role in hearing appeals against certain decisions of Ofgem in the United Kingdom;
- **Performance review**: periodic review of regulatory performance by audit institutions or independent bodies.

The relationship between independence and accountability is a subject of ongoing scholarly debate. Some scholars emphasise the democratic deficit of independent agencies and argue for stronger political control; others emphasise the importance of independence for investment and argue that accountability is best secured through procedural transparency and judicial review. In the United States, the Supreme Court's decision in *Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo* (2024), overruling the *Chevron* doctrine of judicial deference to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes, and its articulation of the "major questions doctrine" in *West Virginia v Environmental Protection Agency* (2022), have significantly altered the balance between regulatory agencies and courts, with major implications for energy and environmental regulation.[^loper]

[^loper]: *Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo*, 603 US 369 (2024); *West Virginia v Environmental Protection Agency*, 597 US 697 (2022).

### Regulatory Independence in Practice: The Developing-Country Experience

The transplantation of the independent regulator model to developing countries has produced mixed results. Formal independence enshrined in legislation has often been undermined in practice by political interference in appointments, ministerial pressure on tariff decisions, inadequate funding, and limited technical capacity. Studies of electricity regulation in Africa have found that regulators frequently lack the authority to set cost-reflective tariffs in the face of political resistance, leading to utility financial deficits, underinvestment, and poor service quality. The Nigerian experience illustrates these challenges: NERC's efforts to implement cost-reflective tariffs under the Multi-Year Tariff Order framework have repeatedly been constrained by political considerations, and the accumulation of tariff shortfalls has required substantial government subsidies. The introduction of a "Band A" tariff structure in 2024, under which customers receiving at least twenty hours of supply daily pay higher, more cost-reflective tariffs, represents an attempt to align tariffs with service quality.

These experiences suggest that formal independence is a necessary but not sufficient condition for effective regulation. It must be supported by political commitment, adequate resources, technical capacity, and a broader governance environment that respects the rule of law. Scholars such as Brian Levy and Pablo Spiller have emphasised that regulatory design must be adapted to the institutional endowment of each country, and that in countries with weak checks and balances, contractual commitments and international guarantees may be needed to supplement regulatory independence.[^levy]

[^levy]: Brian Levy and Pablo T. Spiller, "The Institutional Foundations of Regulatory Commitment: A Comparative Analysis of Telecommunications Regulation" (1994) 10 *Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization* 201.

## 4.8 Regulatory Cooperation and Institutional Reform

### Forms of Regulatory Cooperation

Regulatory cooperation in the energy sector takes several forms. At the bilateral level, regulators conclude memoranda of understanding to exchange information, coordinate decisions on cross-border infrastructure, and share expertise. At the regional level, regulators participate in associations such as the Council of European Energy Regulators (CEER), the Energy Regulators Regional Association (ERRA) for Central and Eastern Europe and beyond, the African Forum for Utility Regulators (AFUR), and RERA. At the global level, the International Confederation of Energy Regulators (ICER) provides a forum for cooperation among regional associations.

Regulatory cooperation is particularly important for cross-border infrastructure. The development of an interconnector or cross-border pipeline requires decisions by regulators in each jurisdiction on matters such as cost allocation, access rules, and tariffs. Within the European Union, Regulation (EU) 2022/869 on guidelines for trans-European energy infrastructure provides for the identification of projects of common interest and a framework for cross-border cost allocation decisions, with ACER empowered to decide where national regulators cannot agree.

### Proposals for Institutional Reform

The fragmentation of global energy governance has prompted numerous proposals for reform. These include:

**Expanding the IEA.** Proposals to open IEA membership to non-OECD states, particularly China and India, would require amendment of the IEP Agreement and the OECD framework. The association initiative represents an incremental step in this direction.

**A World Energy Organization.** Some scholars have proposed the creation of a comprehensive world energy organisation, analogous to the WTO, with universal membership and competence over energy trade, investment, and security. Such proposals face formidable political obstacles, given the divergence of producer and consumer interests and the reluctance of states to cede sovereignty over energy.

**Strengthening the energy dimension of the climate regime.** The UNFCCC and Paris Agreement are not energy institutions, but the decisions of the Conference of the Parties increasingly address energy directly, as in the COP28 global stocktake decision. Some scholars argue that the climate regime should become the primary forum for global energy governance; others caution that it lacks the expertise and instruments to address energy security and market issues.

**Reforming the Energy Charter Treaty.** The modernisation of the ECT, negotiated between 2020 and 2022 and adopted by the Energy Charter Conference in December 2024, sought to align the treaty with the Paris Agreement by permitting parties to exclude protection for fossil fuel investments, clarifying the right to regulate, and refining investment protection standards. The withdrawals of the European Union, Euratom, and several European states, and the continuing participation of other contracting parties, have left the treaty's future uncertain. These developments are examined in Chapter 14.

**Networked governance.** An alternative to institutional consolidation is to improve coordination among existing institutions through networked governance: joint initiatives, memoranda of understanding, shared data platforms, and the alignment of mandates. The joint work of the IEA, IRENA, the World Bank, the World Health Organization, and UN statistical bodies on tracking progress towards Sustainable Development Goal 7 illustrates this approach.

### Data, Transparency, and Institutional Legitimacy

A final dimension of institutional reform concerns data and transparency. Energy governance depends on reliable data on production, consumption, stocks, prices, reserves, emissions, and investment. Much of that data is held by governments and companies that may have strategic or commercial reasons to withhold it, and the quality of data varies widely among states. The Joint Organisations Data Initiative, administered by the International Energy Forum with the participation of APEC, Eurostat, GECF, IEA, OLADE, OPEC, and the United Nations Statistics Division, seeks to improve the transparency of oil and gas statistics. The EITI Standard requires implementing countries to disclose information along the extractive industry value chain, including contracts, licences, beneficial ownership, production, revenues, and the allocation of revenues. Satellite monitoring of methane emissions, gas flaring, and infrastructure is increasingly supplementing official data, as in the United Nations Environment Programme's International Methane Emissions Observatory.

Data has legal significance. Regulatory decisions, arbitral damages assessments, and climate litigation all depend on evidence, and the authority of institutions such as the IEA rests heavily on the credibility of their data and scenarios. Contests over data — over the reliability of emissions inventories, the assumptions of energy scenarios, or the reserves on which valuations depend — are therefore contests over institutional legitimacy. For doctoral researchers, the political economy of energy data is a promising and relatively unexplored field.

### Institutional Reform and the Energy Transition

The energy transition poses fundamental challenges for energy institutions. Institutions designed for a world of centralised fossil fuel systems must adapt to a world of decentralised, digital, and decarbonised systems. Consumer-oriented institutions concerned with oil supply security must address new security challenges arising from critical mineral supply chains, cyber threats, and the variability of renewable generation. Producer-oriented institutions must address the prospect of declining demand. Regulators must design markets that accommodate variable renewable generation, storage, demand response, and distributed resources, and must balance the costs of transition against affordability for consumers. The capacity of institutions to adapt to these challenges will significantly influence the pace and fairness of the transition.

::: casebox
**Federal Republic of Germany v Republic of Poland (OPAL), CJEU, 2021**

*Facts.* In 2016, the European Commission approved a modification of the exemption from third-party access and tariff regulation previously granted to the OPAL pipeline, the German onshore extension of the Nord Stream pipeline, allowing Gazprom to use a greater share of OPAL's capacity. Poland challenged the decision before the General Court, arguing that it threatened Poland's security of gas supply by diverting transit flows from pipelines crossing Polish territory.

*Holding.* The General Court annulled the Commission's decision in 2019 for breach of the principle of energy solidarity. On appeal by Germany, the Court of Justice confirmed that energy solidarity under Article 194(1) TFEU is a general principle of EU law, which entails rights and obligations both for the EU and for the member states and requires that the interests of all stakeholders liable to be affected be taken into account. The Commission had failed to examine the effects of its decision on the energy security of Poland and other member states.

*Significance.* The judgment transformed solidarity from a political aspiration into a justiciable legal principle that constrains EU energy decisions. It illustrates the role of courts in regional energy governance and the legal relevance of energy security considerations.
:::

::: summary
- Global energy governance is a fragmented, polycentric regime complex, reflecting divergent producer and consumer interests, sovereignty sensitivities, path dependence, technological diversity, and shifting centres of economic gravity.
- The IEA, created in 1974, imposes binding emergency stockholding and response obligations on its OECD members and exercises broad analytical influence, but its limited membership constrains its global legitimacy.
- OPEC and the OPEC+ arrangements coordinate producer policies through politically binding but unenforceable production decisions, raising unresolved questions under competition and trade law.
- IRENA has near-universal membership and a promotional mandate without binding powers; the IAEA combines promotion with legally significant safeguards and safety functions.
- Regional institutions range from the supranational EU model to intergovernmental African power pools; the CJEU's recognition of energy solidarity as a binding principle illustrates the legal depth of EU integration.
- Independent regulators are justified by credible commitment, expertise, separation of roles, and insulation from capture, but must be accountable through legislative oversight, judicial review, and procedural transparency; formal independence is often undermined in practice in developing countries.
- Reform proposals include expanding the IEA, creating a world energy organisation, strengthening the climate regime's energy role, modernising the ECT, and improving networked governance.
:::
