## 17.4 Transboundary Environmental Harm

### The Nature of the Problem

Many energy and resource activities have the potential to cause harm beyond the borders of the state in which they take place. Air pollution from power plants and smelters may travel hundreds or thousands of kilometres; oil spills from offshore installations and pipelines may drift into neighbouring waters; tailings dam failures and industrial accidents may pollute shared rivers; nuclear accidents may contaminate entire regions; and dams may alter the flow of rivers downstream. The law of transboundary harm addresses the rights and obligations of states in relation to such harm, and is a central element of the international law governing energy and natural resources. The specific regime governing shared watercourses and hydropower is examined in Chapter 19, and the marine environment in section 17.6 and Chapter 20.

### The Substantive Obligation

The customary obligation of prevention, examined in section 17.2, requires states to exercise due diligence to prevent activities within their jurisdiction or control from causing **significant** harm to the environment of other states or of areas beyond national jurisdiction. Several elements of the obligation require explanation.

**Significant harm.** The obligation does not prohibit all transboundary effects, which would be impossible in an interdependent world, but only harm that is "significant" — a threshold described by the International Law Commission as something more than "detectable" but not necessarily "serious" or "substantial", which must lead to a real detrimental effect on matters such as human health, industry, property, the environment, or agriculture in other states, measurable by factual and objective standards.

**Jurisdiction or control.** The obligation applies to activities within the territory of a state and to activities under its jurisdiction or control elsewhere, such as those of vessels flagged in the state or of installations on its continental shelf. The extent to which home states are responsible for the extraterritorial activities of their corporations is debated, and is examined in Chapters 21 and 22.

**Due diligence.** As examined in section 17.2, the obligation is one of conduct, requiring the state to take appropriate legislative, administrative, and enforcement measures, proportionate to the risk.

**Areas beyond national jurisdiction.** The obligation extends to the global commons — the high seas, the deep seabed, Antarctica, outer space, and the atmosphere — whose protection is of concern to the international community as a whole. The obligations of states in respect of the climate system, as clarified by the 2025 advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice, are examined in Chapter 18.

### Procedural Obligations

The substantive obligation is accompanied by procedural obligations, which the International Court of Justice has treated as distinct from, though closely connected with, the obligation of prevention. They include the obligation to conduct an EIA where there is a risk of significant transboundary harm, examined in section 17.3; the obligation to **notify** potentially affected states of planned activities that may cause significant harm, providing them with available technical and other information; the obligation to **consult** with those states in good faith, with a view to achieving acceptable solutions regarding the measures to be adopted; and the obligation to notify affected states without delay of **emergencies** that may cause harm to them, and to cooperate in responding to them. In the **Lac Lanoux** arbitration (*Spain v France*, 1957), concerning a French hydroelectric project that diverted waters of a river flowing into Spain, the tribunal held that France was obliged to consult Spain in good faith and to take Spain's interests into account, but that it was not required to obtain Spain's prior consent, which would amount to a right of veto. In *Pulp Mills*, the Court held that Uruguay had breached its procedural obligations under the bilateral treaty governing the River Uruguay by authorising the construction of pulp mills without notifying the joint administrative commission established under the treaty, but that it had not breached its substantive obligations, since Argentina had not proved that the mills had caused significant pollution.

### Industrial Accidents and Emergencies

Several major industrial accidents have shaped the law of transboundary harm. The **Chernobyl** nuclear accident of 1986, which spread radioactive contamination across Europe, led to the rapid adoption, under the auspices of the International Atomic Energy Agency, of the Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident and the Convention on Assistance in the Case of a Nuclear Accident or Radiological Emergency, both of 1986, examined in Chapter 11; notably, no state brought a claim against the Soviet Union for the damage caused. The **Sandoz** chemical warehouse fire near Basel in the same year, which released toxic chemicals into the Rhine and killed fish along hundreds of kilometres of the river, contributed to the development of cooperation in the Rhine basin and, together with the Seveso disaster of 1976, to the adoption of the UNECE **Convention on the Transboundary Effects of Industrial Accidents** (Helsinki, 1992), which requires parties to identify hazardous activities, to take preventive and preparedness measures, and to notify and assist each other in the event of an accident. The **Baia Mare** cyanide spill of 2000, in which a tailings dam at a gold mine in Romania failed, releasing cyanide-contaminated water that flowed into Hungary and Serbia via the Tisza and Danube rivers, illustrated the transboundary risks of mining and led to the strengthening of European Union law on mining waste, through the Mining Waste Directive (Directive 2006/21/EC).

### Long-Range Air Pollution

The UNECE **Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution** (1979), adopted in response to evidence that acid rain in Scandinavia was caused by emissions of sulphur dioxide from power plants and industry in other European states, was the first multilateral treaty to address air pollution on a regional basis. Its eight protocols, including the Gothenburg Protocol (1999, amended 2012), set emission ceilings for sulphur, nitrogen oxides, volatile organic compounds, ammonia, and fine particulate matter, and have contributed to substantial reductions in emissions from the energy sector in Europe and North America. The convention is frequently cited as an example of effective science-based cooperation. In South-East Asia, the **ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution** (2002) addresses the smoke haze caused by land and forest fires, associated with the clearing of land for plantations, including on peatlands.

### Hazardous Wastes

The transboundary movement of hazardous wastes, including wastes generated by the petroleum and mining industries, is regulated by the **Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and their Disposal** (1989), which requires the prior informed consent of importing and transit states and the environmentally sound management of wastes, and whose Ban Amendment, prohibiting exports of hazardous wastes from OECD to non-OECD states, entered into force in 2019. African states, concerned that the Basel Convention did not go far enough, adopted the **Bamako Convention** on the Ban of the Import into Africa and the Control of Transboundary Movement and Management of Hazardous Wastes within Africa (1991). The dumping in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire, in 2006 of toxic waste generated on board the vessel *Probo Koala*, chartered by the commodity trader Trafigura, from the processing of petroleum products, which caused deaths and widespread illness, illustrated the risks associated with the waste streams of the petroleum industry and led to criminal proceedings in the Netherlands and civil litigation in England, which was settled. The waste generated by the energy transition, including end-of-life solar panels, wind turbine blades, and batteries, presents new challenges, examined in Chapters 8 and 10.

### State Responsibility and Remedies

A state that breaches its obligations in relation to transboundary harm incurs **state responsibility** under the general rules of international law, codified in the International Law Commission's **Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts** (2001), and is obliged to cease the wrongful act and to make full reparation for the injury caused, in the form of restitution, compensation, or satisfaction. The difficulties of proving causation and damage, and the reluctance of states to bring claims against each other, have meant that inter-state claims for transboundary environmental harm have been rare, and that the law has developed principally through civil liability regimes and the procedural obligations examined above.

Where harm results from lawful activities conducted with due diligence, state responsibility does not arise. To address the resulting gap, the International Law Commission adopted **Principles on the Allocation of Loss in the Case of Transboundary Harm Arising out of Hazardous Activities** (2006), which provide that states should ensure that prompt and adequate compensation is available for victims of transboundary damage caused by hazardous activities, primarily through the imposition of liability on the operator, supported by insurance and, where necessary, by additional funding from industry or state sources. These principles are reflected in the civil liability conventions for oil pollution and nuclear damage examined in section 17.7 and Chapter 11.

## 17.5 Biodiversity Conservation and Resource Extraction

### Biodiversity Loss and the Energy Sector

Biodiversity — the variability among living organisms and the ecosystems of which they are part — is declining at an unprecedented rate. The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), in its Global Assessment of 2019, estimated that around one million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, and identified changes in land and sea use, direct exploitation of organisms, climate change, pollution, and invasive species as the principal drivers. Energy and extractive activities contribute to each of these drivers: mining, oil and gas development, and the associated roads and infrastructure fragment and destroy habitats; hydropower dams transform river ecosystems; oil spills and mining waste pollute land and water; and the combustion of fossil fuels drives climate change. At the same time, the minerals required for the energy transition are frequently located in biodiversity-rich areas, including tropical forests and the deep seabed, creating new tensions between climate and biodiversity objectives.

### The Convention on Biological Diversity

The **Convention on Biological Diversity** (CBD), opened for signature at Rio in 1992 and ratified by almost all states (with the notable exception of the United States), has three objectives: the conservation of biological diversity, the sustainable use of its components, and the fair and equitable sharing of the benefits arising from the utilisation of genetic resources. It reaffirms the sovereign rights of states over their biological resources, while recognising that the conservation of biodiversity is a common concern of humankind. Parties are required to develop national biodiversity strategies and action plans, to integrate biodiversity into sectoral policies, to establish systems of protected areas, to rehabilitate degraded ecosystems, and, under Article 14, to introduce appropriate procedures requiring EIA of proposed projects likely to have significant adverse effects on biodiversity. The **Nagoya Protocol** (2010) addresses access to genetic resources and benefit-sharing, and the **Cartagena Protocol** (2000) addresses biosafety.

The CBD's obligations are framed in general and qualified terms — many are to be implemented "as far as possible and as appropriate" — and implementation has been weak; none of the twenty Aichi Biodiversity Targets adopted for the period 2011–2020 was fully achieved. In December 2022, the fifteenth Conference of the Parties adopted the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework**, which sets twenty-three targets for 2030, including the conservation of at least thirty per cent of terrestrial, inland water, and coastal and marine areas through protected areas and other effective area-based conservation measures (the "30 by 30" target); the restoration of thirty per cent of degraded ecosystems; the reduction of harmful subsidies; and, under Target 15, measures to encourage and enable large companies and financial institutions to monitor, assess, and disclose their risks, dependencies, and impacts on biodiversity. The Framework is significant for the extractive sector because the expansion of protected areas may restrict access to resources, and because the disclosure requirements are reflected in initiatives such as the **Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures** (TNFD), whose recommendations were published in 2023.

### Protected Areas, World Heritage, and "No-Go" Areas

The relationship between protected areas and resource extraction is a recurrent source of conflict. Many national laws prohibit or restrict mining and petroleum activities in national parks and other protected areas, but governments may de-gazette or reduce protected areas to permit extraction, and the boundaries between protection and development are frequently contested.

The **Convention concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage** (World Heritage Convention, 1972) requires parties to protect and conserve sites of outstanding universal value inscribed on the World Heritage List, and the World Heritage Committee may inscribe sites on the List of World Heritage in Danger where they are threatened, including by extractive activities. The Committee has consistently stated that mineral, oil, and gas exploration and exploitation are incompatible with World Heritage status. In 2003, the International Council on Mining and Metals (ICMM) committed its members not to explore or mine in World Heritage sites — the first "no-go" commitment by an industry body — and several oil and gas companies have made similar commitments. The commitment was tested in **Virunga National Park** in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Africa's oldest national park and a World Heritage site, where the grant of oil exploration rights to SOCO International provoked an international campaign, leading the company in 2014 to agree not to undertake drilling in the park unless the government and UNESCO agreed that such activities were compatible with its World Heritage status. In 2022, the government of the DRC included blocks overlapping Virunga and other sensitive areas in a petroleum licensing round, renewing the controversy. In Tanzania, the construction of a large hydroelectric dam at Stiegler's Gorge within the **Selous Game Reserve**, a World Heritage site, proceeded despite the objections of the World Heritage Committee, illustrating the limits of the Convention in the face of national development priorities.

### Wetlands and Migratory Species

The **Ramsar Convention on Wetlands of International Importance** (1971) requires parties to designate wetlands for inclusion in a List of Wetlands of International Importance and to promote their conservation and "wise use". Wetlands such as the Niger Delta, the Sudd in South Sudan, and the Okavango Delta in Botswana are threatened by oil development, drainage, and changes in water flows. The **Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals** (CMS, 1979) protects migratory species, which may be affected by wind farms, power lines, and offshore energy installations, and its parties have adopted guidance on the deployment of renewable energy technologies in ways compatible with the conservation of migratory species, examined in Chapter 10.

### Biodiversity Standards in Project Finance

The most demanding biodiversity standards applicable to extractive projects are frequently those imposed by lenders. **IFC Performance Standard 6** on Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Management of Living Natural Resources requires projects to apply the mitigation hierarchy — avoidance, minimisation, restoration, and, as a last resort, offsetting — and prohibits activities in **critical habitats** unless, among other conditions, the project does not lead to measurable adverse impacts on the biodiversity values for which the habitat was designated and achieves **net gains** in those values. **Biodiversity offsets**, which compensate for residual impacts by conserving or restoring biodiversity elsewhere, are increasingly required, but are controversial because of the difficulty of establishing equivalence between lost and gained biodiversity and the risk that offsets legitimise destructive projects.

### Contested Extraction in Biodiverse Areas

Several cases illustrate the political and legal dynamics of extraction in biodiverse areas. In **Ecuador**, the Yasuní-ITT initiative, launched in 2007, proposed that Ecuador would leave the oil reserves beneath the Yasuní National Park — one of the most biodiverse areas on earth and the home of indigenous peoples living in voluntary isolation — undeveloped, in return for international contributions of half the value of the forgone revenue. The initiative was abandoned in 2013 after failing to attract sufficient contributions, and drilling commenced. In August 2023, following a decision of the Constitutional Court permitting a popular consultation, Ecuadorian voters approved by a substantial majority a proposal to halt oil production in Block 43 (ITT) and leave the oil underground; the implementation of the result, which required the decommissioning of existing production, proceeded slowly and was contested. In **Uganda and Tanzania**, the development of oil fields in and near Murchison Falls National Park and the construction of the **East African Crude Oil Pipeline** (EACOP), which crosses important ecosystems, have been opposed by environmental and human rights organisations and have been the subject of litigation, including in the East African Court of Justice and the French courts under the duty of vigilance law, examined in Chapter 22. In **Botswana and Namibia**, petroleum exploration in the Kavango basin, upstream of the Okavango Delta World Heritage site, raised concerns about effects on water resources and wildlife.

These cases illustrate the tension between the sovereign right of states to develop their resources, which is often exercised in the name of poverty reduction and development, and the international interest in conserving biodiversity of global significance. They also raise the question of who should bear the cost of conservation: if the international community values the conservation of biodiversity in developing countries, there is a strong argument that it should contribute to the opportunity costs of forgoing development, through mechanisms such as payments for ecosystem services, debt-for-nature swaps, and biodiversity finance, as recognised in Target 19 of the Kunming-Montreal Framework.
