## Introduction

Chapter 2 examined permanent sovereignty over natural resources as a principle of international law. This chapter turns to the ways in which that sovereignty is organised and exercised within states. International law recognises that a state controls the natural resources within its territory and continental shelf, but it is silent on the question of how ownership and authority are to be distributed internally: whether resources belong to the state, to the owners of the surface, to subnational units, or to communities; which organs of government may grant rights to explore and exploit them; and how the revenues they generate are to be shared. These questions are answered by constitutional law, statute, and custom, and the answers vary greatly among states.

The internal organisation of resource sovereignty has profound practical consequences. It determines who an investor must negotiate with, what kind of title the investor obtains, who receives royalties, and which courts have jurisdiction over disputes. It shapes the political economy of resource-rich states, influencing the incidence of conflict, the quality of governance, and the distribution of wealth. And it determines the extent to which affected communities can participate in decisions about resource development and share in its benefits.

The chapter proceeds in eight sections. It begins with the legal foundations of resource sovereignty, then compares models of constitutional ownership of subsurface resources. It examines the public trust doctrine, federalism and subnational authority, and the position of indigenous peoples. It analyses resource nationalism and the law of expropriation and compensation, and concludes with a comparative survey of constitutional approaches.

## 3.1 Legal Foundations of Resource Sovereignty

### Territorial Sovereignty and Resource Control

Resource sovereignty is, at its root, an incident of territorial sovereignty. A state's sovereignty over its land territory extends to the subsoil beneath it, without any fixed depth limit, and to the airspace above it. Sovereignty over the territorial sea likewise extends to the seabed and subsoil (UNCLOS, Article 2). Beyond the territorial sea, the coastal state enjoys not sovereignty but "sovereign rights" over the natural resources of the EEZ and the continental shelf — functional rights that are exclusive but limited to the purposes specified in the Convention. The distinction between sovereignty and sovereign rights is important: in the EEZ and on the continental shelf, other states enjoy freedoms of navigation, overflight, and the laying of submarine cables and pipelines, subject to the coastal state's rights.

In the classical conception, the state's territorial sovereignty entitled it to deal with its resources as it saw fit, subject only to obligations it had freely assumed and to the general rules protecting the property of aliens. The doctrine of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, discussed in Chapter 2, reinforced this conception and gave it a new political meaning in the context of decolonisation, emphasising that sovereignty over resources belongs to the people of the state and must be exercised for their development and well-being.

### The People as Bearers of Sovereignty

A significant development in the modern law of resource sovereignty is the recognition that sovereignty over natural resources is vested not merely in the state as an abstract entity but in the people. Common Article 1(2) of the International Covenants refers to the right of "all peoples" to freely dispose of their natural wealth and resources. Article 21 of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights similarly vests the right in "peoples" and requires that it be exercised "in the exclusive interest of the people". Many national constitutions declare that natural resources belong to the people, with the state acting as trustee or custodian. Article 257(6) of the Constitution of Ghana 1992, for example, vests every mineral in its natural state in the President "on behalf of, and in trust for, the people of Ghana". Article 62(1)(f) of the Constitution of Kenya 2010 classifies all minerals and mineral oils as public land, and Article 62(3) provides that such public land vests in and is held by the national government in trust for the people of Kenya.

The recognition of the people as bearers of resource sovereignty has several legal implications. It supports arguments that resource revenues must be managed transparently and for the public benefit. It provides a normative basis for the participation of citizens and communities in resource decisions. And it has been invoked to challenge contracts concluded by unrepresentative or corrupt governments, raising difficult questions about the continuity of state obligations following changes of government — an issue examined in the context of odious debt and illegitimate contracts.

### The Limits of Sovereignty

Resource sovereignty is subject to limits imposed by international law. These include:

- obligations assumed by treaty, including investment treaties, trade agreements, and environmental agreements;
- the customary obligation to prevent significant transboundary harm, recognised in *Trail Smelter* and affirmed by the International Court of Justice in its *Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons* advisory opinion and in *Pulp Mills*;
- the obligation to cooperate in the management of shared natural resources, such as transboundary watercourses and straddling petroleum deposits;
- the international minimum standard of treatment of aliens and the rules governing expropriation;
- human rights obligations, including the rights of indigenous peoples;
- obligations under the law of armed conflict regarding the exploitation of resources in occupied territory.

These limits do not negate sovereignty; they define the conditions under which it must be exercised.

## 3.2 Constitutional Ownership of Subsurface Resources

### Three Models of Ownership

National legal systems allocate ownership of subsurface resources according to three broad models.

**The regalian or domanial system (state ownership).** Under this model, which has its origins in Roman law, medieval European royal prerogatives over precious metals, and the Spanish colonial *derecho minero*, subsurface minerals and petroleum belong to the state (historically, the Crown or the sovereign) irrespective of the ownership of the surface. The state grants rights to explore and exploit through licences, concessions, or contracts. The regalian system prevails in the great majority of states, including virtually all of Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Asia, and in Europe. In the United Kingdom, petroleum in its natural condition in strata was vested in the Crown by the Petroleum (Production) Act 1934, a vesting now continued by section 2 of the Petroleum Act 1998; coal was nationalised in 1946 and is now vested in the Coal Authority; and gold and silver have long been "royal mines" belonging to the Crown.

**The accession or landowner system.** Under this model, derived from the common law maxim *cuius est solum, eius est usque ad caelum et ad inferos* ("whoever owns the soil owns it up to the heavens and down to the depths"), the owner of the surface also owns the minerals beneath it, unless they have been severed by conveyance or reserved by the state. The accession system prevails in the United States (for privately owned land) and was historically important in parts of Canada. It is the principal reason why private mineral ownership, mineral leasing between private parties, and royalty interests owned by individuals are characteristic features of American oil and gas law.

**The *res nullius* and claim systems.** Historically, some systems treated minerals as unowned until discovered and appropriated, giving rise to "free mining" or claim-staking regimes under which the first person to discover and stake a claim acquired rights. The United States General Mining Law of 1872 retains elements of this approach for hard-rock minerals on federal public lands, permitting citizens to locate claims on valuable mineral deposits. Free entry systems have been modified in many Canadian provinces to require consultation with indigenous peoples before claims are registered.

### The Rule of Capture and Its Consequences

The accession system, applied to fugacious resources such as oil and gas that migrate through porous rock, gave rise in the United States to the "rule of capture": a landowner owns all the oil and gas produced from wells drilled on his or her land, even if some of that oil and gas migrated from beneath neighbouring land. In *Westmoreland & Cambria Natural Gas Co v De Witt* (1889), the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania analogised oil and gas to wild animals (*ferae naturae*), which belong to the person who captures them. The rule of capture encouraged a race to drill, leading to wasteful over-production, the premature loss of reservoir pressure, and the dissipation of resources.

Terence Daintith's study of the rule of capture demonstrates how this legal doctrine shaped the organisation of the world oil industry, prompting the development of conservation regulation (well spacing, production prorationing, and compulsory pooling and unitisation) in the United States, and influencing the decision of many other states to adopt state ownership and licensing systems that would avoid the inefficiencies of fragmented ownership.[^daintith] The doctrine of correlative rights, developed to mitigate the rule of capture, recognises that each owner of land overlying a common reservoir has a right to a fair opportunity to produce its just and equitable share of the oil and gas, and a duty not to injure the reservoir or waste its resources.

[^daintith]: Terence Daintith, *Finders Keepers? How the Law of Capture Shaped the World Oil Industry* (RFF Press 2010).

### Constitutional Vesting Clauses

In states following the regalian model, ownership is typically vested in the state by a constitutional provision. Some illustrative examples follow.

**Nigeria.** Section 44(3) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 provides:

> Notwithstanding the foregoing provisions of this section, the entire property in and control of all minerals, mineral oils and natural gas in under or upon any land in Nigeria or in, under or upon the territorial waters and the Exclusive Economic Zone of Nigeria shall vest in the Government of the Federation and shall be managed in such manner as may be prescribed by the National Assembly.

This vesting is reiterated in section 1 of the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 and section 1 of the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007. The Land Use Act 1978 separately vests all land comprised in the territory of each state in the Governor of that state, to be held in trust and administered for the use and common benefit of all Nigerians, with holders of land enjoying rights of occupancy rather than ownership. The combination of federal ownership of minerals and state control of land, overlaid on customary tenure, generates complex legal relationships that are examined further in section 3.4.

**Mexico.** Article 27 of the Mexican Constitution of 1917 vests in the Nation direct ownership of all minerals and hydrocarbons, declares that this ownership is inalienable and imprescriptible, and — following the constitutional reforms of 2013 and their partial reversal in 2024–2025 — regulates the participation of private parties in the hydrocarbon and electricity sectors.

**Bolivia.** Article 349 of the Constitution of Bolivia 2009 declares that natural resources are the property and direct domain, indivisible and imprescriptible, of the Bolivian people, and that their administration corresponds to the state in the collective interest.

**Norway.** Norway, which has no comparable constitutional clause, relies on statute: section 1-1 of the Petroleum Act 1996 provides that the Norwegian state has the proprietary right to subsea petroleum deposits and the exclusive right to resource management.

### Consequences of the Ownership Model

The choice of ownership model has significant consequences. Under state ownership, the state is both owner and regulator; it captures the resource rent through royalties, taxes, and production shares; and it decides which areas will be opened for exploration and on what terms. The relationship between the state and the investor is typically governed by a licence, concession, or contract, and the investor's rights are those conferred by that instrument. Under the accession system, the landowner captures a substantial portion of the rent through lease bonuses and royalties; the state's revenues derive principally from taxation; and the regulator's role is primarily to prevent waste, protect correlative rights, and safeguard the environment. Political economy research suggests that the distribution of resource rents to private landowners in the United States has helped sustain public support for oil and gas development in producing regions, whereas the concentration of rents in the state under the regalian model may contribute to the disconnection between resource extraction and local benefits that characterises many producer states.

::: comparative
**Ownership and community benefit.** In the United States, a farmer in North Dakota who owns the mineral estate beneath his land may receive royalties of 12.5 to 20 per cent or more of production value from a shale well, a direct private share of the resource rent. In Nigeria, a farmer in the Niger Delta whose land overlies a producing field owns neither the petroleum nor, under the Land Use Act, the land itself in the full sense; he is entitled to compensation for surface rights and crops affected by operations, and his community may benefit from the host community development trust established under Chapter 3 of the Petroleum Industry Act 2021. The contrast illustrates how the ownership model shapes the distribution of benefits and, consequently, local attitudes towards resource development.
