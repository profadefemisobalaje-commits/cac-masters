## Introduction

Contracts are the operating system of the energy industry. A single large energy project — an offshore oil field, an LNG export terminal, an independent power plant, or a solar park — is constituted by a web of interlocking contracts: a host government agreement or licence defining the investor's rights; a production sharing contract or concession allocating the resource and its proceeds; a joint operating agreement governing the relationship among co-venturers; engineering, procurement, and construction contracts for the facilities; supply, transportation, and offtake agreements for inputs and outputs; operation and maintenance agreements; loan agreements, security documents, and direct agreements with lenders; and insurance policies. Each contract allocates risks — geological, construction, operational, market, political, regulatory, and environmental — among the parties, and the bankability and success of the project depend on the coherence of the overall allocation.

International energy contracts present distinctive legal features. Many involve states or state entities as parties, raising questions of sovereignty, the internationalisation of contracts, and the relationship between contract and public law. They are typically long-term — twenty, thirty, or more years — and must accommodate changes in economic, political, technological, and regulatory circumstances that cannot be foreseen at the time of contracting. They involve very large sums and complex technical subject matter. They are frequently negotiated on the basis of industry model forms, such as those of the Association of International Energy Negotiators (AIEN, formerly the AIPN) and the International Federation of Consulting Engineers (FIDIC), which have generated a body of transnational contractual practice. And their disputes are usually resolved by international arbitration rather than national courts.

This chapter examines contract formation and applicable law (section 15.1), concession and production sharing agreements as contractual instruments (section 15.2), engineering, procurement, and construction contracts (section 15.3), joint venture and shareholder agreements (section 15.4), force majeure and hardship (section 15.5), change-in-law and stabilisation provisions (section 15.6), indemnities, guarantees, and liability allocation (section 15.7), and contract interpretation, renegotiation, and termination (section 15.8). The licensing frameworks within which petroleum contracts operate have been examined in Chapter 5; gas sales and LNG contracts in Chapter 6; power purchase agreements in Chapter 9; the treaty-based protection of investors against regulatory change in Chapter 14; and the financing documents of energy projects in Chapter 16.

## 15.1 Contract Formation and Applicable Law

### Formation of International Energy Contracts

The formation of international energy contracts follows the general principles of the applicable law of contract, but the complexity and value of energy transactions give certain issues particular prominence.

**Preliminary agreements.** Energy transactions are typically negotiated over long periods, during which the parties may sign letters of intent, memoranda of understanding, heads of terms, term sheets, confidentiality agreements, and exclusivity agreements. The legal effect of these preliminary documents depends on the intention of the parties, as determined by the applicable law. Under English law, which governs a large proportion of international energy contracts, the question is whether the parties, objectively assessed, intended to create legal relations and had agreed all the terms they regarded, or the law requires, as essential. In *RTS Flexible Systems Ltd v Molkerei Alois Müller GmbH* [2010] UKSC 14, the Supreme Court held that a contract had been concluded on the terms agreed by the parties' conduct, notwithstanding that the parties had contemplated that the contract would not become binding until executed, because they had subsequently agreed all the essential terms and acted on them, waiving the requirement of execution. The case illustrates the risk that parties proceeding with work before signing a formal contract may become bound on uncertain terms. Documents expressed to be "subject to contract" are generally not binding, but confidentiality and exclusivity undertakings in preliminary agreements are commonly intended to be binding. Civil law systems may impose pre-contractual duties of good faith, breach of which gives rise to liability (*culpa in contrahendo*), a concept also recognised in Article 2.1.15 of the UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts, which provides that a party who negotiates or breaks off negotiations in bad faith is liable for the losses caused to the other party.

**Standard terms and the battle of the forms.** Trade in crude oil and petroleum products is conducted largely under standard terms, such as the general terms and conditions published by major trading companies (notably the BP Oil International General Terms and Conditions for sales and purchases of crude oil and petroleum products), and incorporating the International Chamber of Commerce's **Incoterms** rules, which define the delivery obligations, transfer of risk, and allocation of costs under terms such as FOB (free on board), CFR (cost and freight), CIF (cost, insurance, and freight), and DAP (delivered at place). Where parties exchange conflicting standard terms, the "battle of the forms" must be resolved by the applicable law.

**Authority and capacity of state entities.** Contracts with states and state entities raise questions of the authority of the officials who sign them and the capacity of state entities to conclude them, including whether necessary approvals — from ministries, cabinets, legislatures, or regulators — have been obtained. In some jurisdictions, petroleum and mining agreements require legislative ratification, which may give them the force of law. A failure to obtain required approvals may render the contract void or unenforceable under the law of the host state, although international tribunals have generally held that a state cannot rely on its own internal law to avoid obligations it has undertaken, a principle reflected in Article 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties and in arbitral jurisprudence. Corruption in the procurement of state contracts may render them unenforceable, as examined in Chapter 16.

### Party Autonomy and Choice of Law

The principle of **party autonomy** — the freedom of the parties to choose the law governing their contract — is recognised by virtually all legal systems for international commercial contracts. In the European Union, Article 3 of the **Rome I Regulation** (Regulation (EC) No 593/2008) provides that a contract shall be governed by the law chosen by the parties, expressly or clearly demonstrated by the terms of the contract or the circumstances of the case. In the absence of choice, Article 4 provides default rules, including that a contract for the sale of goods is governed by the law of the country of the seller's habitual residence and a contract for the provision of services by the law of the service provider's habitual residence, subject to an escape clause in favour of the law of the country with which the contract is manifestly more closely connected. The **Hague Principles on Choice of Law in International Commercial Contracts** (2015) provide a non-binding model of party autonomy that has influenced national laws.

English law is the most commonly chosen governing law for international energy contracts not involving host governments — including LNG sale and purchase agreements, EPC contracts, charterparties, and trading contracts — owing to its predictability, commercial orientation, respect for the literal terms of contracts, and the expertise of the London courts and arbitral community. New York law is also widely used, particularly in contracts involving US parties and financing. Contracts with host governments, such as petroleum agreements and concessions, are typically governed by the law of the host state, as host states generally insist that contracts relating to their natural resources be governed by their own law; in Nigeria, petroleum contracts with NNPC Ltd and the federal government are governed by Nigerian law.

### Overriding Mandatory Rules and Public Policy

Party autonomy is limited by **overriding mandatory rules** — provisions regarded as crucial by a country for safeguarding its public interests, such as its political, social, or economic organisation, to such an extent that they apply irrespective of the law otherwise applicable (Article 9(1) of the Rome I Regulation). In the energy sector, overriding mandatory rules may include sanctions and export control legislation, competition law, environmental and safety legislation, local content requirements, foreign exchange controls, and rules on the ownership of natural resources. Under Article 9(3) of Rome I, effect may be given to the overriding mandatory provisions of the law of the country where the obligations arising out of the contract are to be or have been performed, in so far as those provisions render the performance of the contract unlawful. Sanctions regimes, such as those imposed on Russia following its invasion of Ukraine, have had a profound effect on energy contracts, rendering performance unlawful for parties subject to the sanctioning jurisdictions and giving rise to disputes over force majeure, frustration, and illegality.

### The Vienna Convention on the International Sale of Goods

The **United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods** (CISG, 1980) applies to contracts of sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different contracting states, unless excluded by the parties. Article 2(f) excludes sales of electricity from its scope, but the CISG applies, in principle, to sales of crude oil, petroleum products, natural gas, coal, and other energy commodities. In practice, the CISG is commonly excluded in international energy sales contracts, partly because English law, the most common choice, is not a party to the Convention (the United Kingdom has not ratified it), and partly because parties prefer the certainty of the national law with which they are familiar. Nigeria is not a party to the CISG.

### State Contracts and Internationalisation

Contracts between states (or state entities) and foreign investors — **state contracts** — have raised the question whether they may be governed by international law or general principles of law, rather than by the law of the host state, thereby protecting the investor against unilateral changes by the host state to its own law. In the oil concession arbitrations of the mid-twentieth century, examined in Chapter 3, tribunals held that concessions could be "internationalised". In *Sapphire International Petroleums Ltd v National Iranian Oil Co* (1963), the arbitrator held that the concession was governed by general principles of law, given the absence of an express choice and the parties' evident intention to exclude the exclusive application of Iranian law. In *Texaco Overseas Petroleum Co v Libya* (1977), the sole arbitrator held that the concessions, which referred to the principles of Libyan law common to the principles of international law and, in their absence, to general principles of law, were internationalised contracts, and that Libya was bound by their stabilisation clauses under international law.

Modern practice frequently addresses the applicable law of state contracts through combined clauses — referring to the law of the host state supplemented by applicable principles of international law, or by the principles of law common to the host state and international law. Article 42(1) of the ICSID Convention provides that, in the absence of agreement by the parties, an ICSID tribunal shall apply the law of the contracting state party to the dispute and such rules of international law as may be applicable, which has been interpreted as giving international law a supplemental and corrective function.

### Transnational Rules: The UNIDROIT Principles and Lex Mercatoria

The **UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts**, first published in 1994 and revised in 2004, 2010, and 2016, are a non-binding restatement of general principles of international contract law, prepared by the International Institute for the Unification of Private Law. The Principles may apply where the parties have chosen them, where the parties have agreed that their contract shall be governed by general principles of law or the *lex mercatoria*, where the parties have not chosen any law, or as a means of interpreting or supplementing international uniform law instruments or domestic law. They are frequently referred to in international arbitration, including in energy disputes, and contain provisions of particular relevance to long-term energy contracts, including those on hardship (Articles 6.2.1 to 6.2.3) and force majeure (Article 7.1.7). The 2016 edition was revised specifically to take into account the needs of long-term contracts, adding provisions on, among other things, contracts with open terms, cooperation, and termination for compelling reasons.

The concept of a ***lex mercatoria*** — an autonomous body of transnational commercial law, consisting of trade usages, general principles, and arbitral practice — has been invoked in energy arbitrations, although its existence and content are contested. Industry model contracts, such as the AIEN model forms for joint operating agreements, farmout agreements, gas sales agreements, and other transactions, contribute to a body of transnational contractual practice that informs the interpretation of energy contracts; some scholars describe this body of practice as a *lex petrolea*.

### Arbitration Agreements and Forum Selection

International energy contracts almost invariably provide for dispute resolution by international arbitration, typically under the rules of the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA), the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC), or the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, or, for contracts with states, ICSID. The arbitration agreement is separable from the main contract, so that its validity is not affected by the invalidity or termination of the contract. In Nigeria, the **Arbitration and Mediation Act 2023**, which replaced the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1988, governs arbitration and mediation, adopting the UNCITRAL Model Law framework with modern features, including provisions on emergency arbitrators, third-party funding, and the consolidation of proceedings. Questions of arbitrability — whether a particular type of dispute, such as a tax dispute, may be resolved by arbitration — may be governed by the law of the seat or of the place of enforcement, and have arisen in Nigerian energy disputes, as examined in section 15.2. The international arbitration of energy disputes is examined in Chapter 26.
