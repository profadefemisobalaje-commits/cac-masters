## 13.8 Trade Disputes Involving Energy and Natural Resources

### An Overview of the Jurisprudence

The body of WTO jurisprudence concerning energy and natural resources has grown substantially, although energy disputes remain a small proportion of the WTO's caseload. The principal disputes, several of which have been examined in earlier sections, may be grouped thematically.

| Theme | Principal disputes | Key holdings |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental regulation of fuels | *US — Gasoline* (1996) | Clean air is an exhaustible natural resource; two-tier Article XX test; chapeau requires cooperation and even-handedness |
| Renewable energy support and local content | *Canada — Renewable Energy* (2013); *India — Solar Cells* (2016); *US — Renewable Energy* (panel, 2019) | Local content requirements violate Article III:4 and TRIMs; procurement derogation narrowly construed; benefit benchmark may be a government-created market |
| Export restrictions on raw materials | *China — Raw Materials* (2012); *China — Rare Earths* (2014); *Indonesia — Raw Materials* (panel, 2022) | Article XX unavailable for accession commitments on export duties without textual link; "even-handedness" required under XX(g); XI:2(a) limited to temporary measures |
| Biofuels and sustainability criteria | *EU — Biodiesel (Argentina)* (2016); *EU — Palm Oil (Malaysia)* (panel, 2024) | Costs distorted by export taxes cannot be disregarded in anti-dumping; ILUC-based criteria assessed under TBT Agreement |
| Energy market structure | *EU — Energy Package* (panel, 2018) | Ownership unbundling largely upheld; third-country certification inconsistent with GATS national treatment |
| Transit and security | *Russia — Traffic in Transit* (2019); *US — Steel and Aluminium* (panel, 2022) | Article XXI not wholly self-judging; objective existence of emergency reviewable |
| Trade remedies in clean energy | Numerous anti-dumping and countervailing duty disputes concerning solar products, biodiesel, and electric vehicles | Methodological questions on subsidies, dumping margins, and market distortions |

Table: Principal WTO disputes concerning energy and natural resources.

### Cross-Cutting Themes

Several themes emerge from the jurisprudence.

**Environmental objectives and trade disciplines.** The jurisprudence since *US — Gasoline* has recognised that WTO members may pursue environmental objectives, including the conservation of natural resources and the protection of health, through trade-restrictive measures, provided that the measures are genuinely directed at those objectives and are applied in an even-handed and non-arbitrary manner. However, the energy disputes have also established that environmental objectives do not justify discrimination in favour of domestic industry, as the local content cases illustrate. The tension between the climate imperative and the WTO's non-discrimination disciplines is likely to intensify as states adopt industrial policies in support of clean energy manufacturing.

**Export restrictions and resource sovereignty.** The export restriction cases reflect a tension between the principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources and the disciplines of the GATT. The Appellate Body acknowledged in *China — Rare Earths* that members retain sovereignty over their natural resources, but held that this sovereignty must be exercised consistently with their WTO obligations, which they have freely accepted. For resource-exporting developing states, the jurisprudence constrains the use of export restrictions as instruments of industrial policy.

**The extraterritorial reach of importing-state regulation.** Measures such as the EU's biofuel sustainability criteria, RFNBO rules, deforestation regulation, and CBAM regulate imports by reference to the conditions of production in exporting states. The jurisprudence, particularly *US — Shrimp*, permits such measures in principle, subject to the chapeau's requirements of flexibility and good faith negotiation, but their proliferation raises concerns among exporting states about unilateralism and the imposition of the regulatory preferences of large markets.

**Security.** The *Russia — Traffic in Transit* line of cases has opened the security exception to review, but the United States' rejection of that review, together with the increasing invocation of security in trade policy, including in energy and critical minerals, poses a significant challenge to the system.

### The Consequences of the Appellate Body Crisis

The paralysis of the Appellate Body since December 2019 has had significant consequences for energy trade disputes. Several panel reports in energy-related disputes — including *EU — Energy Package*, *US — Renewable Energy*, *Indonesia — Raw Materials*, and *US — Steel and Aluminium* — have been appealed into the void, leaving the disputes unresolved and the panel findings without binding effect. The MPIA provides an alternative for its participants, which include the European Union, China, and many other members, but not the United States, India, Indonesia, or Russia. The weakening of WTO dispute settlement has encouraged states to resort to unilateral measures, to rely on bilateral and regional dispute settlement mechanisms, and to address energy trade issues through plurilateral and sectoral arrangements, such as critical minerals agreements. Reform of the dispute settlement system was the subject of negotiations aimed at a fully functioning system, but no agreement had been reached at the time of writing.

### The Interaction of Trade and Investment Disputes

Energy measures frequently give rise to both trade and investment disputes. The Ontario feed-in tariff programme was challenged both before the WTO (*Canada — Renewable Energy*) and in investment arbitration under NAFTA (*Mesa Power v Canada*, examined in Chapter 10). The EU's Third Energy Package was challenged both before the WTO (*EU — Energy Package*) and, in the case of the Nord Stream 2 pipeline, under the Energy Charter Treaty (*Nord Stream 2 AG v European Union*). Trade and investment disputes differ in their parties (states only in the WTO, investors against states in investment arbitration), their remedies (prospective compliance in the WTO, compensation in investment arbitration), and their substantive standards, but their interaction raises questions of coherence, forum shopping, and the cumulative constraints on regulatory autonomy. Investment arbitration is examined in Chapters 14 and 26.

::: casebox {title="EU — Palm Oil (Malaysia) (WTO Panel, 2024)"}
**Background.** As examined in Chapter 10, the EU's Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) limits the extent to which biofuels made from food and feed crops with high risk of indirect land use change (ILUC) may count towards renewable energy targets. These biofuels are to be phased out by 2030. Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/807 identified palm oil as the only high-ILUC-risk feedstock. The Delegated Regulation also established criteria for certifying "low ILUC-risk" biofuels, which would be exempt from the phase-out. France additionally excluded palm oil-based biofuels from a tax incentive. Malaysia, a major palm oil producer, challenged the measures under the TBT Agreement, the GATT, and the SCM Agreement.

**Findings.** The panel, reporting in March 2024, accepted that the EU measures pursued a legitimate objective of addressing the greenhouse gas emissions associated with ILUC, a form of environmental protection. It also accepted that the EU was in principle entitled to differentiate between feedstocks on the basis of ILUC risk. However, the panel found deficiencies in certain aspects of the design and implementation of the measures, including:

- the criteria and procedures for certifying low ILUC-risk biofuels;
- the EU's failure to conduct a timely review of the data underlying the high-ILUC-risk determination.

It found these aspects inconsistent with the EU's obligations. It also found aspects of the French measure inconsistent with the GATT. Neither party appealed, and the EU undertook to bring the measures into conformity.

**Significance.** The dispute illustrates how the TBT Agreement applies to sustainability criteria based on production methods and land use impacts. It shows that WTO panels are prepared to accept environmental differentiation based on life-cycle impacts, while scrutinising the evidence, design, and procedures used to implement it. The reasoning is of potential relevance to other measures that differentiate imports by their carbon intensity or environmental footprint, including RFNBO rules, deforestation regulation, and CBAM. A parallel dispute brought by Indonesia (DS593) resulted in a panel report in early 2025.
:::

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise: The Republic of Tawana's Energy Trade Measures (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
*This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The Republic of Tawana, the Republic of Norland, and all companies named are fictional.*

The Republic of Tawana is a WTO member and a party to the AfCFTA. It is a significant producer of natural gas and an exporter of nitrogen fertilisers, 60 per cent of which are sold to the European Union. To promote industrialisation and clean energy, Tawana's government has adopted three measures.

- **Measure 1 — Solar auction with local content.** Tawana's state utility will procure 2,000 MW of solar capacity through auctions. Winning bidders receive twenty-year PPAs. Bidders must source at least 40 per cent of the value of solar modules and inverters from manufacturers located in Tawana, and those meeting a 60 per cent threshold receive a tariff premium of 5 per cent.
- **Measure 2 — Gas export duty and domestic supply obligation.** Tawana imposes an export duty of 25 per cent on LNG and requires producers to sell 30 per cent of their output to domestic fertiliser and power producers at a regulated price equal to half the export parity price. Tawana's accession protocol contains no commitments on export duties.
- **Measure 3 — Response to CBAM.** Tawana's fertiliser exporters face CBAM obligations from 2026. Tawana is considering introducing a domestic carbon tax of USD 15 per tonne on fertiliser production so that the tax can be deducted under CBAM, and challenging CBAM at the WTO.

The Republic of Norland (HYPOTHETICAL), an exporter of solar modules and a major importer of fertilisers, is considering WTO proceedings against Measures 1 and 2. Norland is also considering imposing countervailing duties on fertiliser imports from Tawana.

1. Advise Norland on the strength of claims against Measure 1 under Article III:4 of the GATT, the TRIMs Agreement, and the SCM Agreement. Could Tawana rely on Article III:8(a), Article XX, or special and differential treatment?
2. Assess the legality of Measure 2. Does the export duty violate the GATT? Could the domestic supply obligation and regulated price be treated as a countervailable subsidy to fertiliser producers in Norland's investigation, in the light of the jurisprudence on export restraints and *EU — Biodiesel*?
3. Advise Tawana on its options in relation to CBAM, including the likely WTO arguments, the advantages and disadvantages of introducing a domestic carbon tax, and the use of the revenue.
4. Consider whether the AfCFTA could support an alternative strategy for developing a regional solar manufacturing value chain that complies with Tawana's WTO obligations.
:::

::: summary
- Energy trade is characterised by geographical concentration, export-side concerns, network dependence, state ownership, and environmental externalities. Energy was historically on the margins of the GATT but has been brought within the WTO through the accession of major producers.
- Energy goods are governed by the GATT and associated agreements, and energy services by the GATS. Commitments on energy services are limited.
- The core WTO disciplines apply to energy: MFN, national treatment, the prohibition of quantitative restrictions, state trading, and freedom of transit. The general exceptions in Article XX are applied through a two-tier test established in *US — Gasoline*. The security exception in Article XXI is not wholly self-judging, following *Russia — Traffic in Transit*.
- Local content requirements in renewable energy support schemes violate Article III:4 and the TRIMs Agreement, as *Canada — Renewable Energy* and *India — Solar Cells* establish. Fossil fuel subsidies remain largely undisciplined, and trade remedies are widely used in clean energy sectors.
- Disciplines on export restrictions are weaker than those on imports, and export duties are disciplined only by specific commitments. The Chinese raw materials cases strictly construe the exceptions for export restrictions. OPEC production quotas and dual pricing remain contested.
- The TBT Agreement governs fuel standards, efficiency standards, and sustainability criteria. Measures based on processes and production methods raise unresolved questions.
- RTAs, including the USMCA, EU agreements, the Energy Community Treaty, the AfCFTA, and ECOWAS instruments, contain energy provisions that go beyond WTO rules.
- The EU CBAM imposes a carbon price on imports of carbon-intensive goods from 2026. Its compatibility with WTO law turns on national treatment, MFN, and the Article XX chapeau, and it raises questions under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.
- The Appellate Body crisis has left several energy disputes unresolved and encouraged unilateralism and alternative forums.
:::

::: research
1. Should WTO law accommodate local content requirements in clean energy support schemes? Evaluate proposals for a "climate waiver" or a revival of non-actionable subsidies for environmental purposes.
2. Do OPEC+ production quotas violate Article XI of the GATT? Assess the legal arguments and the reasons why no WTO member has challenged them.
3. Is the EU CBAM compatible with the chapeau of Article XX and with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities? Propose design modifications that would enhance its legitimacy in the eyes of developing states.
4. To what extent does the jurisprudence on export restrictions constrain resource-based industrialisation strategies in Africa? Should special and differential treatment extend to export measures?
5. How has the paralysis of the WTO Appellate Body affected the governance of international energy trade, and what alternatives — the MPIA, regional dispute settlement, or plurilateral arrangements — offer the most promise?
