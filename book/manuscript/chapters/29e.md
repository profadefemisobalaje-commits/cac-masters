::: casebox {title="Case Study: The Long Road to the Petroleum Industry Act 2021"}
**Background.** By the early 2000s, Nigeria's petroleum legislation — principally the Petroleum Act 1969, the NNPC Act 1977, and numerous subsidiary instruments — was widely regarded as outdated. The NNPC combined commercial, regulatory, and policy functions; the fiscal regime was complex and opaque; host communities had no statutory share in petroleum benefits; and the industry was beset by funding problems in the joint ventures, gas flaring, and conflict in the Niger Delta.

**Reform efforts.** In 2000, the government established the Oil and Gas Sector Reform Implementation Committee (OGIC), whose work led to a National Oil and Gas Policy (2004) and, in 2008, to the first **Petroleum Industry Bill**, an omnibus bill consolidating the legal framework. The bill was introduced in successive National Assemblies but not enacted, as disagreements over fiscal terms, the powers of the minister, the structure of NNPC, and host community benefits, together with the opposition of affected interests and the lobbying of international companies concerned about fiscal terms, stalled its progress. In 2016, the National Assembly adopted a strategy of splitting the bill into several components, beginning with the **Petroleum Industry Governance Bill**, which was passed in 2018 but not signed by the President, reportedly owing to concerns about the powers of the regulator and revenue retention.

**Enactment.** In 2020, the executive submitted a new consolidated Petroleum Industry Bill, which, after public hearings and amendment in the National Assembly, including contentious debates over the percentage of operating expenditure payable to host community trusts (eventually set at three per cent) and the allocation of thirty per cent of NNPC's profit oil and gas to a Frontier Exploration Fund, was passed and signed into law in **August 2021**.

**Analysis.** The PIA's history illustrates many themes of this chapter: the difficulty of reforms that redistribute rents among powerful interests; the role of policy windows — in this case the oil price collapse of 2020, the decline in investment, and the government's need for reform to attract capital; the trade-off between comprehensive and incremental reform strategies; the importance of legislative drafting and consultation; and the reality that enactment is the beginning, not the end, of reform. Its implementation — the commercialisation of NNPC Limited, the effectiveness of the new regulators, the functioning of host community trusts, and the response of investors — will determine whether the long road was worth travelling.
:::

::: exercise {title="Practical Exercise 29: Legislative Drafting — The Riverine State Mini-Grid and Clean Cooking Bill (HYPOTHETICAL)"}
**This exercise is entirely hypothetical. The state, institutions, data, and political circumstances described are fictitious and do not refer to any real state or legislation.**

The (fictitious) Riverine State, a state in a federal West African republic whose federal electricity law (modelled on Nigeria's Electricity Act 2023) permits states to regulate electricity markets within their territory, has two million residents, of whom fifty-five per cent lack access to grid electricity and eighty per cent cook with firewood or charcoal. Its government has resolved to establish a state electricity market focused on **mini-grids** and **off-grid solar**, and to promote **clean cooking**. It has commissioned you, as a doctoral graduate in energy law, to lead the preparation of legislation.

**Tasks.**

(a) **Problem definition and RIA.** Prepare a regulatory impact assessment (of no more than 2,500 words) defining the problem, setting measurable objectives, identifying at least three options (including a non-legislative option), and assessing their expected costs, benefits, and distributional impacts, including on women and low-income households.

(b) **Drafting instructions.** Prepare drafting instructions setting out the policy objectives and intended legal effect of the bill.

(c) **Drafting.** Draft the following provisions of a Riverine State Electricity Market and Clean Cooking Bill: (i) the objectives and guiding principles; (ii) the establishment, functions, independence, funding, and accountability of a Riverine State Electricity Regulatory Commission; (iii) a light-handed licensing or registration regime for mini-grids below a specified capacity, including provisions on what happens when the main grid arrives; (iv) tariff principles for mini-grids balancing cost recovery and affordability; (v) a clean cooking promotion framework; (vi) consultation requirements for regulations; and (vii) a review clause.

(d) **Consultation plan.** Design a consultation plan for the bill that reaches rural communities, women's groups, mini-grid developers, the distribution company currently serving the state, and the federal regulator.

## Conclusion

Energy law reform is a continuous, demanding, and essential task. Theories of regulation and the policy process explain why reforms occur, why they often fail, and how policy windows, coalitions, and entrepreneurs can drive change. The quality of reform depends on disciplined processes: regulatory impact assessment that defines problems, considers alternatives, and weighs impacts, including distributional and climate impacts; legislative drafting that achieves clarity, precision, coherence, and, above all, effectiveness, with appropriate use of framework legislation and delegated rule-making; regulatory institutions that combine independence, accountability, and capacity; consultation that is timely, inclusive, and responsive; and implementation planning and evaluation that close the gap between law and outcomes and enable learning from failure. In resource-rich economies, governance reform must confront the political economy of rents, and its success depends on domestic coalitions supported by transparency and international engagement. Nigeria's recent reforms — the PIA, the Electricity Act, subsidy removal, and tax reform — illustrate both the possibilities and the difficulties. Future-ready energy legal systems will combine long-term direction with adaptability, stability with justice, and security with access. Designing and leading such systems is a central vocation for the doctoral graduates for whom this book is written.

::: summary
- Theories of regulation (public interest, public choice, institutionalist) and of the policy process (policy cycle, multiple streams, punctuated equilibrium, advocacy coalitions, policy transfer) explain the drivers of and obstacles to energy law reform; theories of legal change (responsive, reflexive, smart, experimentalist) inform reform design; the political economy of concentrated costs and diffuse benefits shapes outcomes.
- Regulatory impact assessment involves problem definition, objectives, options, impact analysis, comparison, implementation planning, monitoring, and consultation; cost-benefit analysis is valuable but raises difficulties in valuing non-market goods, choosing discount rates, setting the social cost of carbon, and addressing distribution; Nigeria lacks a general RIA requirement.
- Good legislative drafting achieves clarity, precision, unambiguity, coherence, effectiveness, and accessibility; energy statutes follow a recognisable structure; framework legislation and delegated rule-making enhance flexibility but require accountability; drafting for the transition calls for technology neutrality, adaptive provisions, and careful definitions.
- Independent regulators address time inconsistency and capture, but require accountability and capacity; Nigeria's PIA and Electricity Act have restructured regulatory institutions, including at state level.
- Consultation improves quality and legitimacy and gives effect to participation rights; it should be timely, inclusive, informed, adequate, responsive, and accessible, as reflected in the *Gunning* principles.
- The implementation gap requires implementation planning, and policy evaluation — process, outcome, and impact — supported by theories of change, review and sunset clauses, and a culture of learning from failure.
- Governance reform in resource-rich economies addresses the award of rights, fiscal regimes, state enterprises, revenue management, regulation, community benefits, environment, transparency, and anti-corruption; sustainable reform depends on domestic coalitions.
- Future-ready energy legal systems provide long-term direction, adaptive regulation, technology neutrality, integration, investment stability, just transition, universal access, resilience, data governance, and accountability, requiring legal leadership.
:::
