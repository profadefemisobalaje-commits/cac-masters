## 22.5 Sanctions Compliance and Export Controls

### Sanctions as Instruments of Energy Geopolitics

Economic sanctions — measures restricting trade, investment, and financial transactions with targeted states, entities, and individuals — have become a central instrument of foreign policy, and the energy sector, as a principal source of revenue for many targeted states, is among their primary targets. Sanctions against Iraq, Iran, Libya, Venezuela, Syria, and Russia have profoundly affected global oil and gas markets, investment flows, and the operations of energy companies. For energy enterprises, sanctions compliance has become a core legal function, since violations may result in severe criminal and civil penalties, exclusion from the US financial system, and reputational damage. The geopolitical dimensions of sanctions are examined in Chapter 25; this section addresses their legal framework and compliance implications.

### Sources of Sanctions

**United Nations sanctions** are adopted by the Security Council under Chapter VII of the UN Charter and are binding on all member states under Article 25. Energy-related UN sanctions have included the comprehensive sanctions on Iraq from 1990, administered from 1996 to 2003 through the **Oil-for-Food Programme**, whose administration was marred by corruption documented by the Independent Inquiry Committee chaired by Paul Volcker in 2005; measures concerning illicit petroleum exports from Libya; and the sanctions on Iran relating to its nuclear programme, which were lifted in 2016 under Security Council **Resolution 2231** endorsing the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action. In August 2025, France, Germany, and the United Kingdom triggered the "snapback" mechanism of Resolution 2231, alleging significant non-performance by Iran, and the earlier UN sanctions were reimposed at the end of September 2025, a step whose validity Iran, Russia, and China disputed.

**Autonomous sanctions** are imposed by individual states and regional organisations without Security Council authorisation. The **United States** imposes sanctions under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 and specific statutes, administered principally by the Treasury Department's **Office of Foreign Assets Control** (OFAC), which maintains the **Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List** (SDN List). US persons are prohibited from dealing with designated persons, whose property within US jurisdiction is blocked; under OFAC's **fifty per cent rule**, entities owned fifty per cent or more, directly or indirectly, by blocked persons are themselves blocked. The United States also imposes **secondary sanctions**, which threaten non-US persons with penalties — such as exclusion from the US financial system — for engaging in specified transactions with targeted countries or persons, even where no US nexus exists. The **European Union** adopts restrictive measures through decisions of the Council under the Common Foreign and Security Policy and regulations under Article 215 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, which apply within the EU, to EU nationals and companies, and to business done in the EU. The **United Kingdom**, after leaving the EU, imposes sanctions under the Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018, administered by the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation.

### Sanctions on Russian Energy

The sanctions imposed on Russia following its annexation of Crimea in 2014 and, far more extensively, its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, are the most significant energy sanctions in history, given Russia's position as one of the world's largest exporters of oil, gas, and coal. The 2014 sectoral sanctions restricted financing for Russian energy companies and the export of technology for deepwater, Arctic offshore, and shale oil projects. From 2022, Western states imposed sweeping measures, including:

- an **EU embargo** on seaborne imports of Russian crude oil from December 2022 and of petroleum products from February 2023, with temporary exemptions for pipeline imports to certain member states, and a ban on imports of Russian coal from August 2022;
- a **price cap** agreed by the G7, the EU, and Australia, under which Western service providers — shipping, insurance, finance, and brokering — may support the maritime transport of Russian crude oil and products to third countries only if they are sold at or below a specified price, initially set at USD 60 per barrel for crude oil; in 2025, the EU and the United Kingdom lowered the cap and introduced a dynamic mechanism linking it to market prices;
- the designation of large numbers of vessels of the "**shadow fleet**" of ageing tankers, often with opaque ownership and inadequate insurance, used to transport Russian oil outside the price cap regime;
- restrictions on new investment in the Russian energy sector and the export of energy-related technology and equipment;
- measures against Russian LNG, including a ban on transhipment in EU ports from 2025 and steps taken by the EU from 2025 towards a legal framework to phase out imports of Russian gas, including LNG; and
- the designation by the United States, in October 2025, of the major Russian oil companies **Rosneft** and **Lukoil**, with consequences for their international assets and trading partners.

Russia responded with counter-measures, including decrees restricting the exit of Western investors, the placing of assets of Western companies under temporary administration, and prohibitions on compliance with the price cap. These measures led many Western energy companies to exit Russia, incurring substantial write-downs, and generated a wave of disputes, including claims in arbitration and national courts concerning the termination of contracts, the non-delivery of gas by Gazprom to European buyers, and the expropriation of assets, examined in Chapters 6 and 26.

### Export Controls

**Export controls** restrict the export, re-export, and transfer of goods, software, and technology for reasons of national security and foreign policy. They apply to military goods and to **dual-use** items, which have both civilian and military applications. The United States administers export controls principally under the **Export Administration Regulations**, overseen by the Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security, with extraterritorial reach to foreign-made items containing more than a specified proportion of US-origin content and items produced with certain US technology. The European Union's **Dual-Use Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2021/821) establishes a common regime, and the multilateral **Wassenaar Arrangement** coordinates national lists. Export controls on energy-related technology have been used against Russia, Iran, and Venezuela.

Export controls have also become instruments of competition over the minerals and technologies of the energy transition. **China**, which dominates the processing of many critical minerals, imposed export controls on gallium and germanium in 2023, on graphite in December 2023, on technologies for rare earth processing, and on antimony in 2024, and in 2025 announced broad controls on rare earth elements, magnets, and related technologies, prompting negotiations with the United States and other trading partners. The United States has imposed controls on the export to China of advanced semiconductors and manufacturing equipment. These measures, discussed in relation to critical minerals in Chapter 8 and to trade law in Chapter 13, raise questions about their consistency with the security exceptions of the GATT and their implications for supply chain resilience.

### Sanctions and Contracts

Sanctions frequently disrupt existing contracts, raising questions of contract law that have been litigated extensively in the English courts. Parties may rely on **sanctions clauses**, which typically permit a party to suspend or terminate performance where performance would expose it to sanctions; on **force majeure** clauses; or on the doctrine of **frustration**. In ***Lamesa Investments Ltd v Cynergy Bank Ltd*** [2020] EWCA Civ 821, the Court of Appeal held that a clause permitting a borrower to withhold payment in order to comply with any mandatory provision of law extended to US secondary sanctions, which would expose the borrower to serious penalties even though they did not directly prohibit payment. In ***MUR Shipping BV v RTI Ltd*** [2024] UKSC 18, concerning a shipping contract affected by US sanctions on the charterer's parent company, the Supreme Court held that a force majeure clause requiring the affected party to use reasonable endeavours to overcome the force majeure event did not require it to accept performance in a currency other than that provided for in the contract — reinforcing the principle of freedom of contract and commercial certainty. Sanctions may also affect the enforcement of arbitral awards and the payment of judgments, and the European Union's **Blocking Statute** (Council Regulation (EC) No 2271/96), which prohibits EU persons from complying with specified extraterritorial US sanctions, creates potential conflicts of obligation, as considered by the Court of Justice in *Bank Melli Iran v Telekom Deutschland GmbH* (Case C-124/20, 2021).

### Sanctions Compliance

An effective sanctions compliance programme for an energy enterprise requires screening of counterparties, vessels, and transactions against applicable sanctions lists, including the identification of beneficial owners to apply ownership rules; due diligence on the origin and destination of cargoes, including the detection of ship-to-ship transfers, manipulation of vessel location data, and falsified documentation that characterise sanctions evasion in oil trading; contractual protections, including sanctions representations, warranties, and termination rights; controls over the export of technology and equipment; and training and escalation procedures. OFAC's *Framework for OFAC Compliance Commitments* (2019) identifies five essential components: management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing and auditing, and training. Enforcement actions by OFAC and the Department of Justice against energy and shipping companies demonstrate that sanctions violations are treated as serious offences, and the criminalisation of sanctions violations has been extended within the European Union by Directive (EU) 2024/1226.

## 22.6 Environmental, Social, and Governance Disclosure

### The Rise of ESG Disclosure

Investors, lenders, and other stakeholders increasingly demand information on companies' environmental, social, and governance performance and risks, on the basis that such factors may affect financial performance and that investors have an interest in the impact of their investments. Disclosure began as a voluntary practice, guided by frameworks such as the **Global Reporting Initiative** (GRI), established in 1997, the **Sustainability Accounting Standards Board** (SASB), and the **CDP** (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). The proliferation of frameworks led to calls for consolidation and for mandatory, comparable, and decision-useful disclosure, particularly in relation to climate change.

### From the TCFD to the ISSB

In 2017, the **Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures** (TCFD), established by the Financial Stability Board, published recommendations for disclosure of climate-related financial risks and opportunities, organised around four pillars — governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets — and recommending the use of scenario analysis, including a 2°C or lower scenario. The TCFD recommendations were widely adopted and incorporated into mandatory requirements in several jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom, New Zealand, Japan, and Switzerland.

In 2021, the IFRS Foundation established the **International Sustainability Standards Board** (ISSB), which in June 2023 issued its first two standards: **IFRS S1** (General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information) and **IFRS S2** (Climate-related Disclosures), which incorporate the TCFD recommendations and require disclosure of Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse gas emissions, climate-related transition plans, and climate resilience assessed through scenario analysis, with industry-specific guidance derived from SASB. The ISSB standards, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024 where adopted by jurisdictions, provide a "global baseline" for investor-focused sustainability disclosure. Many jurisdictions have announced adoption or alignment, including **Nigeria**, where the Financial Reporting Council announced its adoption of the ISSB standards in 2023 with a phased roadmap — making Nigeria among the first countries to commit to the standards — and other African jurisdictions such as Ghana and Kenya.

### The European Union

The European Union has adopted the most extensive mandatory sustainability disclosure regime. The **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive** (Directive (EU) 2022/2464, CSRD), replacing the Non-Financial Reporting Directive of 2014, requires large companies and listed companies, including non-EU companies with significant EU activities, to report on sustainability matters in accordance with **European Sustainability Reporting Standards** (ESRS), on the basis of **double materiality** — covering both the financial risks and opportunities for the company arising from sustainability matters (financial materiality) and the company's impacts on people and the environment (impact materiality) — with mandatory limited assurance. In 2025, as part of an "Omnibus" simplification package, the EU postponed the application of the CSRD for companies in later waves and proposed to reduce its scope substantially and simplify the ESRS. The **EU Taxonomy Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2020/852) establishes a classification system for environmentally sustainable economic activities, with associated disclosure obligations; its inclusion, through a Complementary Delegated Act of 2022, of certain natural gas and nuclear activities as transitional activities was controversial and was challenged before the EU courts by Austria and environmental organisations. The **Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2019/2088) imposes disclosure requirements on financial market participants.

### The United States

In the United States, the **Securities and Exchange Commission** adopted rules in March 2024 requiring registrants to disclose climate-related risks with a material impact on their business, governance and risk management processes, material climate-related targets, and, for larger companies, Scope 1 and 2 emissions where material. The rules were challenged by business groups and states, stayed pending judicial review, and in March 2025 the Commission voted to end its defence of the rules. **California** enacted legislation in 2023 — the Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act (SB 253) and the Climate-Related Financial Risk Act (SB 261) — requiring large companies doing business in the state to disclose their greenhouse gas emissions, including Scope 3 emissions, and climate-related financial risks, which became the most significant climate disclosure requirements in the United States, subject to ongoing litigation.

### Disclosure Obligations of Energy Companies

For energy companies, ESG disclosure raises specific issues: the disclosure of **Scope 3 emissions** from the use of sold products, which account for the great majority of the emissions associated with oil and gas companies; the disclosure of **reserves** and the risk of "stranded assets" under transition scenarios; the credibility of **net zero targets** and **transition plans**; the disclosure of methane emissions, now subject to measurement, reporting, and verification requirements under the EU Methane Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1787), which will extend to imports of oil and gas; and the interaction between sustainability disclosures and financial statements, including the assumptions used in impairment testing and decommissioning provisions. Disclosure obligations also exist under securities laws of general application: misstatements in disclosure may give rise to liability to investors, as illustrated by securities litigation against energy companies concerning safety, reserves, and climate risks.

### Greenwashing

As companies make increasing claims about their environmental credentials, **greenwashing** — misleading claims about environmental performance — has become a significant source of legal risk. Regulators and courts have acted against misleading claims in advertising and in investment products. In the Netherlands, the District Court of Amsterdam held in March 2024 that advertisements by the airline KLM suggesting that its flights were sustainable were misleading. Advertising standards authorities in the United Kingdom and elsewhere have ruled against advertisements by energy companies that emphasised renewable energy investments while omitting the predominance of fossil fuels in their activities. In Australia, the Federal Court imposed a penalty on a superannuation fund manager in 2024 for misleading claims that its "sustainable" investment options excluded fossil fuel investments, and the Australasian Centre for Corporate Responsibility brought proceedings against the oil and gas producer **Santos**, alleging that its claims about natural gas as "clean energy" and its net zero plan were misleading. The European Union adopted the **Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive** (Directive (EU) 2024/825), which prohibits generic environmental claims that cannot be substantiated and claims based on carbon offsetting that a product has a neutral, reduced, or positive environmental impact. Energy companies must therefore ensure that their sustainability communications are accurate, substantiated, and consistent with their disclosures and business strategies.
