## 7.4 Environmental and Social Obligations

### The Environmental Footprint of Mining

Mining has profound environmental impacts across its life cycle. Exploration may involve the clearing of vegetation, the construction of access roads, and drilling. Development and operation involve the removal of overburden, the excavation of open pits or underground workings, the disposal of waste rock, the processing of ore using chemicals such as cyanide (in gold processing) and sulphuric acid (in copper heap leaching), the consumption of large volumes of water and energy, and the storage of tailings — the finely ground residue remaining after the extraction of valuable minerals — in tailings storage facilities that may be among the largest engineered structures on earth. Acid mine drainage, which occurs when sulphide minerals exposed by mining react with air and water to produce sulphuric acid that mobilises heavy metals, can contaminate watercourses for centuries. The catastrophic failure of tailings dams — at Mount Polley in British Columbia in 2014, at Fundão (Mariana) in Brazil in 2015, and at Córrego do Feijão (Brumadinho) in Brazil in 2019, the last of which killed 270 people — has demonstrated the scale of the risks.

### Environmental Impact Assessment and Permitting

Virtually all modern mining codes and environmental statutes require an environmental impact assessment (EIA), or an environmental and social impact assessment (ESIA), before a mining licence is granted or operations commence. The EIA process, examined generally in Chapter 17, requires the proponent to identify and evaluate the likely environmental and social impacts of the project, to consider alternatives, to propose measures to avoid, minimise, mitigate, and compensate for adverse impacts, and to consult the public and affected communities. The outcome is typically an environmental management plan or programme, the implementation of which becomes a condition of the licence.

In Nigeria, the Environmental Impact Assessment Act 1992 (now the Environmental Impact Assessment Act, Cap E12, Laws of the Federation of Nigeria 2004) requires an EIA for mining projects, and the Minerals and Mining Act 2007 requires the holder of a mining lease, small-scale mining lease, or quarry lease to submit an Environmental Impact Assessment statement and an Environmental Protection and Rehabilitation Programme (EPRP) to the Mines Environmental Compliance Department of the Ministry of Solid Minerals Development. The Federal Ministry of Environment administers the EIA Act, and the National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA) has issued regulations on mining and processing of coal, ores, and industrial minerals.

### Water, Biodiversity, and Protected Areas

Mining's demands on water are a growing source of conflict, particularly in arid regions such as the Atacama Desert in Chile, where copper and lithium mining compete with agriculture and indigenous communities for scarce water. Mining laws increasingly require water use permits, impose limits on extraction, and require the treatment of discharges. In Chile, the 2022 reform of the Water Code (Law No 21.435) recognised access to water for human consumption and sanitation as a human right and limited the duration of new water rights, with significant implications for mining.

Many jurisdictions prohibit or restrict mining in protected areas, such as national parks, wetlands, and areas of high biodiversity value. Colombia's prohibition of mining in páramo ecosystems, upheld by its Constitutional Court in a 2016 decision and challenged in the *Eco Oro* arbitration discussed in section 7.3, is an example. International instruments such as the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands (1971), the World Heritage Convention (1972), and the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) inform these restrictions, and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted in December 2022, sets the target of effectively conserving thirty per cent of the world's terrestrial and marine areas by 2030 (the "30 by 30" target), which may constrain the availability of land for mining. The International Council on Mining and Metals (ICMM) committed its members in 2003 not to explore or mine in World Heritage properties, a commitment that has been influential in the industry.

### Tailings Governance

The tailings dam disasters of the past decade have transformed the governance of tailings facilities. Following the Brumadinho disaster, Brazil enacted Law No 14.066 of 2020, amending its National Dam Safety Policy, which prohibits the construction of "upstream" tailings dams — a design, in which successive embankments are built on top of previously deposited tailings, that was used at both Fundão and Brumadinho — and requires the decommissioning of existing upstream dams. Chile and Peru had prohibited upstream dams in seismically active areas some years earlier.

At the international level, the **Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management** (GISTM), published in August 2020 following an independent review co-convened by the ICMM, the United Nations Environment Programme, and the Principles for Responsible Investment, establishes requirements for the safe management of tailings facilities, with the goal of zero harm to people and the environment. It requires operators to take a precautionary approach, to design and operate facilities to minimise risk, to establish accountability at board level, to engage meaningfully with affected communities, to prepare for emergencies, and to disclose information about their facilities. ICMM members committed to implementing the standard at their highest-consequence facilities by August 2023 and at all other facilities by August 2025. Although the GISTM is a voluntary industry standard, it is increasingly referenced by regulators, investors, and insurers, and its requirements may inform the standard of care in negligence litigation.

### Litigation Arising from Mining Disasters

The legal consequences of the Fundão disaster illustrate the multiple layers of liability arising from mining catastrophes. The collapse of the dam, operated by Samarco Mineração SA, a joint venture between Vale and BHP, released about forty million cubic metres of tailings into the Doce River basin, killing nineteen people and causing extensive environmental and economic damage along hundreds of kilometres of river to the Atlantic coast. In Brazil, the companies entered into a settlement in 2016 establishing the Renova Foundation to carry out remediation and compensation programmes, and in October 2024 the companies and Brazilian public authorities signed a revised settlement valued at approximately BRL 170 billion. In England, more than six hundred thousand claimants, including municipalities, businesses, and individuals, brought proceedings against BHP's English and Australian parent companies. The Court of Appeal in *Municipio de Mariana v BHP Group plc* (2022) reversed the High Court's decision striking out the claims as an abuse of process, allowing them to proceed,[^mariana] and a liability trial took place in 2024 and 2025. The case is one of the largest group actions ever brought in the English courts and is examined further, alongside parent company liability generally, in Chapters 21 and 27.

[^mariana]: *Municipio de Mariana v BHP Group (UK) Ltd* [2022] EWCA Civ 951.

### Social Obligations

Mining codes and licences impose a range of social obligations on title holders, including obligations to employ and train nationals, to procure goods and services locally, to respect labour and occupational health and safety standards, to protect cultural heritage, and to contribute to community development. The destruction by Rio Tinto in May 2020 of the 46,000-year-old rock shelters at Juukan Gorge in the Pilbara region of Western Australia, sacred to the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura peoples, although lawful under a ministerial consent granted under the Aboriginal Heritage Act 1972 (WA), led to the resignation of Rio Tinto's chief executive, a parliamentary inquiry, and the enactment of the Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2021 (WA). That Act was itself repealed in 2023 after objections from landholders that its requirements were unworkable, and the 1972 Act was restored with amendments. The episode illustrates the gap between legal compliance and social acceptability, and the speed with which reputational and investor pressures can transform corporate practice.

Occupational health and safety in mining is governed by domestic legislation and by international standards, notably the ILO Safety and Health in Mines Convention 1995 (No 176), which requires ratifying states to adopt and enforce laws ensuring safe working conditions in mines, to establish inspection systems, and to confer rights on workers, including the right to remove themselves from danger. Labour rights in extractive industries are examined in Chapter 21.

## 7.5 Community Development and Benefit Sharing

### The Rationale for Benefit Sharing

Communities located near mining operations bear a disproportionate share of the costs of mining — loss of land and livelihoods, environmental degradation, social disruption, and health risks — while the principal benefits, in the form of royalties and taxes, accrue to the central government. This mismatch fuels conflict between companies, communities, and governments, and has led to the development of legal mechanisms for sharing the benefits of mining with affected communities. Benefit sharing may take the form of fiscal transfers from central to local government, direct payments or equity participation for communities, local employment and procurement commitments, and investments in community infrastructure and services. These mechanisms aim not only to compensate communities for the costs they bear but also to secure their support for mining operations — the "social licence to operate".

### Community Development Agreements

The community development agreement (CDA) — also called an impact and benefit agreement (IBA) in Canada, an indigenous land use agreement in Australia, or a social and labour plan in South Africa — is a contract between a mining company and a host community that sets out the company's obligations to the community and the community's support for the project. CDAs may be voluntary or mandated by law.

Section 116 of Nigeria's Minerals and Mining Act 2007 requires the holder of a mining lease, small-scale mining lease, or quarry lease to conclude a community development agreement with the host community before commencing development. The CDA must contain undertakings with respect to the social and economic contribution that the project will make to the sustainability of the community, and may address educational scholarships, apprenticeships, technical training, and employment opportunities for indigenes of the community; financial or other forms of contributory support for infrastructural development and maintenance, such as education, health, or other community services, roads, water, and power; assistance with the creation, development, and support of small-scale and micro-enterprises; agricultural product marketing; and methods and procedures of environmental and socio-economic management and local governance enhancement. The agreement must be reviewed every five years, and the Mines Inspectorate Department of the Ministry monitors compliance. The Nigerian Minerals and Mining Regulations 2011 elaborate the procedures for negotiating CDAs.

The CDA model has been adopted in many other African mining codes, including those of Sierra Leone (Mines and Minerals Act 2009), Guinea (Mining Code 2011, as amended in 2013), Kenya (Mining Act 2016), and Mozambique. The Kenyan Mining Act 2016 also provides that a share of royalties shall be distributed to county governments and communities: seventy per cent to the national government, twenty per cent to the county government, and ten per cent to the community where the mining operations occur.

### Challenges in Negotiating and Implementing CDAs

The effectiveness of CDAs depends on several factors that are frequently absent in practice. The first is the identification of the community: mining impacts may extend across multiple villages, ethnic groups, and administrative units, and the determination of which communities are parties to the agreement, and who represents them, is contentious. Traditional leaders may lack democratic legitimacy or may capture benefits for themselves or their families, and women, youth, and marginalised groups may be excluded. The second is capacity: communities typically lack the technical, legal, and financial expertise to negotiate effectively with mining companies, and the law rarely provides for independent advice to be funded. The third is enforceability: CDAs are often vaguely worded, lacking clear obligations, timeframes, and dispute resolution mechanisms, and communities may lack the means to enforce them through the courts. The fourth is sustainability: community development projects may be poorly designed, may create dependency, or may cease when the mine closes.

Good practice guidance, such as the World Bank's *Mining Community Development Agreements: Source Book* (2012), emphasises the importance of inclusive representation, independent advice for communities, clear and measurable commitments, transparent governance of community funds, monitoring and evaluation, and accessible grievance mechanisms.

### Fiscal Decentralisation and Revenue Sharing

In addition to direct agreements, benefit sharing occurs through the allocation of mineral revenues to subnational governments. Nigeria's federal revenue allocation formula provides for thirteen per cent of revenues derived from natural resources to be allocated to the states from which they derive (the "derivation principle" in the proviso to section 162(2) of the Constitution), although in practice the derivation principle has been applied principally to oil revenues. In Ghana, the Minerals Development Fund Act 2016 (Act 912) provides for a share of mineral royalties to be allocated to mining communities and local authorities. In Peru, the *canon minero* allocates fifty per cent of the income tax paid by mining companies to regional and local governments in the producing areas. The design of revenue sharing must balance the claims of producing regions against those of the nation as a whole, and must address the capacity of subnational governments to manage revenues effectively. Resource revenue distribution is examined further in Chapter 23.

### Equity Participation by Communities

Some regimes provide for community equity participation in mining projects. South Africa's Mining Charter, issued under the MPRDA, has required black economic empowerment ownership of mining companies, including a share for host communities and employees. The 2018 Mining Charter required new mining rights to have a minimum of thirty per cent black economic empowerment shareholding, including a carried interest of five per cent for host communities. In *Minerals Council of South Africa v Minister of Mineral Resources and Energy* (2021), the High Court held that the Mining Charter was a policy instrument rather than binding legislation and set aside several of its provisions, including those purporting to require the continuation of empowerment ownership after historical transactions had been completed.[^minerals] The case illustrates the limits of policy instruments as a basis for legally binding obligations.

[^minerals]: *Minerals Council of South Africa v Minister of Mineral Resources and Energy* (High Court, Gauteng Division, Pretoria, 21 September 2021).
