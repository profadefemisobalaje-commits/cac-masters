## 5.5 Joint Operating Agreements

### Purpose of the Joint Operating Agreement

Petroleum titles are commonly held by several companies jointly, in order to spread risk, share capital costs, combine technical capabilities, and satisfy state participation requirements. The relationship among the co-venturers is governed by a joint operating agreement (JOA), a contract that establishes the rules for conducting joint operations, sharing costs and production, making decisions, and addressing default and withdrawal. The JOA does not usually create a partnership or a separate legal entity; most JOAs expressly provide that the parties' rights and obligations are several and not joint or collective, and that nothing in the agreement shall be construed as creating a partnership. The co-venturers hold undivided interests in the title as tenants in common (or the civil law equivalent), each entitled to take and separately dispose of its share of production.

The most widely used international model is the AIEN Model International Operating Agreement, first published in 1990 and most recently revised in 2012 (with subsequent updates). National models also exist, notably the UK Oil and Gas Authority (now NSTA) Master Deed and model JOAs developed under the industry's Standard Agreements initiative. In Nigeria, the historic NNPC joint ventures with international oil companies were governed by JOAs based in part on the AIEN model and in part on bespoke terms.

### Key Provisions

**Scope and participating interests.** The JOA defines the joint property, the contract area, and each party's participating interest, which determines its share of costs, production, and voting power.

**The operator.** One party is designated as operator, responsible for conducting joint operations on behalf of all parties. The operator acts as an agent of the non-operators in some respects but is typically not entitled to profit from its role, recovering its costs through the joint account (including overhead charges) on a "no gain, no loss" basis. The JOA defines the operator's duties — commonly to conduct operations in a diligent, safe, and efficient manner in accordance with good oilfield practice — and limits its liability. A key feature of most international JOAs is that the operator is liable to the non-operators only for losses resulting from its "gross negligence or wilful misconduct", and in some forms only where such conduct is that of senior supervisory personnel. The interpretation of these terms has been considered in English cases, and their meaning depends heavily on the precise contractual wording.

**Operating committee.** Decisions on work programmes, budgets, and major operational matters are taken by an operating committee in which each party is represented. The JOA sets out voting thresholds, often requiring a majority of participating interests together with a minimum number of parties ("pass marks"), and identifies matters requiring unanimity.

**Work programmes, budgets, and authorisations for expenditure.** The operator prepares annual work programmes and budgets for approval, and individual items of expenditure above specified thresholds require authorisations for expenditure (AFEs).

**Sole risk and non-consent operations.** Where a party wishes to undertake an operation that the operating committee has not approved, the JOA may permit it to proceed at its sole risk and cost. A party that elects not to participate in an approved operation ("non-consent") may be excluded from the benefits of that operation unless it pays a substantial premium — often several hundred per cent of the costs it avoided — to re-enter. These provisions balance the interests of parties wishing to proceed against those of parties wishing to limit risk.

**Default.** If a party fails to pay its share of costs ("cash calls"), the JOA provides remedies for the non-defaulting parties, which may include suspension of the defaulting party's rights to vote and to receive production, and ultimately forfeiture of its participating interest. Forfeiture provisions have been challenged as penalties. In *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi* and *ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis*, decided together in 2015, the United Kingdom Supreme Court reformulated the rule against penalties, holding that the test is whether the provision is a secondary obligation that imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in the enforcement of the primary obligation.[^cavendish] In the oil and gas context, the legitimate interest of non-defaulting parties in ensuring the continuation of joint operations has generally been regarded as supporting the enforceability of forfeiture clauses, although each clause must be assessed on its terms.

[^cavendish]: *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi; ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67, [2016] AC 1172.

**Transfer and pre-emption.** The JOA restricts transfers of participating interests, commonly requiring the consent of the other parties (not to be unreasonably withheld) and granting them pre-emption rights. Pre-emption provisions have been a frequent source of disputes, particularly regarding whether they are triggered by indirect transfers through changes of control of a party.

**Withdrawal, decommissioning, and confidentiality.** The JOA governs the right of a party to withdraw, the allocation of decommissioning costs (often addressed in a separate decommissioning security agreement), confidentiality, and dispute resolution.

### Joint Ventures with National Oil Companies

Where the state participates in upstream operations through its national oil company, the JOA governs the relationship between the national oil company and its private partners. In Nigeria, the joint venture arrangements between NNPC and international oil companies such as Shell, ExxonMobil, Chevron, TotalEnergies, and Eni historically required NNPC to fund its share of costs (typically 55 to 60 per cent) through cash calls. Chronic failure by NNPC to meet its cash call obligations, owing to budgetary constraints, led to underinvestment and the accumulation of arrears, prompting the development of alternative funding arrangements, including modified carry agreements, under which the private partners fund NNPC's share and recover it from production. The PIA's provision for the conversion of NNPC joint ventures into incorporated joint ventures, which could raise finance independently, was intended in part to address the cash call problem.

## 5.6 National Oil Companies and State Participation

### Forms of State Participation

States participate in upstream petroleum in several ways:

1. **Full state operation**, in which the national oil company holds and operates all petroleum rights, as in Saudi Arabia (Saudi Aramco), Kuwait (Kuwait Petroleum Corporation), and Mexico (Pemex, historically).
2. **Majority or minority participation in joint ventures**, in which the national oil company holds an equity interest alongside private partners, either funding its share (paid participation) or being carried by the private partners through exploration (carried interest), as in Nigeria's joint ventures, Angola, and Ghana, where the Ghana National Petroleum Corporation holds a carried interest of not less than 15 per cent in petroleum agreements under the Petroleum (Exploration and Production) Act 2016 (Act 919).
3. **Contractual participation**, in which the national oil company is the contracting party under a PSC or service contract and participates in management through the management committee.
4. **Indirect participation through state-owned commercial companies or holding companies** that hold the state's direct financial interests, as in Norway's Petoro.

### The Dual Role Problem

National oil companies frequently combine commercial and governmental functions: they may hold equity interests in joint ventures, act as the state's concessionaire or contracting party under PSCs, perform regulatory functions such as approving work programmes or monitoring compliance, market the state's share of production, and perform social or political functions such as fuel supply and subsidy administration. This combination of roles creates conflicts of interest and impairs both commercial performance and regulatory effectiveness. A national oil company that is both a partner in a joint venture and the regulator of its partners cannot regulate impartially; one that is required to supply fuel at subsidised prices cannot operate on commercial principles.

The separation of the commercial and regulatory roles of national oil companies has been a central objective of petroleum sector reform, as discussed in Chapter 4 in the context of the Norwegian model and the PIA. The PIA's creation of NNPC Ltd as a commercial company without regulatory functions, and its vesting of regulatory functions in the NUPRC and NMDPRA, represents Nigeria's response to the dual role problem.

### National Oil Companies Abroad

Many national oil companies have become international investors in their own right, operating outside their home states. Companies such as Saudi Aramco, Petronas, the China National Petroleum Corporation, the China National Offshore Oil Corporation, Equinor, and Petrobras hold significant upstream interests abroad. When national oil companies invest abroad, questions arise about their status as investors under investment treaties, their entitlement to sovereign immunity, and their susceptibility to the host state's competition and foreign investment screening laws. The ICSID Convention permits claims by "nationals of another Contracting State", and tribunals have held that state-owned companies may qualify as nationals unless they are acting as agents of the government or discharging an essentially governmental function — the test proposed by Aron Broches and applied in *Ceskoslovenska Obchodni Banka AS v Slovak Republic* (1999) and subsequent cases. In *Beijing Urban Construction Group Co Ltd v Republic of Yemen* (2017), the tribunal applied the Broches test to hold that a Chinese state-owned enterprise was a national for the purposes of the Convention.

## 5.7 Unitization and Cross-Border Reservoir Development

### The Problem of Shared Reservoirs

Petroleum reservoirs do not respect licence boundaries or international borders. Where a reservoir straddles the boundary between two licence areas, or between two states, independent development by each owner would lead to the inefficiencies associated with the rule of capture: competitive drilling, wasteful duplication of infrastructure, loss of reservoir pressure, and reduced ultimate recovery. The solution is unitisation: the development of the reservoir as a single unit, with costs and production shared among the owners according to agreed "tract participation" percentages reflecting the proportion of the reservoir located within each area.

### Domestic Unitisation

Within a single state, unitisation of a reservoir straddling licence boundaries may be achieved voluntarily or compelled by the regulator. Most petroleum regimes empower the regulator to require unitisation where it is necessary to secure the most effective recovery of petroleum. In the United Kingdom, the model clauses empower the Secretary of State (now acting through the NSTA) to require licensees to cooperate in the development of a field extending beyond the licensed area. In Nigeria, the PIA empowers the NUPRC to direct unitisation where a petroleum reservoir extends across licence boundaries and the holders cannot agree.

A unitisation and unit operating agreement (UUOA) establishes the unit, determines tract participations, appoints a unit operator, and provides for redeterminations of tract participations as geological knowledge improves. Redeterminations are a frequent source of disputes, because a change in tract participation can transfer very large values between the parties. UUOAs therefore contain detailed procedures for redetermination, often involving independent experts whose decisions are final and binding except for manifest error or fraud.

### Transboundary Unitisation

Where a reservoir straddles an international boundary, unitisation requires cooperation between the states concerned. Customary international law does not clearly establish an obligation to unitise, but state practice, reflected in numerous bilateral agreements, demonstrates a strong preference for cooperation. Many maritime boundary agreements include a "mineral deposits clause", first appearing in the 1965 Agreement between the United Kingdom and Norway relating to the Delimitation of the Continental Shelf, which obliges the parties, where a single geological petroleum structure or field extends across the dividing line, to seek to reach agreement as to the manner in which the structure or field shall be most effectively exploited and the manner in which the proceeds shall be apportioned.

The Framework Agreement concerning Cross-Boundary Petroleum Co-operation between Norway and the United Kingdom of 2005 provides a comprehensive regime for the development of cross-boundary fields, including procedures for approval of unitisation agreements by both governments, apportionment of reserves, and the use of infrastructure on one side of the boundary to develop fields on the other. Similar arrangements exist in the Gulf of Mexico under the Agreement between the United States and Mexico concerning Transboundary Hydrocarbon Reservoirs in the Gulf of Mexico (2012).

### Joint Development Zones

Where states cannot agree on a maritime boundary, they may establish a joint development zone (JDZ) in the disputed area, allowing petroleum activities to proceed without prejudice to their respective claims. The Nigeria–São Tomé and Príncipe Joint Development Zone, established by a treaty signed in Abuja in 2001, is administered by a Joint Ministerial Council and a Joint Development Authority, with revenues shared 60:40 in favour of Nigeria. Other examples include the Malaysia–Thailand Joint Development Area (1979) and the arrangements in the Timor Sea between Australia and Timor-Leste, which were superseded by the maritime boundary treaty of 2018 concluded following the first-ever compulsory conciliation under Annex V of UNCLOS. Joint development is examined further in Chapter 20.
