## 25.3 Strategic Petroleum Reserves and Supply Security

### The Rationale for Emergency Stocks

**Emergency oil stocks** are the principal instrument by which importing states protect themselves against sudden supply disruptions. By holding reserves of crude oil and refined products that can be released in an emergency, states can replace lost supply, dampen price spikes, and reduce the leverage of suppliers who might use supply interruption as a weapon. Stocks may be held by governments (**public stocks**), by specialised agencies (**agency stocks**), or by industry under legal obligation (**obligated industry stocks**). The legal framework of emergency stocks operates at three levels: the international obligations of members of the International Energy Agency, regional obligations in the European Union, and national legislation establishing and governing reserves.

### The International Energy Program

The **International Energy Agency** (IEA) was established in November 1974 by the **Agreement on an International Energy Program** (IEP Agreement), adopted within the framework of the OECD in response to the oil crisis of 1973–1974. The IEP Agreement requires each participating country to maintain emergency reserves sufficient to sustain consumption for at least sixty days with no net oil imports, an obligation increased to **ninety days of net oil imports** in 1976. Net exporters, such as Canada, Mexico, and Norway, are not subject to the stockholding obligation. The Agreement establishes an **emergency sharing system**, under which, in the event of a supply reduction of at least seven per cent of normal supply for the group or any member, participating countries are to restrain demand and share available oil according to a prescribed formula, with an elaborate allocation mechanism. The emergency sharing system has never been formally activated; instead, the IEA developed the more flexible **Coordinated Emergency Response Measures** (CERM) in 1984, under which members may agree on coordinated stock releases and demand restraint in response to actual or imminent disruptions without triggering the formal sharing mechanism.

The IEA has coordinated **collective actions** on five occasions: during the Gulf War in 1991; after Hurricanes Katrina and Rita damaged US Gulf of Mexico production and refining in 2005; during the Libyan civil war in 2011; and twice in 2022, in March and April, in response to the Russian invasion of Ukraine, when members agreed to release a total of more than 180 million barrels — the largest collective actions in the IEA's history, with the United States separately announcing the release of 180 million barrels from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve over six months. The IEA's membership comprises some thirty OECD states, and it has developed **association** arrangements with major emerging economies, including China, India, Indonesia, and South Africa, and has expanded its mandate to encompass gas and electricity security, critical minerals, and clean energy transitions, under a ministerial mandate of 2022 reflecting the evolution of energy security concerns.

### The European Union

The European Union's stockholding obligations are set out in **Council Directive 2009/119/EC**, which requires member states to maintain total oil stocks corresponding to at least ninety days of average daily net imports or sixty-one days of average daily inland consumption, whichever is greater. Member states may hold stocks through a **central stockholding entity**, through obligated economic operators, or both, and stocks may be held in other member states under bilateral arrangements. The Commission monitors compliance and may convene a coordination group in the event of a supply disruption.

The European Union's response to the gas crisis of 2022 extended the logic of strategic reserves to **natural gas**. **Regulation (EU) 2022/1032** amended the Gas Security of Supply Regulation to require member states to fill their underground gas storage facilities to at least ninety per cent of capacity by 1 November each year, with intermediate targets, and introduced the certification of storage system operators to address the risk of control by third-country entities — a response to the low filling levels of storage facilities controlled by Gazprom in 2021. In 2025, the regulation was extended for two years with greater flexibility, allowing member states to meet the ninety per cent target at any time between 1 October and 1 December and to deviate from it under specified market conditions. The EU also established the **AggregateEU** mechanism for the joint purchasing of gas, under Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2576, to leverage the collective bargaining power of member states.

### National Reserves

The **United States Strategic Petroleum Reserve** (SPR) was established by the **Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975** and is held in salt caverns along the Gulf Coast, with a capacity of more than 700 million barrels. The President may order a drawdown upon a finding that a "severe energy supply interruption" exists or is imminent or that a drawdown is required by the IEP Agreement, and Congress has also mandated sales from the SPR to raise revenue, a practice criticised as undermining its security purpose. The SPR's level fell substantially after the 2022 releases, and replenishment has been an ongoing policy issue. Other importing states maintain significant reserves: **Japan** holds government and industry stocks covering well over the IEA minimum; **China** has built substantial strategic and commercial reserves since the 2000s, whose size it does not fully disclose; and **India** has established strategic reserves in underground rock caverns through the Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited, including partnerships with producing states such as the United Arab Emirates under which the partner stores oil in Indian facilities with India having priority access in an emergency.

### Stocks in Africa and Nigeria

Few African states maintain strategic stocks meeting international standards, leaving them vulnerable to supply disruptions and price spikes, as experienced during the crises of 2022. **South Africa** maintains strategic crude oil stocks through the **Strategic Fuel Fund**, whose controversial sale of a large part of its reserves in 2015 was challenged by the Minister of Energy and declared unlawful by the Western Cape High Court, which set aside the sale contracts as having been concluded without proper authorisation. **Nigeria** has historically held limited strategic reserves of refined products, despite periodic proposals to establish a strategic petroleum reserve, and episodes of fuel scarcity have exposed the vulnerability of supply. The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 empowers the NMDPRA to regulate the maintenance of stocks, including strategic stocks, and the increase in domestic refining capacity may facilitate the development of national reserves. At the regional level, proposals for ECOWAS or African Union strategic stocks have been discussed, and the African Refiners and Distributors Association has promoted harmonised fuel specifications and supply security.

### The Transition and New Reserves

The energy transition is generating debate about **reserves of critical minerals**. The United States maintains the **National Defense Stockpile**, which holds strategic materials, including certain critical minerals, for defence purposes, and Japan's JOGMEC manages stockpiles of rare metals. The **European Critical Raw Materials Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252), examined in Chapter 8, requires the Commission and member states to coordinate strategic stockpiling of critical raw materials and provides for joint purchasing, and the EU has developed a **RESourceEU** plan, announced in 2025, which includes pilot stockpiling and a European critical raw materials centre. China maintains state reserves of critical minerals. These developments extend the logic of the IEA's emergency stocks system to the materials of the energy transition, though the heterogeneity of mineral markets complicates its application.

## 25.4 Pipeline Politics and Cross-Border Energy Dependencies

### The Geopolitics of Fixed Infrastructure

Pipelines create **mutual dependence** between producers, transit states, and consumers. Unlike oil shipped by tanker, which can be redirected to alternative markets, gas transported by pipeline is tied to the route and its counterparties, so that pipelines lock in long-term commercial and political relationships. This interdependence can promote cooperation, as the proponents of the Soviet–West European gas trade argued during the Cold War, but it can also create vulnerabilities and leverage, which may be used for political ends. The legal framework of transit, including Article V of the GATT, Article 7 of the Energy Charter Treaty, and intergovernmental and host government agreements, has been examined in Chapter 6; this section examines the geopolitical dimensions of pipeline dependencies and the legal responses to them.

### Russia, Ukraine, and Europe

The history of Russian gas supplies to Europe through Ukraine illustrates the interaction of commerce, politics, and law in pipeline relationships. Disputes between Gazprom and Naftogaz over prices, transit fees, and debts led to interruptions of supplies in January 2006 and, more severely, in January 2009, when supplies to south-eastern Europe were cut off for about two weeks in mid-winter. The 2009 crisis prompted the European Union to strengthen its security of supply legislation, investing in interconnectors and reverse-flow capacity, and to support Ukraine's integration into the EU energy market through the **Energy Community**. The 2009 contracts between Gazprom and Naftogaz, concluded in the aftermath of the crisis, became the subject of arbitrations at the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce, which in 2017 and 2018 resulted in awards that, on balance, required Gazprom to pay Naftogaz approximately USD 2.56 billion, as noted in Chapter 6. A new five-year transit contract was concluded in December 2019, under EU mediation. Russia's construction of pipelines bypassing Ukraine — **Nord Stream** (2011) under the Baltic Sea to Germany, **TurkStream** (2020) under the Black Sea to Turkey, and **Nord Stream 2**, completed in 2021 but never brought into operation — reduced Ukraine's role as a transit state and was opposed by Ukraine, Poland, the Baltic states, and the United States as increasing European dependence on Russia.

Following the invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Russia progressively reduced pipeline gas supplies to Europe, halting supplies to several states that refused to pay in roubles and reducing flows through Nord Stream before stopping them altogether in August 2022, citing technical problems. In **September 2022**, explosions destroyed three of the four strings of the Nord Stream pipelines in the exclusive economic zones of Denmark and Sweden, an act of sabotage whose perpetrators remain the subject of criminal investigation and contested attribution; Sweden and Denmark closed their investigations, while Germany continued its investigation and, in 2025, sought the extradition of a Ukrainian national arrested in Italy. The **transit contract** through Ukraine expired at the end of 2024 and was not renewed, ending Russian gas transit through Ukraine on 1 January 2025. The European Union, through the **REPowerEU** plan and subsequent legislation, has committed to phase out Russian gas imports, agreeing in 2025 on legislation to prohibit imports of Russian pipeline gas and LNG under new contracts and to phase out imports under existing contracts by 2027. The Russian share of EU gas imports fell from around forty per cent in 2021 to below a fifth, with supplies replaced by Norwegian pipeline gas and LNG, particularly from the United States.

### Russia's Pivot to Asia

Russia has sought to redirect its gas exports to Asia. The **Power of Siberia** pipeline, which began deliveries to China in December 2019 under a thirty-year contract between Gazprom and CNPC, has a capacity of thirty-eight billion cubic metres per year. In September 2025, Gazprom and CNPC signed a legally binding memorandum on the construction of the **Power of Siberia 2** pipeline through Mongolia, though key commercial terms, including price, were reported to remain unresolved. The shift illustrates how pipeline politics follows geopolitical realignment, and how the bargaining power of the parties shifts when a supplier loses its principal market.

### Other Pipeline Dependencies

Pipeline politics is not confined to Russia. The **Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan** oil pipeline (2006) and the **Southern Gas Corridor**, comprising the South Caucasus Pipeline, the Trans-Anatolian Pipeline (TANAP), and the Trans-Adriatic Pipeline (TAP), were developed with Western support to transport Caspian resources to Western markets while bypassing Russia and Iran, under intergovernmental and host government agreements examined in Chapter 6. The **Druzhba** oil pipeline continues to supply Russian crude to Hungary and Slovakia under exemptions from the EU embargo, and has been subject to periodic interruptions and to Ukrainian attacks on infrastructure in Russia. The **Iraq–Turkey Pipeline**, examined in Chapter 24, illustrates the interaction of federal disputes and transit agreements. In the Middle East, the **Dolphin** gas pipeline from Qatar to the United Arab Emirates and Oman continued to operate during the blockade of Qatar by Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt between 2017 and 2021, illustrating the resilience of some energy relationships in political crises. The **East Mediterranean** gas discoveries have led to new interdependencies, including exports from Israel to Egypt and Jordan, expanded under a major supply agreement concluded in 2025, and to disputes over maritime boundaries, examined in Chapter 20.

### African Pipelines

In Africa, pipelines have both connected and divided. The **West African Gas Pipeline**, which has transported Nigerian gas to Benin, Togo, and Ghana since 2010 under a treaty of 2003 among the four states, has suffered from supply interruptions and payment disputes, illustrating the challenges of regional gas trade. The **Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline**, a proposed offshore and onshore pipeline traversing up to thirteen West African states, and the **Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline**, proposed to connect Nigeria to Algeria through Niger, have been promoted as means of supplying gas to West Africa and Europe; both face formidable financial, technical, and security challenges, and their realisation would require complex intergovernmental and host government agreements. The pipeline transporting **South Sudan**'s oil through Sudan to Port Sudan illustrates the vulnerability of landlocked producers: South Sudan shut down production in 2012 in a dispute with Sudan over transit fees, and exports were disrupted again in 2024 by the war in Sudan. The **East African Crude Oil Pipeline**, examined in Chapters 6 and 21, will make Uganda's oil exports dependent on transit through Tanzania under an intergovernmental agreement and host government agreements.

### Legal Responses to Pipeline Dependence

The legal responses to pipeline dependence include: (a) **diversification requirements**, such as the EU's infrastructure standard under the Gas Security of Supply Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/1938), which requires member states to ensure that, in the event of a disruption of the single largest gas infrastructure, the remaining infrastructure can satisfy total gas demand on a day of exceptionally high demand (the "N-1" standard); (b) **solidarity mechanisms**, under which member states must assist each other in severe emergencies, as provided in the same regulation and given legal force by the principle of energy solidarity in Article 194 TFEU, applied by the Court of Justice in *Germany v Poland* (2021), examined in Chapter 24; (c) **regulation of third-country pipelines**, through the extension of EU unbundling and third-party access rules to pipelines from third countries by Directive (EU) 2019/692, which was challenged by Nord Stream 2 AG before the EU courts and in an Energy Charter Treaty arbitration; (d) **investment screening** of foreign acquisitions of energy infrastructure, examined in section 25.8; and (e) **transit dispute resolution**, including the conciliation mechanism of Article 7(7) of the Energy Charter Treaty, which has never been used.
