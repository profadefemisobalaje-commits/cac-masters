## Introduction

International investment law has been shaped, more than by any other sector, by disputes over energy and natural resources. The nationalisations of foreign oil concessions in Mexico in 1938, Iran in 1951, and Libya in the 1970s generated the foundational controversies over the standard of compensation for expropriation, examined in Chapter 3. The arbitrations arising from those nationalisations — *Anglo-Iranian Oil Co*, *Aramco*, *Texaco*, *BP*, *LIAMCO*, and *Aminoil* — established principles on the internationalisation of contracts and stabilisation clauses that continue to influence the law. In the modern era of investment treaty arbitration, the energy sector accounts for a larger share of disputes than any other: oil, gas, and mining, together with electricity and other energy, account for a substantial proportion — around two-fifths in recent years — of the cases registered at the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID), and many of the largest awards in the history of investment arbitration, including the awards of around USD 50 billion against Russia in the *Yukos* cases and of several billion dollars against Venezuela, have concerned energy investments.

The reasons for this prominence lie in the characteristics of energy investment. Energy projects are capital-intensive, with high upfront costs and long payback periods; once constructed, they are immobile and cannot be relocated if conditions deteriorate. These features create what economists call the **"obsolescing bargain"**: before an investment is made, the investor has bargaining power, because the host state needs its capital and technology; once the investment is sunk, bargaining power shifts to the host state, which may be tempted to change the terms — through taxation, regulation, renegotiation, or expropriation — to capture a greater share of the returns, especially when commodity prices rise. Energy is also politically salient, closely connected with sovereignty and national identity, and subject to frequent regulatory intervention for reasons of security, affordability, and environmental protection. International investment law seeks to mitigate the obsolescing bargain by providing investors with enforceable international legal protections against certain forms of state conduct.

At the same time, investment law has become intensely contested. Critics argue that investment treaties constrain the regulatory autonomy of states, including their ability to pursue the energy transition, by exposing them to claims for compensation when they phase out fossil fuels, reform renewable support schemes, or strengthen environmental regulation; that investor–state arbitration lacks legitimacy, consistency, and accountability; and that the regime is asymmetrical, conferring rights on investors without corresponding obligations. These critiques have driven a wave of reform, including the termination of treaties, the renegotiation of treaty standards, proposals for a multilateral investment court, and the withdrawal of the European Union and many of its member states from the Energy Charter Treaty.

This chapter examines foreign direct investment in energy projects (section 14.1), bilateral and multilateral investment treaties (section 14.2), fair and equitable treatment (section 14.3), national treatment and most-favoured-nation treatment (section 14.4), direct and indirect expropriation (section 14.5), stabilisation clauses and regulatory change (section 14.6), investor–state dispute settlement (section 14.7), and investment treaty reform and the energy transition (section 14.8). The customary international law of expropriation and compensation, and the doctrine of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, have been examined in Chapters 2 and 3; contractual stabilisation and change-in-law clauses are examined from a contract-drafting perspective in Chapter 15; and the procedure of investment treaty arbitration, including jurisdiction, evidence, damages, and enforcement, is examined in Chapter 26.

## 14.1 Foreign Direct Investment in Energy Projects

### The Scale and Structure of Energy Investment

Global investment in energy amounts to around three trillion US dollars per year, of which an increasing proportion — now around two-thirds — is directed to clean energy, including renewable power, grids, storage, low-emission fuels, efficiency, and electrification, with the remainder directed to fossil fuel supply and power. Foreign direct investment (FDI) is a significant component of energy investment, particularly in developing countries that lack domestic capital and technology. However, the distribution of energy investment is highly uneven: Africa, which is home to around a fifth of the world's population, receives only a small fraction of global clean energy investment, owing in large part to the high cost of capital, which reflects perceived political, regulatory, currency, and offtaker risks.

The actors in energy FDI are diverse. **International oil companies** (IOCs), such as ExxonMobil, Shell, BP, Chevron, TotalEnergies, and Eni, have historically dominated upstream petroleum investment in developing countries. **National oil companies** (NOCs) of producing states, such as Saudi Aramco, Petrobras, and the Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited (NNPC Ltd), increasingly invest abroad. **State-owned enterprises** of China, including China National Petroleum Corporation, Sinopec, CNOOC, State Grid, and China Three Gorges, together with Chinese policy banks, have become major investors and financiers in energy infrastructure in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. **Independent power producers** and **renewable energy developers**, often backed by private equity and infrastructure funds, invest in power generation. **Sovereign wealth funds** and **pension funds** invest in energy infrastructure as long-term assets. And **development finance institutions** — the World Bank Group (including the International Finance Corporation and the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency), the African Development Bank, and bilateral DFIs — provide finance, guarantees, and political risk insurance, examined in Chapter 16.

### Domestic Legal Protection of Foreign Investment

Foreign investors in energy are protected, in the first instance, by the domestic law of the host state — its constitution, investment legislation, sectoral legislation, and contracts with the state. Many developing countries have enacted **investment codes** offering guarantees to foreign investors. In Nigeria, the **Nigerian Investment Promotion Commission Act 1995** established the Nigerian Investment Promotion Commission (NIPC) and provides, in section 25, that no enterprise shall be nationalised or expropriated by any government of the Federation, and that no person who owns, whether wholly or in part, the capital of any enterprise shall be compelled by law to surrender his interest in the capital to any other person, except where the acquisition is in the national interest or for a public purpose and under a law which makes provision for payment of fair and adequate compensation and a right of access to the courts for the determination of the investor's interest or right and the amount of compensation. The Act also guarantees the unconditional transferability of funds through an authorised dealer, and, in section 26, provides for the settlement of investment disputes by mutually agreed procedures, including arbitration under the ICSID rules or under a bilateral or multilateral investment protection agreement to which Nigeria is a party. Section 44 of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 prohibits the compulsory acquisition of property except in the manner and for the purposes prescribed by a law that, among other things, requires prompt payment of compensation, while section 44(3) vests minerals, mineral oils, and natural gas in the Government of the Federation, as examined in Chapter 3.

Domestic law protections may, however, be changed by the host state, and may be enforced only in the host state's courts, which investors may not trust to be independent. Contracts with the state — concessions, production sharing contracts, host government agreements, and power purchase agreements — offer more specific protection, including stabilisation clauses and international arbitration clauses, but their enforcement depends on the availability of effective international remedies. International investment law supplements these protections with treaty-based standards enforceable through international arbitration.

### Investment Screening and the Return of the State

While investment treaties protect investments once made, states have increasingly regulated the admission of foreign investment in the energy sector on national security grounds. The European Union's **FDI Screening Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2019/452) established a framework for the screening of foreign direct investments into the Union on grounds of security or public order, identifying critical infrastructure, including energy, and critical inputs, including energy and raw materials, as relevant factors. The United States expanded the jurisdiction of the **Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States** (CFIUS) through the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act of 2018, including over investments in critical infrastructure. The United Kingdom's **National Security and Investment Act 2021** requires mandatory notification of acquisitions in seventeen sensitive sectors, including energy and civil nuclear. These regimes reflect concerns about control of strategic energy infrastructure by foreign state-owned enterprises, and have been used to block or condition investments in energy networks and critical minerals. Most investment treaties apply only to investments that have been admitted in accordance with the host state's law, and do not confer a right of establishment, although some treaties, notably those of the United States and Canada, extend national treatment and MFN to the pre-establishment phase.

## 14.2 Bilateral and Multilateral Investment Treaties

### Bilateral Investment Treaties

The first **bilateral investment treaty** (BIT) was concluded between Germany and Pakistan in 1959. BITs proliferated from the late 1980s, and there are now more than 2,000 BITs in force, together with several hundred other treaties with investment provisions, including free trade agreements with investment chapters. BITs typically define the investments and investors protected; provide substantive standards of protection — fair and equitable treatment, full protection and security, national treatment, MFN treatment, protection against expropriation without compensation, free transfer of funds, and, in some treaties, an umbrella clause; and provide for the settlement of disputes between the host state and investors of the other party through international arbitration, as well as state-to-state dispute settlement.

Nigeria has signed BITs with a number of states, of which a smaller number are in force. The **Morocco–Nigeria BIT** (2016) has attracted attention as an innovative treaty that includes obligations on investors — including to comply with environmental and social impact assessment requirements, to maintain an environmental management system, to uphold human rights in the host state, to act in accordance with core labour standards, and not to engage in corruption — and provides for the possibility of civil liability actions against investors in their home state for acts causing significant damage, personal injuries, or loss of life in the host state. It also preserves the right of states to regulate and requires the exhaustion or attempt of domestic remedies and amicable settlement before arbitration.

### The Energy Charter Treaty

The **Energy Charter Treaty** (ECT), signed in Lisbon in 1994 and in force since 1998, is the only multilateral treaty dedicated to the energy sector. It originated in the European Energy Charter, a political declaration of 1991 intended to promote energy cooperation between Western Europe and the states of the former Soviet Union and Central and Eastern Europe, which possessed vast energy resources but needed Western investment and technology. The ECT has had around fifty contracting parties, including the European Union and Euratom, most European states, Japan, Turkey, and the states of Central Asia and the Caucasus; Russia signed the Treaty and applied it provisionally until 2009, but never ratified it.

The ECT covers trade in energy materials and products (applying GATT rules among parties, including those not WTO members), transit (Article 7, examined in Chapter 6), energy efficiency and environmental aspects (Article 19 and the Protocol on Energy Efficiency and Related Environmental Aspects), and, most significantly in practice, investment. **Part III** of the ECT provides for the promotion and protection of investments in the energy sector: Article 10(1) requires each contracting party to encourage and create stable, equitable, favourable, and transparent conditions for investors of other contracting parties, to accord their investments fair and equitable treatment, the most constant protection and security, and treatment no less favourable than that required by international law, including treaty obligations, and contains an umbrella clause requiring each party to observe any obligations it has entered into with an investor or an investment. Article 13 prohibits expropriation except for a purpose in the public interest, not discriminatory, carried out under due process of law, and accompanied by the payment of prompt, adequate, and effective compensation, amounting to the fair market value of the investment immediately before the expropriation or before it became known. **Article 26** provides for the settlement of disputes between an investor and a contracting party by international arbitration under the ICSID Convention, the ICSID Additional Facility, the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, or the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce. Article 17 permits a contracting party to deny the advantages of Part III to a legal entity owned or controlled by nationals of a third state if that entity has no substantial business activities in the territory of the contracting party in which it is organised.

The ECT has been the most frequently invoked investment treaty in the world, with more than one hundred and fifty known cases, the majority of them brought by investors from EU member states against other EU member states, principally concerning renewable energy support schemes (examined in Chapter 10). The *Yukos* arbitrations, in which tribunals constituted under the ECT awarded the former majority shareholders of Yukos Oil Company around USD 50 billion against Russia on the basis of Russia's provisional application of the Treaty, are among the largest awards in the history of international arbitration, examined in section 14.5. The modernisation of the ECT and the withdrawal of the European Union and many member states are examined in section 14.8.

### Regional Investment Instruments in Africa

Several regional instruments address investment in Africa. The **ECOWAS Supplementary Act on Investments** (A/SA.3/12/08), adopted in 2008, establishes common investment rules for ECOWAS member states, including both investor rights and investor obligations — such as obligations to conduct environmental and social impact assessments, to comply with labour and human rights standards, and to refrain from corruption — and the right of states to regulate. The **ECOWAS Energy Protocol** (2003), examined in Chapter 13, provides investment protection in the energy sector modelled on the ECT. The **SADC Protocol on Finance and Investment** (2006), as amended in 2016 to remove investor–state arbitration and to narrow the fair and equitable treatment standard, and the **SADC Model Bilateral Investment Treaty Template** (2012) reflect a "development-oriented" approach to investment protection. The **Protocol on Investment to the AfCFTA Agreement**, adopted in 2023, seeks to establish a continental framework for the promotion, facilitation, and protection of investment, emphasising sustainable development, the right to regulate, and investor obligations, with the question of investor–state dispute settlement left to a separate annex. The **Pan-African Investment Code** (2016), a non-binding instrument adopted under the auspices of the African Union, influenced the AfCFTA protocol. These instruments reflect a distinctive African approach to investment law, emphasising balance between investor rights and host state interests.

### Threshold Questions: Investment and Investor

Before a tribunal can consider the substantive merits of a claim, it must be satisfied that the claimant is a protected **investor** and that the dispute concerns a protected **investment**. Investment treaties typically define "investment" broadly, as "every kind of asset", followed by an illustrative list including shares, loans, contractual rights, intellectual property, and concessions under public law, including concessions to search for, extract, or exploit natural resources. Article 1(6) of the ECT defines investment as every kind of asset, owned or controlled directly or indirectly by an investor, including any right conferred by law or contract or by virtue of any licences and permits granted pursuant to law to undertake any economic activity in the energy sector.

Where arbitration is brought under the ICSID Convention, Article 25(1) requires that the dispute arise "directly out of an investment", and tribunals have debated whether this imposes an objective requirement independent of the treaty definition. The *Salini* test, derived from *Salini Costruttori SpA v Morocco* (ICSID, 2001), identified as indicative criteria a contribution of money or assets, a certain duration, an element of risk, and, more controversially, a contribution to the economic development of the host state. Energy investments generally satisfy these criteria without difficulty, but questions arise in relation to pre-investment expenditures, purely contractual arrangements such as sales contracts, and financial instruments.

The definition of "investor" turns principally on nationality, which for corporations is typically determined by incorporation or seat, sometimes with additional requirements of substantial business activity or control. Investors may structure their investments through holding companies in states with favourable treaties — a practice known as **nationality planning** or "treaty shopping". Tribunals have generally accepted nationality planning undertaken before a dispute arises, but have rejected as an **abuse of process** restructurings undertaken when a specific dispute was foreseeable. In *Philip Morris Asia Ltd v Australia* (PCA, 2015), the tribunal declined jurisdiction over a claim brought by a Hong Kong company that had acquired its Australian subsidiary after the Australian government had announced its intention to introduce plain packaging legislation, holding that the restructuring was an abuse of rights because the dispute was foreseeable at the time. In *Plama Consortium Ltd v Bulgaria* (ICSID, 2005), a leading ECT case, the tribunal held that the denial of benefits clause in Article 17 operates prospectively and must be exercised by the state, and that the state had not effectively exercised it before the claim was brought. Subsequent tribunals, including in the *Yukos* cases, considered whether the claimants — companies incorporated in Cyprus and the Isle of Man, ultimately owned by Russian nationals — were protected investors, and held that the ECT did not exclude claims by entities incorporated in a contracting party, even if ultimately controlled by nationals of the respondent state.
