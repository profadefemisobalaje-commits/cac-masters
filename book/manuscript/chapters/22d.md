## 22.7 Whistleblower Protection and Corporate Investigations

### The Importance of Whistleblowers

Whistleblowers — employees, contractors, and others who report wrongdoing they have observed in the course of their work — are among the most important sources of information on corruption, fraud, safety violations, environmental offences, and sanctions breaches. Many of the major enforcement actions in the energy sector originated in disclosures by insiders. Yet whistleblowers often face retaliation, including dismissal, demotion, harassment, blacklisting, legal action, and, in some contexts, physical danger. Legal protection of whistleblowers, and the establishment of effective internal reporting channels, are therefore essential components of both public enforcement and corporate compliance.

### Legal Frameworks for Whistleblower Protection

The **United Kingdom's Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998**, which inserted provisions into the Employment Rights Act 1996, protects workers who make "qualifying disclosures" of information which, in their reasonable belief, is made in the public interest and tends to show that a criminal offence, breach of a legal obligation, miscarriage of justice, danger to health and safety, environmental damage, or concealment of any of these has occurred, is occurring, or is likely to occur. Protection depends on the disclosure being made to an appropriate recipient — the employer, a prescribed regulator, or, under stricter conditions, a wider audience. Workers are protected against dismissal and detriment, with uncapped compensation for unfair dismissal.

The **EU Whistleblower Protection Directive** (Directive (EU) 2019/1937) requires member states to protect persons who report breaches of EU law in specified areas, including public procurement, financial services, anti-money laundering, product safety, transport safety, protection of the environment, nuclear safety, and public health. Private entities with fifty or more workers must establish internal reporting channels; member states must establish external reporting channels; and reporting persons are protected against retaliation, with a reversal of the burden of proof in retaliation proceedings, provided they had reasonable grounds to believe that the information was true at the time of reporting.

In the **United States**, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 protects employees of public companies who report securities fraud, and the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 established the **SEC Whistleblower Program**, which awards whistleblowers who voluntarily provide original information leading to successful enforcement actions resulting in sanctions exceeding USD 1 million between ten and thirty per cent of the sanctions collected. The programme has made awards totalling more than two billion dollars, including in FCPA cases, and is open to non-US persons. The Department of Justice launched a **Corporate Whistleblower Awards Pilot Program** in 2024, covering foreign corruption and other corporate crimes. The False Claims Act provides for *qui tam* actions in which private persons may sue on behalf of the government for fraud against federal programmes, including in the energy sector concerning royalties on federal lands.

In **Nigeria**, the Federal Ministry of Finance introduced a **Whistleblower Policy** in December 2016, which provides for rewards of between 2.5 and 5 per cent of recovered funds to whistleblowers who provide information leading to the voluntary return of stolen or concealed public funds or assets, and for protection against retaliation. The policy led to significant recoveries in its early years, but it is an executive policy rather than legislation, and bills to establish a comprehensive statutory whistleblower protection regime have been considered by the National Assembly. Whistleblowers are also protected to some extent under the Freedom of Information Act 2011, the Money Laundering (Prevention and Prohibition) Act 2022, and the Nigerian Code of Corporate Governance 2018, which requires companies to have whistleblowing frameworks. The absence of comprehensive statutory protection and concerns about the confidentiality of reports and the security of whistleblowers remain obstacles.

### Internal Reporting Channels

Effective internal reporting — "speak-up" — channels allow employees and third parties to raise concerns confidentially and, where permitted, anonymously, and are a central element of compliance programmes. Enforcement authorities evaluate whether companies have such channels, whether employees know about and trust them, whether reports are investigated promptly and independently, and whether retaliation is prohibited and punished. The **ISO 37002** standard on whistleblowing management systems (2021) provides guidance on their design. In the energy sector, reporting channels should be accessible to contractors and workers at remote sites and in joint ventures, and should cover safety and environmental concerns as well as corruption and fraud, given the importance of a "safety culture" in which workers feel able to raise concerns, a lesson emphasised by the investigations into the Piper Alpha and *Deepwater Horizon* disasters.

### Corporate Investigations

When allegations of misconduct arise — from a whistleblower, an audit, a regulator's inquiry, or media reports — companies must decide whether and how to investigate. An internal investigation may be conducted by in-house lawyers, compliance or audit functions, or external counsel and forensic accountants, and may be overseen by the board or a committee of independent directors. A well-conducted investigation enables the company to establish the facts, stop misconduct, discipline those responsible, remediate control failures, and decide whether to self-report to authorities. Key issues include:

- **Scope and independence.** The investigation's terms of reference should be defined in light of the allegations, and the investigators should be independent of those whose conduct is under examination, particularly where senior management is implicated.
- **Legal privilege.** The protection of investigation materials from disclosure depends on the rules of privilege of the relevant jurisdictions, which differ significantly. In the United States, *Upjohn Co v United States* (1981) established that communications between corporate counsel and employees for the purpose of providing legal advice to the company are protected by attorney-client privilege, and lawyers interviewing employees give "*Upjohn* warnings" that the privilege belongs to the company. In England, legal advice privilege is narrowly construed to cover only communications between lawyers and those authorised to seek and receive legal advice on behalf of the company, as held in *Three Rivers District Council v Bank of England (No 5)* (2003) and reaffirmed in later cases, while **litigation privilege** protects communications made for the dominant purpose of conducting adversarial litigation that is reasonably in contemplation. In ***Director of the Serious Fraud Office v Eurasian Natural Resources Corporation Ltd*** [2018] EWCA Civ 2006, concerning an internal investigation into allegations of corruption at the company's mining operations in Kazakhstan and Africa, the Court of Appeal held that documents generated in the investigation were protected by litigation privilege, since a criminal prosecution was reasonably in contemplation once the SFO had become involved, and that the avoidance of such litigation could be the dominant purpose of the investigation. The decision is significant for companies investigating misconduct in the shadow of potential enforcement.
- **Data protection and employment law.** Investigations involving the collection and review of emails and other personal data must comply with data protection laws, such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation and Nigeria's **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**, and with employment law and contractual obligations concerning interviews and disciplinary processes. Cross-border investigations must navigate blocking statutes, bank secrecy laws, and state secrecy laws that may restrict the transfer of documents to foreign authorities.
- **Reporting and remediation.** The investigation's findings must be reported to the board, and decisions taken on remediation, discipline, and disclosure.

### Self-Reporting, Cooperation, and Non-Trial Resolutions

Enforcement authorities in many jurisdictions offer incentives to companies that voluntarily self-report misconduct, cooperate fully with investigations, and remediate. The US Department of Justice's **Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy**, revised in 2023 and 2025, provides that companies that voluntarily self-disclose misconduct, fully cooperate, and timely and appropriately remediate will generally receive a declination, absent aggravating circumstances, subject to disgorgement. Resolutions may take the form of **non-prosecution agreements** or **deferred prosecution agreements**, under which prosecution is deferred or not pursued in exchange for the payment of penalties, cooperation, and compliance undertakings, sometimes including the appointment of an independent **compliance monitor**.

In the United Kingdom, **deferred prosecution agreements**, introduced by Schedule 17 to the Crime and Courts Act 2013, may be entered into by the SFO or the Crown Prosecution Service with organisations (not individuals) for specified economic offences, subject to approval by a judge who must be satisfied that the agreement is in the interests of justice and that its terms are fair, reasonable, and proportionate. The SFO's DPA with **Rolls-Royce** in 2017, concerning bribery in several countries including in the energy sector, involved payments of around £497 million plus costs. France's CJIP and similar mechanisms in other jurisdictions reflect a global trend towards negotiated corporate resolutions. Nigeria's **Administration of Criminal Justice Act 2015** provides for plea bargaining, which has been used in corruption cases, and anti-corruption agencies have entered into negotiated settlements with companies, including in the TSKJ case.

Negotiated resolutions have been criticised for allowing companies to "buy their way out" of prosecution, for the failure to prosecute individuals, and for the limited participation and compensation of the victims of corruption, including the populations of the states whose officials were bribed. Responding to the last concern, some resolutions have included compensation for affected countries, and the OECD's 2021 Recommendation encourages consideration of the interests of affected states.

## 22.8 Compliance Programme Design and Enforcement

### Why Compliance Programmes Matter

A **compliance programme** is the set of policies, procedures, controls, and organisational arrangements through which a company seeks to prevent, detect, and respond to violations of law and its own ethical standards. Compliance programmes matter legally because the existence of an effective programme may provide a **defence** — as under section 7 of the UK Bribery Act 2010 and the corporate offence of failure to prevent fraud — or may be a significant factor in decisions whether to prosecute and in the determination of penalties, as under the US Sentencing Guidelines and the Department of Justice's policies. They may also be required by law, as under France's Sapin II Law for large companies, and by contracts with lenders, partners, and governments. Beyond their legal significance, effective compliance programmes protect companies from the financial, operational, and reputational consequences of misconduct.

### Authoritative Frameworks

Several authoritative frameworks guide the design of compliance programmes:

- **The UK Ministry of Justice Guidance on the Bribery Act** (2011) identifies **six principles** for adequate procedures: **proportionate procedures** (procedures proportionate to the bribery risks faced and to the nature, scale, and complexity of the organisation's activities); **top-level commitment** (by the board and senior management to preventing bribery, fostering a culture in which bribery is never acceptable); **risk assessment** (periodic, informed, and documented assessment of the nature and extent of exposure to external and internal risks of bribery); **due diligence** (proportionate and risk-based due diligence in respect of persons who perform or will perform services for or on behalf of the organisation); **communication**, including training (ensuring that prevention policies and procedures are embedded and understood throughout the organisation); and **monitoring and review** (of procedures, and improvement where necessary).
- **The US Department of Justice's *Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs*** (first published in 2017 and updated in 2019, 2020, 2023, and 2024) guides prosecutors in assessing programmes through three fundamental questions: whether the programme is **well designed**; whether it is **adequately resourced and empowered to function effectively**; and whether it **works in practice**. The 2024 update addressed the management of risks arising from emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence, and the use of data analytics, lessons learned from misconduct, and protection of whistleblowers.
- **International standards**, including **ISO 37001** (anti-bribery management systems, 2016, revised in 2025) and **ISO 37301** (compliance management systems, 2021), provide certifiable frameworks, although certification does not guarantee effectiveness.
- **The World Bank Group's Integrity Compliance Guidelines** set out the expectations for companies that have been sanctioned by the Bank and seek release from debarment.

### The Elements of an Effective Programme

Drawing on these frameworks, the elements of an effective compliance programme for an energy enterprise, illustrated in Figure 22.1, include:

1. **Governance and tone at the top.** Board oversight of compliance, a senior compliance officer with adequate authority, independence, resources, and direct access to the board or audit committee, and visible commitment by leadership, reinforced by the "tone in the middle" set by line managers.
2. **Risk assessment.** Periodic assessment of compliance risks by country, business line, transaction type, and third party, taking account of the specific risks of the energy sector: interactions with government officials and NOCs in licensing, permitting, and customs; local content and community development obligations; agents and intermediaries; joint venture partners; commodity trading; and sanctions exposure.
3. **Policies and procedures.** A code of conduct and specific policies on anti-bribery, gifts and hospitality, facilitation payments, charitable donations and community investments, political contributions, conflicts of interest, sanctions and export controls, anti-money laundering, competition, and data protection, with clear procedures for approvals.
4. **Third-party due diligence and management.** Risk-based due diligence on agents, consultants, contractors, suppliers, joint venture partners, and local content partners, including the identification of beneficial owners and politically exposed persons; contractual protections (compliance representations, audit rights, and termination rights); and ongoing monitoring of payments and performance. Third parties are the source of the great majority of foreign bribery cases, and the TSKJ case illustrates the risk of sham consultancy arrangements.
5. **Training and communication.** Risk-based training tailored to roles, with particular attention to employees who interact with officials, and communication of policies to third parties.
6. **Speak-up and whistleblowing.** Confidential reporting channels, protection against retaliation, and prompt follow-up, as examined in section 22.7.
7. **Investigation and discipline.** Procedures for investigating allegations, consistent disciplinary measures for misconduct, regardless of seniority, and incentives — including in remuneration — that reward ethical conduct.
8. **Monitoring, testing, and review.** Internal audit and compliance testing, data analytics to detect anomalies in payments, root cause analysis of incidents, and periodic review and improvement of the programme.

### Compliance in Joint Ventures and Local Content Arrangements

Compliance in energy joint ventures and local content arrangements deserves particular attention. Non-operating partners may be exposed to liability for corrupt payments made by the operator or the joint venture, especially where they have knowledge of red flags, and should secure compliance undertakings, information and audit rights, and the right to participate in the selection of agents and contractors. Local content requirements, examined in Chapter 7, may require the engagement of local partners or subcontractors, whose ownership must be scrutinised for links to officials. Community development obligations, such as contributions to host communities development trusts under the PIA, examined in Chapter 24, and social investment programmes must be structured and documented to avoid their use as conduits for improper payments to officials or their associates.

### Debarment by Multilateral Development Banks

Companies participating in projects financed by multilateral development banks are subject to the banks' **sanctions systems**, under which companies found to have engaged in fraud, corruption, collusion, coercion, or obstruction may be debarred from future bank-financed contracts. Under the **Agreement for Mutual Enforcement of Debarment Decisions** of 2010 among the African Development Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the Inter-American Development Bank, and the World Bank Group, debarments of more than one year by one bank are generally enforced by the others. Debarred companies may be released subject to conditions, often including the implementation of an integrity compliance programme. Debarment is a significant risk for contractors in energy and infrastructure projects in developing countries.

### Enforcement and the Measurement of Effectiveness

Enforcement authorities increasingly emphasise that compliance programmes must be effective in practice, not merely on paper — "paper programmes" receive no credit. Indicators of effectiveness include the use of data to monitor risks, the investigation of and response to reported concerns, the integration of compliance into business processes such as procurement and mergers and acquisitions, the consequences imposed for misconduct, compensation structures that reward compliance, and the lessons learned from incidents, including at peer companies. For energy enterprises operating in high-risk environments, compliance is not merely a legal function but a strategic necessity, closely related to the management of safety, environmental, human rights, and sanctions risks and to the company's licence to operate.

## Conclusion

The governance and compliance frameworks examined in this chapter shape how energy enterprises are directed and how they respond to the legal and ethical risks inherent in their activities. Corporate governance standards, including those for state-owned enterprises, seek to ensure that enterprises are managed in the interests of their owners and stakeholders; Nigeria's transformation of NNPC into a limited liability company under the PIA is a significant test of whether legal restructuring can improve the governance of a national oil company. Directors' duties require boards to oversee compliance and manage material risks, including climate risks, although courts have been reluctant to second-guess the commercial judgments of directors, as *ClientEarth v Shell* illustrates.

The international anti-corruption framework, led by the OECD Convention, UNCAC, and the extraterritorial enforcement of the FCPA and the UK Bribery Act, has produced landmark enforcement actions in the energy sector, from Bonny Island to the commodity trading cases, although the political commitment to enforcement has fluctuated. Beneficial ownership transparency, EITI, and payments-to-governments reporting address the secrecy that facilitates corruption. Sanctions and export controls have become pervasive features of energy trade, especially since 2022, requiring sophisticated compliance systems and raising difficult contractual issues. ESG disclosure has moved from voluntary practice to mandatory regulation, though its scope is contested, and greenwashing has become a significant legal risk. Whistleblower protection, sound investigations, and well-designed compliance programmes are the instruments through which companies translate legal obligations into practice. For lawyers advising energy enterprises — whether in Lagos, London, or Houston — mastery of these frameworks is indispensable.
