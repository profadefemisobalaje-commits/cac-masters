::: casebox {title="Eiser Infrastructure Ltd and Energía Solar Luxembourg SÀRL v Kingdom of Spain (ICSID, 2017)"}
**Facts.** The claimants, a United Kingdom investment fund and its Luxembourg affiliate, invested in three concentrated solar power plants in Spain in 2007, relying on the regulated tariff regime of Royal Decree 661/2007. Following the 2013–2014 reforms, the plants' revenues fell dramatically, and the claimants alleged that the plants could no longer service their project finance debt.

**Decision.** The tribunal held that Article 10(1) of the ECT, which requires contracting parties to accord fair and equitable treatment, did not give investors a right to regulatory stability in the sense of a regulatory regime frozen in time; states retain the right to modify their regulatory regimes to meet changing circumstances in the public interest. However, the FET obligation protected investors against the "total and unreasonable" change of the regime on which they had relied in making long-term investments. Spain's new regime had been applied to existing installations, on the basis of a "standard installation" model that disregarded the claimants' actual costs and the capital-intensive character of their investment, and had eliminated the essential characteristics of the regime on which they had relied, depriving them of essentially all of the value of their investment. The tribunal awarded around EUR 128 million, using a discounted cash flow valuation.

**Annulment.** In 2020, an ICSID *ad hoc* committee annulled the award in its entirety because one of the arbitrators, appointed by the claimants, had failed to disclose long-standing professional connections with the firm of damages experts that had given evidence for the claimants, which the committee found could have had a material effect on the award. The claimants subsequently resubmitted their claims.

**Significance.** *Eiser* was the first award against Spain and set the template for many subsequent awards. Its distinction between permissible regulatory change and the "radical" or "fundamental" alteration of a regime on which investors had relied influenced the jurisprudence on regulatory stability under the ECT. Its annulment is a leading illustration of the importance of arbitrator disclosure, a theme examined in Chapter 26.
:::

### The Italian and Czech Arbitrations

Italy introduced a series of *Conto Energia* decrees offering feed-in tariffs for solar PV, and in 2014, by the so-called *Spalma-incentivi* decree, required existing installations to choose between reduced tariffs spread over a longer period and other options reducing their incentives. Investors brought claims under the ECT. In *Blusun SA and others v Italy* (ICSID, 2016), the tribunal dismissed the claims, holding that the investors had not demonstrated that Italy had made specific commitments that the incentives would not be altered and that, in any event, the failure of the project was not caused by the measures. Other tribunals reached different results: in *Greentech Energy Systems A/S and others v Italy* (SCC, 2018) and *CEF Energia BV v Italy* (SCC, 2019), tribunals found that Italy had breached the FET standard by reducing incentives that had been specifically guaranteed to the investors' installations by agreements with the state agency responsible for administering the incentives, while in *Eskosol SpA in liquidazione v Italy* (ICSID, 2020), the tribunal dismissed the claim.

The Czech Republic introduced a levy on revenues from solar installations commissioned in 2009 and 2010 (the "solar levy") and withdrew certain tax exemptions. Claims under the ECT and BITs were largely unsuccessful: in *Antaris Solar GmbH and Dr Michael Göde v Czech Republic* (PCA, 2018) and in *JSW Solar (zwei) GmbH and others v Czech Republic* (PCA, 2019), the tribunals dismissed the claims, finding that the investors could not legitimately expect the incentives to remain unchanged, that the Czech Republic had acted reasonably in response to the solar boom, and that the investors, many of whom invested late in the boom, had been on notice of possible changes.

Beyond Europe, *Mesa Power Group LLC v Canada* (UNCITRAL, NAFTA, 2016) concerned the allocation of feed-in tariff contracts under Ontario's Green Energy Act, which included domestic content requirements found inconsistent with WTO law in *Canada — Renewable Energy*. The tribunal dismissed the claims, holding, among other things, that the domestic content requirements fell within the procurement exception of NAFTA Article 1108.

### Doctrinal Themes

The renewable energy arbitrations have produced a substantial body of jurisprudence on several doctrinal questions.

**Legitimate expectations and general legislation.** A central question is whether general legislation — as distinct from specific commitments made to the investor — can create legitimate expectations protected by the FET standard. The prevailing view, reflected in *Charanne*, *Blusun*, and many subsequent awards, is that general legislation does not, by itself, create a legitimate expectation that it will not be changed; states retain the right to regulate. However, tribunals have differed as to whether legislation that specifically targets a defined class of investors and promises them particular treatment for a defined period (such as RD 661/2007's promise of tariffs for the operational life of registered installations) can create legitimate expectations, and as to the significance of registration and other individualised acts.

**Due diligence and foreseeability.** Tribunals have consistently held that investors must conduct reasonable due diligence into the legal framework and its stability, including judicial decisions (for example, Spanish Supreme Court judgments upholding earlier regulatory changes), and that expectations of stability are not legitimate if regulatory changes were reasonably foreseeable at the time of investment. The timing of investment has therefore been critical: investors who invested late, after warnings of reform, have generally fared worse.

**Proportionality and the reasonable return.** Many tribunals have assessed whether the measures were reasonable and proportionate, considering the public interest in addressing the tariff deficit and the impact on investors. Spain's argument that its new regime satisfied investors' legitimate expectations by guaranteeing a reasonable return was accepted by some tribunals and rejected by others, depending on whether the reasonable return was in fact achieved and whether the regime clawed back past remuneration.

**Valuation of damages.** The arbitrations generated extensive debate over the valuation of damages, including the use of discounted cash flow methods for operating assets, the appropriate discount rate, the useful life of installations, and the treatment of regulatory risk. These questions are examined in Chapter 26.

### The Intra-EU Objection and Enforcement

Most of the claimants against Spain and Italy were investors from other EU member states, and the disputes became entangled in the controversy over intra-EU investment arbitration. In *Slovak Republic v Achmea BV* (Case C-284/16, 2018), the Court of Justice held that investor–state arbitration clauses in intra-EU BITs are incompatible with EU law, because they remove disputes that may concern the interpretation or application of EU law from the EU judicial system. In *Republic of Moldova v Komstroy LLC* (Case C-741/19, 2021), the Court held that Article 26 of the ECT must be interpreted as not applicable to disputes between a member state and an investor of another member state concerning an investment in the first member state. In 2017, the Commission adopted a decision concluding that Spain's renewable energy support scheme constituted State aid, and in 2025 it adopted a decision concluding that the payment of the award in *Antin* would constitute unlawful State aid. Arbitral tribunals have, almost without exception, rejected the intra-EU objection, holding that their jurisdiction derives from the ECT as a matter of international law, and that EU law does not override the clear terms of the treaty.

The result has been a conflict between the obligations of EU member states under EU law and their obligations under the ECT and the ICSID Convention, which has played out in enforcement proceedings outside the European Union. In *Kingdom of Spain v Infrastructure Services Luxembourg Sàrl* [2023] HCA 11, the High Court of Australia held that Spain's agreement to Article 54 of the ICSID Convention, which requires contracting states to recognise ICSID awards as binding and enforce the pecuniary obligations imposed by them as if they were final judgments of their own courts, amounted to a waiver of immunity from the jurisdiction of Australian courts to recognise and enforce the award, although not of immunity from execution against particular assets. Courts in the United Kingdom and the United States have also rejected Spain's immunity objections at various stages of enforcement proceedings; in *NextEra Energy Global Holdings BV v Kingdom of Spain* (2024), the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held that federal courts had jurisdiction to enforce intra-EU ECT awards against Spain under the arbitration exception to the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, and reversed injunctions that had been granted to prevent award creditors from pursuing proceedings in Europe. Further proceedings have continued, and the conflict between EU law and the international obligations of member states remains unresolved.

In response to the arbitrations, Spain adopted Royal Decree-law 17/2019, which offered installations whose owners renounced or discontinued arbitration claims a fixed reasonable return of around 7.4 per cent until 2031. The European Union and many of its member states have since withdrawn from the ECT, a development examined in Chapter 14.

### Lessons for Regulatory Design

The renewable energy arbitrations offer important lessons for the design of renewable energy support. First, support schemes should be designed with mechanisms that allow costs to be controlled — such as volume caps, automatic degression of tariffs, and competitive allocation — so that governments are not forced into retroactive changes. Second, governments should distinguish clearly between general policy statements and specific commitments, and should avoid individualised assurances that they may not be able to honour. Third, changes to support schemes should, as far as possible, apply prospectively to new installations, with transitional arrangements for existing installations. Fourth, investors should conduct thorough due diligence into the stability of the legal framework, and price regulatory risk accordingly. These lessons are relevant to developing countries, including Nigeria, that seek to attract private investment in renewable energy through long-term contracts and support schemes.

::: summary
- Renewable energy has become the dominant source of new generating capacity worldwide. The law has driven its growth through targets, support mechanisms, and grid access, and has constrained it through permitting delays, land conflicts, and regulatory instability.
- There is no comprehensive international treaty on renewable energy. Its international foundations lie in climate law, SDG 7, the IRENA Statute, the International Solar Alliance, and the ECT. The EU's Renewable Energy Directive, as amended in 2023, sets a binding Union target of at least 42.5 per cent by 2030.
- Wind and sunlight are generally regarded as things common to all. The law therefore concerns rights to use land and to access the resource, together with the allocation of offshore areas under the coastal state's EEZ rights.
- Utility-scale solar raises land rights issues, particularly where land is held under customary tenure. Mini-grid regulations, such as Nigeria's, must address cost-reflective tariffs and the consequences of the main grid's arrival.
- Wind permitting addresses landscape, wildlife, and indigenous rights. The *Fosen* judgment of the Supreme Court of Norway demonstrates that renewable projects are subject to human rights constraints.
- Hydropower law governs water rights, environmental flows, long-term concessions, reversion, and dam safety. Geothermal law turns on the classification of the resource, and bioenergy is subject to sustainability criteria addressing land use change.
- Support mechanisms include feed-in tariffs and premiums, quota obligations with certificates, auctions with long-term contracts or CfDs, and fiscal incentives. Auctions have become dominant, and EU support schemes are subject to State aid control.
- Certificates separate environmental attributes from physical electricity. They serve compliance, disclosure, and voluntary functions, and the integrity of corporate claims is increasingly subject to legal scrutiny.
- The Spanish, Italian, and Czech arbitrations established that general legislation does not, by itself, freeze the regulatory framework. However, fundamental and retroactive changes that defeat specific commitments or a reasonable return may breach the FET standard. Their enforcement has exposed a conflict between EU law and the ECT and ICSID Convention.
:::
