## 6.7 Cross-Border Gas Infrastructure and Transit

### The Distinctive Legal Problem of Cross-Border Pipelines

A cross-border gas pipeline links a producing state, a consuming state, and frequently one or more transit states through a single, fixed, and very expensive physical asset. Once built, the pipeline cannot be relocated, and each state through which it passes acquires a degree of leverage over the others: the producing state can withhold supply, the consuming state can refuse to purchase or delay payment, and the transit state can interrupt flows, demand higher tariffs, or divert gas for its own use. Unlike LNG, which can be redirected to alternative markets, pipeline gas offers no easy exit. The legal framework for cross-border pipelines must therefore address not only the ordinary questions of project finance and regulation but also the political risks that arise from the interdependence of sovereign states.

The legal architecture of cross-border pipelines typically operates at three levels: an **intergovernmental agreement** (IGA) among the states concerned, establishing the principles governing the project, the rights and obligations of the states, and the framework for dispute resolution; **host government agreements** (HGAs) between each state and the project company, setting out the detailed legal and fiscal regime applicable in each territory; and the commercial contracts among the project participants, shippers, and buyers. This structure was developed and refined in the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan (BTC) oil pipeline project, completed in 2006, and has been applied to gas pipelines including the South Caucasus Pipeline, the Trans-Anatolian Natural Gas Pipeline (TANAP), and the Trans Adriatic Pipeline (TAP), which together form the Southern Gas Corridor bringing gas from Azerbaijan to Europe.

### International Law of Transit

General international law does not confer a comprehensive right of transit for energy across the territory of another state. Several treaty regimes, however, address transit.

**Article V of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade 1994** provides that there shall be "freedom of transit through the territory of each contracting party, via the routes most convenient for international transit, for traffic in transit to or from the territory of other contracting parties", and that such traffic shall not be subject to unnecessary delays or restrictions and shall be exempt from customs duties and transit duties, other than charges commensurate with the administrative expenses entailed by transit or with the cost of services rendered. Whether Article V applies to fixed infrastructure such as pipelines, as distinct from goods moving by conventional means of transport, has been debated. The WTO panel in *Russia — Measures Concerning Traffic in Transit* (2019), which concerned Russian restrictions on road and rail transit from Ukraine, is significant chiefly for its interpretation of the national security exception in Article XXI, but it confirmed the justiciability of Article XXI defences, a matter of considerable importance for energy trade (discussed in Chapters 13 and 25).

**Article 7 of the Energy Charter Treaty** provides that each contracting party shall take the necessary measures to facilitate the transit of energy materials and products consistent with the principle of freedom of transit and without distinction as to the origin, destination, or ownership of such energy materials and products or discrimination as to pricing on the basis of such distinctions, and without imposing any unreasonable delays, restrictions, or charges. Article 7(6) provides that a contracting party through whose area energy materials and products transit shall not, in the event of a dispute over any matter arising from that transit, interrupt or reduce the existing flow prior to the conclusion of the conciliation procedure set out in Article 7(7), except where specifically provided in a contract or other agreement or permitted in accordance with the conclusion of the conciliator. Article 7 does not, however, oblige a contracting party to permit the construction or modification of energy transport facilities or to allow new or additional transit through existing facilities that it demonstrates would endanger the security or efficiency of its energy systems.

Negotiations on a more detailed **Energy Charter Transit Protocol** began in 2000 but were suspended after Russia, which had signed but never ratified the ECT and which terminated its provisional application in 2009, objected to key provisions, including the treatment of existing long-term transit contracts and the "right of first refusal" for holders of expiring transit capacity. The failure of the Transit Protocol, and Russia's withdrawal from provisional application of the ECT, left European gas transit governed largely by bilateral arrangements and by EU law. The modernisation of the ECT adopted in December 2024, and the withdrawal of the EU and many of its member states from the Treaty, are discussed in Chapters 4 and 14.

**Article 125 of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea** confers on land-locked states a right of access to and from the sea and freedom of transit through the territory of transit states by all means of transport, the terms and modalities of which are to be agreed through bilateral, subregional, or regional agreements. Submarine pipelines on the continental shelf and in the exclusive economic zone are governed by Articles 58 and 79 of UNCLOS, examined in Chapter 20.

### Transit Tariffs and Transit Disputes

Transit states are compensated for the use of their territory through transit fees or tariffs, which may be set by reference to the costs of providing the service, the value of the gas transported, or the bargaining power of the parties. Transit tariffs have been a recurrent source of disputes. The gas disputes between Russia and Ukraine in January 2006 and January 2009 — the latter resulting in a complete cessation of gas flows through Ukraine to south-eastern Europe for almost two weeks in mid-winter — arose from linked disputes over the price of gas supplied to Ukraine, the tariff for transit of Russian gas to Europe, and Ukraine's accumulated debts. They exposed the vulnerability of European consumers to disputes between producer and transit states and prompted the European Union to adopt measures to strengthen security of supply, including the requirement of reverse-flow capacity on interconnectors, and to support the diversification of supply routes. The 2009 crisis was resolved by a set of ten-year contracts between Gazprom and Naftogaz, the disputes under which are examined in the casebox at the end of this chapter. Gas transit through Ukraine to the EU ended on 1 January 2025, when the transit agreement concluded in 2019 expired and was not renewed.

### Regional Gas Pipeline Frameworks in Africa

Africa offers several examples of regional and transnational gas pipeline frameworks. The **West African Gas Pipeline** (WAGP), which transports Nigerian gas to Benin, Togo, and Ghana, is governed by the Treaty on the West African Gas Pipeline Project signed by the four states in 2003 and by an International Project Agreement between the states and the West African Gas Pipeline Company. The Treaty established the West African Gas Pipeline Authority as a regional regulator, with powers over tariffs and access, and provided for a harmonised legal and fiscal regime for the pipeline across the four states. The WAGP has suffered from supply interruptions, attributable in part to supply shortfalls in Nigeria and to damage to the pipeline, and from payment arrears by downstream buyers, illustrating the challenges of regional gas integration where domestic supply and payment discipline are weak.

Other initiatives include the proposed **Nigeria–Morocco Gas Pipeline** (also referred to as the African Atlantic Gas Pipeline), intended to transport Nigerian gas along the West African coast to Morocco and potentially to Europe, for which intergovernmental memoranda have been signed with numerous ECOWAS states and Mauritania; and the **Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline**, intended to link Nigeria with Algeria via Niger, for which the three governments signed a memorandum of understanding in 2022. These projects face formidable challenges of financing, security, and coordination, but they illustrate the aspiration to use gas infrastructure as an instrument of regional integration. The ECOWAS Energy Protocol of 2003, modelled on the Energy Charter Treaty, contains provisions on investment protection and transit, and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) may in time provide a broader framework for cross-border energy trade (examined in Chapter 13).

### Cross-Border LNG: The Greater Tortue Ahmeyim Project

Cross-border cooperation is not confined to pipelines. The Greater Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) gas field straddles the maritime boundary between Mauritania and Senegal. In 2018, the two governments concluded an Inter-Governmental Cooperation Agreement providing for the joint development of the field as a single unit, with resources and revenues shared equally in the initial phase, subject to future redeterminations, and for the liquefaction of the gas on a floating LNG vessel located at a hub near the maritime boundary. The project, operated by BP with Kosmos Energy and the national oil companies of both states, illustrates the application of the unitisation principles examined in Chapter 5 to a transboundary LNG development, and the use of a bilateral treaty framework to provide the legal stability required for financing in states with no prior history of petroleum production. First LNG cargoes from the project were exported in 2025.

Elsewhere in Africa, the Mozambique LNG project in the Rovuma Basin illustrates the vulnerability of LNG projects to security risks: TotalEnergies declared force majeure in April 2021 following insurgent attacks on the town of Palma near the project site, suspending construction for an extended period, while the Coral South floating LNG project, operated by Eni, began production in 2022. The force majeure declaration, and the subsequent review of the human rights situation around the project commissioned by the operator, raise issues examined in Chapters 15 and 21.

### Host Government Agreements and Human Rights

Host government agreements for cross-border pipelines have attracted criticism for conferring extensive stabilisation protections on project companies, potentially restricting the ability of host states to adopt new environmental, labour, and human rights legislation. In response to such criticism concerning the BTC pipeline, the project participants executed a Human Rights Undertaking in 2003 clarifying that the stabilisation provisions would not be invoked to challenge host state regulation in the fields of health, safety, environment, and human rights that was consistent with international standards. The episode contributed to the development of the *Principles for Responsible Contracts* discussed in Chapter 5 and has influenced the drafting of later agreements.

## 6.8 Gas Supply Disputes and Regulatory Intervention

### Typology of Gas Disputes

Gas disputes may be classified into several categories: price review disputes between sellers and buyers under long-term contracts (examined in section 6.6); disputes over volumes, including take-or-pay claims and claims for failure to deliver; disputes over quality and measurement; transit disputes; disputes between investors and host states arising from regulatory measures, such as changes in fiscal terms, domestic supply obligations, or export restrictions; and disputes arising from emergency interventions in gas markets. Many of these disputes are resolved through international commercial arbitration under the rules of the ICC, the LCIA, or the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce (SCC), or through investment treaty arbitration.

### Supply Failure and Diversion: The Egyptian Disputes

The supply failures in Egypt following the political upheavals of 2011 generated a series of significant awards. Egypt had committed to supply gas for export, both by pipeline to Israel and Jordan through the Arab Gas Pipeline and the East Mediterranean Gas (EMG) pipeline, and to LNG export plants at Damietta and Idku. As domestic demand outstripped production and the pipeline to Israel was repeatedly attacked in the Sinai, Egypt diverted gas to the domestic market and terminated the EMG supply contract in 2012.

The resulting claims included an ICC arbitration brought by the Israel Electric Corporation against the Egyptian General Petroleum Corporation (EGPC) and the Egyptian Natural Gas Holding Company (EGAS), in which the tribunal in 2015 awarded the Israel Electric Corporation approximately USD 1.76 billion; and the ICSID case *Ampal-American Israel Corp v Egypt*, in which the tribunal found that Egypt had breached the fair and equitable treatment standard by failing to protect the pipeline and by the manner in which the supply contract was terminated. In *Unión Fenosa Gas SA v Arab Republic of Egypt* (2018), an ICSID tribunal held that Egypt had breached the fair and equitable treatment standard of the Spain–Egypt bilateral investment treaty by failing to supply gas to the Damietta LNG plant, in which the claimant held an interest, and awarded damages of approximately USD 2 billion. The tribunal rejected Egypt's defence of necessity, holding that Egypt had contributed to the situation of necessity through its domestic gas pricing policies, which had encouraged domestic consumption. These disputes were eventually resolved through settlements, including arrangements that facilitated the resumption of LNG exports from Damietta and the reversal of the EMG pipeline to carry Israeli gas to Egypt.

The Egyptian disputes illustrate the tension between export commitments and domestic supply obligations, the limits of the necessity defence in investment law (examined in Chapter 14), and the way in which regional gas flows can reverse dramatically as production patterns change.

### The European Gas Crisis of 2021–2023

The most significant gas supply crisis of the modern era began in 2021, when Gazprom reduced deliveries to European buyers to the minimum contractual levels and ceased selling additional volumes on the spot market, while European storage levels fell. Following the Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Russia required European buyers to pay for gas in roubles through a mechanism established by Presidential Decree No 172 of 31 March 2022, and suspended supplies to buyers that refused to comply, including buyers in Poland, Bulgaria, Finland, Denmark, and the Netherlands. Gazprom progressively reduced flows through the Nord Stream pipeline, citing technical problems with turbines, before halting them entirely in August 2022. In September 2022, explosions damaged both Nord Stream pipelines. European hub prices reached unprecedented levels, peaking in August 2022 at more than ten times their historical average.

The crisis generated extensive commercial arbitration between European buyers and Gazprom. In 2024, the German importer Uniper announced that an arbitral tribunal had awarded it damages of more than EUR 13 billion for Gazprom's failure to deliver contracted gas, and that it had been granted the right to terminate its long-term contracts. Other European buyers obtained similar awards. Gazprom responded by obtaining anti-arbitration and anti-suit injunctions from Russian courts under Articles 248.1 and 248.2 of the Russian Arbitrazh Procedure Code, introduced in 2020, which assert exclusive jurisdiction of Russian courts over disputes involving sanctioned Russian persons. The enforcement of awards against Gazprom's assets outside Russia raises complex questions examined in Chapter 26.

### Regulatory Intervention: The European Emergency Measures

The European Union responded to the crisis with an unprecedented series of emergency measures, many adopted under Article 122 TFEU, which permits the Council to adopt measures appropriate to the economic situation "in a spirit of solidarity between Member States", in particular if severe difficulties arise in the supply of certain products, notably in the area of energy. The principal measures included:

- **Regulation (EU) 2022/1032** on gas storage, amending the Security of Gas Supply Regulation (EU) 2017/1938, which required member states to fill their underground storage facilities to 80 per cent of capacity by 1 November 2022 and 90 per cent by 1 November in subsequent years;
- **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1369** on coordinated demand-reduction measures, which established a voluntary target to reduce gas consumption by 15 per cent compared with the average of the previous five years, with the possibility of a mandatory target in a "Union alert";
- **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2576** on enhancing solidarity, which established the AggregateEU mechanism for joint purchasing of gas and default solidarity rules;
- **Council Regulation (EU) 2022/2578** establishing a market correction mechanism, which capped prices for TTF front-month derivatives in defined circumstances; and
- the **REPowerEU** plan of May 2022, which set the objective of ending EU dependence on Russian fossil fuels, and subsequent legislative proposals in 2025 to phase out remaining imports of Russian gas.

At national level, member states nationalised or recapitalised gas importers facing insolvency as a result of the need to purchase replacement gas at spot prices to meet their supply obligations — notably Germany's stabilisation of Uniper, in which the federal government acquired a stake of approximately 99 per cent — placed Russian-owned subsidiaries under trusteeship, as Germany did with Gazprom Germania (later renamed SEFE), and introduced price caps, windfall taxes, and subsidies for consumers.

### The Principle of Energy Solidarity

The crisis gave new prominence to the principle of energy solidarity in Article 194(1) TFEU, which provides that EU energy policy shall aim "in a spirit of solidarity between Member States" to ensure the functioning of the energy market, ensure security of energy supply, promote energy efficiency and renewable energy, and promote the interconnection of networks. In *Germany v Poland* (Case C-848/19 P), decided in July 2021, the Court of Justice confirmed the judgment of the General Court in the OPAL case (examined as a casebox in Chapter 4), holding that the principle of energy solidarity is a legally binding principle that entails rights and obligations for both the EU and its member states, and that EU institutions must assess, in adopting measures in the energy field, whether there is a risk to the energy interests of member states. The principle has been invoked in support of the emergency measures adopted in 2022 and 2023.

### Limits on Regulatory Intervention

Regulatory interventions in gas markets, however justified by emergency, raise legal questions concerning their compatibility with property rights, contract rights, investment treaties, and the rule of law. Windfall taxes and price caps have been challenged before national courts and the Court of Justice of the European Union, and some interventions have given rise to investment treaty claims. The legal limits of emergency intervention, including the doctrine of necessity in international law and the treatment of emergency measures under investment treaties, are examined in Chapters 14 and 25. The general lesson of the crisis for gas law is that the commercial framework of long-term contracts and liberalised markets operates within a political and security environment that can override it, and that the law must provide mechanisms both for decisive intervention in emergencies and for the protection of legitimate expectations and the restoration of normal market functioning.
