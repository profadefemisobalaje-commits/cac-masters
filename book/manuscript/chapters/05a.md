## Introduction

The upstream petroleum industry — the exploration for and production of crude oil and natural gas — is the historical core of international energy law. It was in the upstream sector that the foundational instruments of the field were developed: the concession, the licence, the production sharing contract, the joint operating agreement, and the unitisation agreement. It was disputes over upstream rights that generated the classic arbitral awards on stabilisation, internationalisation, and expropriation. And it is in the upstream sector that the tension between the sovereign interests of host states and the commercial interests of international oil companies is most acute.

The upstream sector has distinctive economic characteristics that shape its legal regime. Exploration is a high-risk activity: most exploration wells fail to discover commercial quantities of petroleum, and the costs of a single deep-water well can exceed one hundred million United States dollars. Development requires enormous capital investment, often billions of dollars, before any revenue is earned. Production continues for decades, during which prices, costs, technologies, and political conditions change unpredictably. And the resource is non-renewable and owned, in most jurisdictions, by the state. The legal instruments of the upstream sector are designed to allocate the risks and rewards of this activity between the state, as resource owner, and the investor, as provider of capital and technology.

This chapter examines the legal framework of upstream petroleum. It begins with the legal classification of petroleum resources, proceeds to the nature of exploration and production rights, and analyses the principal contractual and licensing systems. It then examines joint operating agreements, national oil companies, unitisation, and decommissioning. Throughout, Nigerian law — particularly the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 (PIA) — is used as a principal reference point, alongside the laws of the United Kingdom, Norway, and other leading jurisdictions.

## 5.1 Legal Classification of Petroleum Resources

### What Is "Petroleum"?

The definition of "petroleum" in legislation and contracts determines the scope of rights granted and obligations imposed. Definitions vary, but most encompass naturally occurring hydrocarbons in liquid, gaseous, or solid form. Section 318 of the PIA defines "petroleum" as "crude oil, natural gas and condensates", and separately defines "crude oil" as "mineral oil, asphalt, ozokerite and all kinds of hydrocarbons and bitumen, whether in solid or in liquid form, in their natural state or obtained from oil shales, tar sands or any other mineral or rock, whether gaseous or not" and "natural gas" as "gas obtained from boreholes and wells and consisting primarily of hydrocarbons". Section 1(1) of the United Kingdom's Petroleum Act 1998 defines petroleum to include "any mineral oil or relative hydrocarbon and natural gas existing in its natural condition in strata", but expressly excludes coal and bituminous shales and other stratified deposits from which oil can be extracted by destructive distillation.

The precise definition can have significant legal consequences. Whether coal-bed methane, shale gas, oil shale, or helium falls within the definition of petroleum determines which licensing regime applies and who has the right to exploit the resource. The distinction between crude oil and natural gas matters because many legal regimes treat them differently for fiscal purposes, ownership of associated and non-associated gas, and domestic supply obligations.

### Associated and Non-Associated Gas

Natural gas may occur either in association with crude oil (associated gas), dissolved in the oil or as a gas cap above it, or in reservoirs that contain predominantly gas (non-associated gas). The distinction is legally important. Historically, many petroleum agreements granted rights to crude oil with only limited provision for associated gas, which was often flared because no market existed for it. Nigeria has long been among the world's largest gas flaring countries. The Associated Gas Re-Injection Act 1979 prohibited gas flaring without ministerial permission after 1 January 1984, but permission was routinely granted subject to payment of nominal fees. Section 104 of the PIA prohibits the flaring or venting of natural gas except in specified circumstances such as emergencies, or under an exemption granted by the NUPRC for facility start-up or for strategic operational reasons, and requires licensees and lessees to submit a natural gas flare elimination and monetisation plan. The Act further provides that payments of flare penalties shall not be cost-recoverable or tax-deductible. The Flare Gas (Prevention of Waste and Pollution) Regulations 2018 established the Nigerian Gas Flare Commercialisation Programme, under which flare gas is auctioned to third parties for commercial use.

### Conventional and Unconventional Resources

A further classification distinguishes conventional resources — petroleum that can be produced from reservoirs by natural pressure, pumping, or conventional secondary recovery — from unconventional resources, such as shale oil and gas, tight gas, coal-bed methane, oil sands, and gas hydrates, which require specialised techniques such as horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing. The shale revolution in the United States, which transformed that country into the world's largest producer of both oil and natural gas, was made possible not only by technology but also by the American legal framework of private mineral ownership, which permitted rapid leasing and development without the need for state licensing. Elsewhere, unconventional resources have been subject to moratoria or bans on hydraulic fracturing, including in France (Law No 2011-835 of 13 July 2011), and, for a period, in the United Kingdom, where a moratorium imposed in 2019 was briefly lifted in 2022 and then reimposed.

### Reserves Classification

The classification of petroleum reserves has legal and financial significance, since reserves underpin company valuations, borrowing capacity, and, in disputes, the assessment of damages. The Petroleum Resources Management System (PRMS), developed by the Society of Petroleum Engineers and other professional organisations, classifies petroleum quantities according to their commerciality and the degree of uncertainty in recoverable volumes: reserves (proved, probable, and possible, commonly abbreviated as 1P, 2P, and 3P), contingent resources, and prospective resources. Securities regulators impose disclosure requirements based on reserves definitions; in the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission's rules on oil and gas reserves disclosure apply to listed companies. The distinction between proved reserves and more speculative categories is frequently central to the valuation of expropriated petroleum assets in arbitration.

## 5.2 Exploration and Production Rights

### The Nature of Petroleum Titles

In jurisdictions where petroleum is owned by the state, the right of an investor to explore for and produce petroleum derives from a grant by the state. The legal nature of that grant varies. It may be characterised as:

- an **administrative licence**, a permission granted by the state in the exercise of public power, which creates rights and obligations defined by statute and regulation and which may be varied or revoked in accordance with administrative law;
- a **contract**, a bilateral agreement between the state (or its agency) and the investor, creating reciprocal obligations enforceable in accordance with contract law;
- a **property right**, a real right in the petroleum or in the land, such as a lease or *profit à prendre*, enforceable against third parties; or
- a **hybrid** combining elements of each.

The characterisation has practical consequences. If the title is an administrative licence, the state may be able to vary its terms by legislation or regulation, subject to the protection afforded by administrative law and any applicable investment treaty. If it is a contract, unilateral variation will constitute a breach. If it is a property right, it may be mortgaged, assigned, and protected against third-party interference, and its taking may constitute expropriation.

In the United Kingdom, a petroleum licence granted under the Petroleum Act 1998 is a licence in the sense of a statutory permission, but it incorporates "model clauses" prescribed by regulation which take effect as terms of the licence and have a contractual character. The licence confers exclusive rights to "search and bore for, and get" petroleum within the licensed area. In *Shell UK Ltd v Enterprise Oil plc* and related litigation, English courts have treated licences as conferring valuable proprietary interests capable of assignment, subject to the consent of the regulator.

In Nigeria, petroleum titles under the PIA comprise the petroleum exploration licence (non-exclusive, for reconnaissance), the petroleum prospecting licence (exclusive, for exploration and appraisal), and the petroleum mining lease (exclusive, for production). Under the PIA, a petroleum prospecting licence confers the exclusive right to drill exploration and appraisal wells and to carry out test production, while a petroleum mining lease confers the exclusive right to win, work, carry away, and dispose of petroleum. These titles are statutory instruments granted by the Minister on the recommendation of the NUPRC following a competitive bid round, and their terms are supplemented by the regulations made under the Act and by any associated contracts with NNPC Ltd.

### Allocation of Rights: Competitive Bidding and Discretionary Award

States allocate petroleum rights through competitive bidding, discretionary award (negotiation), or first-come, first-served application systems. Competitive bidding, in which rights are awarded on the basis of bids comprising signature bonuses, work programme commitments, royalty or profit-share terms, or a combination of these, is now the dominant method in most producing states and is generally regarded as more transparent and efficient. The PIA requires that petroleum prospecting licences and petroleum mining leases be granted through a "fully transparent and competitive bid process" in accordance with regulations and guidelines issued by the NUPRC, subject to limited exceptions. Discretionary award was historically widespread in Nigeria and elsewhere and was associated with allegations of corruption, most notoriously in the award of Oil Prospecting Licence (OPL) 245, which generated criminal proceedings in Italy and Nigeria and civil litigation in England.

The design of bidding systems involves trade-offs. Signature bonuses generate immediate revenue for the state but may deter exploration in high-risk areas; work programme bidding encourages exploration activity but may lead to over-commitment; profit-share bidding defers state revenue but aligns state returns with project outcomes. Norway's licensing rounds, conducted through the Awards in Predefined Areas (APA) system for mature areas and numbered licensing rounds for frontier areas, award licences on the basis of discretionary criteria (technical competence, financial capacity, and the quality of work programmes) rather than monetary bids, reflecting a policy choice to capture rent through taxation rather than through bonuses.

### Duration, Relinquishment, and Work Obligations

Petroleum titles are granted for defined periods. An exploration phase, typically lasting between three and ten years and often divided into sub-phases, is followed, upon discovery of a commercial field and approval of a development plan, by a production phase lasting twenty to thirty years or more. Under the PIA, a petroleum prospecting licence for onshore and shallow water acreage is granted for an initial exploration period of three years, which may be extended by a further three years, with longer periods available for deep offshore and frontier acreage; a petroleum mining lease is granted for a term not exceeding twenty years, renewable in accordance with the Act.

Titles typically impose minimum work obligations — commitments to acquire seismic data and drill a specified number of wells within defined periods — backed by financial guarantees. Failure to perform the work programme may result in forfeiture of the guarantee and revocation of the title. Relinquishment provisions require the titleholder to surrender portions of the licensed area at specified intervals, ensuring that acreage that is not actively explored is returned to the state for re-allocation. The PIA provides for the relinquishment of fifty per cent of the area of a petroleum mining lease ten years after the grant, subject to specified conditions, a provision designed to address the problem of large, under-explored acreage held under legacy oil mining leases.

### Assignment and Change of Control

Petroleum titles are valuable assets that are frequently transferred through farm-outs, sales, and corporate acquisitions. Because the identity, competence, and financial capacity of the titleholder are matters of public interest, most regimes require the consent of the state or regulator to any assignment of an interest in a title and, increasingly, to any change in control of the titleholder. The PIA prohibits the assignment, novation, or transfer of an interest in a licence or lease, or any change of control of the holder, without the prior consent of the Minister on the recommendation of the NUPRC. The divestment of onshore assets by international oil companies to Nigerian independents between 2021 and 2025 has generated significant regulatory scrutiny under these provisions, with the NUPRC requiring assurances concerning the purchasers' technical and financial capacity, their assumption of decommissioning and environmental liabilities, and the protection of host communities.
