## Introduction

Every stage of the energy and natural resource cycle has environmental consequences. Exploration disturbs land, seabed, and wildlife; extraction generates waste rock, tailings, produced water, and emissions; transport by pipeline and tanker risks spills; refining, processing, and combustion pollute air and water and emit greenhouse gases; and decommissioning and mine closure leave legacies that may persist for generations. Even renewable energy, as examined in Chapter 10, requires land, water, and minerals, and may affect biodiversity, landscapes, and communities. International environmental law, and the national environmental laws through which it is largely implemented, therefore form an indispensable part of the legal framework of energy and natural resources.

The relationship between environmental law and resource law has historically been one of tension. The principle of permanent sovereignty over natural resources, examined in Chapter 2, affirms the right of states to exploit their resources in accordance with their own development policies, while international environmental law imposes limits on that right in the interests of other states, the global commons, future generations, and the environment itself. The formula adopted in Principle 21 of the Stockholm Declaration of 1972 and repeated in Principle 2 of the Rio Declaration of 1992 captures this tension: states have the sovereign right to exploit their own resources pursuant to their own environmental and developmental policies, and the responsibility to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction or control do not cause damage to the environment of other states or of areas beyond the limits of national jurisdiction. The concept of **sustainable development** — development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs — was developed to reconcile these imperatives, and has been described by the International Court of Justice as expressing the need to reconcile economic development with protection of the environment.

This Part of the book examines the environmental dimension of energy and resource law. The present chapter sets out the general framework of international environmental law as applied to energy and natural resources: its foundations (section 17.1); the principles of prevention, precaution, and polluter-pays (section 17.2); environmental impact assessment (section 17.3); transboundary harm (section 17.4); biodiversity conservation and resource extraction (section 17.5); marine pollution from offshore operations (section 17.6); environmental liability and remediation (section 17.7); and compliance and enforcement (section 17.8). Chapter 18 examines the climate change regime and the energy transition; Chapter 19, the law of freshwater resources and transboundary watercourses; and Chapter 20, the law of the sea, including the protection of the marine environment under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea and the regulation of deep-seabed mining.

## 17.1 Foundations of International Environmental Law

### Origins

International environmental law is a relatively recent branch of public international law. Before the 1970s, environmental questions were addressed in a limited number of treaties concerned principally with the protection of particular species or with the use of shared resources, such as fisheries and boundary waters, and in a handful of arbitral and judicial decisions. Two decisions laid the foundations of the modern law.

In the **Trail Smelter** arbitration between the United States and Canada (awards of 1938 and 1941), concerning damage to crops and forests in the State of Washington caused by sulphur dioxide emissions from a zinc and lead smelter at Trail, British Columbia, the tribunal stated that, under the principles of international law, no state has the right to use or permit the use of its territory in such a manner as to cause injury by fumes in or to the territory of another or the properties or persons therein, when the case is of serious consequence and the injury is established by clear and convincing evidence. The tribunal awarded compensation and prescribed a regime to control the smelter's future emissions.

In the **Corfu Channel** case (*United Kingdom v Albania*, 1949), the International Court of Justice affirmed, in a different context, that every state is under an obligation not knowingly to allow its territory to be used for acts contrary to the rights of other states — a principle that underpins the modern obligation of prevention.

### The Stockholm and Rio Conferences

The **United Nations Conference on the Human Environment**, held in Stockholm in 1972, marked the emergence of international environmental law as a distinct field. The conference adopted the Stockholm Declaration, whose Principle 21 has been examined above, an Action Plan, and the recommendation to establish the **United Nations Environment Programme** (UNEP), which was created by the General Assembly later that year with its headquarters in Nairobi. The following two decades saw the conclusion of numerous multilateral environmental agreements (MEAs), on subjects including marine pollution, trade in endangered species, long-range air pollution, the ozone layer, and hazardous wastes.

The **World Commission on Environment and Development**, chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland, published its report, *Our Common Future*, in 1987, popularising the concept of sustainable development. The **United Nations Conference on Environment and Development**, held in Rio de Janeiro in 1992, adopted the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development, whose twenty-seven principles articulate the core principles of the field, and Agenda 21, a programme of action, and opened for signature the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Convention on Biological Diversity. Subsequent conferences — the World Summit on Sustainable Development in Johannesburg in 2002 and the United Nations Conference on Sustainable Development (Rio+20) in 2012 — reaffirmed and developed these commitments. The **2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development**, adopted by the General Assembly in 2015, set seventeen Sustainable Development Goals, including Goal 7 on affordable and clean energy, Goal 13 on climate action, and Goals 14 and 15 on life below water and on land. Rio+20 led to the strengthening of UNEP and the establishment of the **United Nations Environment Assembly**, with universal membership, which first met in 2014.

### Sources

The sources of international environmental law are those of public international law generally, as listed in Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice.

**Treaties** are the principal source. There are several hundred MEAs, global and regional, which establish specific obligations and, frequently, institutional machinery in the form of conferences of the parties, secretariats, scientific bodies, and compliance mechanisms. Many MEAs are **framework conventions**, which establish general principles and institutions and are supplemented by protocols containing more detailed obligations, as in the cases of the Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer (1985) and its Montreal Protocol (1987), and the Framework Convention on Climate Change and its Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement, examined in Chapter 18.

**Customary international law** includes the obligation to prevent significant transboundary harm, the obligation to conduct environmental impact assessment for activities likely to have significant adverse transboundary effects, and procedural obligations of notification, consultation, and cooperation, as examined in sections 17.2 to 17.4. The customary status of other principles, such as precaution and polluter-pays, remains debated.

**General principles of law** and **judicial decisions** have played an important role in developing the field, through the decisions of the International Court of Justice, the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, arbitral tribunals, and regional human rights courts.

**Soft law** — declarations, resolutions, guidelines, and codes of conduct that are not formally binding — is particularly significant in international environmental law. Instruments such as the Stockholm and Rio Declarations have shaped the development of treaty and customary law, guided the interpretation of binding obligations, and influenced national legislation.

### The Expanding Jurisprudence

The International Court of Justice has developed the law through a series of decisions. In its Advisory Opinion on the **Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons** (1996), the Court recognised that the existence of the general obligation of states to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction and control respect the environment of other states or of areas beyond national control is now part of the corpus of international law relating to the environment. In the **Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project** case (*Hungary v Slovakia*, 1997), concerning a treaty for the construction of a system of dams on the Danube, examined further in Chapter 19, the Court referred to the concept of sustainable development and to the need to take account of new environmental norms in the implementation of long-term projects. In **Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay** (*Argentina v Uruguay*, 2010), the Court held that the obligation to conduct an environmental impact assessment where there is a risk of significant transboundary harm is a requirement of general international law, and in **Certain Activities Carried Out by Nicaragua in the Border Area** and **Construction of a Road in Costa Rica along the San Juan River** (*Costa Rica v Nicaragua*; *Nicaragua v Costa Rica*, 2015), it extended this reasoning to activities beyond industrial projects. In 2018, in the compensation phase of the *Certain Activities* case, the Court for the first time awarded compensation for environmental damage, as examined in section 17.7.

Most significantly, international courts and tribunals have recently addressed the obligations of states in relation to climate change in a series of advisory opinions: the opinion of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea on climate change and the marine environment (May 2024), the opinion of the International Court of Justice on the **Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change** (July 2025), and the opinion of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights on the climate emergency and human rights (2025). These opinions, examined in Chapter 18, confirm that the general principles of international environmental law — including the obligation of prevention, the standard of due diligence, and the requirement of environmental impact assessment — apply to greenhouse gas emissions, and have significant implications for energy policy.

### Environmental Protection and Human Rights

The relationship between environmental protection and human rights has become increasingly prominent. Regional human rights courts have recognised that environmental degradation may violate rights to life, health, private and family life, and property, and the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights expressly recognises, in Article 24, the right of all peoples to a general satisfactory environment favourable to their development — a provision applied by the African Commission in *SERAC v Nigeria* (2001), concerning the environmental and human rights impacts of oil production in Ogoniland, examined in Chapter 21. In 2021, the UN Human Rights Council recognised the human right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment, and the General Assembly did so in Resolution 76/300 of July 2022. More than one hundred and fifty states recognise a right to a healthy environment in their constitutions, legislation, or regional treaties. Section 20(1) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, which provides that the state shall protect and improve the environment and safeguard the water, air, land, forest, and wildlife of Nigeria, is located among the non-justiciable Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy, but Nigerian courts have recognised environmental protection as an aspect of the right to life, as in *Gbemre v Shell Petroleum Development Co of Nigeria Ltd* (2005), in which the Federal High Court held that gas flaring in the Niger Delta violated the applicants' constitutional rights to life and dignity. The human rights dimension is examined further in Part VI.

### Institutions

The institutional landscape of international environmental law is fragmented. UNEP and the UN Environment Assembly provide a general forum for environmental policy, but lack the powers of a global environmental organisation. Most MEAs have their own conferences of the parties and secretariats, creating a complex system of treaty bodies. Other organisations with significant environmental roles include the International Maritime Organization, examined in section 17.6; the International Seabed Authority, examined in Chapter 20; the Food and Agriculture Organization; the World Health Organization; the World Bank and regional development banks, through their environmental and social standards; and the Global Environment Facility, established in 1991, which finances environmental projects in developing countries and serves as a financial mechanism for several MEAs. A French initiative for a **Global Pact for the Environment**, which would have codified the principles of international environmental law in a binding treaty, was considered by a UN working group but failed to secure agreement in 2019, reflecting the continuing resistance of many states to comprehensive codification.
