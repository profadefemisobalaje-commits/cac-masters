## 18.6 Climate Litigation and Corporate Accountability

### The Growth of Climate Litigation

Climate litigation has become a significant driver of climate law and policy. Databases maintained by the Sabin Center for Climate Change Law at Columbia University and the Grantham Research Institute at the London School of Economics record more than two thousand climate cases worldwide, the majority in the United States but with a rapidly increasing number in other jurisdictions, including in the Global South. This section examines the substantive legal theories on which the principal strands of climate litigation rest, and their implications for states and energy companies. The procedural dimensions of such litigation — standing, justiciability, the separation of powers, evidence, and remedies — are examined in Chapter 27.

Climate cases may be classified by their targets and objectives: **framework cases** against governments, challenging the adequacy of their overall climate policies; **project cases**, challenging the authorisation of particular fossil fuel projects, typically on the basis of environmental assessment or planning law, as in *Finch*, examined in Chapter 17; **corporate cases**, seeking to hold energy companies liable for their contribution to climate change or to require them to reduce their emissions; **disclosure and greenwashing cases**, concerning the accuracy of companies' climate-related statements; and **investor-state cases**, in which investors challenge climate measures, as examined in Chapter 14.

### Framework Cases against States

**Urgenda.** The foundational framework case is *State of the Netherlands v Urgenda Foundation*, in which the Supreme Court of the Netherlands held in December 2019 that the Dutch state was obliged, under Articles 2 and 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by at least twenty-five per cent below 1990 levels by the end of 2020, on the basis that the state has a positive obligation to take reasonable and appropriate measures to protect its residents against the real and immediate risk of dangerous climate change. The Court rejected the argument that the Netherlands' small share of global emissions relieved it of responsibility, holding that each state is responsible for its share. The decision led the Dutch government to adopt measures including the accelerated closure of a coal-fired power plant.

**Neubauer.** In *Neubauer v Germany*, the German Federal Constitutional Court held in March 2021 that the Federal Climate Change Act was partly unconstitutional because it did not make adequate provision for emission reductions after 2030, thereby placing a disproportionate burden of reductions on future periods and threatening the future freedoms of the young complainants — an "intertemporal" protection of freedom. The legislature amended the Act within months, bringing forward the target date for climate neutrality to 2045 and increasing the 2030 target.

**KlimaSeniorinnen.** In *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz v Switzerland*, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held in April 2024 that Switzerland had violated Article 8 of the Convention by failing to adopt an adequate regulatory framework for mitigating climate change, including by failing to quantify a national carbon budget or emission limits, and had violated Article 6 because the domestic courts had not seriously examined the applicants' claims. The Court recognised the standing of an association representing older women particularly affected by heatwaves, while holding the individual applicants' claims inadmissible, and in two parallel cases — *Duarte Agostinho v Portugal and 32 Others* and *Carême v France* — declared the applications inadmissible for lack of exhaustion of domestic remedies and lack of victim status, respectively. The judgment establishes that states have a positive obligation under the Convention to adopt and effectively apply regulations and measures capable of mitigating the existing and potentially irreversible future effects of climate change.

**Other jurisdictions.** Framework cases have been brought in many other jurisdictions. In *Leghari v Federation of Pakistan* (2015), the Lahore High Court ordered the government to implement its climate policy and established a Climate Change Commission. In *Future Generations v Ministry of the Environment* (2018), the Supreme Court of Colombia ordered the government to address deforestation in the Amazon, recognising the Colombian Amazon as a subject of rights. In the United States, *Juliana v United States*, a constitutional claim by young people, was dismissed for lack of standing by the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit in 2020 and finally ended in 2025, but in *Held v State of Montana*, the Supreme Court of Montana held in December 2024 that provisions of state law preventing the consideration of greenhouse gas emissions in environmental reviews of energy projects violated the right to a clean and healthful environment guaranteed by the Montana Constitution. In Australia, the Full Court of the Federal Court in *Minister for the Environment v Sharma* (2022) reversed a first-instance finding that the environment minister owed a duty of care to young people in approving a coal mine extension, and in 2025 the Federal Court dismissed the negligence claim brought by Torres Strait Islanders in *Pabai v Commonwealth*, while acknowledging the gravity of the harm they faced.

### Corporate Cases

Claims against energy companies seek either to require companies to reduce their emissions or to hold them liable for the costs of climate change.

::: casebox {title="Milieudefensie v Royal Dutch Shell plc (District Court of The Hague 2021; Court of Appeal of The Hague 2024)"}
**The claim.** Milieudefensie (Friends of the Earth Netherlands), with other organisations and thousands of individual claimants, sued Royal Dutch Shell plc, the parent company of the Shell group, then headquartered in The Hague, seeking an order requiring Shell to reduce the carbon dioxide emissions of the group and of the products it sells. The claim was based on the unwritten standard of care in the Dutch Civil Code, interpreted in the light of human rights, the Paris Agreement, and soft law instruments, including the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.

**First instance.** In May 2021, the District Court of The Hague held that Shell was obliged, through its corporate policy, to reduce the net carbon dioxide emissions of the Shell group's activities and of the end-use of its products (Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions) by forty-five per cent by 2030, relative to 2019 levels. The court characterised the obligation as an obligation of result in respect of the group's own emissions and a "significant best-efforts obligation" in respect of the emissions of suppliers and customers. It was the first time a court had ordered a private company to reduce its emissions in line with the Paris Agreement.

**Appeal.** In November 2024, the Court of Appeal of The Hague overturned the order. The court confirmed that Shell, like other companies, has a duty of care to contribute to the protection of people against dangerous climate change, informed by human rights and the Paris Agreement, and that companies such as Shell, which have contributed substantially to the problem and are able to contribute to its solution, have a special responsibility. However, the court held that it could not determine a specific reduction percentage applicable to Shell, because the forty-five per cent figure was a global average that could not be applied to individual companies or sectors without more specific scientific consensus, and because a court-ordered reduction of Shell's Scope 3 emissions might not be effective if other suppliers filled the gap. The court observed that Shell's investments in new oil and gas fields might be at odds with the energy transition, but did not need to decide that question. A further appeal to the Supreme Court of the Netherlands was lodged.

**Significance.** Although the order was overturned, the appellate judgment affirms that private companies owe a legally enforceable duty to contribute to climate mitigation, a principle with implications for corporate governance, disclosure, and future litigation, including claims targeting specific investments in new fossil fuel production. The case also illustrates the difficulty of translating global carbon budgets into obligations for individual companies.
:::

**Liability for climate damage.** In *Lliuya v RWE AG*, a Peruvian farmer sued the German energy company RWE, claiming that its historical emissions had contributed to the melting of a glacier above his home town, increasing the risk of a glacial lake outburst flood, and seeking a contribution proportionate to RWE's share of historical global emissions — approximately 0.5 per cent — towards the costs of protective measures. In May 2025, the Higher Regional Court of Hamm dismissed the claim on the facts, finding that the risk to the claimant's property was too low, but accepted in principle that a major emitter could be liable under the German Civil Code for its contribution to climate-related risks. In *Smith v Fonterra Co-operative Group Ltd* [2024] NZSC 5, the Supreme Court of New Zealand allowed a claim by a Māori elder against seven major New Zealand emitters, including an energy company, in public nuisance, negligence, and a proposed novel tort, to proceed to trial, declining to strike it out.

In the United States, numerous states and municipalities have sued major oil companies, alleging that they knew of the dangers of their products and engaged in campaigns of deception, seeking damages for the costs of adaptation under state tort and consumer protection laws. The companies have sought, largely unsuccessfully, to move these cases to federal courts, where they argue that the claims are displaced by federal law; the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit held in *City of New York v Chevron Corp* (2021) that claims based on global emissions were displaced by the Clean Air Act and foreign policy considerations, but other courts have allowed state-law deception claims to proceed in state courts. Several states, including Vermont and New York, have enacted "climate superfund" laws imposing liability on major fossil fuel producers for a share of the costs of climate adaptation, which have been challenged by industry groups and the federal government.

### Disclosure and Greenwashing

Claims concerning the accuracy of companies' climate-related statements have increased with the growth of mandatory climate disclosure, examined in Chapters 17 and 22. Regulators and private parties have challenged advertising and corporate statements alleged to overstate companies' climate credentials or net zero commitments, under consumer protection and securities laws. In *ClientEarth v Shell plc* [2023] EWHC 1897 (Ch), the High Court refused permission for a shareholder to bring a derivative claim against Shell's directors for alleged breaches of their duties under the Companies Act 2006 in managing climate risk, holding that the directors' management of the company's transition strategy was a matter of commercial judgment with which the court would not interfere on the evidence presented — a decision that illustrates the limits of directors' duty claims, examined in Chapter 22. The European Union's Directive on empowering consumers for the green transition (Directive (EU) 2024/825) prohibits generic environmental claims and claims based on offsetting that are not substantiated.

### Assessment

Climate litigation has achieved significant successes, particularly against governments in jurisdictions with strong human rights protections, and has contributed to the development of legal principles — such as the duty of states to protect against climate harm and the duty of care of companies — that are now reflected in the advisory opinions of international courts. Its limitations are also apparent: courts are reluctant to prescribe specific emission reductions for companies or to award damages for climate harm, causation remains a significant obstacle, and litigation outcomes depend heavily on the legal culture and institutional context of each jurisdiction. For energy companies, climate litigation is a material legal and financial risk, which must be managed through governance, disclosure, and transition planning.

## 18.7 Just Transition and Distributional Justice

### The Concept

The concept of a **just transition** originated in the labour movement in the United States in the 1980s and 1990s, as a demand that workers in polluting industries affected by environmental regulation should receive support. It has since broadened to encompass the fair distribution of the costs and benefits of the transition to a low-carbon economy among workers, communities, regions, and countries. The preamble to the Paris Agreement refers to "the imperatives of a just transition of the workforce and the creation of decent work and quality jobs in accordance with nationally defined development priorities". The **International Labour Organization's Guidelines for a just transition towards environmentally sustainable economies and societies for all** (2015) provide a framework for policies on social dialogue, social protection, skills development, and active labour market policies. The **Solidarity and Just Transition Silesia Declaration**, adopted at COP24 in Katowice in 2018, and the **Just Transition Work Programme**, established at COP27 in 2022, have further developed the concept within the climate regime. The theoretical foundations of energy justice — distributional, procedural, and recognition justice — are examined in Chapter 23; this section focuses on the legal and financial instruments through which just transition is pursued.

### Just Transition in Coal Regions

The phase-out of coal raises the most acute just transition challenges, because coal mining and coal-fired power are often concentrated in particular regions, where they provide employment, tax revenues, and community identity. The European Union's **Just Transition Fund**, established by Regulation (EU) 2021/1056, supports regions most affected by the transition, in accordance with territorial just transition plans; the **Social Climate Fund**, established by Regulation (EU) 2023/955, will use revenues from ETS2 to support vulnerable households, micro-enterprises, and transport users. Germany's coal phase-out was accompanied by legislation providing approximately EUR 40 billion in structural support for coal regions, as well as adjustment payments for older workers. In South Africa, where coal provides most electricity and supports substantial employment in Mpumalanga province, a Presidential Climate Commission was established in 2020 to oversee a just transition, and adopted a Just Transition Framework in 2022, and the JETP examined in Chapter 16 aims to finance the transition.

### The International Dimension

At the international level, just transition concerns the distribution of the burden of the energy transition between developed and developing countries. Around 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa lack access to electricity, and many more lack access to clean cooking, as examined in Chapter 23. African governments have argued that their countries, which have contributed least to climate change and have the lowest per capita emissions, should not be denied the opportunity to use their natural gas and other resources to achieve universal energy access and industrialisation. The **African Common Position on Energy Access and Just Energy Transition**, adopted by the African Union Executive Council in 2022, affirms that African countries will continue to deploy all forms of their abundant energy resources, including renewable and non-renewable energy, to address energy demand, and calls for the use of natural gas as a transition fuel, combined with the expansion of renewable energy. This position contrasts with calls for an end to new fossil fuel investment and with the policies of some development finance institutions and banks that have restricted financing for upstream oil and gas, generating debate about "energy colonialism" and the fairness of international climate finance. Nigeria's Energy Transition Plan, with its emphasis on gas as a transition fuel and a net zero target of 2060, reflects this perspective.

### Workers, Communities, and Consumers

A just transition also involves the protection of workers in fossil fuel industries through retraining, redeployment, and social protection; the involvement of affected communities in decision-making; the fair distribution of the costs of the transition, including the protection of low-income consumers from increases in energy prices resulting from carbon pricing and subsidy reform; and the fair distribution of the benefits of new energy industries, including through community ownership of renewable energy, local content, and benefit-sharing, as examined in Chapters 10 and 21. The mining of critical minerals for the energy transition raises its own justice concerns, including the rights of communities and workers in mining regions and the risk of reproducing patterns of extraction that have characterised the fossil fuel economy, as examined in Chapter 8.

### Legal Instruments

The legal instruments of just transition remain under-developed compared with mitigation instruments. They include national climate framework laws that incorporate just transition principles, such as Scotland's Climate Change (Emissions Reduction Targets) (Scotland) Act 2019, which requires climate policy to be consistent with just transition principles, and South Africa's Climate Change Act 2024; sectoral legislation providing for structural support, retraining, and social protection; the conditions attached to climate finance, including JETPs; and the incorporation of social criteria in the procurement of renewable energy. The question whether just transition is merely a policy objective or is capable of generating enforceable rights — for example, as an element of the human rights obligations of states, or as a constraint on the design of climate measures — is an emerging area of legal scholarship and litigation.
