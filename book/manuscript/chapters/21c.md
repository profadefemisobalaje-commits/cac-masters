## 21.5 Labour Rights and Occupational Safety

### Labour in the Energy and Extractive Industries

The energy and extractive industries employ tens of millions of people worldwide, from highly skilled engineers on offshore platforms to artisanal miners digging by hand. They are among the most hazardous sectors of the economy: mining, in particular, accounts for a disproportionate share of occupational fatalities, and petroleum operations carry risks of fires, explosions, blowouts, and exposure to toxic substances. Workers in remote locations, on offshore installations, and in fly-in, fly-out arrangements face distinctive pressures, and migrant and contract workers are often vulnerable to exploitation. The energy transition is creating new forms of employment in renewable energy and transition mineral supply chains, while threatening jobs in coal and other fossil fuel industries — a challenge addressed through the concept of just transition, examined in Chapter 18.

### The International Labour Framework

The **International Labour Organization** (ILO), founded in 1919, adopts conventions and recommendations establishing international labour standards. The **ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work** (1998) commits all member states, by virtue of their membership and regardless of ratification, to respect, promote, and realise principles concerning **freedom of association and the effective recognition of the right to collective bargaining**, the **elimination of all forms of forced or compulsory labour**, the **effective abolition of child labour**, and the **elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation**. In June 2022, the International Labour Conference amended the Declaration to add a fifth category, **a safe and healthy working environment**, and designated the Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 1981 (No. 155) and the Promotional Framework for Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 2006 (No. 187) as fundamental conventions.

Conventions of particular relevance to energy and extractive industries include the **Safety and Health in Mines Convention, 1995 (No. 176)**, which requires states to adopt and enforce legislation on mine safety, imposes duties on employers to eliminate or minimise risks, and grants workers rights, including the right to remove themselves from danger; the **Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention, 1999 (No. 182)**, which classifies hazardous work, including underground mining, among the worst forms of child labour; the **Minimum Age Convention, 1973 (No. 138)**; the **Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29)** and its **Protocol of 2014**; the **Violence and Harassment Convention, 2019 (No. 190)**; and the **Maritime Labour Convention, 2006**, which applies to seafarers, including those on vessels serving offshore installations. Nigeria has ratified the core conventions, including Conventions Nos. 29, 87, 98, 138, and 182.

### Occupational Safety in Petroleum and Mining

The regulation of occupational safety in petroleum operations has been shaped by major disasters. As noted in Chapter 20, the **Piper Alpha** disaster in 1988 led to the adoption of the safety case regime in the United Kingdom, which has been widely emulated, and the *Deepwater Horizon* disaster, in which eleven workers died, led to reforms in the United States and the European Union. In mining, disasters such as the Soma mine explosion in Turkey in 2014, which killed 301 miners, and the Brumadinho tailings dam collapse in Brazil in 2019, which killed 270 people, most of them workers of Vale, have prompted reforms of mine safety and tailings management, including the **Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management** (2020), developed by the ICMM, UNEP, and the Principles for Responsible Investment.

The **Marikana** massacre in South Africa in August 2012, in which police shot dead thirty-four striking mineworkers at Lonmin's platinum mine, after ten other people had been killed in the preceding days, illustrated the intersection of labour rights, living conditions, the role of security forces, and corporate responsibility in the extractive sector. The **Marikana Commission of Inquiry**, chaired by Judge Ian Farlam, reported in 2015, criticising the police, the unions, and Lonmin, including for its failure to implement the housing obligations of its social and labour plan, a requirement under South Africa's mining legislation. Civil claims by the families of those killed against the state were subsequently settled in part.

In **Nigeria**, labour relations are governed by the **Labour Act**, the **Trade Unions Act**, the **Factories Act**, and the **Employee's Compensation Act 2010**, which provides for compensation for workplace injuries, diseases, and death through a fund administered by the Nigeria Social Insurance Trust Fund. Occupational safety in petroleum operations is regulated under petroleum legislation and regulations, with the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission and the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority exercising regulatory functions under the PIA, while mine safety falls under the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007 and its regulations. The petroleum sector's unions — the **Petroleum and Natural Gas Senior Staff Association of Nigeria** (PENGASSAN) and the **National Union of Petroleum and Natural Gas Workers** (NUPENG) — have historically played a significant role in industrial relations and in national politics, including through strikes concerning fuel subsidies and the casualisation of labour through contract staffing. The Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act 2010, examined in Chapter 7, promotes the employment and training of Nigerians in the petroleum industry.

### Forced and Child Labour in Supply Chains

The globalisation of energy supply chains has brought attention to forced and child labour in the production of minerals and components for the energy transition. Two cases illustrate the issues.

**Cobalt in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.** The DRC produces around seventy per cent of the world's cobalt, a mineral used in lithium-ion batteries. A significant share is produced by **artisanal and small-scale miners**, in conditions that have been documented as dangerous and involving child labour. Amnesty International's report *This Is What We Die For* (2016) traced cobalt mined by children through supply chains to major electronics and electric vehicle manufacturers, prompting due diligence initiatives such as the **Responsible Minerals Initiative**, the **Fair Cobalt Alliance**, and the DRC government's establishment of the Entreprise Générale du Cobalt to formalise artisanal cobalt production. The **OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas** (2011, revised 2016) provides the principal framework for supply chain due diligence in the minerals sector.

**Polysilicon and forced labour.** Investigations by researchers and journalists, and subsequent findings by governments, have alleged that a significant share of the world's polysilicon, the key material in solar panels, is produced in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region of China in circumstances involving state-sponsored labour transfer programmes that may amount to forced labour. In 2022, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights published an assessment finding that serious human rights violations had been committed in Xinjiang. The **Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act** of the United States (2021), in force since June 2022, establishes a rebuttable presumption that goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in Xinjiang are made with forced labour and are prohibited from importation, and US Customs and Border Protection has detained solar panel shipments under the Act. The European Union adopted the **Forced Labour Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2024/3015), which will prohibit the placing on the EU market, and the export, of products made with forced labour, irrespective of origin, and will apply from December 2027. These measures illustrate the tension between the rapid and low-cost deployment of renewable energy and the respect for labour rights — a tension that also engages the trade law issues examined in Chapter 13.

### Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining

**Artisanal and small-scale mining** (ASM) employs an estimated forty million or more people worldwide, many times the number employed in large-scale mining, and supports the livelihoods of many more. In Africa, ASM is a major source of gold, cobalt, tin, tantalum, tungsten, diamonds, and gemstones. It is frequently informal or illegal, and associated with dangerous working conditions, child labour, mercury use in gold processing, environmental degradation, and, in conflict-affected areas, the financing of armed groups. In Nigeria, artisanal gold mining in Zamfara State led in 2010 to a lead poisoning epidemic that killed more than four hundred children, according to Médecins Sans Frontières, and illegal mining has been linked to banditry in the north-west. Legal approaches to ASM include formalisation through simplified licensing, the creation of designated artisanal mining zones, the establishment of cooperatives, and support for responsible sourcing; the **Minamata Convention on Mercury** (2013) requires parties with more than insignificant artisanal gold mining to develop national action plans to reduce and, where feasible, eliminate mercury use. The **Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007** provides for small-scale mining leases and the formation of mining cooperatives, and the federal government has pursued formalisation programmes, including through the Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining Department of the Ministry of Solid Minerals Development.

## 21.6 Community Grievance Mechanisms

### The Role of Grievance Mechanisms

Resource projects inevitably generate grievances among affected communities and workers — about land, compensation, employment, pollution, noise, dust, traffic, security, and the distribution of benefits. If these grievances are not addressed promptly and fairly, they may escalate into conflict, protests, blockades, sabotage, and litigation, with consequences for the human rights of affected people and the viability of projects. **Grievance mechanisms** — procedures through which individuals and communities can raise concerns and seek remedies — therefore serve both a human rights function, as a means of access to remedy, and a risk management function, as an early warning system for problems.

### The Effectiveness Criteria of the UNGPs

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, examined in section 21.7, provide that business enterprises should establish or participate in effective **operational-level grievance mechanisms** for individuals and communities who may be adversely impacted (Principle 29). Principle 31 sets out **effectiveness criteria** for non-judicial grievance mechanisms, both state-based and non-state-based. They should be:

- **legitimate**: enabling trust from the stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended, and being accountable for the fair conduct of grievance processes;
- **accessible**: being known to all stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended, and providing adequate assistance for those who may face barriers to access;
- **predictable**: providing a clear and known procedure with an indicative time frame for each stage, and clarity on the types of process and outcome available and means of monitoring implementation;
- **equitable**: seeking to ensure that aggrieved parties have reasonable access to sources of information, advice, and expertise necessary to engage in a grievance process on fair, informed, and respectful terms;
- **transparent**: keeping parties informed about the progress of the grievance, and providing sufficient information about the mechanism's performance to build confidence in its effectiveness;
- **rights-compatible**: ensuring that outcomes and remedies accord with internationally recognised human rights;
- **a source of continuous learning**: drawing on relevant measures to identify lessons for improving the mechanism and preventing future grievances and harms; and, for operational-level mechanisms,
- **based on engagement and dialogue**: consulting the stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended on their design and performance, and focusing on dialogue as the means to address and resolve grievances.

### Operational-Level Grievance Mechanisms

Operational-level grievance mechanisms are now standard in large resource projects, required by the IFC Performance Standards (PS1 requires a grievance mechanism for affected communities and PS2 for workers) and by many national laws and contracts. They range from simple complaint-logging systems to multi-tiered processes involving community liaison officers, joint committees, independent mediators, and appeal to external bodies. Experience has revealed several challenges: mechanisms controlled by companies may lack legitimacy and independence; communities may fear retaliation or the loss of benefits; the most serious allegations, such as those involving security forces or sexual violence, may be beyond the capacity of operational mechanisms; and mechanisms may be used to require complainants to waive their legal rights.

The **Porgera** gold mine in Papua New Guinea illustrates these difficulties. Following reports of sexual violence by mine security personnel, Barrick Gold, the mine's operator, established a remedy framework in 2012 under which women who had been subjected to sexual violence could receive individual reparations packages. The framework was criticised by human rights organisations because recipients were required to sign waivers of their right to sue the company, because the packages were considered inadequate, and because of a lack of independent oversight; subsequently, some claimants who had pursued legal action outside the framework obtained larger settlements. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, in a letter of 2013, addressed the compatibility of such legal waivers with the UNGPs, indicating that they should be avoided where possible and, if used, subject to safeguards, including access to independent legal advice.

### Community Development Agreements and Host Community Structures

In some jurisdictions, legal frameworks establish structures that combine benefit-sharing with dispute resolution. Nigeria's Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007 requires that **Community Development Agreements** include procedures for the resolution of disputes between the mining lease holder and the community. The **Petroleum Industry Act 2021** requires each **host communities development trust** to have a management committee and an advisory committee, and provides for the resolution of disputes, while also providing that where acts of vandalism, sabotage, or civil unrest occur that cause damage to petroleum facilities in a host community, the community forfeits its entitlement to the extent of the cost of repairs — a provision that has been criticised for imposing collective punishment and for failing to recognise that vandalism may be committed by outsiders. These provisions are examined further in Chapter 24.

### Accountability Mechanisms of International Financial Institutions

International financial institutions have established independent accountability mechanisms through which people affected by projects they finance can raise complaints:

- The **World Bank Inspection Panel**, established in 1993, investigates complaints from people who claim to have been harmed by projects financed by the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development or the International Development Association as a result of the Bank's failure to comply with its own policies and procedures. In 2020, the Bank established an Accountability Mechanism, including a Dispute Resolution Service, alongside the Panel.
- The **Compliance Advisor Ombudsman** (CAO), established in 1999, addresses complaints concerning projects of the **International Finance Corporation** and the **Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency** through dispute resolution and compliance investigations. The CAO's compliance investigation into the Tata Mundra coal-fired power plant in India found that the IFC had failed to ensure adequate protection of local fishing communities, which led to the litigation in *Jam v International Finance Corporation*, examined in Chapter 7, in which the United States Supreme Court held in 2019 that international organisations enjoy only the restrictive immunity accorded to foreign governments. Following consultations that began in 2022, the IFC and MIGA adopted an approach to remedial action, under which they may contribute to remedy where they have contributed to harm.
- Regional development banks have equivalent mechanisms, including the **Independent Recourse Mechanism** of the African Development Bank, the **Accountability Mechanism** of the Asian Development Bank, the **Independent Project Accountability Mechanism** of the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, and the **Independent Consultation and Investigation Mechanism** of the Inter-American Development Bank.

These mechanisms have provided communities with avenues for redress and have influenced the policies of the institutions, but they cannot award compensation, and their findings are often not fully implemented. Their effectiveness depends on the willingness of the institutions' boards and management to act on findings and to use their leverage over clients.

### State-Based Non-Judicial Mechanisms

State-based non-judicial mechanisms include **national human rights institutions**, such as Nigeria's National Human Rights Commission, established under the National Human Rights Commission Act 1995 as amended in 2010, which may receive and investigate complaints of human rights violations, including those involving companies; ombudsman institutions; labour inspectorates; environmental regulators; and the **National Contact Points** established under the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct. National Contact Points, which every adhering government must establish, handle "specific instances" — complaints that an enterprise has not observed the Guidelines — through good offices, mediation, and, where agreement is not reached, final statements that may include recommendations and determinations. Numerous specific instances have concerned energy and extractive companies, including complaints concerning oil pollution in the Niger Delta, mining in Africa and Latin America, and hydropower and coal projects in Asia. Nigeria is not an adherent to the OECD Guidelines, but complaints concerning the conduct of companies from adhering states in Nigeria may be brought before the National Contact Points of those states. Specific instances have produced mediated agreements in some cases, but the National Contact Points vary considerably in their independence, capacity, and willingness to make determinations, and their outcomes are not binding.

### Nigerian Mechanisms for Oil Spill Complaints

In Nigeria, complaints concerning oil spills are addressed through the **National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency** (NOSDRA), established by the National Oil Spill Detection and Response Agency (Establishment) Act 2006, which is responsible for the detection of and response to oil spills and the coordination of joint investigation visits to determine their cause and volume. The integrity of the **joint investigation visit** process, in which company representatives, regulators, and community representatives jointly inspect spill sites, has been contested, since the determination that a spill was caused by sabotage, rather than operational failure, relieves the operator of liability to pay compensation under its policies and Nigerian law. The reliability of these determinations was a significant issue in the litigation in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom examined in section 21.8 and in Chapter 17. The **Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project** (HYPREP), established in 2016 to implement the recommendations of UNEP's Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland, provides another, albeit imperfect, institutional channel for addressing the legacy of pollution.
