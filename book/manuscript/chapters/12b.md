## 12.2 Green, Blue, and Other Hydrogen Production Pathways

### The Colour Spectrum

Hydrogen production pathways are commonly described using a spectrum of colours. **Grey hydrogen** is produced from natural gas by steam methane reforming without carbon capture, and accounts for most current production; **brown** or **black hydrogen** is produced from coal by gasification. **Blue hydrogen** is produced from natural gas with the capture and storage of a large share of the resulting carbon dioxide. **Green hydrogen** is produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity. **Pink** or **purple hydrogen** is produced by electrolysis using nuclear electricity. **Turquoise hydrogen** is produced by methane pyrolysis, which splits methane into hydrogen and solid carbon. **White** or **natural hydrogen** is hydrogen occurring naturally in geological formations, whose potential has attracted recent exploration interest.

The colour terminology is a useful shorthand, but it is legally imprecise and potentially misleading. The climate impact of hydrogen depends not on its colour but on its life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions, which vary within each colour: green hydrogen produced by electrolysers drawing electricity from a grid dominated by fossil fuels may have higher emissions than grey hydrogen, and blue hydrogen's emissions depend on the capture rate and the upstream methane emissions from natural gas production. Law and policy have therefore moved towards **carbon intensity** approaches, which define hydrogen by reference to maximum life-cycle emissions per unit of hydrogen, sometimes combined with criteria relating to the source of energy.

### The EU Definition of Renewable Hydrogen

The European Union has adopted the most detailed legal definition of renewable hydrogen. Under RED II and RED III, hydrogen and other fuels produced from renewable electricity are classified as **renewable fuels of non-biological origin** (RFNBOs), which count towards renewable energy targets, including RED III's binding target that at least forty-two per cent of the hydrogen used in industry should be RFNBOs by 2030 and sixty per cent by 2035, and its sub-target for RFNBOs in transport. To qualify as RFNBOs, fuels must satisfy two Commission Delegated Regulations adopted in 2023.

**Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/1184** establishes rules for determining when electricity used to produce RFNBOs may be counted as fully renewable. Electricity taken directly from a renewable installation connected to the electrolyser may be counted as renewable if the installation came into operation no earlier than thirty-six months before the electrolyser. Electricity taken from the grid may be counted as fully renewable if the bidding zone in which the electrolyser is located has a share of renewable electricity above ninety per cent, if the emissions intensity of the electricity is below a specified threshold, or if the producer has concluded renewable PPAs that satisfy three criteria:

- **Additionality**: the renewable installations from which the electricity is sourced must have come into operation no earlier than thirty-six months before the electrolyser, and must not have received operating or investment aid, so that the hydrogen production is matched by new, unsubsidised renewable capacity rather than diverting existing renewable electricity from other uses. Electrolysers in operation before 1 January 2028 are exempted from the additionality requirement until 1 January 2036.
- **Temporal correlation**: the renewable electricity must be produced in the same calendar month as the hydrogen until 31 December 2029, and in the same one-hour period from 1 January 2030.
- **Geographical correlation**: the renewable installation must be located in the same bidding zone as the electrolyser, in an interconnected bidding zone with higher electricity prices, or in an offshore bidding zone interconnected with the electrolyser's bidding zone.

**Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/1185** establishes the methodology for assessing the greenhouse gas emissions savings of RFNBOs and recycled carbon fuels, and sets a minimum emissions savings threshold of seventy per cent compared with a fossil fuel comparator of 94 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per megajoule, equivalent to approximately 3.4 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of hydrogen.

The additionality, temporal, and geographical correlation requirements are intended to ensure that the production of renewable hydrogen does not increase emissions by diverting renewable electricity from the grid and increasing fossil generation elsewhere. Industry stakeholders have argued that they are excessively strict and increase the cost of renewable hydrogen, while environmental organisations have defended them as necessary to ensure environmental integrity. The rules apply to RFNBOs imported into the European Union as well as those produced within it, with significant implications for exporting states, as examined in section 12.3. In 2025, the Commission adopted a delegated act setting out the methodology for determining the greenhouse gas emissions of low-carbon fuels, including low-carbon hydrogen produced from natural gas with carbon capture or from nuclear electricity, under the Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package.

### The United States Clean Hydrogen Production Credit

In the United States, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 introduced a **clean hydrogen production tax credit** under section 45V of the Internal Revenue Code, with four tiers of credit value according to the life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions of the hydrogen, up to a maximum emissions rate of four kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram of hydrogen, determined by a "well-to-gate" analysis. Final regulations issued by the Treasury Department in January 2025 adopted three requirements for hydrogen produced using grid electricity to be credited with the emissions of the clean electricity procured through energy attribute certificates — incrementality (new clean generation), temporal matching (annual matching until 2029, and hourly matching thereafter), and deliverability (generation in the same region) — closely analogous to the EU's criteria, while providing flexibilities for existing nuclear plants at risk of retirement and for states with robust clean electricity standards. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025 curtailed the credit by limiting it to facilities beginning construction before 1 January 2028, an illustration of the political volatility of hydrogen support.

### Other National Approaches

The **United Kingdom's Low Carbon Hydrogen Standard** sets a maximum threshold of twenty grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per megajoule of hydrogen (on a lower heating value basis) for hydrogen to be eligible for government support, applicable to all production pathways, including electrolysis and natural gas reforming with carbon capture. The United Kingdom supports low-carbon hydrogen production through the **Hydrogen Production Business Model**, a contract for difference-style mechanism under which producers receive the difference between a strike price and a reference price, and the first hydrogen allocation round resulted in contracts with eleven electrolytic hydrogen projects in 2023. **Japan's Hydrogen Society Promotion Act** of 2024 provides for fifteen-year price-gap support for low-carbon hydrogen and ammonia supply chains, including imports, meeting carbon intensity requirements. Other states, including India, China, Australia, and Chile, have adopted standards defining green or low-carbon hydrogen, some based on carbon intensity alone and others combining intensity with source-based criteria.

### Natural Hydrogen and Legal Classification

The discovery of naturally occurring hydrogen accumulations — notably at Bourakébougou in Mali, where a well drilled for water in the 1980s was found to produce almost pure hydrogen, which has since been used to generate electricity for a village — has generated interest in exploration for natural hydrogen in several countries. Natural hydrogen raises the classification question in acute form: is it a mineral, a petroleum substance, a gas, or a sui generis resource, and which licensing regime applies to its exploration and production? South Australia amended its Petroleum and Geothermal Energy Act in 2021 to include hydrogen as a regulated substance, enabling the grant of exploration licences for natural hydrogen, and France amended its Mining Code to include natural hydrogen among the substances subject to mining titles. In most jurisdictions, including Nigeria, the classification of natural hydrogen is unaddressed, and the definitions of "petroleum" and "minerals" in the PIA and the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act may not clearly extend to it.

## 12.3 Hydrogen Certification and Cross-Border Trade

### The Function of Certification

Because the climate benefit of hydrogen depends on how it was produced, and because molecules of hydrogen produced by different pathways are physically identical, regulatory and commercial claims about low-carbon hydrogen depend on reliable certification of its origin and emissions. Certification serves several legal functions: demonstrating eligibility for support schemes and tax credits; demonstrating compliance with renewable energy targets and obligations; enabling consumers to make claims under emissions accounting frameworks; and enabling the differentiation of hydrogen in international trade. Certification systems may follow a **book-and-claim** model, analogous to guarantees of origin, under which certificates are traded separately from the physical product, or a **mass balance** model, under which certificates and the physical product move together along the supply chain, with the quantity of certified hydrogen withdrawn not exceeding the quantity injected. EU law requires the mass balance approach for the purposes of demonstrating compliance with the RFNBO and biofuel sustainability criteria under Article 30 of the Renewable Energy Directive, while guarantees of origin, which follow a book-and-claim model, are used for disclosure to consumers.

### EU Certification Through Voluntary Schemes

Under Article 30 of the Renewable Energy Directive, economic operators may demonstrate compliance with the RFNBO criteria, as with the biofuel sustainability criteria, through national schemes or voluntary international schemes recognised by the Commission. The Commission has recognised a number of voluntary schemes for the certification of RFNBOs, which audit producers' compliance with the additionality, temporal and geographical correlation, and emissions savings requirements, including for producers located outside the European Union. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/996 sets out rules for verifying sustainability, greenhouse gas emissions saving, and low indirect land use change risk criteria, including the requirements for audits and the use of the Union Database, which tracks the transactions of certified fuels to prevent fraud and double counting.

### International Standards

The proliferation of national definitions and certification schemes threatens to fragment the international market for hydrogen, increasing the costs of compliance for producers seeking to export to multiple markets. Efforts to develop internationally harmonised methodologies have been led by the **International Partnership for Hydrogen and Fuel Cells in the Economy** (IPHE), whose working paper on a methodology for determining the greenhouse gas emissions associated with the production of hydrogen informed the development of **ISO 19870:2023**, the first international standard on the methodology for determining greenhouse gas emissions associated with the production, conditioning, and transport of hydrogen to the consumption gate. The ISO standard provides a common accounting methodology, but does not set thresholds or determine which hydrogen is "clean", which remain matters for national and regional law. Mutual recognition of certification schemes between importing and exporting states has been proposed as a means of reducing fragmentation, and the European Union has explored cooperation with partner countries on certification.

### Trade Arrangements and Support for Imports

Several importing states have developed instruments to support imports of renewable hydrogen and derivatives. Germany's **H2Global** mechanism, managed by an intermediary company (Hintco), uses a double-auction model: the intermediary concludes long-term purchase agreements with producers outside the European Union, selected through competitive tenders on the basis of the lowest price, and sells the products in the European market through short-term auctions to the highest bidder, with the difference between the purchase and sale prices funded by the German government. The first H2Global tender, concluded in 2024, resulted in a contract for the supply of renewable ammonia produced in Egypt. The European Union's **European Hydrogen Bank**, funded by the Innovation Fund, conducts auctions for fixed premium support for renewable hydrogen production in the European Economic Area, with the first auction in 2024 awarding around EUR 720 million to projects selected on the basis of the lowest premium requested, and is developing an international component to support imports.

### Hydrogen and International Trade Law

International trade in hydrogen and its derivatives is subject to the WTO agreements examined in Chapter 13. Several questions arise. First, the **classification** of hydrogen and its derivatives in the Harmonized System — hydrogen is classified under heading 28.04 and ammonia under heading 28.14 — does not distinguish between low-carbon and high-carbon production, so that tariff differentiation based on production methods would require new subheadings or other instruments. Second, the **non-discrimination** obligations of the GATT raise the question whether low-carbon and high-carbon hydrogen are "like products" — a question that turns on the debate over whether non-product-related processes and production methods can be a basis for regulatory distinction, examined in Chapter 13. Third, the **TBT Agreement** applies to technical regulations and standards defining low-carbon hydrogen and the conformity assessment procedures by which compliance is certified, requiring them not to be more trade-restrictive than necessary to fulfil a legitimate objective and encouraging the use of international standards, such as ISO 19870. Fourth, **subsidies** for hydrogen production, such as the US 45V credit and EU support schemes, may be subject to the disciplines of the Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures, particularly where they are conditioned on domestic content. Fifth, the EU **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** (Regulation (EU) 2023/956) covers hydrogen and ammonia among its initial sectors, so that imports of carbon-intensive hydrogen and ammonia into the European Union will bear a carbon price from the definitive period beginning in 2026, as examined in Chapter 13.

### Transport of Hydrogen and Derivatives

International trade in hydrogen requires its transport over long distances, by pipeline or by ship. Pipeline transport of hydrogen within Europe is being developed through the conversion of natural gas pipelines and the construction of new hydrogen pipelines, including the planned "European Hydrogen Backbone" and cross-border corridors such as the H2Med project linking the Iberian Peninsula with France and Germany, and the proposed SoutH2 Corridor linking North Africa and Italy with Austria and Germany. Such cross-border pipelines raise issues of intergovernmental agreements, transit, and regulatory coordination similar to those examined for natural gas pipelines in Chapter 6.

Shipping of hydrogen requires its conversion into a form with higher energy density: liquefied hydrogen (at minus 253 degrees Celsius), ammonia, methanol, or liquid organic hydrogen carriers. The *Suiso Frontier*, the world's first liquefied hydrogen carrier, completed a pilot voyage from Australia to Japan in 2022. The International Maritime Organization adopted interim recommendations for the carriage of liquefied hydrogen in bulk, pending amendment of the International Code for the Construction and Equipment of Ships Carrying Liquefied Gases in Bulk (IGC Code). Ammonia, which is already traded internationally in large quantities, is likely to be the principal carrier in the near term, but its toxicity raises safety and environmental issues in its handling, transport, and use as a fuel, which the IMO has begun to address through interim guidelines on the safety of ships using ammonia as fuel.
