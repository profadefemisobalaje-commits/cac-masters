## 2.3 State Responsibility and International Legal Obligations

### The Articles on State Responsibility

The law of state responsibility determines when a state has committed an internationally wrongful act and what consequences follow. Its authoritative statement is the International Law Commission's *Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts* (ARSIWA), adopted in 2001 and commended to governments by the General Assembly. Although ARSIWA is not a treaty, many of its provisions are recognised as reflecting customary international law, and they are routinely applied by the ICJ and by investment tribunals.

Article 1 of ARSIWA states the basic principle: "Every internationally wrongful act of a State entails the international responsibility of that State." Under Article 2, an internationally wrongful act exists when conduct consisting of an action or omission (a) is attributable to the state under international law and (b) constitutes a breach of an international obligation of the state.

### Attribution

Attribution is of particular importance in the energy sector, where states frequently act through state-owned enterprises, national oil companies, regulatory agencies, and subnational authorities. ARSIWA provides several grounds of attribution:

- **Organs of the state** (Article 4). The conduct of any state organ — legislative, executive, judicial, or other, whether central or territorial — is attributable to the state. A federal state is therefore responsible for the conduct of its constituent units, and a state is responsible for the decisions of its courts and regulators.
- **Persons or entities exercising elements of governmental authority** (Article 5). The conduct of an entity that is not a state organ but is empowered by law to exercise elements of governmental authority is attributable to the state when the entity acts in that capacity. A national oil company that is empowered to grant licences, collect royalties, or regulate operations exercises governmental authority in performing those functions.
- **Conduct directed or controlled by the state** (Article 8). The conduct of a person or group is attributable to the state if it acts on the instructions of, or under the direction or control of, the state in carrying out the conduct.
- **Conduct acknowledged and adopted by the state** (Article 11).

The attribution of the conduct of national oil companies and state-owned utilities has been extensively litigated. In *Noble Ventures v Romania*, the tribunal held that acts of a state privatisation agency were attributable to Romania because the agency exercised governmental authority.[^noble] In *EDF (Services) Limited v Romania*, the tribunal examined whether the conduct of state-owned companies controlled by the state was attributable under Articles 5 and 8.[^edf] The general principle that emerges from the jurisprudence is that the separate legal personality of a state enterprise does not of itself shield the state from responsibility, but neither does state ownership of itself render the enterprise's commercial conduct attributable. The analysis turns on the function performed and the degree of state direction or control over the specific conduct at issue. Investment treaties sometimes contain specific provisions on the treatment of state enterprises, and the ECT, in Article 22, requires each contracting party to ensure that any state enterprise that it maintains or establishes conducts its activities in a manner consistent with the party's obligations under Part III of the treaty.

[^noble]: *Noble Ventures, Inc v Romania*, ICSID Case No ARB/01/11, Award, 12 October 2005, paras 68–86.

[^edf]: *EDF (Services) Limited v Romania*, ICSID Case No ARB/05/13, Award, 8 October 2009, paras 185–213.

### Breach of an International Obligation

A breach occurs when an act of a state is not in conformity with what is required of it by an international obligation, regardless of the origin or character of the obligation (Article 12). In the energy field, relevant obligations may arise under investment treaties, trade agreements, environmental treaties, human rights treaties, the law of the sea, and customary international law. A state may also incur responsibility for failing to exercise due diligence to prevent harm caused by private actors — for example, failing to prevent pollution from private oil operations from causing transboundary harm, or failing to provide full protection and security to a foreign investor's installations against attack.

The relationship between breach of contract and breach of international law is a recurring issue. A mere breach of a contract between a state and a foreign investor does not, of itself, constitute a breach of international law. Something more is required: an exercise of sovereign power (*puissance publique*) that goes beyond the conduct of an ordinary contracting party, a denial of justice in the resolution of the contractual dispute, or the operation of an "umbrella clause" in an investment treaty elevating contractual undertakings to treaty obligations. These issues are examined in Chapter 14.

### Circumstances Precluding Wrongfulness

ARSIWA identifies circumstances that preclude the wrongfulness of conduct that would otherwise breach an international obligation: consent (Article 20), self-defence (Article 21), countermeasures (Article 22), *force majeure* (Article 23), distress (Article 24), and necessity (Article 25).

The defence of **necessity** has been extensively litigated in the energy sector, particularly in the arbitrations arising from Argentina's economic crisis of 2001–2002, during which Argentina froze utility tariffs, converted dollar-denominated tariffs into pesos, and suspended tariff adjustment mechanisms that foreign investors in its gas and electricity sectors had been guaranteed. Article 25 permits a state to invoke necessity only if the act "is the only way for the State to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril" and "does not seriously impair an essential interest of the State or States towards which the obligation exists, or of the international community as a whole"; necessity may not be invoked if the obligation excludes it or if the state has contributed to the situation of necessity.

The Argentine cases produced divergent results. In *CMS Gas Transmission Co v Argentina*, the tribunal rejected Argentina's necessity defence, finding that the measures were not the only way to respond to the crisis and that Argentina had contributed to the situation.[^cms] In *LG&E Energy Corp v Argentina*, by contrast, the tribunal accepted that a state of necessity existed for a defined period between December 2001 and April 2003, during which Argentina was exempt from liability.[^lge] The *ad hoc* annulment committee in *CMS* criticised the tribunal for conflating the customary necessity defence with the non-precluded measures clause in Article XI of the United States–Argentina BIT, while declining to annul the award on that ground.[^cmsann] The inconsistency of these outcomes on substantially similar facts became a prominent illustration of the legitimacy concerns surrounding investment arbitration.

[^cms]: *CMS Gas Transmission Company v Argentine Republic*, ICSID Case No ARB/01/8, Award, 12 May 2005, paras 315–331.

[^lge]: *LG&E Energy Corp, LG&E Capital Corp and LG&E International Inc v Argentine Republic*, ICSID Case No ARB/02/1, Decision on Liability, 3 October 2006, paras 226–266.

[^cmsann]: *CMS Gas Transmission Company v Argentine Republic*, ICSID Case No ARB/01/8, Decision of the ad hoc Committee on the Application for Annulment, 25 September 2007, paras 128–136.

### Consequences: Cessation and Reparation

A state responsible for an internationally wrongful act is under an obligation to cease the act, if it is continuing, and to offer appropriate assurances and guarantees of non-repetition (Article 30). It is also under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused (Article 31). The classic statement of the reparation principle is that of the Permanent Court of International Justice in the *Factory at Chorzów* case:

> The essential principle contained in the actual notion of an illegal act ... is that reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act and re-establish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed.[^chorzow]

[^chorzow]: *Factory at Chorzów (Germany v Poland)*, Merits, 1928 PCIJ Series A, No 17, p. 47.

Reparation may take the form of restitution, compensation, or satisfaction, either singly or in combination (Article 34). Restitution — re-establishing the situation that existed before the wrongful act — is the primary form, but in practice it is rarely ordered in energy disputes, both because of the practical difficulty of compelling a sovereign to reverse a nationalisation and because of respect for sovereignty. Compensation, covering financially assessable damage including loss of profits insofar as established (Article 36), is the usual remedy. The valuation of compensation for energy assets — using discounted cash flow, comparable transactions, or asset-based methods — is one of the most technically demanding aspects of energy arbitration and is examined in Chapter 26.

## 2.4 Jurisdiction and Extraterritorial Regulation

### Principles of Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction in international law refers to the authority of a state to prescribe rules (prescriptive or legislative jurisdiction), to apply those rules through its courts (adjudicative jurisdiction), and to enforce them (enforcement jurisdiction). The bases of prescriptive jurisdiction are well established:

- **Territoriality.** A state may regulate conduct occurring within its territory. The subjective territorial principle covers conduct initiated within the territory; the objective territorial principle covers conduct completed or producing its effects within it.
- **Nationality.** A state may regulate the conduct of its nationals, including corporations incorporated under its law, wherever they act.
- **Protective principle.** A state may regulate conduct abroad that threatens its security or essential governmental functions.
- **Universality.** For certain grave international crimes, any state may exercise jurisdiction.
- **Passive personality.** A state may, in limited circumstances, regulate conduct abroad that harms its nationals.

The so-called **effects doctrine**, developed in United States antitrust law, permits the regulation of conduct abroad that has substantial, direct, and foreseeable effects within the regulating state. Its application has been controversial, particularly when used to regulate the conduct of foreign companies abroad.

Enforcement jurisdiction is strictly territorial. In the *Lotus* case, the Permanent Court of International Justice held that a state "may not exercise its power in any form in the territory of another State" without a permissive rule to that effect, while affirming broad discretion as to prescriptive jurisdiction.[^lotus]

[^lotus]: *SS Lotus (France v Turkey)*, 1927 PCIJ Series A, No 10, pp. 18–19.

### Jurisdiction over Maritime Resources

Jurisdiction over offshore energy and resources is governed by UNCLOS, which establishes a graduated system of maritime zones. The coastal state has full sovereignty over its internal waters and territorial sea (up to 12 nautical miles from the baselines), subject to the right of innocent passage. In the exclusive economic zone (EEZ), extending to 200 nautical miles, the coastal state has sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring and exploiting, conserving, and managing natural resources, and with regard to other activities for the economic exploitation and exploration of the zone, "such as the production of energy from the water, currents and winds" (Article 56). On the continental shelf, the coastal state exercises sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring it and exploiting its natural resources (Article 77); these rights are exclusive and do not depend on occupation or express proclamation. Beyond national jurisdiction, the mineral resources of the seabed — "the Area" — are the common heritage of mankind and are administered by the International Seabed Authority. These rules are examined in Chapter 20.

### Extraterritorial Regulation in the Energy Sector

Extraterritorial regulation has become increasingly important in the energy sector. Four categories may be distinguished.

**Anti-corruption legislation.** The United States Foreign Corrupt Practices Act 1977 prohibits the bribery of foreign officials by issuers of securities listed in the United States, domestic concerns, and persons acting within United States territory. The United Kingdom Bribery Act 2010 creates a corporate offence of failure to prevent bribery that applies to any organisation that carries on a business or part of a business in the United Kingdom, regardless of where the bribery occurs. Both statutes have been enforced extensively against energy and mining companies. The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention 1997 requires parties to criminalise the bribery of foreign public officials.

**Sanctions.** Economic sanctions targeting the energy sectors of particular states — including Iran, Russia, and Venezuela — are a prominent instrument of foreign policy. Some sanctions regimes have "secondary" effects, threatening penalties against foreign persons who engage in specified transactions with sanctioned parties even where there is no connection with the sanctioning state's territory. The European Union has adopted a "Blocking Statute" (Council Regulation (EC) No 2271/96) prohibiting EU persons from complying with certain extraterritorial sanctions. Sanctions are examined in Chapters 22 and 25.

**Supply chain due diligence.** A growing body of legislation imposes due diligence obligations on companies with respect to their global supply chains. Section 1502 of the United States Dodd–Frank Act 2010 required disclosure of the use of conflict minerals from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and adjoining countries. The European Union's Conflict Minerals Regulation (EU) 2017/821 imposes due diligence obligations on importers of tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold. The EU Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 requires due diligence regarding raw materials used in batteries, and the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (EU) 2024/1760 establishes broader human rights and environmental due diligence obligations for large companies, subject to amendments under the European Union's 2025 simplification initiatives.

**Climate and environmental measures.** The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), established by Regulation (EU) 2023/956, requires importers of specified carbon-intensive goods — including iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen — to purchase certificates reflecting the embedded emissions of those goods, with the definitive regime applying from 2026. The EU Methane Regulation (EU) 2024/1787 imposes monitoring, reporting, and verification requirements on fossil fuel imports. Such measures regulate production processes occurring abroad by attaching conditions to market access, and their compatibility with international trade law is examined in Chapter 13.

## 2.5 International Organizations and Specialized Energy Institutions

### The Fragmented Institutional Landscape

There is no single international organisation with comprehensive competence over energy. Instead, the institutional landscape is fragmented among organisations with specialised mandates, overlapping memberships, and varying legal powers. This fragmentation reflects the political sensitivity of energy, the divergent interests of producers and consumers, and the historical development of energy governance through issue-specific responses to particular crises. Chapter 4 provides a detailed analysis of the principal energy institutions. This section introduces the main categories and their legal significance as sources and agents of international energy law.

### Categories of Institutions

**Consumer-oriented institutions.** The International Energy Agency was established in 1974 within the framework of the OECD in response to the 1973–1974 oil crisis. Its founding treaty, the Agreement on an International Energy Program, obliges members to maintain emergency oil stocks equivalent to at least ninety days of net imports and to participate in coordinated emergency response measures.

**Producer-oriented institutions.** OPEC, established in 1960, and the Gas Exporting Countries Forum, established as an intergovernmental organisation in 2008, coordinate the policies of producing states. Since 2016, OPEC and a group of non-OPEC producers including the Russian Federation have coordinated production through the "OPEC+" arrangements under the Declaration of Cooperation.

**Technology- and sector-specific institutions.** The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), established in 1957, promotes the peaceful uses of nuclear energy and administers safeguards to verify that nuclear material is not diverted to weapons. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), established in 2009 with headquarters in Abu Dhabi, promotes the adoption of renewable energy.

**Treaty-based energy organisations.** The Energy Charter Conference is the governing body of the ECT. The Energy Community, established by treaty in 2005, extends the European Union's internal energy market rules to contracting parties in South-East Europe and the Black Sea region, including Ukraine.

**Dialogue forums.** The International Energy Forum, headquartered in Riyadh, provides a forum for dialogue between producers and consumers, and administers the Joint Organisations Data Initiative (JODI) on oil and gas statistics.

**General international organisations with energy competences.** The United Nations and its agencies (including the United Nations Environment Programme, the UNFCCC secretariat, and UN-Energy), the World Bank Group (including the IFC and the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency), regional development banks, the WTO, and the OECD all exercise significant influence over energy policy.

**Regional organisations.** The European Union exercises extensive legislative competence over energy under Article 194 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. In Africa, the African Union's Agenda 2063, the African Energy Commission (AFREC), the ECOWAS Regional Electricity Regulatory Authority, the West African Power Pool, and the Southern African Power Pool promote regional cooperation.

### Legal Powers and Outputs

The legal powers of these institutions vary greatly. Most energy organisations lack the power to adopt decisions binding on their members, and their influence derives from information, expertise, coordination, and soft law. Exceptions include the IEA's emergency response mechanism, the IAEA's safeguards agreements (which are binding treaties between the Agency and individual states), the Energy Community's dispute settlement procedure, and, most significantly, the European Union, whose regulations and directives are binding on member states and, in the case of regulations, directly applicable.

The outputs of international organisations contribute to international energy law in several ways: they may be the forum for the negotiation of treaties; their resolutions and declarations may constitute evidence of customary international law or contribute to its formation; their standards and guidelines may be incorporated into domestic law, contracts, or investment conditions; and their data and analysis frame policy debates.
