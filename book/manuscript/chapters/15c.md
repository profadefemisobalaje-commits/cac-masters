## 15.4 Joint Venture and Shareholder Agreements

### The Rationale for Joint Ventures

Energy projects are commonly undertaken by groups of companies acting together in joint ventures. The reasons include the sharing of risk and capital requirements, particularly in high-cost, high-risk exploration and development; the combination of complementary expertise, assets, and access to markets; compliance with legal requirements for state participation or local ownership; and the management of political risk through the participation of national oil companies, development finance institutions, or local partners. Joint ventures take two principal forms: **unincorporated joint ventures**, in which the parties hold undivided interests in the licence or contract and associated assets and conduct operations through a contractual arrangement, typically a **joint operating agreement** (JOA); and **incorporated joint ventures**, in which the parties hold shares in a jointly owned company, which holds the assets and conducts the business, governed by its constitutional documents and a **shareholders' agreement**. Upstream petroleum ventures are typically unincorporated, reflecting the tax advantages of direct ownership and the desire of each party to take and dispose of its share of production separately, while midstream, downstream, and power projects are more commonly incorporated.

### The Joint Operating Agreement

The JOA governs the relationship among the holders of a petroleum licence or contract, who are usually described as the "parties" or "co-venturers". The **AIEN Model Form International Joint Operating Agreement**, first published in 1990 and most recently revised in 2012, is the most widely used international model, and national model forms exist in several jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom and Norway (where the state prescribes the JOA as a condition of the licence). The principal provisions of a JOA address the following matters.

**Participating interests.** Each party holds a specified participating interest, which determines its share of costs, production, and voting rights, and its share of liabilities.

**The operator.** One of the parties is appointed as **operator**, responsible for conducting joint operations on behalf of all the parties, in accordance with approved work programmes and budgets. The operator is typically not entitled to profit from its role as operator, recovering only its costs (including an overhead charge) from the joint account. The AIEN model provides that the operator shall not be liable to the non-operators for any losses arising from joint operations, except where the losses result from the **gross negligence or wilful misconduct** of the operator's senior supervisory personnel. This limitation reflects the "no gain, no loss" principle of operatorship — since the operator does not profit from its role, it should not bear disproportionate liability — and has significant consequences for the allocation of loss in major incidents. The meaning of "gross negligence" is notoriously uncertain; English law does not recognise gross negligence as a distinct legal concept, and courts have interpreted it, in a contractual context, as connoting a serious disregard of or indifference to an obvious risk.

**The operating committee.** Decisions on joint operations, including approval of work programmes, budgets, and development plans, are made by an **operating committee** composed of representatives of the parties, voting according to their participating interests and specified pass marks. The pass mark determines whether a single party with a large interest can control decisions, or whether a minority can block them, and is a key subject of negotiation.

**Sole risk and non-consent operations.** Where the operating committee does not approve an operation that some parties wish to undertake, the JOA may permit those parties to conduct the operation at their sole risk and expense (**sole risk** or **exclusive operations**), with the non-participating parties having the option to join subsequently on payment of a premium, which compensates the participating parties for the risk they have borne. These provisions balance the interest of parties who wish to proceed with an opportunity against the interest of parties who do not wish to fund it.

**Default.** A party that fails to pay its share of costs (a **cash call**) is in default, and the JOA provides remedies, which may include the suspension of the defaulting party's rights, the payment of its share by the non-defaulting parties, the withholding of its share of production, and ultimately the **forfeiture** of its participating interest to the non-defaulting parties, with or without compensation. The enforceability of forfeiture provisions under the rules against penalties and relief against forfeiture has been debated. Following *Cavendish v Makdessi*, examined in section 15.3, forfeiture provisions in JOAs negotiated between sophisticated parties are likely to be upheld under English law, given the legitimate interest of the non-defaulting parties in the continuation of joint operations.

**Transfers.** JOAs restrict the transfer of participating interests, typically requiring the consent of the other parties or the host government and granting the other parties **pre-emption rights** (rights of first refusal) on transfers to third parties. Pre-emption provisions have generated substantial litigation, including over whether indirect transfers through changes of control trigger pre-emption, and over the valuation of interests in "package deals" in which the transferred interest is sold together with other assets.

**Disposal of production.** Each party is entitled and obliged to take in kind and separately dispose of its share of production, subject to lifting arrangements. Where the parties market gas jointly, as is common for gas projects, a separate gas marketing agreement may be required.

### Joint Ventures with National Oil Companies

In many producing states, national oil companies participate in petroleum ventures alongside international companies. In Nigeria, the federal government has historically participated in onshore and shallow water petroleum operations through joint ventures between NNPC and international oil companies, under which NNPC typically held a majority participating interest of fifty-five or sixty per cent, governed by JOAs and memoranda of understanding. These **unincorporated joint ventures** imposed on the government an obligation to fund its share of costs through cash calls, and chronic underfunding by the government, leading to large arrears, constrained investment and production. Alternative funding arrangements, including modified carry agreements and incorporated joint ventures, were developed to address the problem. Under the PIA, NNPC was converted into a limited liability company, NNPC Ltd, operating on commercial principles, and the Act provides for the conversion of existing joint ventures into incorporated joint ventures, as examined in Chapter 24. The participation of a state company in a joint venture raises distinctive issues, including the potential conflict between the company's commercial role as a co-venturer and its role as an instrument of state policy, the application of public procurement and transparency rules, and the risk that the state company's funding obligations will be subordinated to other demands on the public budget.

### Shareholders' Agreements in Incorporated Ventures

In incorporated joint ventures — common in LNG, refining, pipelines, and power projects — the relationship among the shareholders is governed by a shareholders' agreement and the company's articles of association. Key provisions include:

- **reserved matters**, which require the unanimous or super-majority approval of shareholders or directors, protecting minority shareholders against decisions on fundamental matters, such as changes to the business, major capital expenditure, borrowing, related-party transactions, and the admission of new shareholders;
- **deadlock** provisions, which provide mechanisms for resolving disagreements on reserved matters, including escalation to senior management, mediation, expert determination, and, ultimately, "Russian roulette" or "Texas shoot-out" buy-sell mechanisms, or winding up;
- **transfer restrictions**, including pre-emption rights, lock-up periods, and **tag-along** and **drag-along** rights;
- **funding** obligations, including commitments to provide equity and shareholder loans and the consequences of failure to fund;
- **offtake** and **supply** arrangements between the joint venture company and its shareholders, which may raise conflicts of interest;
- **dispute resolution**, typically by international arbitration.

### Good Faith and Relational Contracts

Whether parties to joint venture agreements owe each other duties of good faith or fiduciary duties varies across legal systems. Civil law systems generally recognise a duty of good faith in the performance of contracts, as in Article 1104 of the French Civil Code and § 242 of the German Civil Code (BGB), and Article 1.7 of the UNIDROIT Principles provides that each party must act in accordance with good faith and fair dealing in international trade. English law has traditionally refused to recognise a general duty of good faith in commercial contracts, but in *Yam Seng Pte Ltd v International Trade Corp Ltd* [2013] EWHC 111 (QB), the High Court suggested that a duty of good faith may be implied in "relational contracts" — long-term contracts involving a high degree of communication, cooperation, and predictable performance based on mutual trust and confidence — and the concept has been applied in subsequent cases, including *Bates v Post Office Ltd (No 3)* [2019] EWHC 606 (QB). Long-term energy joint ventures and supply agreements may be characterised as relational contracts, although English courts have emphasised that express terms excluding or limiting such duties will be respected. Parties to unincorporated joint ventures do not generally owe each other fiduciary duties under English law merely by virtue of their relationship, although an operator may owe fiduciary duties in relation to specific functions, such as the handling of joint funds, and parties may agree to assume such duties expressly.

## 15.5 Force Majeure and Hardship

### Supervening Events in Long-Term Contracts

Long-term energy contracts are particularly exposed to supervening events that prevent, delay, or make more onerous the performance of contractual obligations: natural disasters, armed conflict, terrorism, epidemics, strikes, government action, sanctions, and extreme market disruptions. The 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted energy demand, supply chains, and construction; the 2022 disruption of Russian gas supplies to Europe; the attacks on shipping in the Red Sea from late 2023; and armed insurgency affecting LNG projects in northern Mozambique, have each tested the legal mechanisms for dealing with supervening events. Three principal mechanisms are relevant: contractual **force majeure** clauses; contractual **hardship** clauses and their civil law counterparts; and the common law doctrine of **frustration**.

### Force Majeure Clauses

A **force majeure** clause excuses a party from liability for failure or delay in performing its obligations to the extent that the failure or delay is caused by an event beyond its reasonable control, as defined in the contract. The common law does not recognise force majeure as a general doctrine, so that the scope and effect of force majeure in common law contracts depend entirely on the terms of the clause. Civil law systems recognise force majeure as a general excuse for non-performance — under Article 1218 of the French Civil Code, for example, force majeure is an event beyond the debtor's control, which could not reasonably have been foreseen at the time of contracting and whose effects cannot be avoided by appropriate measures, which prevents the debtor from performing — but contractual clauses are typically used to define its scope precisely. Article 79 of the CISG and Article 7.1.7 of the UNIDROIT Principles provide similar excuses for impediments beyond a party's control.

Force majeure clauses in energy contracts typically contain several elements: a definition of force majeure, usually combining a general description (an event beyond the reasonable control of the affected party, which it could not have avoided or overcome by the exercise of reasonable diligence) with a non-exhaustive list of specific events; exclusions, such as financial inability to perform and changes in market conditions; requirements of causation, notice, and mitigation; the consequences of force majeure, including suspension of affected obligations and extension of time; and the right to terminate if the force majeure continues beyond a specified period. In PPAs and other contracts with state counterparties, force majeure is often divided into **natural force majeure** (events such as natural disasters, which are generally borne by each party without compensation) and **political force majeure** (events such as war, expropriation, and changes in law attributable to the host government, which may entitle the private party to compensation or to require the government to purchase the project).

### The Interpretation of Force Majeure Clauses

English courts interpret force majeure clauses according to their terms, with several recurring principles.

**Market changes are not force majeure.** In *Thames Valley Power Ltd v Total Gas & Power Ltd* [2005] EWHC 2208 (Comm), the court held that a substantial increase in the market price of gas, which made the supplier's performance of a long-term gas supply contract uneconomic, did not constitute force majeure, because the supplier was not prevented from performing — it could buy gas at the higher price — and a clause excusing performance for events beyond a party's control does not generally cover an increase in the cost of performance.

**Causation.** In *Classic Maritime Inc v Limbungan Makmur Sdn Bhd* [2019] EWCA Civ 1102, a case arising from the collapse of the Fundão tailings dam in Brazil, examined in Chapter 7, which halted the supply of iron ore pellets for shipment under a contract of affreightment, the Court of Appeal held that the charterer could not rely on the force majeure clause because it would not have performed even if the dam had not collapsed, owing to the lack of demand; the clause required that the force majeure event be the cause of the failure to perform, applying a "but for" test.

**Reasonable endeavours and non-contractual performance.** In *RTI Ltd v MUR Shipping BV* [2024] UKSC 18, the Supreme Court held that a force majeure clause requiring that the event "cannot be overcome by reasonable endeavours from the party affected" did not require the affected party to accept an offer of non-contractual performance — in that case, payment in euros rather than the contractually stipulated US dollars, where sanctions against the charterer's parent company impeded payment in dollars — because the principle of freedom of contract entitles a party to insist on performance in accordance with the contract's terms. The decision is significant for the operation of force majeure clauses in the context of sanctions.

### Hardship

**Hardship** clauses address events that do not make performance impossible but make it excessively onerous, fundamentally altering the equilibrium of the contract. Article 6.2.2 of the UNIDROIT Principles defines hardship as occurring where the occurrence of events fundamentally alters the equilibrium of the contract, either because the cost of a party's performance has increased or because the value of the performance a party receives has diminished, and the events occur or become known after the conclusion of the contract, could not reasonably have been taken into account by the disadvantaged party, are beyond its control, and the risk of the events was not assumed by it. Under Article 6.2.3, the disadvantaged party is entitled to request renegotiation, and if the parties fail to reach agreement within a reasonable time, either party may resort to the court, which may terminate the contract or adapt it with a view to restoring its equilibrium. The **ICC Hardship Clause 2020**, published by the International Chamber of Commerce together with the ICC Force Majeure Clause 2020, provides model clauses that parties may incorporate.

Several civil law systems recognise hardship as a general doctrine. Article 1195 of the French Civil Code, introduced by the reform of the law of obligations in 2016, provides that if a change of circumstances unforeseeable at the time of the conclusion of the contract renders performance excessively onerous for a party who had not accepted the risk of such a change, that party may ask the other party to renegotiate the contract, and, if renegotiation fails, the court may, at the request of a party, revise or terminate the contract. Section 313 of the German Civil Code provides for the adaptation of a contract where circumstances that became the basis of the contract have significantly changed, a provision that was invoked in the context of long-term gas contracts during the 2022 energy crisis. In long-term gas supply contracts, the price review clauses examined in Chapter 6 function as a specialised form of hardship mechanism, permitting the adjustment of prices in response to significant changes in market conditions.

### Frustration

The common law doctrine of **frustration** discharges a contract where, without the default of either party, a supervening event renders performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from that contemplated by the parties. The doctrine was established in *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863), in which the destruction by fire of a music hall discharged a contract for its hire. In *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, the House of Lords held that frustration occurs whenever the law recognises that, without default of either party, a contractual obligation has become incapable of being performed because the circumstances in which performance is called for would render it a thing radically different from that which was undertaken by the contract, and that the fact that performance has become more onerous or expensive is not sufficient. In *Tsakiroglou & Co Ltd v Noblee Thorl GmbH* [1962] AC 93, the closure of the Suez Canal during the 1956 crisis did not frustrate a contract for the shipment of groundnuts from Sudan to Hamburg, because the goods could be shipped via the Cape of Good Hope, although at greater cost and over a longer period. Frustration is applied narrowly; it does not apply where the contract makes provision for the event, where the event was foreseeable and the parties could have provided for it, or where the event was caused by the party seeking to rely on it (self-induced frustration, as in *J Lauritzen AS v Wijsmuller BV (The Super Servant Two)* [1990] 1 Lloyd's Rep 1). The narrowness of frustration underscores the importance of carefully drafted force majeure and hardship clauses in long-term energy contracts.

### Recent Crises and Energy Contracts

The disruption of Russian gas supplies to Europe in 2022 generated extensive disputes under long-term gas contracts. Gazprom invoked force majeure in respect of reductions in deliveries, citing the unavailability of turbines for the Nord Stream pipeline owing to sanctions, an argument rejected by European buyers. As noted in Chapter 6, an arbitral tribunal awarded the German company Uniper more than EUR 13 billion in damages against Gazprom Export for non-delivery, and other European buyers obtained awards. In northern Mozambique, TotalEnergies declared force majeure on the Mozambique LNG project in April 2021 following an attack by insurgents on the town of Palma, suspending construction for an extended period; the declaration affected contractors, lenders, and the host government, and illustrates the use of force majeure in the context of armed conflict. The COVID-19 pandemic led to numerous force majeure declarations, including by Chinese LNG buyers in early 2020, which were largely rejected by sellers, and construction delays, whose treatment under EPC contracts depended on the precise wording of force majeure and change-in-law clauses.
