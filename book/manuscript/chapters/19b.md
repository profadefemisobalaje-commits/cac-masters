## 19.3 Equitable and Reasonable Utilization

### The Principle

The principle of **equitable and reasonable utilisation** is the cornerstone of international watercourse law. Article 5(1) of the UN Watercourses Convention provides that watercourse states shall in their respective territories utilise an international watercourse in an equitable and reasonable manner, and in particular shall use and develop it with a view to attaining optimal and sustainable utilisation thereof and benefits therefrom, taking into account the interests of the watercourse states concerned, consistent with adequate protection of the watercourse. Article 5(2) adds the principle of **equitable participation**: watercourse states shall participate in the use, development, and protection of an international watercourse in an equitable and reasonable manner, including both the right to utilise the watercourse and the duty to cooperate in its protection and development.

The principle does not require an equal division of waters, but an equitable one, determined by reference to all relevant factors. It confers on each riparian state a right to an equitable and reasonable share of the uses and benefits of the watercourse, and correspondingly limits each state's use to its equitable share. The application of the principle is inherently contextual, and its indeterminacy is both its strength — allowing it to accommodate the diversity of watercourses and the evolution of needs — and its weakness, since it provides limited guidance to states in dispute.

### Relevant Factors

Article 6(1) of the Convention lists factors and circumstances relevant to equitable and reasonable utilisation:

- geographic, hydrographic, hydrological, climatic, ecological, and other factors of a natural character;
- the social and economic needs of the watercourse states concerned;
- the population dependent on the watercourse in each watercourse state;
- the effects of the use or uses of the watercourse in one watercourse state on other watercourse states;
- existing and potential uses of the watercourse;
- conservation, protection, development, and economy of use of the water resources of the watercourse and the costs of measures taken to that effect; and
- the availability of alternatives, of comparable value, to a particular planned or existing use.

Article 6(3) provides that the weight to be given to each factor is to be determined by its importance in comparison with that of other relevant factors, and that in determining what is a reasonable and equitable use, all relevant factors are to be considered together and a conclusion reached on the basis of the whole. Article 10 provides that, in the absence of agreement or custom to the contrary, no use of an international watercourse enjoys inherent priority over other uses, but that in the event of a conflict, special regard is to be given to the requirements of **vital human needs** — understood as sufficient water to sustain human life, including drinking water and water for the production of food to prevent starvation.

The inclusion of "existing and potential uses" among the factors is significant for disputes between states that have historically developed a river — often downstream states — and states that seek to develop it for the first time, often upstream. Existing uses are relevant but not decisive; they do not confer an absolute right to the continuation of historic shares, but must be weighed against the potential uses of other states. This issue lies at the heart of the dispute over the Nile, examined in section 19.5.

### The Relationship with the No-Harm Rule

The relationship between the principle of equitable utilisation and the obligation not to cause significant harm, in Article 7, was the most contentious issue in the negotiation of the Convention. Downstream states generally favoured the primacy of the no-harm rule, which protects existing uses against interference, while upstream states favoured the primacy of equitable utilisation, which permits new uses that may cause harm to others if they are equitable. The compromise reached in the Convention subordinates the no-harm rule to equitable utilisation in substance, while preserving its procedural significance: Article 7(1) requires watercourse states to take "all appropriate measures" to prevent the causing of significant harm — an obligation of due diligence rather than an absolute prohibition — and Article 7(2) provides that where significant harm is nevertheless caused, the state whose use causes it shall, in consultation with the affected state, take all appropriate measures, having due regard to Articles 5 and 6, to eliminate or mitigate the harm and, where appropriate, to discuss the question of compensation. The prevailing interpretation is that a use causing significant harm may nevertheless be permissible if it is equitable and reasonable in the light of all relevant factors, but that the causing of significant harm is a factor weighing heavily against the equitableness of a use, and that harm to vital human needs is particularly difficult to justify.

### Equitable Utilisation in the Jurisprudence

The leading judicial application of the principle is the decision of the International Court of Justice in the *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros* case, which concerned a project for the joint development of hydropower on the Danube.

::: casebox {title="Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary/Slovakia) [1997] ICJ Rep 7"}
**Background.** In 1977, Hungary and Czechoslovakia concluded a treaty providing for the construction and joint operation of a system of locks, comprising a barrage and hydropower plant at Gabčíkovo in Czechoslovakia and another at Nagymaros in Hungary, on the stretch of the Danube forming their common border. The project was designed to produce hydroelectricity, improve navigation, and protect against floods. In the late 1980s, amid growing environmental concerns and political change, Hungary suspended and then abandoned work on its part of the project. Czechoslovakia proceeded with a unilateral alternative, known as "Variant C", which involved the diversion of the Danube into a bypass canal on Czechoslovak territory, putting it into operation in 1992 and diverting the majority of the river's flow. Hungary purported to terminate the treaty in 1992. After the dissolution of Czechoslovakia, Slovakia succeeded to its rights and obligations.

**The decision.** The Court held that Hungary was not entitled to suspend and abandon the works, rejecting its plea of a "state of ecological necessity", because the alleged environmental perils were not sufficiently certain and imminent and Hungary had other means of responding to them. The Court also held that Hungary's notification of termination did not terminate the treaty, rejecting arguments based on necessity, impossibility of performance, fundamental change of circumstances, material breach, and the development of new norms of environmental law. Conversely, the Court held that Czechoslovakia was entitled to proceed with preparatory works for Variant C, but not to put it into operation, because by unilaterally assuming control of a shared resource and depriving Hungary of its right to an equitable and reasonable share of the natural resources of the Danube, Czechoslovakia had failed to respect the proportionality required by international law. Referring to the community of interest in an international river and to the draft articles of the International Law Commission, the Court described Hungary's right to an equitable and reasonable share as a basic right.

**Sustainable development and evolving norms.** The Court observed that, owing to new scientific insights and a growing awareness of the risks for mankind of interventions in nature, new norms and standards had been developed, which must be taken into consideration and given proper weight not only when states contemplate new activities but also when continuing activities begun in the past; this need to reconcile economic development with protection of the environment, it said, is aptly expressed in the concept of sustainable development. The Court held that the treaty remained in force, and directed the parties to negotiate in good faith, taking account of the objectives of the treaty and the norms of international environmental law, to find an agreed solution, including on the environmental consequences of the operation of the Gabčíkovo plant.

**Significance.** The judgment remains the leading authority on equitable utilisation, the community of interest in international rivers, the stability of treaties governing energy infrastructure in the face of changed circumstances, and the relevance of evolving environmental norms to long-term projects. Notably, the parties have not, more than a quarter of a century later, reached a full agreement implementing the judgment, illustrating the limits of judicial settlement in complex water disputes.
:::

The principle of equitable utilisation has also been applied in arbitral decisions, including the *Lac Lanoux* arbitration, examined in Chapter 17, and the *Indus Waters Kishenganga* arbitration, examined in section 19.5, and in state practice through the allocation of waters in basin treaties.

## 19.4 Transboundary Water Cooperation

### The Duty to Cooperate

Article 8 of the UN Watercourses Convention provides that watercourse states shall cooperate on the basis of sovereign equality, territorial integrity, mutual benefit, and good faith in order to attain optimal utilisation and adequate protection of an international watercourse, and that they may consider the establishment of joint mechanisms or commissions. Cooperation is both a legal obligation and a practical necessity: the optimal development of a river basin, including the siting of dams where they will produce the most power with the least harm, often requires that states plan jointly rather than individually, and the management of floods, droughts, and pollution requires the exchange of data and coordinated responses. The duty to cooperate is given content by the procedural obligations of notification, consultation, and the exchange of information, examined in Chapter 17, and by the establishment of joint institutions.

### River Basin Organisations

Joint institutions for the management of shared waters — river basin organisations (RBOs) — vary widely in their mandates and powers. At one end of the spectrum are bodies with limited functions, such as the exchange of data and the coordination of national plans; at the other are organisations with powers to plan, build, own, and operate infrastructure, and to allocate waters among member states. Prominent examples include:

- the **International Joint Commission**, established by the Boundary Waters Treaty of 1909 between the United States and the United Kingdom (on behalf of Canada), which approves projects affecting boundary waters and investigates matters referred to it by the governments, and is often cited as a model of long-standing bilateral cooperation;
- the **International Commission for the Protection of the Rhine** and the **International Commission for the Protection of the Danube River**, which coordinate environmental protection in two of Europe's most heavily used rivers;
- the **Mekong River Commission**, established by the 1995 Agreement on the Cooperation for the Sustainable Development of the Mekong River Basin among Cambodia, Laos, Thailand, and Viet Nam, whose effectiveness is limited by the non-membership of China, the upstream state, and by the weakness of its procedures for prior consultation on mainstream dams, as illustrated by the construction of the Xayaburi dam in Laos despite the objections of downstream states; and
- the **Nile Basin Initiative**, launched in 1999 as a transitional partnership among Nile riparian states pending the conclusion of a permanent legal and institutional framework, examined in section 19.5.

### African River Basin Organisations

Africa has a rich tradition of basin institutions, reflecting the large number of shared rivers on the continent.

**The Senegal River Basin Development Organisation** (Organisation pour la mise en valeur du fleuve Sénégal, OMVS), established in 1972 by Mali, Mauritania, and Senegal, and joined by Guinea in 2006, is frequently cited as one of the most advanced examples of basin cooperation in the world. Under the conventions governing the OMVS, the Senegal River is declared an international river, and common infrastructure — including the Manantali dam and hydropower plant in Mali and the Diama anti-salt barrage near the river's mouth — is the **joint property** of the member states, which share the costs and benefits according to an agreed formula, including the allocation of electricity from Manantali among Mali, Mauritania, and Senegal. The **Senegal River Water Charter** (2002) establishes principles for the allocation of water among uses, including the priority of drinking water, and procedures for approving new projects. The OMVS model illustrates the concept of **benefit-sharing** — the allocation among states of the benefits derived from the use of a river, rather than of the water itself — which can facilitate cooperation by allowing states to focus on the optimal development of the basin as a whole.

**The Niger Basin Authority** (NBA), established by the Convention Creating the Niger Basin Authority of 1980 as successor to the Niger River Commission of 1964, comprises nine member states: Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Chad, Côte d'Ivoire, Guinea, Mali, Niger, and Nigeria. Its **Water Charter**, adopted in 2008, establishes principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation, the obligation not to cause significant harm, prior notification of planned measures, environmental protection, and the participation of water users, and provides for the allocation of water among uses and states, the maintenance of minimum flows, and the joint review of new infrastructure. The NBA has developed a Shared Vision and Sustainable Development Action Plan identifying priority infrastructure, including dams such as Kandadji in Niger, Taoussa in Mali, and Fomi in Guinea, whose effects on downstream flows — including the inflows to Nigeria's Kainji and Jebba hydropower plants and the Inner Niger Delta wetland in Mali — require coordinated management.

**The Lake Chad Basin Commission** (LCBC), established in 1964 by Cameroon, Chad, Niger, and Nigeria, later joined by the Central African Republic and Libya, addresses a basin in which the surface area of the lake has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s, owing to drought, climate variability, and increased water abstraction, with severe consequences for livelihoods and contributing to insecurity in the region. The LCBC adopted a Water Charter in 2012, and has studied the controversial "Transaqua" proposal for an inter-basin transfer of water from the Congo basin to replenish the lake.

**The Zambezi River Authority**, established by Zambia and Zimbabwe in 1987, owns and operates the Kariba dam on their common border, while the hydropower stations on each bank are owned by the national utilities, and the **Zambezi Watercourse Commission** (ZAMCOM), whose agreement was concluded in 2004 and entered into force in 2011, provides a framework for basin-wide cooperation among the eight Zambezi riparian states. The **Orange-Senqu River Commission** (ORASECOM), established in 2000 by Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, and South Africa, and the bilateral treaty of 1986 between Lesotho and South Africa establishing the **Lesotho Highlands Water Project**, under which water is transferred from the highlands of Lesotho to South Africa's industrial heartland in return for royalties and hydropower for Lesotho, illustrate further models of cooperation.

### Data Sharing, Flood Management, and Dam Operations

The exchange of data and information, required by Article 9 of the UN Watercourses Convention, is a fundamental element of cooperation, particularly in relation to the operation of dams. The release of water from upstream reservoirs during periods of heavy rainfall can cause severe flooding downstream, and the timely notification of releases is critical to allow downstream authorities to warn and evacuate populations. Nigeria has experienced severe floods, notably in 2012 and 2022, which were exacerbated by releases of water from the **Lagdo dam** on the Benue River in Cameroon, during periods of exceptional rainfall, causing loss of life, the displacement of large numbers of people, and extensive damage to property and agriculture. The floods have led to calls for improved notification and coordination between Cameroon and Nigeria, including through the Nigeria Hydrological Services Agency and the Niger Basin Authority, and for the construction of the long-planned Dasin Hausa dam in Nigeria to buffer releases from Lagdo. The case illustrates the importance of procedural obligations of notification and the exchange of data, and of agreed operating rules for dams on shared rivers.

### Benefit-Sharing and the Political Economy of Cooperation

Cooperation over shared waters is shaped by asymmetries of power, geography, and development. Upstream states are often in a stronger position to develop rivers, but downstream states may be more powerful militarily or economically, as in the cases of Egypt on the Nile and India on certain rivers shared with Nepal and Bangladesh. The concept of benefit-sharing has been promoted as a means of moving beyond zero-sum disputes over water quantities, by identifying the benefits that cooperative development can generate — hydropower, irrigation, flood control, navigation, environmental protection — and negotiating their distribution, including through the trade of electricity across borders. The development of regional power pools, such as the West African Power Pool and the Southern African Power Pool, examined in Chapter 9, creates opportunities for benefit-sharing from hydropower, since electricity generated in one state can be exported to others.
