# Introduction {.front}

## The Field

International energy and natural resources law is the body of international, regional, transnational, and national legal rules, principles, institutions, and practices that govern the ownership, exploration, development, production, transformation, transportation, trade, consumption, and conservation of energy and natural resources, and the allocation of the benefits, risks, and burdens that these activities generate. It is not a single, codified body of law. It is, rather, a field defined by its subject matter, drawing on many legal disciplines: public international law (sovereignty, treaties, state responsibility, the law of the sea), international economic law (trade and investment), international environmental and climate law, international human rights law, constitutional and administrative law, contract and commercial law, property law, tax and public finance law, corporate law, and the law of dispute resolution.

The field matters because energy and natural resources are foundational to economic life, central to state revenues in many countries, decisive for the climate and the environment, and deeply entangled with questions of power, conflict, and justice. Its law determines who owns resources and on what terms they may be exploited; how rents are shared between states, investors, and communities; how markets for oil, gas, electricity, and minerals are structured and regulated; how environmental harm is prevented and remedied; how the rights of affected people are protected; how disputes are resolved; and how societies manage the transition from an energy system dominated by fossil fuels to one built on low-carbon sources.

## Why This Book, and Why Now

Three developments make an integrated, advanced treatment of the field especially timely.

First, the **energy transition** is transforming the field's subject matter. The law of petroleum and coal, which long dominated the discipline, must now be studied alongside the law of renewable energy, storage, hydrogen, carbon capture, and critical minerals — and in the shadow of climate obligations that, as the International Court of Justice's 2025 advisory opinion makes clear, bear directly on decisions about fossil fuel production. The questions of managed decline, stranded assets, decommissioning, and just transition have moved from the margins to the centre.

Second, the field's **centre of gravity is shifting**. Most of the growth in energy demand is now in emerging and developing economies; Africa holds much of the world's renewable potential and transition minerals and most of the world's people without electricity; and producer states in Africa, Latin America, and Asia are asserting greater control over their resources and greater value from them. The perspectives and legal experience of these regions demand attention commensurate with their importance.

Third, the field's **legal architecture is in flux**. Investment treaty protection is being reformed, and in some cases abandoned, as illustrated by the withdrawal of the European Union and many of its member states from the Energy Charter Treaty. Trade law is under strain from green industrial policy, carbon border measures, and geopolitical rivalry. Human rights and corporate accountability are being translated into binding due diligence obligations. Climate litigation is expanding. Dispute resolution is being reshaped. At the national level, countries including Nigeria have undertaken fundamental reforms of their petroleum and electricity laws.

## Structure of the Book

The book is organised in eight Parts.

**Part I — Jurisprudential and Institutional Foundations (Chapters 1–4)** establishes the conceptual framework. Chapter 1 examines the nature, scope, and evolution of the field, from the early concessions to the energy transition. Chapter 2 analyses its sources and principles — treaties, custom, general principles, soft law, and the principles of permanent sovereignty, prevention, precaution, sustainable development, and common but differentiated responsibilities. Chapter 3 addresses resource sovereignty, state ownership, and constitutional governance, including models of ownership, the role of national resource companies, and constitutional resource provisions. Chapter 4 examines the international institutions and regulatory systems that govern energy, from OPEC and the IEA to the IAEA, IRENA, and regional bodies.

**Part II — Petroleum, Gas, and Extractive Industries Law (Chapters 5–8)** treats the law of the extractive industries. Chapter 5 examines upstream petroleum law, licensing, and the principal contractual models. Chapter 6 addresses natural gas law, LNG transactions, and international gas markets. Chapter 7 treats mining law and mineral governance. Chapter 8 examines critical minerals, strategic resources, and supply chain security.

**Part III — Electricity, Renewable Energy, and Emerging Technologies (Chapters 9–12)** addresses the law of the power sector and new technologies: electricity law and market regulation (Chapter 9), renewable energy law (Chapter 10), nuclear energy law (Chapter 11), and hydrogen, storage, carbon capture, offshore renewables, smart grids, and regulatory sandboxes (Chapter 12).

**Part IV — International Trade, Investment, and Energy Contracts (Chapters 13–16)** examines the international economic law of energy: trade law (Chapter 13), investment law, including the Energy Charter Treaty and its modernisation (Chapter 14), energy contracts and risk allocation (Chapter 15), and project finance, taxation, and fiscal governance (Chapter 16).

**Part V — Environmental Law, Climate Governance, and Resource Sustainability (Chapters 17–20)** addresses environmental protection: international environmental law (Chapter 17), climate change law and the energy transition (Chapter 18), water resources law (Chapter 19), and marine resources, offshore energy, and the law of the sea, including deep seabed mining (Chapter 20).

**Part VI — Human Rights, Corporate Responsibility, and Resource Justice (Chapters 21–23)** examines the human dimension: human rights, indigenous peoples, and extractive industry accountability (Chapter 21); corporate governance, compliance, and anti-corruption (Chapter 22); and energy justice, resource equity, and sustainable development (Chapter 23).

**Part VII — Comparative Law, Geopolitics, and International Dispute Resolution (Chapters 24–27)** provides comparative perspective (Chapter 24), examines energy geopolitics and security (Chapter 25), and analyses international arbitration (Chapter 26) and litigation, judicial review, and regulatory enforcement (Chapter 27).

**Part VIII — Doctoral Scholarship, Legal Reform, and Professional Leadership (Chapters 28–30)** turns to the reader's own contribution: advanced research methodology (Chapter 28), law reform, regulatory design, and policy leadership (Chapter 29), and the future of the field (Chapter 30).

The **appendices** provide practical and research materials: selected treaties; model and annotated agreements (petroleum, production sharing, gas sales, renewable power purchase); compliance checklists and frameworks (mining, environmental and social impact assessment, human rights due diligence, climate and transition compliance); risk allocation, finance, and fiscal templates; arbitration clauses and procedural documents; comparative regulatory frameworks; templates and criteria for doctoral proposals and dissertations; a research, citation, and case analysis guide; selected judicial decisions; drafting and advisory exercises; a terminology glossary; and further research topics.

## Distinguishing Overlapping Themes

Some themes recur across chapters, and the book has been designed so that each treatment adds a distinct perspective rather than repeating another. For example, resource sovereignty is examined as a constitutional and ownership question in Chapter 3 and as a matter of investment protection and expropriation in Chapter 14; contractual models are introduced as instruments of upstream regulation in Chapter 5 and analysed as instruments of commercial risk allocation in Chapter 15; investor–state arbitration is examined substantively in Chapter 14 and procedurally in Chapter 26; climate obligations are analysed as a regime in Chapter 18 and as the subject of litigation in Chapter 27; hydropower is treated as a matter of energy regulation in Chapter 10 and of water allocation and transboundary governance in Chapter 19; and environmental principles are introduced in Chapter 17 and applied to marine resources in Chapter 20. Cross-references guide the reader between related treatments.

## Africa and Nigeria

Africa, and Nigeria in particular, receive sustained attention throughout. The book examines Nigeria's Petroleum Industry Act 2021 and its institutional, fiscal, and host community provisions; the Electricity Act 2023 and the decentralisation of electricity regulation to the states; the Climate Change Act 2021; the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007; the litigation over oil pollution in the Niger Delta in Nigerian, English, and Dutch courts and before the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights; and the role of NEITI. It examines regional frameworks, including ECOWAS, the West African Power Pool, the African Continental Free Trade Area, and the Africa Mining Vision, and the experience of other African states in mining, petroleum, and renewable energy governance.

## A Note on Sources and Hypotheticals

The book is grounded in primary authority. Where it describes treaties, legislation, cases, and awards, it does so on the basis of the authoritative texts and reliable reports, and recent or uncertain developments are described with appropriate caution. The practical exercises frequently use invented states, companies, communities, and disputes to allow readers to practise professional skills. **All such material is expressly labelled hypothetical**; it does not describe real events, and any resemblance to actual persons, companies, or disputes is coincidental.
